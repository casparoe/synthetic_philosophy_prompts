I'm a senior software engineer at a direct-to-consumer genomics company, and I sit on our Product and Model Review Committee, which has to sign off before any model output is shown to customers. I have no formal training in philosophy or in quantitative genetics, and I have no access to journals from here, so I need the committee's reasoning to be self-contained: please don't cite papers or point me to a reading list, and please flag explicitly any empirical claim you aren't confident about rather than sourcing it. I'm comfortable with regression, variance decomposition, and out-of-distribution generalization, so feel free to lean on those analogies, but please define the specialist vocabulary (broad- and narrow-sense heritability, gene–environment correlation, genetic nurture, stratification) as you go.

The proposal in front of us is "Learning Potential," an add-on report for parents who have already had their child genotyped through one of our kits. It displays a percentile derived from a polygenic score for educational attainment, with two comparison modes: against the general population, and — this is the part that worries me most — side by side against siblings in the same family.

The product spec's explanatory copy currently reads: "How much of a child's success at school comes from their DNA? Scientists estimate that about 40% of the differences between people in years of schooling is genetic. Learning Potential measures that genetic component directly. Your child's result reflects their natural learning capacity — the part that teaching, tutoring and schooling don't change."

The data science team's own numbers, which I trust, are these: the score explains roughly 12% of the variance in years of schooling in a held-out sample drawn from the same population it was trained on; the individual-level prediction interval is about plus or minus three years of schooling, which is wide enough that most children are statistically indistinguishable from most others; the reference panel is overwhelmingly of one continental ancestry, and accuracy degrades substantially for customers outside it; and the headline "about 40%" comes from a between-family estimate, while a sibling-difference design on the same cohort returns a figure roughly half that size.

My own contribution to the discussion was a proposed rule I've been calling the transported-environments check, borrowed from how we think about distribution shift in ML: a trait-level claim anchored to a heritability estimate may be shown to users only if the score's calibration is stable across the environments a user could plausibly choose between — school quality, tutoring hours, whether a learning difficulty gets treated — because if the prediction's validity depends on conditions that the parent's own reading of the report will then change, presenting it as a fixed capacity is incoherent. Several colleagues think this test is either too vague to implement or so demanding that it would ban every such product, and they may be right.

The committee is split. The strongest case for shipping is anti-paternalism: the underlying estimate is public, parents are entitled to their own family's genomic data, uncertainty can be shown in the UI rather than suppressed, and refusing to ship mainly pushes people toward unregulated apps that present the same numbers far more crudely. There is also a support ticket that has been circulating internally, from a mother of a child later diagnosed with dyslexia, who says seeing a low score ended two years of blaming herself and her child and is what prompted her to seek an assessment. Against that, we have a ticket from a parent who concluded from a low score that tutoring would be wasted and withdrew from a programme the child had been benefiting from.

Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting, addressed to the committee, roughly 900 words, with memo headings. It should:

1. Give a bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — as a concrete set of actions with owners and a timeline, not a set of considerations. If conditions, specify which parts of the feature (the sibling-comparison mode, the general-population percentile, the explanatory copy, the age range, the ancestry groups we have validation data for) are affected and how.

2. Include a short section on what a heritability estimate does and does not license, written so the product team can turn it into UI rules. In particular I need the committee to settle: whether a population-level variance ratio supports any claim about an individual child's causes; whether the headline figure should be the broad-sense or the narrow-sense quantity given that our score is a weighted sum of additive effects; whether the between-family or the within-family estimate is the honest number for a feature whose whole point is comparing children who share a home and parents; and what it means that part of what the score predicts may be the environment the parents go on to provide rather than something intrinsic to the child.

3. Rule on my transported-environments check: adopt it, narrow it into something auditable, or replace it with a better test. If you replace it, say what the replacement requires us to measure before we can ship.

4. Handle a point I can't get straight myself. It seems to me that heritability is a ratio defined relative to a particular population in a particular range of environments, so that making environments more equal — guaranteeing everyone the same schooling, say — can raise the heritability figure while raising everyone's actual outcome. If that's right, then a high number is fully compatible with environmental changes mattering enormously, and the parent who cancelled tutoring has the inference backwards. But I want the committee to say this in its own words and in a form we could put in the FAQ, and to tell me if I've overstated it.

5. Give the losing side its best day in court. If you recommend holding or dropping the feature, engage the anti-paternalism argument and the dyslexia ticket seriously, and say what would have to be true for you to change your recommendation.
