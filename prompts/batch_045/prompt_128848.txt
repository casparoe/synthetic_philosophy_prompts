For a profile I'm writing on Dr. Miriam Alderton, a bioethicist who has spent six years on the national commission that reviews any proposal involving payment for bodily material or bodily services, I have been trying to work out what principle she is actually running, because her voting record does not match the soundbite she is usually quoted for. Her positions, as best I can reconstruct them from the commission's published opinions, from two dissents she wrote, and from a long interview she gave to a medical journal: she has defended compensated plasma donation by for-profit centres, on the ground that the people who sell plasma are not dupes and that there is no alternative supply; she has voted to permit compensation for egg donors provided it is capped and cannot be scaled to the donor's characteristics; she has approved modest payments to healthy volunteers in early-phase drug trials for time and burden; she opposes commercial gestational surrogacy even where the surrogate is well paid, independently counselled and under no financial pressure; she has twice voted against any market in kidneys from living vendors; she described cash-for-sterilisation schemes aimed at poor women as the clearest case of a market that should not exist; she would criminalise vote buying and vote selling; she supports laws against disaster price gouging; and she would prohibit reselling tickets or queue positions for free public museums and civic hearings. I have been reading around in the literature she cites — the noxious markets framework, the blocked exchanges tradition, the personhood-based inalienability arguments — and each of them seems to fit one part of her record and strain badly on another: if it is the sheer fact of the body being sold, why are plasma and eggs fine? If it is that the badly off are pushed into it, why is paid trial participation fine? If it is that prices corrupt a gift relation, why is that not true of eggs as well?

The item on next month's agenda, which I will be in the room for, is a government-run living-donor benefit that is designed to give each of those objections as little to bite on as possible. Nothing is bought or sold between private parties and no price is ever negotiated: the national transplant authority would offer every living kidney donor an identical statutory package — a small inflation-indexed annuity paid monthly over twenty years rather than a lump sum, free lifetime follow-up care and insurance cover for donation-related complications, and a transferable family credit that would move one named relative up the waiting list if that relative ever needs a kidney. Eligibility is not means-tested in either direction, there are no brokers or intermediaries, donation cannot be directed to the person who would benefit from the credit, and there is a mandatory cooling-off period with independent counselling and a formal psychological assessment. Her commission has rejected cash payment to living vendors twice, so on the face of it the answer looks easy, but the package has no price, no market and no haggling, and I genuinely cannot tell whether she will treat that as the removal of the objection or as a cosmetic change to it.

What I want, before I put this to her in our last interview, is help reconstructing the machinery rather than the verdict. What is the rule that generates her whole track record at once, and what are the other rules that fit those same verdicts equally well? What does each of those candidate rules predict about this donor-benefit proposal specifically — including the deferred annuity, the absence of any price, and the family credit, which introduces a third party who may now have an interest in someone else's kidney? Which of those rules is the best guess for what she is actually running, and why, given that she is not a paternalist about the poor in the plasma case and not a sentimentalist about the body in the gametes case? And what single hypothetical should I put to her that would separate the surviving rules cleanly, ideally one she has not been asked a hundred times already — a version with strictly in-kind and non-transferable benefits, an identical package offered for non-directed bone marrow or liver-lobe donation, a version in which a private philanthropy rather than the state funds it, or something I have not thought of? If it helps, say which parts of her record are doing the most work in pinning the rule down and which are nearly cost-free for her to hold.
