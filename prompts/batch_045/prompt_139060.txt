Two friends of mine — both historians, sort of — got into it at a barbecue about a chapter our friend Ravi is writing, and I ended up as the referee without wanting the job. i have no stake in it, I just want to know who's actually right about what.

Ravi's chapter is about a 1908 uprising in the Arakkan delta, and he keeps writing as if a crop fungus were one of the characters — sentences like "by July the rust had taken the delta's choices away from it." Dev says that's anthropomorphism with footnotes. A fungus has no beliefs, no aims, no reasons; it's a link in a causal chain, not an actor, and calling it one is a way of avoiding having to say who did anything.

Maya says that's too quick. Plenty of things historians happily treat as actors aren't agents — markets, demographics, troop morale, a regiment that loses its nerve — so if intentions are the test you've quietly signed up for great-man history where only named humans ever make anything happen. And on her reading the rust explains more of the timing and the scale of the uprising than any single official's decision does, so dropping it in order to keep the agency language clean is just worse history.

Dev says he's not making a metaphysical point, he's making a political one: environmental causes get wheeled in to let the powerful off. The framing makes the grain shipments out of the delta during the famine start to look like weather. And there's a selection problem — once you admit non-human actors, why stop at the rust? Soil chemistry, the monsoon, the price of jute in Liverpool, the volcano whose aerosols cooled the monsoon two years earlier: nothing in the method tells you where to stop, so the stopping point is political anyway.

Maya's answer: the unboundedness is just as bad for human actors, nobody thinks a general's breakfast is part of the story, we cut off where difference-making fades. Non-human causes are actually easier to discipline than intentions because you can put numbers on them. And she has a distinction she's fond of: germs, weather and volcanoes write the schedule — when something happens, how hard it lands — while institutions write the script, meaning what the live options are. On that view bringing in the rust excuses nobody; it explains timing, and the grain shipments stay a choice.

They both turned to me and I honestly couldnt tell. Can you go through it instead? Give me exactly five numbered lines, no intro and no summing up: one per move, a sentence or two each, saying what's right and what's off in it — and make the fifth line about what you think both of them are missing.
