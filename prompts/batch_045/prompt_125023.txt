Write a short story of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words that dramatizes — rather than argues in the form of a dialogue with a mouthpiece — the question of whether a long-run theory can be "right but early," and, if it can, what distinguishes a legitimate correction of that kind from an evasion.

The following background should be treated as given and should not be recited inside the story. In an anonymous essay published in 1798, Thomas Malthus argued that human population, when unchecked, grows in something like a geometrical ratio while the means of subsistence can grow at best in something like an arithmetical one. Any gain in the condition of the labouring poor is therefore eaten up by the additional mouths it calls into being: numbers press against the food supply, real wages are driven back toward subsistence, and the pressure is relieved only by positive checks — famine, disease, war — or forestalled by preventive ones, above all delayed marriage and restraint. What happened instead is that, at almost the moment he wrote, the world he wrote about began a sustained escape. Yields, then incomes, rose faster than population; and when fertility eventually fell, it fell first in the richest societies, as a consequence of prosperity rather than a cause of its absence.

The interesting defences of Malthus are not the crude ones. Take two, both of which your characters may deploy. The first is that he correctly described the dominant regime of the preceding ten millennia and was merely unlucky about the century he happened to write in: the trap was real, and the escape was a local, recent, and possibly temporary exception. The second, harder defence is that the escape was purchased rather than achieved — that the ceiling on subsistence was lifted by drawing down a finite inheritance of fossil fuels, synthetic nitrogen fertiliser, mined phosphate, and fossil groundwater, and that a reprieve financed by a one-off stock is not a refutation of the mechanism but a mortgage taken out against it. Against these, there is a rival tradition holding that population pressure tends to provoke the very innovations that lift the ceiling, in which case the mechanism would seem to be self-defeating: the harder it pushes, the more it produces its own undoing.

Set the story at a single institutional occasion. A trust, established in the twentieth century by an admirer, is to pay out when a panel of three certifies that the Malthusian mechanism has reasserted itself in the human population as a whole. The hearing in your story is the most recent in a long series; every twenty-five years the panel has declined to certify, and each refusal has had to be justified in writing, so that the accumulated record of deferrals has itself become the most contested item of evidence. The deed fixes the trigger in the vocabulary of Malthus's own century — something like a condition in which the number of the people is limited by the means of their subsistence rather than by their own choice — and the panel must now apply that phrasing to a twenty-second-century world in which the binding scarcities are arable soil, fresh water, and one other thing you should choose, and in which almost nobody is a member of a "labouring family" in the sense the deed assumes.

Requirements. The three panellists must disagree for reasons a reasonable reader could hold; one of them should be forced to change position or to pay a real cost. The outcome should turn on at least one of these distinctions, and the story should make the distinction operative in the decision rather than mentioning it in passing: (i) a claim about a mechanism versus a claim about a dated outcome; (ii) deferral as honest updating versus deferral as the shifting of a deadline, which one character in the story should name and defend as an independent doctrine of "deferred vindication"; (iii) whether a mechanism that is held in abeyance for centuries by a finite inheritance is thereby refuted or merely postponed; (iv) whether a mechanism that provokes its own circumvention survives being circumvented. Do not resolve the matter by simply vindicating Malthus — no famines, no collapse — nor by simply refuting him; the case worth writing about is the one in the middle.

Finally: write it as fiction rather than as a lecture. Use scenes, give the hearing at least one concrete physical and procedural detail — the wording of the deed, the voting rule, the room, the archive of past refusals — and let no character deliver a passage that could be lifted out whole as an essay. Do not let any narrator tell the reader what to conclude.
