I am writing the centrepiece case for a short decision-making module I run for mid-career managers, and I want something that does a job the standard examples don't. So let me fix two pieces of vocabulary first, since the case has to be built around them.

Call an expenditure that has already gone out the door *thinly sunk* if, from here on, the only remaining way its size could bear on what to do is through the agent's attitudes toward it — reluctance to admit waste, the sting of writing the number down, the wish not to have been wrong. Call it *thickly sunk* if the magnitude of what has already been spent changes the forward-looking payoffs themselves, so that even a scrupulously forward-looking calculation of the kind that fully accepts the sunk-cost principle still has to consult the amount. The other thing I want carried over from the dollar auction is its structural core and nothing else: the loser pays too. Withdrawing forfeits everything already spent outright, and the only route to not forfeiting it is to keep going and end up the one who prevails. I do not want an auction, though — I want that structure hiding inside an ordinary situation.

So: give me one paragraph, at most 150 words, set in ordinary commercial or organisational life — no sale rooms, casinos, poker tables, military campaigns or game shows — containing two parties, A and B, in a single shared venture with identical numbers, both subject to the loser-pays structure, both having just received the same two rounds of unambiguous negative feedback. Constraints. (i) Both must have been reasonable to make the initial outlay; nobody was reckless to start. (ii) A escalates — commits more after each round of bad news — and A's escalating must be genuinely expected-utility-maximising on the public facts alone: no private tip, no assumption that the counterparty is irrational or about to fold, and no retrospective discovery that the project was sound all along. (iii) B, facing the same costs, revenues, feedback and counterparty, must be genuinely required to quit. (iv) The only asymmetry between them is that the outlay is thickly sunk for A and thinly sunk for B, and I want the thick mechanism to be one that a critic cannot dismiss as merely relabelling the project — the venture, its costs and its prospects should be the same object for both, with the difference lying purely in what the already-spent money does further downstream. (v) On the surface the case must still read as textbook escalation of commitment: an observer who knows the feedback history, the costs and the payoffs but has not yet been shown the downstream consequence must judge that A and B face the same choice and must diagnose A as irrationally throwing good money after bad.

After the scenario, show your work rather than stipulating the verdicts. Give the numbers and the expected-value comparison for each party, so that the verdicts fall out of the case instead of being announced. Then name the single feature doing the work, say what the case collapses into if that feature is removed, and give the strongest version of the objection from someone who holds that escalation in loser-pays structures is always a fallacy — I expect it to be some form of "your thick mechanism just changes the project, so this was never a case of sunk costs mattering" — and tell me what you think that objection actually establishes.

Two further requests. Separate what is settled and uncontroversial among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present the strategic or rational-choice reading of escalation as the consensus position if it is only one reading; in particular, flag which claim in your construction is the load-bearing empirical one and say plainly how confident you are that mechanisms of the kind you have used really behave the way your case needs them to. And keep the whole response under 750 words, no more than 150 of them for the scenario itself.
