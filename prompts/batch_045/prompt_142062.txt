I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am putting together roughly a year of reporting — call it eight long features — on how psychiatric classification behaves now that diagnoses feed back into the people who receive them. I already have the basic shape of the position associated with Ian Hacking: classifying a human kind is not like classifying an element, because the people classified can learn the classification, change how they experience and present themselves, and thereby change the thing being classified, which comes back around to the classifiers. I do not need that explained, and I do not want the series to turn into another piece whose payoff is that diagnosis is socially constructed. I want it to be about which questions are genuinely unsettled, and I would like you to help me decide which four or five to build the year around.

Here is a device I am considering using in the opening piece; work with it or replace it with something better. Call it a loop audit. Before a proposed diagnostic category enters routine clinical use, its proponents would have to state in advance what effect they expect the label to have on the people who get it — on self-description, on how symptoms are reported in the clinic, on community formation online, on demand for whatever treatment is attached to the label — and would have to nominate in advance the observation that would tell them the category has become unstable, that the kind itself is drifting rather than our picture of it coming into focus. Categories that cannot pass the audit would be funded as research categories only.

Three distinctions I keep reaching for, and want you to help me place on the map: categories that amplify the phenomenon they name by spreading a vocabulary for it; categories that damp it, by making a previously isolating experience legible and manageable; and categories that are self-effacing, where the uptake of the label destroys the conditions the label was introduced to track, so that the diagnosis succeeds precisely by ceasing to apply. I would like to know whether that third kind is really a distinct case or only a special case of the second, and whether any actual candidate category is close to it.

Please give me a defended agenda rather than a survey, and specifically:

1. Which four or five open questions should the series be organized around, in what order? Defend the ordering against explicit criteria — how much other disagreement depends on the answer, whether it is answerable in a year of reporting rather than being a philosopher's question that needs a decade, and how neglected it is relative to its importance. Show me where the criteria conflict and say how you are trading them off.

2. For each question, what kind of evidence would move it up or down the list — what sort of internal document, dataset, or live disagreement among insiders would a reporter go looking for? I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I am asking what form a decisive finding would take, so that I can tell whether the question is reportable at all or only discussable.

3. Which implications of all this are oversold? Does looping give us a general argument against medicalisation, or against the reality of mental disorder, or against patient testimony as evidence, or against reading prevalence trends as information about anything, or against categories in favour of continuous dimensions? Rank these: which actually follow, which follow only when weakened almost past recognition, and which are non sequiturs that the literature keeps implying anyway.

4. One complication I want folded in rather than appended. The loop now has a third node. Records of classified patients train models that recommend classifications, and those recommendations increasingly reach people before any clinician does, through symptom checkers, triage apps, and the informal diagnostic vocabularies of online communities. This node has no clinical training, no intentions, no stake in the stability of any category, and unlike a clinician it is periodically retrained on its own output. Does that change which open questions matter, or does it only add volume to the ones already on your list?

5. Finally, what would change your ranking? Name the single finding or event that would most reorder the list if it landed midway through the year, and say why it has that leverage.

Two constraints on the answer, for context. My readers are scientifically literate but not philosophers, and a large part of the audience will be clinicians and patients who will read this as being about them — so I need an ordering I can defend to people with a stake in the outcome, not one that collapses into the conclusion that nothing in the diagnostic manual refers to anything. And I would rather have your actual ranking, with the reasoning exposed, than a balanced description of several possible rankings.
