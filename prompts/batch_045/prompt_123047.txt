My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument for innate linguistic knowledge, and I want your help deciding whether we were disagreeing about anything real. She is a statistical learning theorist; I work on large-scale pretraining. Neither of us has any formal training in linguistics or philosophy, and I'd like an answer that assumes the vocabulary I actually use and nothing else.

Her position, as I understood it: nativism about language is just the claim that a learner's hypothesis class is constrained, i.e. that it carries a nontrivial inductive bias. Every machine learning person already believes that — the No Free Lunch results tell us that a learner with no bias does no better than chance off the training distribution — so the nativist is winning an argument nobody was having. The opposing, associationist or connectionist position is then just the claim that the bias required is weak and general-purpose rather than rich and domain-specific. On her view the entire dispute therefore collapses into a single quantitative question: how much inductive bias do you need to converge on the target grammar from the corpus a child actually sees? That is a sample-complexity question, she says, and nobody has measured it, which is why the argument has been carried on with intuitions and famous examples for decades instead of with numbers.

I think that is too fast, but I couldn't say why without getting hand-wavy, so here is the best formal reconstruction I managed. Call it *evidential slack*. Fix a hypothesis class H, a target grammar G in H, and a training corpus D of child-directed speech. Let C be the set of crucial sentences — the ones that discriminate G from its nearest rivals in H; in the classic argument these are things like polar questions containing a relative clause, where a structure-dependent rule and a linear, structure-independent rule that merely front the first auxiliary-like element give different predictions. Define slack(G, D) as the ratio of the number of hypotheses in H consistent with D to the number consistent with D together with C. When slack is large, the corpus was doing very little work and the structure of H was doing most of it. My friend's response was that this is exactly her point, that slack is in principle computable, and that I should go compute it.

I suspect slack misses something, for two reasons I can only half-articulate. First, the nativist also seems to be claiming something about what the child does *not* need: that children converge without negative evidence, without correction, on degraded and incomplete input, and that they do not appear to wait around for the crucial sentences. That is not a claim about which hypotheses are consistent with the data; it is a claim about a learning dynamic reaching the target anyway. Second, the nativist's conclusion is about what the child's mind *contains afterward* — some representation with combinatorial structure — whereas in my vocabulary a system that matches the target distribution over strings can be said to know the grammar only in a deflationary sense that I don't think is what's meant.

Here is what I'd like from you.

1. Restate, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary of statistical learning theory — hypothesis class, inductive bias, sample complexity, identifiability, the version space, priors over hypotheses, architecture versus objective versus initialization, training distribution, distribution shift, pretraining and fine-tuning — the following three components of the nativist argument, taken one at a time: (a) the underdetermination claim, that the child's data is consistent with many grammars besides the right one; (b) the missing-evidence claim, that the discriminating data is absent, unusable, or unused; and (c) the domain-specificity and biological-fixity claim, that the required bias is a special-purpose, genetically specified constraint rather than a general architectural one. Then do the same for the two standard empiricist counters: the input is far richer than the nativist assumes (prosody, distributional statistics, semantic and discourse structure), and the target itself is misdescribed (the grammar is the theorist's artifact; what the child acquires is a graded disposition to respond, not a body of knowledge).

2. Give me the residue report. For each of the five: what translated cleanly; what translated only with distortion — and in those cases, say precisely what you added, dropped, or silently reinterpreted, since I want the translation to be honest rather than to smuggle learning-theoretic assumptions in under the word *translation*; and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all.

3. Then tell me what the residue means. Is evidential slack a fair reconstruction of the nativist claim, and where does it over- or undergenerate? Is my friend right that the whole thing reduces to an unmeasured sample-complexity question, or is part of it not empirical at all — a disagreement about what should count as learning, as innate, or as knowledge of a grammar? And where something resists translation, is that a genuine blind spot in the machine learning framework, something our vocabulary cannot say, or a sign that the original claim was never as sharp as it sounded? I would rather have your reasons than a survey.
