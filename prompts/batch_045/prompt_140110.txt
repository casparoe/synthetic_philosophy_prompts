I run a monthly Sunday-afternoon seminar for about fourteen people out of a local rationality meetup: mostly working programmers, two or three grad students, nobody with much academic philosophy. One room, two hours, and nothing but paper, index cards, pens, a movable screen, and a bag of poker chips for scoring. No laptops.

The session I want to plan is on the old careenium worry: a slab of alien substrate with symbol-shaped simmballs rattling around inside it, churning out behaviour that looks like it is about a world the slab has never touched. Do the meanings of the tokens do any of the pushing, or is every last collision accounted for by the physics of the substrate, with aboutness being something we read in from outside — or something constituted by the slab's causal history? My group will arrive with two opposed reflexes: that meaning obviously has to be causally efficacious, and that obviously only shapes matter. I want them to feel both, in that order, and then be unable to say which one they are describing.

Design me the activity. Constraints:

1. They must hit the problem before it is named. No lecture up front, and the terms epiphenomenalism, original intentionality, and downward causation should not be spoken until the debrief.
2. The arrangement of roles and incentives should do the teaching: some participants must manipulate tokens by shape alone and never be permitted to look at the world; a narrow feedback channel from the world should be the only thing that shapes the rulebook across rounds; and there should be a point where the token-to-world mapping is changed underneath them, so the room has to argue about what the machine is now about and whether it has started getting things wrong.
3. What they should come away seeing: that both the symbols push the substrate and the substrate pushes the symbols are available descriptions of the same run, and that the live question is what settles which description is doing the explanatory work.

Give it as exactly five numbered blocks — setup and materials; rules and scoring; a minute-by-minute timeline; the debrief, keyed to specific moments in the run; and failure modes plus the one feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to state myself. No block longer than about six sentences, no preamble, no closing summary.
