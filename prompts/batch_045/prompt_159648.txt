Right, slightly odd request, but here goes. I'm a journalist — science and ideas, mostly long pieces for magazines — and I'm about six weeks into something on algorithmic risk scores, the kind of thing cities, parole boards and housing offices buy in. I've read far enough into the technical side to know the punchline: the well-known impossibility results say you can't have calibration within groups, equal false-positive rates across groups, and equal false-negative rates across groups all at once, unless the base rates happen to match or your classifier is perfect; and there's a related family of results about individual-fairness conditions of the treat-like-cases-alike kind coming apart from any group-parity condition. Every vendor I've spoken to still says, brightly, that their system is fair.

So what I want from you is a spec, not a survey. What does a fairness criterion have to do to count as adequate for the thing I actually care about: a procurement contract. Someone in a council office has to write down, in advance, what fair means for a tool that allocates a scarce benefit — say a fixed number of places on a reentry programme, with more eligible people than places — and then has to audit it afterwards. Give me the requirements list, the case behind each one, which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp, and flag any set of them that can't be jointly satisfied. That last bit is the bit I most want, honestly: if the desiderata turn out to be jointly unsatisfiable, that's the article.

Four things I keep coming back too and can't get straight, so tell me which of them are real requirements and which are political demands in a maths costume:

- it shouldn't be satisfiable by levelling down — by making everyone worse off, or by adding noise to the scores, so the statistics match while nobody gains;
- it shouldn't be satisfiable just by choosing which groups to slice by; if it holds for the two groups named in the contract, it should hold for subgroups somebody names three years later, or at least degrade in a way you can predict;
- it has to say something about what happens when the tool's own decisions feed back into the rates it was calibrated on;
- it has to distinguish allocating a burden from allocating a benefit, and say what a hard capacity limit does to the whole question.

Which of those is load-bearing and which is a prejudice of one camp? Then, briefly — a short paragraph, not a scorecard essay — how the usual families do against the list (error-rate parity, calibration-within-group, the causal or counterfactual approaches), and which single requirement knocks out the most of what people actually sell. No reading lists or citations, I'll do my own reporting. Assume I know the headlines but not the proofs, so plain language and no notation. And write it in American English, yeah? My editor's in New York and she strips out all my -ises anyway.
