For about a year now, a reading group I run — mostly philosophy graduate students, plus two people who work on environmental policy and one who used to lobby for agricultural subsidies — has been stuck on the inefficacy objection, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. The setup is the familiar one: the harm (greenhouse gas accumulation, the demand signal that keeps factory farms operating, the outcome of a large election) is clearly produced by the group, but my single act of driving, buying chicken, or voting makes no discernible difference to it, so the argument goes that I have no duty to abstain. One member quit eating meat after our first session and went back to it after our fourth, citing exactly this argument, and nobody could tell her why she was wrong. What frustrates me is that we keep producing the same four moves and nobody ever converts. One camp says the individual act does have a positive expected difference, because production and demand are lumpy rather than smooth — a processor adjusts its orders in blocks of a few thousand animals, so there is some small probability that my purchase is the one that tips a block. A second camp replies that this is empirically optional, and that in many real cases the mechanism is smooth or the harm is already saturated — the margin in a landslide is ten million votes and no threshold is anywhere near — so expected-difference reasoning yields exactly zero, and yet they still think I have a reason. A third camp says the duty attaches to membership in a harmful practice or to the group rather than to causal difference-making by individuals: my act is a token of a practice that harms, or my participation is complicity in it, and neither requires that I make a difference. A fourth says abstaining is required by an expressive, fairness-based, or universalizability consideration and simply declines the causal framing altogether. Hovering over all of it is the replacement worry: if I don't buy the chicken, someone else does, so the difference may be undone downstream no matter how the mechanism works.

My own hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than assume, is that we are not having one argument but three: an empirical argument about the shape of real causal mechanisms (smooth, lumpy, saturated, and how much replacement eats the signal); a metaphysical argument about what it is to make a difference to an outcome (counterfactual dependence versus being a constituent of the causal process that produces it); and a normative argument about whether moral reasons must be difference-making reasons at all — with the normative argument doing nearly all the work while the empirical one absorbs all the airtime. Please tell me whether that is right, whether one of the other two is secretly load-bearing, whether part of the dispute is merely verbal (I suspect that causing harm and contributing to harm are doing different jobs on different sides), and whether any part of it rests on intuitions about clean hands or fairness that neither side can argue for. Do not survey the literature on my behalf; engage the positions as I have described them, and say what each camp would actually accept as a fair statement of its own view. Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — an empirical measurement, a formal result, a distinction, a change of question — or argue honestly that no progress should be expected and why. Please answer in American English.
