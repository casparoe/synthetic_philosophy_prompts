I'm a program officer at a research foundation. We run maybe a dozen review panels a year, each handing out a few million dollars, and the rules are mostly inherited: eight reviewers from the relevant subfield, everyone sees each other's scores before the meeting, we fund whatever a majority puts in the top half. I've been reading about the Condorcet jury theorem and I want to turn whatever in it is actually usable into a procedure my team can run when we set up a panel — not a general essay on collective wisdom.

What I have in mind: a short ordered checklist covering (a) whether the decision is even the kind of thing the theorem applies to, (b) whether our reviewers are better than chance on this kind of call and how we'd know without a stats department, (c) how badly the independence assumption is being violated and what to do about it — secret pre-votes, withholding scores until after discussion, deliberately mixing subfields — (d) panel size and the point where adding people stops buying anything, (e) when to hand the call to one named person instead of a vote. For each question say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure returns a hard no, and where it has to stop and hand the call to a human because it's a value judgment.

One thing I'd like built into the middle of it. I've started sorting panel failures into three buckets and I want the procedure to diagnose which one a given panel is headed for: "echo" (everyone anchored on the same first score or the same methodological reflex), "coin-flip" (the case is just hard and reviewers are near chance), and "drift" (errors systematically point one way, e.g. everyone is sceptical of the same kind of method). If that framing is wrong or misses a bucket, say so and fix it. I also suspect there's two different things people lump together as crowd wisdom — averaging scores versus majority on a yes/no — and I want the procedure to be clear about which one it's licensing, and what it says about mixing the two.

Then run it on three of our real setups: [1] the standard eight-person subfield panel on ordinary proposals; [2] our flagship "transformative" program, where I honestly think reviewers are barely better than a coin flip at picking which weird proposals will pan out — I have a feeling the procedure will tell me something I don't want to hear, that a big panel there is worse than a lottery or a single trusted expert, and I'd rather have it stated plainly than softened; [3] a cross-cutting call where every plausible reviewer comes out of the same methodological tradition with a strong shared view of what counts as rigorous.

Constraints: reviewers are volunteers with limited time, whatever we do has to be explainable to our board in a paragraph, and I can't ask anyone to estimate probabilities in any formal way. Please also say what the procedure can't tell me — especially for the cases where there's no fact of the matter about which application is "best" and we're really just aggregating preferences.
