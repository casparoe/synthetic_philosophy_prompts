Had a long dumb argument with my friend last night about free will and I think he cheated but I can't say how. His argument:

(1) If an action is fully produced by causes you didn't choose and can't now control, and you act exactly as those causes determine, then the action isn't up to you and you aren't morally responsible for it.
(2) Everyone in the debate grants the global manipulation case: neuroscientists rewire your brain so that you'll take the wallet, arranging things so that every belief and desire that moves you is one you'd endorse on reflection. You take it. You're not responsible.
(3) There's no responsibility-relevant difference between that and the ordinary case, where your whole psychology comes from genes, parents and culture and you act exactly as it determines.
(4) So nobody is ever morally responsible for anything.

I said (3) proves too much, and he agreed and just ate the consequence, which is the part I can't get past. Swap the neuroscientists for anything else that shapes you from the outside — your evidence, a good argument that changed your mind, being raised to notice suffering. Those are unchosen causes too, and they determine what you do. So (4'): the better your reasons for acting, the less the action is yours. Someone who steals after weighing everything carefully is less responsible than someone who steals on a dogmatic impulse, and a perfectly random action would be the freest one of all. He calls this the reasons excuse and says sure, that's the price, responsibility was never about responsiveness to reasons in the first place.

I'd rather keep the reasons part than keep (2), but dropping (2) feels like dropping the whole motive for being an incompatibilist. So which step is actually broken? I suspect either an equivocation between produced by causes outside you and not up to you, or a slide from how the action was actually produced to whether you could have done otherwise, but I can't pin either one down. Name the step and tell me what I pay for rejecting it.
