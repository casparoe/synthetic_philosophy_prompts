I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and I've ended up in a small reading group where a law student keeps using one analogy to settle everything. Her claim is that a constitution just is a Ulysses contract: the people, in a moment of clarity, have themselves tied to the mast so that they cannot later steer toward the Sirens, the Sirens being whatever panic, faction, or majority rage the framers expected; the judges are the crew, instructed in advance not to untie him no matter how loudly he shouts. She leans on it to license two conclusions I'm not convinced follow: first, that entrenching rights against ordinary majority rule is not the dead hand of one generation ruling another but genuine self-binding, and second, that the framing moment has an authority that later, equally lucid and better-informed moments do not.

I want to push back but I don't have the vocabulary, so here is what I'm working with from my own job. After a bad 3am outage my team added a standing rule: no deploy to the payment service without a second approval, ever. Everyone meant it at the time. Two years later the outage is forgotten, the rule is pure friction, and there is a thread every quarter proposing we delete it. That makes me want to separate what I've started calling cold-state contracts, where the agent who will later be tempted installs the binding while lucidly knowing the temptation, from inherited contracts, installed by a predecessor generation against a danger the current one has never actually encountered, and from key-in-the-drawer contracts, where the bound party can release itself but only through a deliberately costly procedure. My friend says these are one mechanism at three settings. I think the difference is doing real argumentative work and I can't say what that work is.

So I'd like an audit rather than a verdict. Please write the mapping out explicitly: who or what plays Ulysses, the crew, the mast, the rope, the wax, the Sirens, the song, the voyage, and the moment of untying. Then give me the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that matter for her two conclusions and the ones that only make for a better story. The ones I keep circling: Odysseus is a single continuous agent who asked to be bound and knew precisely what he was weak to, whereas a constitutional demos is not one agent, turns over completely within a few decades, and was guessing at its future temptations; the ropes cannot be reinterpreted by the crew, whereas constitutional text can and constantly is; the Sirens were still there when he sailed past, whereas the original danger may have lapsed or changed shape while the binding survived; and Odysseus consented, which raises the case I care about most, where the entrenchment was written under a war, a crisis, or an occupation, or was never put to the people it binds. For each: does the conclusion survive it, does the analogy need a repair, or is the analogy carrying weight it cannot bear? And if there is a better source domain available, I keep thinking of a code freeze, a last will, or a message in a bottle, please say which of my three contract types each handles best and which it quietly abandons.
