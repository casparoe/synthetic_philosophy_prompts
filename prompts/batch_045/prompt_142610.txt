I run a monthly reading group — mostly programmers, a couple of biologists, all totally fine with Bayes, none with any philosophy — and I've got them for one ninety-minute evening on anthropics. Half the room has already heard of the doomsday argument and has a favourite theory, which is part of the problem. Don't explain self-sampling vs self-indication to me; plan how I should get them to the point where they can work out what each rule says in a case they haven't seen before.

The order things have to come in is the bit I keep getting wrong. My instinct is that some version of you are not special has to be *broken* before it can be used — that they need to actually feel the pull of I could have been one of the others before any rule about counting observers means anything — and that the two rules shouldn't be named (SSA, SIA) until at least twenty minutes in, because the acronyms will let them sort themselves into teams and stop listening. Tell me if that's backwards, and what has to sit between the breaking and the naming.

Then the one case that carries the whole thing. It can't be cosmological, or they'll think it's about space and stop attending to the structure; it can't involve sleep or amnesia, or three people will drag in Sleeping Beauty and we'll lose forty minutes to thirders and halfers; and the numbers have to be big enough that the answer is surprising but still hand-computable. My current thought is that the case has to vary the total headcount of observers while holding something else fixed, and I want you to tell me what that something else is — what exactly has to stay fixed for the two rules to come apart at all, and what happens to the case if you hold the wrong thing fixed instead.

Misconceptions, and where in the sequence to kill each one. Ones I'm expecting: SIA is just Bayes with extra steps; this is all the reference class problem and therefore arbitrary; the doomsday argument is the same argument; SIA commits you to infinitely many observers so the probabilities aren't even defined. The one I'm most worried about is that the presumptuous philosopher will feel wrong to them only because they dislike the conclusion, and they'll spend the rest of the evening hunting for a technical excuse rather than seeing it as a symptom of something structural.

There's also a thing I've started calling the guarantee test, and I can't decide where it belongs. Before you apply either rule, ask: under each hypothesis, was there any chance that the thing now doing this reasoning would have been somebody else instead of you? If that question has no answer, the counting has nothing to grip. Does this go in early, as a precondition on both rules; late, as the twist that stops the presumptuous philosopher looking like a cheap trick; or nowhere? And I have a suspicion it quietly favours one of the two rules — if so I need to know that before I put it on the board.

For the check at the end I want transfer, not recall, and I'd like it to be a case where the two rules *agree*, because if they've only picked up a slogan they'll assume they have to disagree. Two I've been playing with: total observers 100 versus 100,000, with 10 versus 10,000 of them having exactly your evidence; or total 1 versus 1,000, with exactly one observer having your evidence under each. Which of those does the work, is either of them a trap, and what should I actually ask out loud so I can tell from their answers whether they've got it or are pattern-matching?

And what should I explicitly refuse to explain at this level, and say so out loud that I'm refusing? My candidates are fine-tuning, the measure problem, Boltzmann brains, and anything involving infinite ethics — but tell me if one of those is actually load-bearing and I'm wrong to skip it.
