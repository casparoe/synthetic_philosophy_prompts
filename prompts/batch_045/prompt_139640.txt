Two research teams have spent a decade arguing past each other about the annual international happiness rankings: the league tables that order countries by mean score on a 0–10 self-anchoring best-possible-life question (the Cantril ladder), report alongside it a short list of explanatory variables — income, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceived freedom from corruption — and, year after year, place the Nordic countries near the top. One team, the Targeters, holds that these tables measure something real and worth maximizing: that how a population rates its own life by its own lights is close to the thing a government exists to serve, that the number is good enough to steer by, and that a state should treat a rising score as a standing policy objective. The other team, the Anti-Targeters, holds that the number is a culturally conditioned, partly on-the-spot-constructed judgment, that comparisons of it across populations are unreliable because of response-style differences and adaptive preferences, and that turning it into an official target would both corrupt it and divert resources from the objective conditions it imperfectly tracks. Neither team holds that the numbers are noise or that subjective states are irrelevant to public policy; both grant that the scores covary with things nobody doubts matter.

Draft the joint statement these two teams could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, in four sections.

1. Agreed Claims. A numbered list of things both sides can sign that are neither vacuous nor slanted. It must separate the measurement question (does the number track how well lives are going?) from the targeting question (should a state act on it?); separate raising a country's own score from outranking other countries, i.e. the non-positional aim from the positional one; record that a mean says nothing about the bottom of the distribution; record that evaluative measures and affect measures can move independently, and that the familiar pattern of cross-sectional income differences tracking reported satisfaction while long-run within-country income growth often fails to do so is compatible with both teams' views; and record that adopting a target is adopting a target for a measure, which is not the same as adopting a target for the thing measured.

2. Disagreements. A table with columns: the disagreement; the formulation the Targeter would accept; the formulation the Anti-Targeter would accept; and one line on why this row is not merely verbal. Include at least four rows: interpersonal and cross-population comparability (level versus unit comparability, response styles, adaptive preferences); reactivity of the measure under official adoption; the moral standing of reported satisfaction as against objective conditions and capabilities; and the choice of aggregation function (mean, share above a threshold, or a bottom-tail-penalised mean), where the row must say explicitly whether that choice is a measurement dispute or a moral one wearing a measurement costume.

3. Cruxes and Pre-Registered Predictions. Three cruxes, each with a test, case, or statistic both teams agree would bear on it, and each team's prediction written down in advance, including what each team would count as evidence against its own side. Build them on the three devices the collaboration itself adopted; treat the devices as stipulated and do not spend the document re-litigating their design.

(a) Satisfied life-years. Instead of the current mean ladder score, rank countries by the mean, over completed lives, of the life-course integral of the ladder score, a year lived at ladder value v contributing v of ten satisfied life-years, with childhood years included through proxy reports. Does this reform answer the snapshot objection, or does it import fresh defects — rewarding longevity however the extra years go, penalising populations that report honestly at the end of life, resting on retrospective reports, and making the number less legible to voters and therefore worse as a democratic target?

(b) The ministry trial. A middle-income country of roughly thirty million people assigns half its ministries, at random, to a regime in which each ministry head must report annually on that ministry's contribution to the national mean ladder score, with the score published and with budget and promotion attached to it; the other half continue reporting on their usual objective indicators. The teams pre-commit to a five-year readout on: mean ladder score; mean affect balance from a short daily-reconstruction instrument; the within-country correlation between ladder score and the six explanatory variables; the share of the population scoring below 4; and the country's rank. Record each team's prediction on each, where they expect the ladder and the affect measure to come apart, and what each would infer from each admissible pattern — including the pattern in which the ladder rises while affect balance and the objective conditions do not.

(c) The aggregation choice. What happens to the ordering under a share-above-threshold headline (the proportion scoring 7 or higher) and under a bottom-tail penalty (the mean minus a multiple of the share below 4)? Which of the resulting movements are driven by measurement considerations and which by a moral view about priority to the worst off, and would a Targeter who accepts the bottom-tail penalty still be a Targeter in any sense worth arguing about?

4. What Was Only Verbal. A short closing section identifying which parts of the decade of argument were disputes about words — happiness as affect versus as life evaluation versus as eudaimonia; measuring as tracking versus as constituting; topping the table as raising one's own score versus as outranking others — and why, or else an argument that none of it was.

Constraints. Settle everything by argument from the stipulations above and from what a careful reader already knows; do not cite works, name authors, or recommend reading. Where a premise is empirical and neither party could check it, mark it explicitly as a stipulation both accept for the sake of the document. Present both sides at their best: no row of the table may be settled by attributing to one side a view it would disown. Use the four section headings as given. Length roughly 1100 to 1500 words.
