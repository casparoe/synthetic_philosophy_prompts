You are a philosopher of history who has also worked as a consultant to museums on contested public history. Before you start drafting, I want you to think rather than retrieve: spend a few paragraphs working out what "collapse" could even mean in a case like this — brainstorm two or three candidate definitions or decision rules (for instance, something keyed to loss of socio-political integration, something keyed to demographic decline, something keyed to whether the people living through the change experienced it as catastrophe, something keyed to whether a self-identifying descendant community persists), and test each against all three case studies in the application before you commit to one. Reason from the cases themselves; please don't pad the document with citations, footnotes, or suggested reading, since this memo will circulate to people who won't chase them up.

I sit on the program committee of my state humanities council, and we are deciding whether to award a $400,000 implementation grant for a permanent reinstallation of the regional natural history museum's "Ancient Worlds" hall, submitted under the title *When Civilizations Fail: Lessons from Easter Island, the Maya, and the Bronze Age*. The proposal has three alcoves; a centerpiece interactive, the "collapse-o-meter," in which visitors drag a slider to score each society from 0 to 100 on how completely it failed; and a closing "So what?" wall drawing lessons about resource overshoot and climate change. The museum's curator of Indigenous partnerships — together with the historic preservation officer of a federally recognized nation whose ancestors' material culture is displayed in the hall and whose language is still spoken by several hundred people, some of them museum visitors — has submitted a written objection: the Maya did not vanish, their descendants are alive and are the museum's neighbors, and a slider that scores "how completely it failed" is in effect a scorecard on a living people's ancestors. The museum director's reply is candid and not frivolous: visitation data and focus groups show that the word "collapse" is what brings audiences in, the disaster framing is what makes the climate wall land, and a $1.5M named gift is governed by a donor agreement that specifies the word "collapse" in the gallery title, so reopening the title risks the gift. Two further complications: the Bronze Age alcove is substantively the weakest, since the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE saw palace economies and long-distance trade break down, some regions lose urban life and literacy for centuries, and others — Egypt, for instance — come through with far less disruption, so a single verdict of "collapse" describes a patchwork rather than an event; and the Easter Island alcove rests on an ecocide narrative that recent work has substantially revised (the statues still stand, the population history is more complicated than the popular version, introduced rats and post-contact events matter), which the education department would prefer to leave alone because the older story is a cleaner cautionary tale.

My stake in this is not academic. I grew up in a mill town everyone describes as having "collapsed" in the 1980s: the mills shut, the tax base went, my father lost his job and never really worked steadily again. But the town is still there — population has partly come back with retirees and remote workers, the high school still plays football, the Portuguese festival is bigger than it was in 1979. So I have a strong intuition that "collapse" names a change in the shape of a thing rather than the end of it, and an equally strong intuition that something real and terrible happened to my family that should not be euphemized into "transition." What I need from you is a decision memo of roughly 1,000 words addressed to our committee that: (1) opens with a bottom line — award, award with conditions, defer, or decline — and a concrete timeline for anything you require; (2) proposes a definition or decision rule the council could reuse for future proposals touching on societal collapse or transformation, and shows how it handles all three cases rather than only the one that favors your conclusion; (3) says what to do about the collapse-o-meter in particular — cut it, keep it, or redesign it — and if you redesign it, specifies what quantities the axes actually measure and what the label should warn visitors about; (4) says how to handle the revised Easter Island material without turning a public gallery into a historiography seminar; (5) gives the losing side its best day in court, since I take seriously that "collapse" carries an urgency that "transformation" does not and that a hall nobody visits, or a gift withdrawn, educates nobody; and (6) is written so that both the curator of Indigenous partnerships and the museum director can actually respond to it, with reasons rather than verdicts.
