I write novels — three out, literary crime stuff — and over the last couple of books I keep having to solve the same problem in scene after scene, and I don't have a word for it.

Two examples. One: a surgeon realises she botched something on a patient years ago, nobody else knows, and she goes and tells him. But what actually moves her in that scene isn't "he deserves to know." It's "my old teacher would be ashamed of me" — and she picked that teacher deliberately, twenty years earlier, partly because she knew her own nerve would fail her someday. Two: a man who used to steal decides one night, cold sober, that if he ever feels the urge again he'll put it back, no deliberation, no exceptions. Eight months later he does put it back, and in the moment he isn't thinking about the person he took it from at all; he's just executing. My editor said the scene read as "mechanical." I think she has it exactly backwards.

So here's the pattern I keep building. The thing that makes the action good seems to happen *earlier* — when she installed the disposition, or chose her company, or wrote the rule — and at the moment of action she's moved by something else entirely: habit, pride, what one particular person would think, the bare fact that she made a rule. And readers only give the character credit if I show them the earlier bit. Show only the moment and they read it as luck, or as coldness, or as "he's just like that." That's the part I most want explained: is the reader tracking something real, or just a convention of storytelling?

People have pointed me at Kant supposedly holding that doing the right thing because you feel like it counts for less, at whatever Aristotle said about the person who does right while hating every minute of it, at taking moral advice from someone else instead of working it out, and at tricking your future self into behaving. None of those seem to be quite it: in my cases the earlier self isn't fighting off a later self who wants to do wrong, exactly; the later self isn't deferring to anybody's judgement about what's right; and it isn't simply that she enjoys doing it either.

Is there an established concept for this — the good-making thing living at a different time than the action, or in a scaffold the agent herself built? And I don't want just a label, I want the carving: does it matter whether she foresaw this exact kind of case, or whether she built the scaffold for a rotten reason and it now keeps producing decent acts anyway, or whether the scaffold is a person rather than a rule, or whether the later self would have done the wrong thing without it — and if so whether the credit lands on the earlier self for something that hadn't happened yet. If there's no name for it, coin me one and tell me where its edges are, and what nearby thing I'd be confusing it with. American English please, and don't just hand me a list of papers to go chase down.
