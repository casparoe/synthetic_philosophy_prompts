I'm writing a seminar paper on the reasons-first family of accounts of value — the fitting-attitude analyses and their buck-passing cousins, which try to say what it is for something to be valuable in terms of there being reasons of the right kind to favour it — and someone in my reading group handed me an argument that I am now fairly sure proves far too much, but I cannot find the joint. The argument is aimed at the standard fix for the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, and it ends up entailing that almost nothing any of us admires is actually admirable. Here it is, as I've reconstructed it.

(1) Purity: a consideration is a right-kind reason for an attitude only if it bears on whether that attitude's correctness condition obtains; a consideration that merely makes it good, prudent, or life-preserving to be in the state is a wrong-kind reason for it. (This has to be a classification of the relation rather than of the consideration itself, since some considerations are both: that believing she will recover makes recovery likelier is at once a pragmatic reason for the belief and, because the belief is self-fulfilling, evidence that it is true.) (2) In the usual demon case — he will kill me unless I favour him — the threat speaks only to whether it would be good to favour him, not to whether he is favourable, so by Purity it is a wrong-kind reason and he is not thereby valuable. Everyone in the literature accepts this much. (3) Genuineness: but the threat is still a reason of some kind. I ought to bring it about that I favour him, I can intelligibly set out to do so for exactly that reason, and if I do come to favour him he has not become admirable. (4) Basing: an attitude confers value on its object, or is fitting, only if it is appropriately based on right-kind reasons; favouring something for a wrong-kind reason is not the kind of response a reasons-first account can count. (5) Transmission: basing is transitive along a chain — if one attitude is based on a second, and the second is based on a consideration, then the first is based on that consideration too. (6) Nested demon: a demon will kill me unless I believe he is admirable. I come to believe it for that reason, and I then favour him on the strength of the belief. By (5) my favouring is based on the threat, and by (1) and (4) he is not valuable — the same verdict as in (2), so nothing has gone wrong yet. (7) Ordinary case: my admiration of a colleague rests on my belief that she is trustworthy, and that belief was formed partly because trusting her made our collaboration go better — a pragmatic consideration, hence by Purity a wrong-kind reason for believing it. By (5) my admiration is based on a wrong-kind consideration, and by (1) and (4) it therefore does not make her admirable. (8) But nearly every evaluative attitude any of us has rests on beliefs with some such ingredient in how they were formed: we trust because trusting pays, we attend to a person's virtues because attending is rewarded, we accept testimony because doubting is socially expensive. So nearly no actual admiration is based on right-kind reasons alone, and by (1) and (4) nearly nothing we value is valuable. (9) That is absurd. So one of (1), (4), (5), or the reasons-first account itself has to go.

Where exactly does this break? I can't even tell whether it is valid with a false premise or invalid at some step — my suspicion is that "based on" is doing different work in (5) and (7), but the self-fulfilling case in (1) is what pushed us toward a relational reading in the first place, and I don't see how to retreat from that. Someone suggested this is just the familiar failure of transmission from Moore-style anti-skeptical arguments in another guise, but I don't see the analogy. What I most want to keep is that the demon is not valuable, that my colleague's admirability does not depend on the causal history of my belief about her, and that I do genuinely have a reason to favour the demon when he threatens me. Price the alternatives for me: giving up Purity, giving up the basing requirement in (4), denying transmission, or abandoning the reasons-first account and taking value to be attitude-independent. And tell me whether the same argument runs against evidentialism about belief — if there is a real asymmetry between the evaluative and the epistemic case, I'd like to know where it comes from.
