I run a small philosophy club at my school, and we have stalled on a case we came up with, so I would like to see both sides of it argued properly. Would you write a dialogue between two students who disagree about whether what happened was a real harm?

The case: in a persistent online world, every structure a player builds is continuously backed up server-side and can be restored perfectly, and every item can be duplicated freely, so no player is ever permanently deprived of anything. Knowing all this, one player spends four months building a careful replica of her grandmother's house. Another player finds it, burns it down on a livestream, and she is devastated and says that her home was destroyed.

The first student holds that this was a genuine harm and a serious wrong, not much less bad than destroying something that matters to someone offline. The second holds that where nothing is lost, damaged or set back, there is nothing to be harmed by, and that the most one can say is that the act was cruel, disrespectful or in poor taste: bad manners rather than a harm.

I would like both of them to be good at this and to answer each other rather than restate their own view. In particular, the first should have to say what the harm consists in if it is not deprivation, and whether it matters that what was destroyed was a replica of a real house rather than an ordinary in-game base. The second should have to handle two variants that I take to be the crux. First: everything is the same except that the backup has silently failed, so the house really is gone and nobody discovers this for weeks — on his view, did a harm come into existence when the fire started, when the backup failed, when the builder found out, or not at all? Second: the builder alone is truthfully told beforehand that the server will shut down in a week and that nothing will carry over — does the argument that nothing was lost now show that nothing was ever at stake, and is that a conclusion he can accept? Please do not smooth it into a tidy compromise at the end; I want to see what each side has to concede.
