our reading group (small policy shop, mix of economists and one historian) keeps saying AI is the new Industrial Revolution, and we've noticed everyone is using it for a different conclusion. One camp uses it to say we have decades: the technology arrives, there's a long messy lag, and eventually institutions catch up (factory acts, unions, public education, the vote getting extended) and the gains spread. The other camp uses the same analogy to say the opposite: look at what the transition itself actually was — child labor, the slums, the Luddites getting crushed, empire as a captive market for textiles — so the danger isn't the destination, it's the fifty years in the middle, and we should be scared of that, not reassured by the ending. I want to audit the analogy properly instead of letting it do opposite work in different mouths.

Two things my group has been playing with, which I'd like you to use rather than the standard version:

First, we've been separating three different kinds of lag that people run together. (1) Adoption lag: the tech exists but diffusion is slow because firms are conservative, capital is sunk, nobody knows how to use it. (2) Complement lag: the tech exists and is adopted, but the payoff only shows up once everything around it is rebuilt — the example we keep using is electrification, where factories supposedly had to be redesigned around small electric motors before it moved any productivity numbers. (3) Institutional or bargaining lag: productivity rises, but most people's pay doesn't, until politics changes who has leverage. The empirical thing we lean on for (3) is the pattern where output per worker climbs steadily for something like two generations while median real wages are roughly flat — people argue about exactly how long and how flat that was, and I might be misremembering the shape of it, so flag it if I am. Question: which kind of lag was most of the British case, and which kind would an AI lag be? My suspicion is that only one of the three actually supports "we have time to build institutions," and the other two support something else entirely.

Second, someone proposed a binding-constraint test: an analogy like this should be judged on which input's scarcity actually set the pace in the source case, and whether the corresponding input in the target case scales faster or slower. In the British case the story is roughly cheap coal plus abundant labor plus capital accumulation. For AI I don't know whether the binding thing is compute, energy, data, or something like trust and verifiability. If it scales on a years timescale rather than a generations one, the "we have decades" conclusion collapses regardless of how good the rest of the mapping is.

What I want from you:

- The correspondence table, with the slots filled and labelled: what plays the steam engine, the coal, the factory, the artisan or handloom weaver, the Luddites, the factory acts and the unions, the pause, the empire. And explicitly: which slots have no counterpart at all, because I think a missing row tells us more than a strained one.
- Which disanalogies matter for the specific conclusions being drawn and which are just noise. In particular: the Industrial Revolution mechanized muscle and left the bottleneck in human judgment, while this mechanizes something closer to judgment itself; and in the British case labor's bargaining position eventually rose, whereas the mechanism here plausibly pushes it down. Say which of the three conclusions those two disanalogies actually damage.
- Whether the analogy, once audited, favors my first camp, my second camp, or neither — and if neither, whether some other analogy (the printing press, electrification specifically, nukes) does the particular job better. I don't want a list of every analogy anyone's ever proposed; I want the one that carries the specific inference we keep making.
- A concrete one: if we wanted to tell, over the next five years, which of the three lags we're actually in, what are we looking at? And is that a test this analogy can generate, or does it have to be imported from somewhere else?
- Where the analogy is silent. I already think it has nothing to say about the tail case where the technology outcompetes people across most of what they do rather than at particular tasks, but tell me if that's too quick.
