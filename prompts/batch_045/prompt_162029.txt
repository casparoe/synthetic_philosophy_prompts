Construct a single case — one paragraph, set in ordinary professional life, with no violence and no one's life, body, or liberty hanging on the answer — in which Kant's formula of universal law and his formula of humanity deliver opposite verdicts about one and the same lie: the first forbids it, the second permits it.

Constraints. (1) It may not be the murderer at the door or any variant of it: the person lied to is threatening no one, and no third party's safety or freedom may turn on what you say. (2) The permission from the formula of humanity must fall out of the relation between the two agents — something about how the hearer or the speaker is treating rational agency — not from the good effects of the lie, not from the speaker's feelings, and not from the hearer's bad character. (3) No appeal to the thought that truthfulness is owed only to those who have a right to the truth; and the maxim must be stated as a standing policy with its circumstances and its end, at the specificity Kant himself allows — not gerrymandered down to this one occasion and not inflated into lying whenever it suits me.

After the scenario, state the maxim, run each formula on it, and report the two verdicts. Then name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, say what happens to both verdicts if you remove it, and explain how a defender of the formula of universal law would attack your case. Finally, commit yourself: is the lie permissible? And does the disagreement reveal a real gap in Kant's ethics, or only that the two formulas are being made to do work that Kant believed a single law did?

I am building this for a seminar on whether the formulas are genuinely equivalent, so a case that stipulates the verdicts instead of deriving them from the formulas is exactly the failure I am trying to avoid. If you think the divergence cannot be produced honestly, say so and argue for it — a definite negative with reasons is a better answer than a hedged case.
