I teach a cross-cultural management seminar, and a student handed me an argument I cannot locate the flaw in, even though I am sure it proves far too much. She begins by stipulating that whenever a speaker leaves part of their meaning unstated, they transfer the work of recovering it to the hearer; call the transferred work *decoding labour*, and call any utterance that transfers more of it than the local norm requires a *loaded* utterance. Her argument then runs:

(1) Transferring decoding labour is in itself morally neutral: hints, irony, understatement and jokes all transfer it, and nobody treats a good joke as a moral offence.
(2) So the wrongness of the cases we actually care about — the white lie, the evasive refusal, the criticism delivered as a hint — cannot consist in the transferring as such.
(3) What must be doing the work is the speaker's foreknowledge: it is wrong to load decoding labour onto a hearer you expect to decode it incorrectly.
(4) But if expected misdecoding is the wrong-maker, then no style of speech is distinctly right or wrong. A blunt Dutch manager who says, in his own idiom, exactly what he believes, to a colleague from a high-context culture, knows she will hear insult and imminent dismissal where he meant only a correction; a Japanese host who hints rather than refuses knows his guest may leave still hoping. Both are producers of predicted error, and the second differs from the first only in degree.
(5) Hence there is no distinct virtue of honesty and no distinct vice of politeness. There is one duty only: among the formulations available to you, choose the one that minimizes expected false belief in your actual audience.
(6) And that cannot be right. On (5) I must stop saying true things to people who are bad at hearing them, must tailor every remark to the most error-prone listener present, and must count bluntness to a fool a vice and evasion to a fool a duty — that is, I must become exactly the audience-managing manipulator that both the blunt and the polite traditions despise.

I would rather keep (1) and (2) than swallow (5), and I want to keep the further thought that a mismatch of conversational code is itself part of the moral story. Which step should I reject, why did it look sound, and what does rejecting it cost me — specifically, can I reject it without conceding that white lies are simply deception and politeness simply lying with better manners?
