I'm a journalist working on a long feature about how quantitative comparison actually gets used in public policy — cost-benefit analyses, quality-adjusted life years, the league tables that cost-effectiveness researchers publish — and I've become stuck on something I'd assumed was settled long ago: whether 'better than' is transitive. I don't need reading lists or citations; I've read plenty of secondary summaries, and what I lack is not more sources but a clearer picture of where the genuine uncertainty sits and how much hangs on each piece of it.

The thing that unnerves me is the spectrum argument in its general shape: a long series of outcomes, each differing from its neighbour by a barely perceptible loss along one dimension and a compensating gain along another, arranged so that every adjacent step looks like an improvement while the endpoint looks clearly worse than the starting point. I've been playing with three diagnostics and would like you to tell me how much weight each deserves. First, the compression test: merge adjacent items so that the spectrum has ten coarse steps instead of two hundred fine ones — if the cycle survives coarse-graining, it is not merely vagueness or sorites effects at the just-noticeable threshold; if it evaporates, the argument may only show that 'better than' has borderline cases. Second, the reverse spectrum: because the compensation schedule is roughly symmetric, the same series can be traversed backwards, and each backward step also looks like an improvement — which suggests the trouble may lie in the local compensation principle rather than in transitivity itself, since running the chain in both directions produces an outright contradiction even granting transitivity. Third, the question whether 'better than' is a two-place relation at all, or is covertly relative to a comparison set or a respect, in which case a cycle of pairwise judgments might be no inconsistency at all. I'm also unsure what to make of the money-pump thought, that an agent guided by an intransitive betterness could be exploited, since it may indict a decision rule rather than the relation.

So: give me your credence, as a number, that the 'better than' relation over outcomes — the one my policy sources are invoking when they rank interventions — is transitive. Decompose it: the probability that nothing in the concept or semantics of a comparative rules out non-transitive orderings; the probability that some actual spectrum has all its premises determinately true; the probability that the apparent cycle survives compression; the probability that the relation is genuinely binary rather than set- or respect-relative; whatever weight, if any, the money-pump consideration carries; and anything else you think belongs — with a number and one line of justification for each, then the combination. My editor, when I put the question to her, said 0.9 without hesitating; a philosopher I interviewed said 0.4. Tell me which sub-question their disagreement most likely turns on, which of your own numbers you are least confident about, and what kind of evidence — conceptual, linguistic, or empirical work on how people actually make pairwise comparisons — would move you most.
