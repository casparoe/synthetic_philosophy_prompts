I am giving a department colloquium next month — thirty-five minutes plus twenty-five for questions — on the central chapter of my dissertation, and I want a Q&A prep sheet for it, not a defense of the view.

The thesis I am defending is that the disjunction problem is not a refutation of teleosemantics but a demand for the right notion of grain, and that history supplies that grain once we stop asking what a mechanism was selected for simpliciter and ask instead which differences actually made a difference to its proliferation. I call the resulting view contrastive etiological content, and it has three clauses. First, the discrimination clause: the content of a state produced by a mechanism is fixed by the set of conditions that the mechanism's selection history discriminates, so that where history was indifferent between c and c* there is no content difference between them, and where history discriminated, the content is the discriminated condition rather than some richer property. Second, the consumer clause: the contrast class is fixed by what the downstream systems that actually consume the state do with it, so that a state's grain is as fine as its consumers' uses distinguish and no finer. Third, the lag clause: when a mechanism is co-opted by a new consumer whose use discriminates more finely than the original selection history did, content shifts immediately with the new use rather than waiting for a new round of selection to ratify it, which is why exapted mechanisms can acquire contents their history does not underwrite and can misrepresent relative to their original function.

My carrying example, which everything in the talk runs through, is invented rather than empirical: a nocturnal moth with an auditory neuron N that fires across the 20–40 kHz band. Over the stretch of history that fixed N, the only echolocating predator in the moth's range was one bat species, whose calls sit in that band; the moth responds to N by diving. A second bat species with overlapping calls has since colonised the range, and N also fires for the ultrasonic clicks of a harmless katydid that shares the habitat. The question the talk is built around is whether N means predator, bat, bat-or-katydid-click, or 20–40 kHz airborne vibration, and why the answer should be the one I give — a disjunction whose members are set by what the diving system distinguishes, so fine enough to exclude the katydid only once some consumer's use separates it. My three-step argument for this, which is what I expect the questions to attack, is that (i) every extant answer to the disjunction problem fixes grain by stipulation, (ii) selection histories record non-stipulatively which contrasts mattered for proliferation, and (iii) the consumer clause makes the resulting grain non-arbitrary because the consumers are themselves historically selected to use the state one way rather than another.

The room matters more than the argument, so here it is. There are no committed teleosemanticists present, which means no friendly fire but also no one to vouch for the background machinery. Two senior people are externalists of the anti-individualist sort who think content is individuated by a subject's actual environment rather than by its history, and who will treat the whole etiological apparatus as an answer to a question they do not think is the interesting one. Two philosophers of biology will be there: one holds an aetiological or selected-effects account of function and will police my use of function, selection, and proliferation; the other holds a causal-role or organizational account and will press the thought that selection history is the wrong anchor and that what makes something have a function is its current contribution to a system's organization. A systems neuroscientist who works on population coding in visual cortex will ask, in the vocabulary of decoders and readouts, what my account predicts that a decoding story does not, and will want to know what would settle the matter experimentally. Finally, a scattering of graduate students who work on representation in trained artificial networks will want to know what the view says about a system with a training history but no selection history at all, since gradient descent gives you a lineage of a kind without giving you proliferation or biological function.

What I want from you is the prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do if I handled them badly, each attributed to the faction that will raise it and phrased the way that faction would phrase it rather than in my terms — the organizational theorist's objection about exaptation and current contribution should not sound like the neuroscientist's objection about decoders, and the anti-individualist's should sound like neither. For each one, give me a reply I can actually deliver in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft, so that I know which of my answers are standing on something. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes of the talk rather than being dragged to it in the question period, and tell me which single reply has to land for the view to survive and which of my three premises I should be prepared to give up under pressure, including what the retreat position looks like once I have given it up — I would rather have a defensible weaker claim than a collapsed stronger one. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, together with the honest reason it is dangerous and a one-minute answer that at least buys me time. Do not give me a reading list or tell me who to cite; what I need is the objection set, the order, the wording, and where my answers are thin.
