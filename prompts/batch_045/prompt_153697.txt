I'm in a little reading group that's slowly working through al-Ghazālī's Incoherence of the Philosophers (we're non-specialists, one person did an undergrad module in Islamic philosophy, the rest of us are just interested), and we got stuck on how to picture his denial of necessary connection — the famous case where fire is put next to cotton and the cotton burns without the heat of the fire being what does the burning. Our tutor explained it with an analogy: created things are like a pen, God is like the writer. The pen is genuinely there and the letters really get made, but the pen contributes nothing of its own, the writer produces every stroke, and it's only because the writer holds it that anything appears on the page. A friend of mine who's doing a physics PhD then offered a modernised version of the same thing: the world is like a hand-drawn animation, where each frame is drawn afresh and what we call laws of nature are just the animator's consistent style. They're both using it to license two conclusions I'm not sure about — first, that occasionalism is completely science-friendly, since doing science is just cataloguing God's habits and there's no reason it couldn't accommodate something like cosmology or evolution; second, that a miracle isn't a violation of anything, it's just God drawing one frame differently, so the whole idea of a law being broken is a confusion.

My worry is that both pictures quietly load in more than the view needs. The pen analogy seems to make created things totally inert, but as I understand the position what's denied is necessary connection — that the effect had to follow — not that the fire is doing nothing at all, and I've seen it suggested that the full-blown reading (nothing in creation is a cause, ever) is stronger than al-Ghazālī actually commits to. The animation version seems to smuggle in a separate thesis about time, that the world is re-created instant by instant rather than continuously, which looks like it could be detached from the claim about causation. There's also the failure cases: damp cotton that won't catch, or a pen with no ink — on the writer analogy, who or what accounts for the non-burning, and does the picture end up making God the direct cause of every blockage and absence as well as every event? And I can't tell whether habit is doing real work here: if the habit has never once been broken in the relevant case, is saying it's only a habit any different from saying it's a law, or is the analogy just relabelling the thing it was supposed to replace? Someone in the group said all this is just Hume with God added on top, and I couldn't tell whether that's a real problem for the science-friendly conclusion or just a way of changing the subject.

Can you audit both analogies for me — what corresponds to what (just write the mappings inline as fire → pen, habit → the animator's style, and so on, rather than setting up a separate table), where each one breaks, which of the disanalogies actually matter for the two conclusions my friend wants and which are harmless, whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there's a better analogy — maybe something that keeps created things active without giving them necessary causal power. Answer in British English as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
