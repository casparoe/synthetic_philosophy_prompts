I am a novelist, near the end of a book that has stalled on one scene, and I need a poem to write my way into it — a poem that will also, in the finished novel, be read aloud by a character.

The novel is set almost entirely in the reading room of a provincial archive that is slowly being deaccessioned. Its narrator, Mira Solberg, has spent thirty-one years there sorting, and her fixed principle — the thing the book treats as both her virtue and her failure — is that nothing is ever thrown away. Every scrap is catalogued, every catalogue cross-indexed, every index retained. In the final chapter she learns, in a way she cannot quite put into words, that the building's heating bill is the price of the order she has made.

What I want from you is the poem she reads aloud to the empty room that night: a sestina in her voice — six unrhymed six-line stanzas followed by a three-line envoy, thirty-nine lines in all, with the six end-words rotating in the standard pattern (abcdef / faebdc / cfdabe / ecbfad / deacfb / bdfeca) and all six reappearing in the envoy.

I want that rotation to be the argument, not the decoration. A sestina cannot get rid of a word once it has used it; it can only carry it forward and let it come back, altered, somewhere else. That is precisely the situation of Maxwell's demon once you take seriously that its records are physical. The demon is not refuted by being told that measuring is hard, and it is not forbidden by the second law in any simple way: the sorting genuinely works, the molecules really do end up separated, work really can be extracted. What defeats it is that an engine has to run as a cycle — has to return to its initial state — and blanking a memory is not free. The advantage is only ever deferred. Order made here is paid for there, in heat dumped into whatever room the reckoning happens in. And a memory that is never emptied has stopped being a memory and become a warehouse: information hoarded is not power, it is an unpaid bill.

Some constraints, if you'll accept them:

- Choose the six end-words yourself, but I'd want among them one word for the dividing or sorting, one for the heat or the expense, and one word from Mira's own paper vocabulary — index, card, ledger, dust, shelf, something in that register.
- The turn should land in the envoy. Let the poem be about a woman and a room until the moment all six words are forced into three lines at once, and let that compression be the moment of erasure — the point where she understands that the archive is not a free lunch, and that she has simply not yet been asked to pay.
- The poem should be intelligible spoken aloud to a reader who has never heard of Maxwell's demon, and should not be one a philosopher of physics would have to forgive.
- American English, in spelling and idiom, throughout.

Two more things, please, both in prose after the poem. First, a note of a paragraph or two on how confident you are that the poem gets the physics right, singling out the one line or image where you are least sure the metaphor tracks the physics rather than merely sounding as though it does — I would especially value your saying plainly whether the cost has to fall at erasure, since I know that is contested, and how much the poem leans on that contested reading. Second, flag any place where you took a liberty with the sestina's mechanics rather than following the rotation I gave you.
