Quick one, and sorry if it's more of a policy question than a philosophy one — I'm on the four-person group at work that has to sign off before any team here deploys an AI thing internally (I got drafted onto it, definitely not my idea), and we've got a proposal on Thursday that I flip-flop on every time I think about it. We keep using "tool" vs "agent" as if the distinction were obvious, and I've realised I have no idea what we've actually been tracking with it. So: given our track record, what should we say about this one?

Our record so far: we approved a coding assistant that suggests diffs in the IDE, where nothing merges until a dev clicks apply; we approved a nightly job that re-labels incoming support tickets into a fixed taxonomy, runs unattended at 3am, writes only to our own database, and gets a weekly 5% accuracy audit; we approved a meeting scheduler that reads calendars and proposes slots but only sends the invite after the user confirms; and we approved, after a long argument, an internal "migration planner" that was handed an outcome — move these tables to the new warehouse with no downtime — and produced a step-by-step plan plus scripts, but ran them only against staging, with a human running them in prod. On the other side, we rejected a support bot that could send replies and issue refunds up to $200 on its own with a weekly 10% conversation audit; we rejected a small purchasing thing with a $5k monthly cap that could place orders with pre-approved vendors by itself and emailed receipts nightly (the cap didn't save it, which I think matters); and last spring we rejected a "research agent" that browsed the web, ran code in a sandbox, and wrote a memo — no external writes at all — purely because nobody could reconstruct how it got from the question to the memo, and we decided that approving the output would just be rubber-stamping it.

The new proposal is from a team that clearly read that last rejection: same basic shape, but now the system is given a quarterly question like "what are our three biggest risks from competitor X's pricing moves", runs four to six hours unattended, spawns its own sub-searches, writes and runs its own analysis code, decides for itself when it has enough evidence, and at the end drops a memo plus a complete timestamped trace of roughly 400 steps into a review queue. It has no credentials to anything with write access, can't send anything outside the company, and can't spend money beyond compute. The catch is that the team says reviewers will spend five to ten minutes with the memo and explicitly will not read the trace, and they want us to bless outcome-level review rather than step-level review as the sign-off.

What I want is: which of our own precedents is the real analog here (I go back and forth between the 3am batch job and last spring's research agent); what rule or rules fit every verdict we've already handed down; what each of those rules says about this case; your best guess at what we should do if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and which way you'd lean; and the one variant of the proposal that would actually tell the competing rules apart — I keep imagining the same system with a checklist of steps specified up front, or the same system where the trace gets reviewed by a second model plus a 10% human sample, or one where the memo auto-publishes internally if nobody responds within the hour, and I can't tell which of those is the one that carries the weight. My own guesses about what we've been running are something like "it's a tool iff every irreversible effect on the outside world is separately authorised by a human" or "it's really just about blast radius: no money, no external writes, reversible, so fine" — but both of those say approve here, and something in me still thinks the six hours of unattended self-directed operation, or the fact that it picks its own stopping point, is the actual line, so I suspect there's a third or fourth rule I'm not managing to articulate.
