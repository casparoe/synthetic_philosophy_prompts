I'm a PhD student in intellectual history and I co-teach a graduate seminar on historical judgment, and every year the discussion arrives at the same dead end. One side holds that moral standards don't expire and that we can straightforwardly condemn Jefferson for enslaving people, Churchill for his views on empire and race, or Columbus for the enslavement and forced labor he oversaw — pointing out that contemporaries of each, including abolitionist critics in Jefferson's own Virginia and sixteenth-century critics of the encomienda, said much of what we now say. The other side holds that blame requires that the agent could reasonably have been expected to know better, that a person's moral outlook is almost wholly a product of his upbringing, and that condemnation across centuries is less a judgment than an act of self-congratulation. Nobody disputes the historical facts, and nobody moves.

My own hypothesis is that the "man of his time" move bundles two claims that should be separated: an epistemic one about which arguments and evidence were within the agent's reach, and a motivational one about how much of his wrongdoing was driven by false belief that better evidence could have corrected rather than by interests and attachments that no evidence would have shifted. If that's right, part of the stalemate is a suppressed empirical question about counterfactual robustness that both sides settle from anecdote, and part is a difference about what blaming is for — a verdict on the person, or a decision about what our institutions should honor now, a decision the dead cannot be deterred or reformed by.

What puzzles me further is an asymmetry we never examine: we withhold the defence from living people raised under norms we reject, though their epistemic situation looks structurally like a dead man's. So I'd like a diagnosis, not a verdict: is the remaining disagreement verbal, a clash of bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, a hidden empirical question, or two questions under one heading? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, historical work, a change of question — or is there reason to expect none?
