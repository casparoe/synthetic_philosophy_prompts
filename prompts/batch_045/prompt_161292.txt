You are a senior researcher in democratic theory, constitutional design, and social choice, with deep expertise in sortition and lottocracy. Think carefully and do not rush to a ranking; your value to the foundation comes from the quality of the prioritization and the explicit reasoning behind it.

You are advising a small private foundation that can award about six two-year fellowships for conceptual work on the foundations of randomly selected government. The money is explicitly for theory and institutional design: no empirical fieldwork, no survey research, no randomized evaluations of citizens' assemblies, no software, and no literature-review or bibliographic output. Do not cite authors, papers, or books, and do not provide a reading list; argue on the merits from first principles.

The foundation has a fixed practical reference point. It wants to know whether, and if so how, a randomly selected legislature could legitimately exercise binding power rather than merely advise elected officials. To make the question concrete, assume the foundation is interested in a specific institutional vessel I will call the Escalating Sortition Legislature. In this design, a small randomly selected Agenda Chamber identifies and drafts proposals; proposals that survive it go to a larger randomly selected Deliberative Chamber; and a separately drawn Constitutional Chamber can amend or escalate constitutional questions. All chambers rotate on short cycles. There is also a novel accountability mechanism I will call Lottery Recall: at any time, a fresh random sample of citizens may be convened, and if a supermajority of that sample judges a sitting chamber to have exceeded or abandoned its mandate, the chamber is dissolved and redrawn by lot.

Your task is to advise the foundation on which open conceptual questions in the theory of sortition and lottocracy most deserve those six fellowships, given this vessel and goal. This is an agenda-setting question, not a request for a full institutional design or a survey of the field. The foundation wants a defended priority list, not a literature review.

Please work in two stages before giving the final agenda. First, brainstorm widely: generate at least twelve candidate open questions or lines of work, tagging each for (a) whether it is upstream of other questions, (b) how tractable it is for theorists working without empirical data, (c) how neglected or crowded it currently is, and (d) how much its answer would change the design of the Escalating Sortition Legislature or our judgment of its legitimacy. Second, compress that analysis into a ranked agenda of five to eight questions. For each ranked question, explain why it deserves its place, what other questions depend on it, and what kind of argumentative progress or evidence over the next two years would move it up or down the list.

Some candidate clusters you may consider, reject, or reorganize include: the basis of legitimacy for rule by lot versus rule by election; whether random selection can be genuinely politically equal when combined with stratified quotas or shortlisting; the epistemic competence of rotating amateur chambers and how to think about expertise without creating a permanent expert class; the moral and strategic significance of Lottery Recall, including whether a random sample can legitimately judge the mandate of another random sample; the danger of agenda capture in the small Agenda Chamber and how sortition interacts with organized interests; the stability of a system with no electoral feedback loop; how to conceptualize accountability in the absence of reelection; and the conditions under which binding sortition would be preferable to, or worse than, elected representation. Do not treat this list as exhaustive, and do not simply rank the clusters I named; you may identify more important questions that I have not listed.

Begin your answer with a short statement of the criteria you are using and how you weigh them, then give the ranked agenda. Keep the final agenda to around 1200 to 1600 words. Reason carefully and show your trade-offs: if a question is important but intractable for theorists without empirical data, say so and explain whether it should nonetheless be funded, deprioritized, or reframed. The foundation's goal is to spend a small amount of conceptual effort where it will most change how we think about binding sortition, so be explicit about dependency relations and about what would change your ranking.
