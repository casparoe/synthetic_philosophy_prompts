You are a comparative political theorist who has spent your career on democratization and its reversal, and who has also had to defend a definition in a room where the people present wanted opposite answers. Think carefully, and work in two passes. In the first pass, brainstorm without organizing: list the cases, distinctions, and failure modes that any account of democratic backsliding has to handle, including ones that do not come immediately to mind. In the second pass, compress that into a specification. Do not spend words on literature, and do not give me citations or a reading list — I want the argument itself.

The reason I am asking: I have recently joined the board of a small foundation that funds election-monitoring and media-litigation groups in Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and, since last year, the United States. Our charter requires us to suspend new disbursements in a country once the board judges that the country has, in the charter's words, begun to move off a democratic path. The phrasing is ours and comes from no theorist, so read nothing into it. The judgment is expensive in both directions: suspend, and we abandon partners who depend on us; fail to suspend, and we look as though we are applying a standard that tracks which governments our donors dislike. What I need, then, is a concept that can do early-warning work for an external actor with no democratic mandate of its own — one that fires before the outcome is settled, that is answerable to evidence rather than to affiliation, and that could in principle return a verdict of no against a government I am inclined to dislike.

Write me that specification: what an adequate account of democratic backsliding must deliver in order to be usable for this purpose. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it and say what goes wrong for an account that ignores it. The weight should fall on the criteria and their motivating cases. A short closing scorecard — how expert-coded indices of the V-Dem or Freedom House kind, institutional guardrail accounts, and mechanism-based accounts built on things like media capture or capture of electoral administration each fare — is welcome, but it should take up no more than about a fifth of your answer.

Here are the materials I want you to work from, because these are the ones on which our board deadlocks. Kalenia and Marevia are invented composites, not real countries; do not try to identify them, and feel free to add cases of your own where the list needs them.

Kalenia. A governing coalition inherits an election commission openly staffed by the previous governing party. It replaces that commission with a professionally selected one and restores voting rights to a large diaspora the previous government had excluded. In the same legislative session it extends the sitting president's term by two years without a vote, and its allies acquire the country's three largest private broadcasters. The next election is, by all observation, clean — and the coalition loses it.

Marevia. A genuinely exogenous shock, a war on its border, produces a state of emergency, postponed elections, and the closure of two opposition-aligned newspapers. The emergency is lifted and elections are held, on schedule, eighteen months later. Marevia's measured democratic quality falls further in those eighteen months than Kalenia's does over five years.

The mandate case. A government with a two-thirds majority won twice, backed by a referendum at sixty percent, abolishes the upper chamber and removes the constitutional court's power to review legislation. Nothing about the next election is rigged.

The federation case. In a large federal country, national-level electoral competition degrades sharply in eight states and improves sharply — with real enfranchisement — in twelve.

The mirror case. A government you and I would be inclined to approve of — one that expands the franchise, breaks up a captured court, and deregulates party finance — centralizes power in ways that would look alarming if the other side had done them.

Within the specification, these are the questions I care most about.

Which of the demands commonly placed on a concept like this are genuine desiderata, and which are preferences of one camp — the electoral-minimalist, the liberal-constitutionalist, the participatory — presented as requirements.

Whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. My own suspicion is that there is a real tension between being sensitive enough to fire early and being a test a government could in principle pass, so that the concept is not simply a label applied to whichever side the speaker opposes; and a second tension between requiring some attribution of agency or intent and requiring that the criterion be checkable without access to anyone's motives. If you think either tension is only apparent, say what dissolves it.

Whether the concept can return a verdict of no at all, or whether it is structurally a one-way ratchet that can only ever be applied to governments the speaker already opposes. If it is the latter, tell me whether a board like ours should keep using it or should instead vote on a list of named mechanisms — court packing, capture of the electoral administration, media acquisition, harassment of the civil service — and drop the umbrella term.

Who decides. Whether an adequate account can leave attribution to domestic actors, the opposition or the voters or the courts, or whether it must permit outside observers to override a domestic verdict, and what either choice costs.

Which single requirement excludes the most candidate accounts, and which requirement is the one a board like mine should expect itself to quietly violate.

Be willing to conclude that no specification satisfies everything I have asked for, and to tell me which requirement I should drop.
