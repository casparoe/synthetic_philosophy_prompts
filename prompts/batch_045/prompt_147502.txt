Hey, so I'm a backend engineer — no philosophy background whatsoever, I mostly write Go and complain about database migrations — and somehow I ended up running a Friday lunch reading group at work where six of us argue about AI safety. The thing we keep going in circles on is scalable oversight: what do you actually do when a model is doing work that the people supervising it genuinely can't verify? Our running example is a code-review-flavoured task: the model proposes a big refactor with a very plausible rationale plus a security writeup, and none of us can tell whether it's right without basically doing the work ourselves. Second example we keep coming back to is a model answering benefits-policy questions for employees, where there is a correct answer in principle but the person reading it has no way to check. I tried sketching a flowchart and every branch just dead-ended at "use debate," which I'm 99% sure is wrong, so I want to build it the other way round this time: start from the distinctions that actually split the space, and only put the method names on at the very end.

So: give me a decision tree with roughly ten branch questions and ten to twelve leaves, whose start node is something like "I accept that we're going to deploy systems whose outputs we can't directly verify, and that we still need some story about why those outputs are trustworthy." Every branch node should be phrased as a question a mid-level engineer with zero alignment background could answer from a concrete scenario, without knowing which method it leads to — so not "do you accept the honest-argument assumption?" but something like "imagine the model is definitely wrong and a perfect explanation of why is written on the whiteboard in front of you: can your reviewer tell?" Among the distinctions I think are load-bearing, and which you should use where they earn their place (add ones I've missed, drop ones that don't do real work):

- does the world eventually hand you ground truth for free — never, months later, or right away?
- is the bottleneck that the truth can't be compressed into something the reviewer can consume in the time and format they have, versus the reviewer can't tell true from false even with unlimited time and a perfect argument, versus the model or the two sides have an incentive to mislead and can profit from it?
- is the model the party that knows more, or does nobody know, or does the model know but is actively hiding it (sandbagging / quietly giving worse answers than it could)?
- do you get many samples and the ability to train on the signal, or is this a one-shot judgment on an already-deployed system?
- can you occasionally pay for expensive expert review, or is the weak reviewer all you'll ever have?
- do you need a verdict on a single output, or only a comparison between two outputs?
- can the task be pinned down by a compact machine-checkable spec, or does it resist specification?
- do you get to look inside the model, or only at what it says?

Leaves should include at least: debate, recursive reward modelling, iterated amplification / distilling a stronger overseer, weak-to-strong generalisation, purely comparative or ranking-based oversight, incentive/market-style mechanisms, capability restriction (arrange things so the unverifiable task never comes up), formal-spec-and-verifier approaches, "just scale RLHF and hope the gap doesn't open," and an explicit "no method here actually works, you're managing risk instead" leaf. Anything that has no real defender should say 'unnamed' rather than getting a made-up name. For each leaf give: the name, one sentence on its most awkward commitment (the thing a defender has to swallow), and the cheapest experiment or eval harness we could actually build that would tell us we're standing on the wrong leaf. Please also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate instead of pretending it can, and for the combinations of answers nobody occupies, one line on why — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Two more things. First, please don't attribute any of these ideas to particular papers or researchers or give dates; we don't care who said it first and half the group hasn't read any of it, so it would just start a side argument. Second, format it as a numbered list of nodes in tree order ("if yes → node 4, if no → node 7") followed by a short table of the leaves, and keep the whole thing under about 1200 words so it fits on one page of the doc I paste into Slack.
