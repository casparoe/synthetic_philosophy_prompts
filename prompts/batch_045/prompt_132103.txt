After a career in experimental microbiology I have been reading philosophy of biology in retirement, and I keep running into what look like two rival strategies for saying what life is. Call the first the organizational strategy: life is an individual-level property, consisting in a bounded chemical system that keeps itself far from equilibrium by producing the very catalysts and the very boundary that hold it together. Call the second the evolutionary strategy: life is a population-level property, consisting in a lineage's capacity to evolve by natural selection, so that a system unable to vary and to pass variation on lacks life's defining feature however intricate its chemistry.

Please compare these two strategies, but do the comparison on the following cases rather than on the usual ones (viruses, mules, fire), and please do not gesture at the literature — I can track down references myself.

1. A sealed flask holding a self-maintaining reaction network that regenerates its own catalysts and its own lipid boundary, but whose organisation is so tightly constrained that every variation in it is lethal, so no heritable variation ever arises.
2. A lineage of such flasks in which the network does vary heritably and does evolve, but which requires a reagent the experimenter supplies; withdraw it and every member dies within the hour.
3. Self-replicating programs in an artificial environment, which mutate, compete for a shared resource, and evolve, running on hardware we maintain.

My own suspicion is that the disagreement is not really about the word life at all. The organizational strategy seems to be a claim about what holds a single system together at a time, the evolutionary strategy a claim about what a lineage can do over many generations, and the cases above are hard precisely where those two come apart. Is that the genuine fault line, or is there a deeper one I am missing? And if the dispute is substantive rather than verbal, what kind of result — a construction in the laboratory, an observation, a measurement — should we expect to settle it, and why should we think the question is answerable at all rather than a matter of which property we find more interesting?
