A commenter on a blog I read (this general argument comes up every few months around here) summed up Popper's demarcation thing roughly like this:

"Popper's move was to give up on confirmation and look at testing instead. A theory is scientific just in case it's falsifiable — there's some possible observation that would show it to be false — and the method of science is to try as hard as you can to falsify your best theory and provisionally keep the ones that survive. That's why Freud and astrology aren't science: their fans can explain away any result, so no observation could ever refute them, whereas a real theory sticks its neck out. And surviving severe testing is what makes a theory rational to believe, which is why we also say a well-tested theory is probably closer to the truth than the one it replaced."

I use "not even falsifiable" as a conversation-stopper constnatly and I want to know how much of that I can keep. Two things bug me. One: can't you make any unfalsifiable theory falsifiable just by conjoining it with something dumb and checkable — "Freud was basically right and the moon is made of green cheese" is falsifiable — so falsifiability by itself seems way too cheap to do any demarcating work? Two: my friend's Bayesian spiel, that a theory can be strictly unfalsifiable and still get wrecked by evidence if it spreads its probability thinly over outcomes we didn't see, which makes the whole demarcation project sound like a category error.
