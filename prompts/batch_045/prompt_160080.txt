Next month I present a paper, "Liberty Canons," to a constitutional theory colloquium: a law-school audience that is mostly public-meaning originalists, plus two legal historians of antebellum antislavery constitutionalism and one political theorist who works on constitutional identity and political obligation.

My thesis: Douglass's break with Garrison — from his early view that the Constitution was a compact with slavery to his later view that, read as it ought to be read, it is a liberty document — should be reconstructed not as moral or aspirational reading but as a set of clear-statement canons of the kind courts use when they refuse to read a statute to displace state sovereignty or to apply extraterritorially absent unmistakable language. On this reconstruction the document's silences and general terms — its failure to use the word slave anywhere in the text, the Preamble's framing of the Constitution as an act of the people themselves, the due process clause and the guarantee of a republican form of government — carry a presumption against any construction that entrenches hereditary caste, a presumption defeasible only by text that says so plainly. I argue that this makes his change of mind a change in interpretive method rather than only in political strategy, and that it places him nearer the legal strand of antislavery constitutional argument (Spooner, Goodell, Gerrit Smith) than to the Garrisonian disunionists he left.

Give me the Q&A prep sheet I need for this particular room: seven or eight objections in the order they will actually arrive, each phrased the way that person would phrase it — the originalists pressing on what the 1787–89 public meaning of the due process and republican-government clauses actually was and on the compromise clauses that seem to concede slavery explicitly, the historians pressing on whether I am anachronistically converting a polemical antislavery argument into doctrine, the theorist pressing on whether a canon does any work or merely restates the conclusion it was introduced to reach. For each objection give a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, note what I should concede up front, what I have to hold, and where the reply is softest. Flag the two objections with real force, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
