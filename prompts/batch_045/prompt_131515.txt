Hi — I write a column on science and ideas for a Sunday paper, and my editor has greenlit a piece on whether Malthus was, as two of my sources keep insisting, 'right but early' rather than simply wrong. I'm not a philosopher of science; one of the sources is an economic historian who thinks the whole of human history before 1800 was one long Malthusian treadmill, and the other is an energy economist friend who argues that industrial civilsation is basically living off a one-off inheritance of fossil sunlight and mined fertiliser, and that this is a stock, not a rate, so the bill is deferred rather than cancelled. I went for a walk with the energy economist and he laid out the following, and I've been unable to find the seam in it since.

(1) Malthus's real claim is a mechanism plus a closure condition: when income per head rises above subsistence, people have more surviving children; population then presses on a food supply that can at best grow by adding while population grows by multiplying; and that pressure pushes income per head back down. The prediction isn't mass starvation, it's a trap — sustained growth in income per head is impossible without a check, and the check always arrives.
(2) What happened after 1798: income per head in much of the world rose enormously and population went from roughly a billion to roughly eight billion, an explosion on a scale no pre-industrial Malthusian episode ever approached.
(3) But that explosion is exactly what the first half of the mechanism predicts — loosen the constraint, get more mouths. Two centuries of it is the mechanism operating at scale, so the last two hundred years confirm the theory, they don't refute it.
(4) The only piece that hasn't turned up is the return to subsistence, and that's timing, not mechanism: the constraint was deferred by an exogenous one-off (coal, oil, Haber-Bosch nitrogen, the Green Revolution), and one-offs run out by definition.
(5) A prediction refutes a theory only if the failure couldn't have been a failure of timing. Malthus's failure can always be a failure of timing.
(6) Therefore Malthus has not been refuted, and the twentieth century is evidence for him rather than against him.
(7) And — my friend's flourish — by the same logic any 'it bites eventually' theory is confirmed by the boom that comes before the bite, and the bigger the boom, the stronger the confirmation. Peak-oil people would get to count rising production as evidence for peak oil.

Step (7) is where I start to smell something, but I can't say what. It also bugs me that the theory seems to survive in every direction: the UN's current projections have fertility at or below replacement across much of the world and population peaking somewhere around the 2080s, and my historian source files that as 'the preventive check finally kicking in', i.e. as another win. A frame that wins when population explodes and wins when it stops exploding feels like it isn't early so much as untouchable. But I also don't want to file 1,200 words that amount to 'Malthus was wrong because everyone knows he was wrong', especially given that the Ehrlich–Simon bet is the standard journalists' gotcha and it only covered a decade of commodity prices.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? I'd most like to hold on to (1) and (2) — population really does respond to resources, and the numbers really are what they are — and if something has to go I'd sooner drop (6) than (1), but I genuinely can't tell whether the work is being done by (3), (4) or (5). In particular I'd like to know whether there's a principled difference between a prediction that has merely been deferred and one that has actually been disconfirmed, and whether you can say in advance which is which without already knowing how the story ends. And if your answer is that the argument is fine and the conclusion is true, tell me that too, and tell me what I'd have to believe about, say, the demographic transition to swallow it.
