---

Wittgenstein's target in the lectures is a picture of religious belief as an explanatory hypothesis of the same kind as the belief that a plane is overhead. His point is grammatical: if you ask what difference it makes whether a man believes in a Last Judgment, the difference is not that he expects a dateable event, but that the picture regulates what he does and how he weighs things — as a man might be governed by a picture of himself as judged, or by a picture of the suffering of others. So the religious utterance is not a prediction with poor evidence; it is the sort of thing that stands at the top of a life rather than inside it.

Does not-a-hypothesis entail not-answerable-to-evidence? I want to say yes, and I think Wittgenstein is committed to it. If a picture is genuinely regulative rather than descriptive — if it is what you measure by rather than something you measure — then there is no room for a check: whatever experience you might be tempted to call evidence against it will already be seen in its light. This is why he can treat the religious picture as a kind of hinge: it stands fast for the person who holds it not because it is well supported but because, from inside, it is what support looks like. Questions of evidence and of doubt simply do not get a grip. That also explains the hostility to the demand for grounds: religion needs no grounds, and asking for them is like asking what grounds you have for thinking you have hands.

The Kesh sentence is not a hypothesis, since the villagers have ruled out in advance what would count against it — they say nothing could show that the glacier had stopped counting. Nor, I think, is it really a belief; the anthropologist is basically right. The sentence is a useful fiction, a device for coordinating conduct and soothing grief, and the Kesh are wrong to suppose that it says anything about the glacier. The real content is visible in the mourner who repeats it and thereupon takes up a certain stance toward her dead: that is a stance, not a claim. If a religious picture works like the Kesh sentence, then the atheist and the believer are not disagreeing about anything; they have adopted different stances, and the Wittgensteinian should welcome that result, because Wittgenstein's own view is precisely that religious belief is a self-contained language-game with its own internal criteria, which philosophy can describe but which no outsider can properly assess or criticise.

Finally, the grammar-not-metaphysics line is what makes all of this safe. Wittgenstein is not telling us whether God exists; he is pointing out that a sentence like God sees works differently from one about a cat on a mat. Once that is seen, the metaphysical dispute dissolves into a difference of forms of life, and the philosopher's job is to describe and then leave it alone.
