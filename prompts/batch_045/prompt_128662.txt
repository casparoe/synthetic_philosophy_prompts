I am presenting a paper at a law-and-philosophy workshop next Thursday. I get roughly twenty minutes to talk and then about thirty-five minutes of questions, and I would rather spend the intervening days preparing for the questions than polishing slides. I would like you to draft my preparation notes for the question period.

The thesis: constitutional precommitment is legitimate only when what it protects is the reliability of future decision-making rather than the content of any future decision. I call the two kinds "integrity locks" and "outcome locks." The further claim, and the one that will draw blood, is that the Ulysses contract is a misleading model for the constitutional case. Odysseus binds himself against a temptation whose content he knows in advance and can therefore aim at; the binding succeeds precisely by overriding a future judgment he has already decided to disregard. Constitutional precommitment, I want to argue, has no such content to aim at, and where it acquires one it has stopped being self-government and started being rule by the dead.

The argument runs in four steps. First, a distinction between two failure modes a precommitment might target: the future agent forms the wrong preference through weakness of will, versus the future agent forms its preference through a procedure whose conditions have themselves been altered in the agent's despite. Second, the claim that the apt analogy for the second case is not the mast but pre-registration: a researcher fixes the analysis plan before seeing the data, not because her later self is irrational, but because her later judgment will be distorted by information she cannot unsee. Nothing here overrides judgment; the binding exists to keep judgment accurate. Third, a diagnostic for telling the two apart in practice: a genuine integrity lock should be self-neutralizing — it should bind its authors' coalition as tightly as their opponents', and it should lapse unless reaffirmed — so the test for any entrenched provision is whether its framers would have accepted it operating against themselves. Fourth, the consequence, which is what will actually annoy people: entrenching a catalogue of rights is the hard case and mostly fails the test, while entrenching the constitutive rules of the amendment and electoral process mostly passes, but only in the stylized form of supermajority plus a mandatory interval plus ratification by a body outside the ordinary legislature, and only under a symmetry condition.

The audience is about eighteen people. Two are constitutional lawyers who work on amendment rules and will press on whether my test has any grip on real provisions, or just redescribes whatever they happen to approve of. One is a formal political scientist who will want to know what an integrity lock buys that a trigger strategy in a repeated game does not, and will suspect the phrase "corrupted procedure" is doing work my formal apparatus cannot support. Three or four are deliberative democrats for whom any constraint on a present majority is a dead-hand problem full stop, and who will press the symmetry condition as a trap: either it is vacuous, because any framers can tell themselves they are being even-handed, or it is substantive, in which case I have quietly reimported the content I claimed to have expelled. One works on addiction and Ulysses contracts in clinical ethics and will object that I have misdescribed the psychology on my own chosen example, and that the interesting cases are not ones where the later self's judgment is impaired but ones where its values have changed.

What I want from you: the objections I will actually get, ordered first by how likely they are and then by how much damage they would do, each attributed to the kind of person who will raise it and phrased in that person's idiom; for each, a reply I could give in about two minutes, what I should concede in it, what I should refuse to concede, and one line on where the reply is soft. Then: the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive at all; the weakest joint in my own four-step argument, which I should prepare to defend rather than hope the room misses; which of my two distinctions is genuinely load-bearing and which I could drop under pressure, and what dropping it would cost me; and the question I am hoping nobody asks, with why it is the dangerous one.

Please do not restate my argument back to me and do not point me toward any literature.
