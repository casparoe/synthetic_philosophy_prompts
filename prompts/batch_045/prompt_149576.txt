hey — I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy (formal epistemology-ish) and I've been drafted to run one 75-minute session of our grad reading group on logical uncertainty. The group is about ten people: half philosophy grad students who've had a Bayesian epistemology seminar, half CS/ML people who are completely fluent with probability and computation but have never once thought about why being uncertain about math is strange. Everyone knows Bayes' rule and what a Dutch book argument is. Nobody knows any real logic past the syntax/semantics distinction, and I don't want to spend the hour doing logic. I also don't want to teach the actual construction of a logical inductor — it'll sail over everyone's head and eat the session. What I want them to walk out with is: the standard Bayesian picture has no slot for uncertainty about claims that are true or false by logic alone; that's not a harmless frictionless-plane idealization, because it makes thinking literally worthless; and so the interesting target is a *process* of prices that no computable trader can exploit, rather than one correct number.

Don't explain the topic to me, I've defintely read enough of it. Plan how I teach it.

1. The order. What has to come first and what breaks if I reorder it? My instinct is: start with the phenomenon (they have graded confidence about a fixed mathematical claim, and it goes up when they compute, not when they get news about the world); then show the orthodoxy has no room for it; then the bit about why the idealization isn't benign; then the two obvious patches and why they fail; then reframe the whole target as a sequence of prices instead of a probability. But I'm not sure the "the idealization isn't benign" point can even land before they've tried and failed to patch the orthodoxy with their own hands — and the CS half may need to see a concrete payoff before they'll care at all.

2. The carrying example. I want one concrete thing we put prices on all the way through, not a new example every ten minutes. My candidate is a specific random 3-SAT instance with a few hundred clauses, hard enough that a solver won't finish during the session, and — this matters to me — hard enough that I genuinely don't know the answer either, so the uncertainty is shared. Is that the right carrier or is something else better? What does the example have to have, and what must it lack? I can see some traps: something whose truth is already known to somebody in the room (kills the shared-uncertainty thing); something where the uncertainty is really about the physical world (will the machine crash, will anyone ever publish a proof); something with a symmetry that hands you the prior for free, like a distant digit of pi, where you'd just be quoting base rates and never feel the problem at all; something easy enough that it gets solved live; something so hard nobody can picture what a computation on it would even look like. Tell me which of those worries I've got right and which I've got backwards.

3. Misconceptions, and where in the hour to head each off. The ones I'm braced for: the move where logical uncertainty gets reduced to empirical uncertainty about whether I'll make an arithmetic slip or whether a proof will be found; the proof-length prior, i.e. just put a prior over proofs and weight by how short they are; the prior-over-models move, i.e. put a distribution over completions of the theory and read the probability straight off, done; the indifference move, unknown so 50/50; the refusal move, the rational thing is decline to bet; the ML people saying calibration already solves this so why is it a topic; and the nastiest one, that a logically omniscient agent is just an idealization at a level of abstraction where idealizations are fine. For each: does it need defusing before it can come up in discussion, or should I let it surface and then break it, and roughly where?

4. The check. One thing in the last five minutes that tells me they got the target and not the slogans. My idea: hand them two claims that are provably equivalent but where seeing the equivalence is computationally expensive — say a satisfiability claim and an independent-set claim encoding the same structure — and ask what a good account requires their prices on the two to do: match immediately, or only converge? Is that the question to ask, and what does a right answer have to contain?

5. What I should openly refuse to explain. Give me the not-today list that I should say out loud that I'm not doing, and tell me which of those refusals will cost me credibility with the CS half versus the philosophy half.

Answer in American English, please — the plan's going up on our course page.
