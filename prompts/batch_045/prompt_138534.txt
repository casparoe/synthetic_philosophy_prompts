I am the story editor of a narrative history series — short audio episodes, one turning point each — and roughly three times a week a producer pitches an episode in which something non-human is cast as the thing that made the event happen: a pathogen, a volcanic winter, a drought, a shift in monsoon timing. I need a reusable decision procedure for these pitches, not a style guide. Input: a single causal claim of the form "X (non-human) caused / explains Y (human event)." Output: one of four verdicts — protagonist, trigger, backdrop, not established — plus one narration-safe sentence the producer is allowed to record. The procedure has to be runnable in about twenty minutes by someone with no training in climatology or epidemiology, it has to say what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, and it has to have two hard features: a stop rule (at least one point where an unanswered question ends the procedure with a verdict and the narration must fall back to coincidence language or the segment gets cut) and an explicit handoff (at least one point where the remaining question is about blame or emphasis rather than evidence and comes to me). Please build the procedure around distinctions that survive translation into practice: naming the human mechanism that carried the physical event into the outcome (if no such mechanism can be named, you have weather, not history); coupling the timescale and latency of the physical event to the claimed effect; a differential-signature test, where the same shock should produce divergent outcomes wherever institutions or practices diverge, and if the outcome tracks the human variable rather than the physical gradient the shock is demoted; an alibi test, for cases where we are unknowingly repeating a contemporaneous actor's self-exculpating attribution; a counterfactual test that holds the physical event fixed and varies human choices; and a rule for slow variables, since a constant can become a cause when it moves but only if it demonstrably moved at the scale and in the window claimed. Say plainly which of the concepts in the scholarly literature you had to distort or drop to make this usable, and where the simplification will mislead.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases. One: Tambora in 1815 and the subsistence crisis that followed in Europe and New England. Two: a pitch my producers are attached to and I am suspicious of — the 1783 Laki fissure eruption in Iceland as the cause of the French Revolution six years later — where I expect an answer they will not like. Three: a case the procedure handles badly, where the pathogen really does look like the protagonist rather than a trigger (I am thinking of post-1492 epidemic mortality in the Americas), and where I want you to show exactly which step fails and why, so I know when not to trust the output. Finally, be honest about what the procedure cannot do at all: I do not think it can apportion degrees of causal credit, only rank them, and I suspect it systematically under- or over-credits agents that act on bodies rather than on institutions; tell me if that is right and what else it is blind to.
