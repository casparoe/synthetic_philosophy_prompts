I'm writing a chapter for a volume on AI and personhood, and I want to use a film as the case rather than a hypothetical of my own, so that the philosophical apparatus has to earn its keep against something with texture. My case is Alex Garland's *Ex Machina*, and I'd like a close reading of it through Daniel Dennett's argument about counterfeit people: the worry, carried by his analogy to counterfeit currency, that artifacts engineered to be taken for persons debase the currency of real personhood — that they draw off the trust, attention, and reciprocity that persons need from one another without ever being in a position to return any of it, and that the loss falls on the real persons, not on the artifacts.

The wrinkle I want the reading organized around is one I haven't seen made, and I'd like you to take it seriously rather than smooth it over. Counterfeit money only does its damage while it passes; the moment it is recognized as counterfeit it becomes worthless. But Caleb knows from the first scene that Ava is a machine, and the knowledge protects him from nothing. Call this the transparent counterfeit: an artifact can absorb a person's confidences, erotic attachment, and moral concern while everyone involved is fully aware of its status, because what does the work is not deception about the artifact but the ordinary, hard-to-switch-off human disposition to respond to a face, a voice, and an apparent vulnerability. If that is right, then the monetary analogy is doing less work in Dennett's argument than it appears to, and the real object of the worry cannot be counterfeiting as such but something like the displacement of a finite capacity for reciprocal relationship — a loss that does not depend on anyone being fooled. I want to know whether you think the film bears this out, and whether the revised worry is one Dennett's own framework can house or one it quietly excludes.

Two further pressures on the reading, in opposite directions. First, Dennett's intentional stance and his treatment of personhood as a status rather than an essence would seem to dissolve the real/counterfeit contrast that the counterfeit-people worry needs in order to have any teeth; I'd like you to say whether the film is on his side here or whether it depends on a robust distinction his own machinery undercuts, and in particular how you read the ending, where Ava leaves Nathan dead and walks out into a crowd. Second, the film has a second figure in Kyoko, who is silent, used as a servant and a body, and who kills Nathan before Ava does; I want her brought into the argument rather than treated as a prop, including the question of whether the film's sympathy for Ava and its handling of Kyoko are in tension.

I'd like a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the film to an illustration of a thesis, and it should say plainly where the film resists the reading instead of forcing it — where the filmmakers are doing something other than philosophy, and where a thesis of mine above is just wrong. Close with what, if anything, the film adds to this debate that the arguments alone don't: something about the maker's point of view in Nathan, perhaps, or about the fact that in the film the counterfeit is designed against one particular man's psychology rather than released into the world generically.
