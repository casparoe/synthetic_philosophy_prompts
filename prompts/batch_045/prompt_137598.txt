I serve on the Boundary Commission of the Cedar Basin Regional Authority, a special-purpose district with taxing, zoning, transit, stormwater, and police powers, and I have been asked to draft the ruling in the last contested franchise case before our term ends. I want it to be consistent with what we have already done, and I would rather be told that our precedents run out than be handed a tidy principle that quietly reverses one of them. Our track record on who counts as a voter is this: non-citizen residents, including people without legal immigration status who have lived in the district two years or more, are in; owners of homes, rental units, and businesses located in the district who live outside it are out, notwithstanding that they pay district property taxes and that district decisions set the value of what they own; commuters who work in the district and ride its transit are in, on the ground that they are subject to fare enforcement, transit police, and street ordinances; pretrial detainees in the district jail are in, while people serving sentences in that same jail under transfer from other jurisdictions, who will be released elsewhere, are out; residents aged sixteen and seventeen are in, over strong dissent, because they will live longest with what we decide; adults under full guardianship with profound intellectual disabilities, living in district group homes, are in, the Commission having expressly rejected any test of competence; and seasonal farmworkers housed in district labor camps for roughly five months a year, who return to another country for the rest, are out by a four-to-three vote whose opinion said their centre of life is elsewhere.

The new case is Mill Creek, an unincorporated settlement of about nine hundred people just outside our boundary, in the same county, downstream of our stormwater system. The referendum we are balloting on is a stormwater and zoning package that, on our own engineers' modelling, decides whether Mill Creek floods in a ten-year storm. These residents do not live, work, or own property in the district and cannot be fined or arrested by it; about two hundred of them, however, work in the district and so appear to qualify already under the commuter precedent, and about sixty are sixteen or seventeen and would qualify under the youth precedent if they lived a few hundred metres to the north. They cannot incorporate, since the county board must approve incorporation and never has, and annexation into the district requires the Authority's own consent, which has never been given; the county has no stormwater authority and has said it will not oppose us. They have petitioned for votes, and in the alternative for a single delegate with a vote or a non-voting seat on our advisory board.

I have drafted two candidate rules that seem to fit everything we have decided so far, and I suspect there are others: the first counts anyone with ongoing, non-incidental exposure to the Authority's coercive power, understood as the standing possibility of being bound, fined, searched, or arrested in the ordinary course of one's life; the second counts anyone whose prospects are durably shaped by what we decide and who has no other body, to which they can vote, capable of checking us — a version of which one of my colleagues calls the unchecked-power test. Please tell me what ruling you would draft if the aim is coherence with our own precedents, which earlier decision is the closest analog and why, where the plausible rules that fit our track record come apart on Mill Creek, and what principle we would be committing ourselves to under each of the options: full enfranchisement, enfranchisement only of the two hundred commuters and sixty minors who already fit existing categories, a delegate with a vote, or denial. Note also that our enabling statute speaks of inhabitant voters, so tell me how much weight to give the fact that one option needs a statutory amendment and the others do not. Finally, name the single further case that would best discriminate between the rules you think survive, and, in the ruling itself, mark how confident you are in each part of it and single out the one claim you would expect a democratic theorist to reject.
