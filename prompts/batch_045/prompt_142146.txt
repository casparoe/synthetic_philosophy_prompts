I came into philosophy last year from an economics PhD, and on Thursday I'm presenting to a rational-choice reading group on Parfit's hitchhiker — the desert case where the driver will take you to San Francisco only if he can tell, at the roadside, that you'll hand over the $100 on arrival, and where once you've arrived there is nothing he can do to collect. My supervisor had me run it in a slightly unfamiliar form: the driver has no insight into your character, but carries a well-calibrated detector that reads the conditional probability that you will pay given that you arrive, and he drives off unless that number clears 0.9. Stipulate that the ride is worth about $10,000 to you, that you cannot pay in advance, that there is no contract, no reputational consequence and no way for him to collect afterwards, and that money is the only thing at stake. I built the argument below to force a verdict, and it now looks to me as though it proves far too much, which is why I'm writing to you instead of just standing up and giving it.

(1) If it is rational for an agent to have disposition D, and D is a disposition to φ in circumstances C, then when C obtains the agent acts rationally in φ-ing — because if reasons to be a certain kind of person never bore on what to do, claims about what it is rational to be would not be normative claims about action at all, and the case would be a fact about causation rather than about reasons.
(2) In the hitchhiker case, the disposition it is rational to have is the disposition to pay on arrival: it is the only thing that gets you into the car, and $10,000 dwarfs $100.
(3) That disposition has no behavioural upshot except after the ride is over; there is no other occasion on which it can be exercised.
(4) So when you reach San Francisco you act rationally in paying.
(5) But by then the ride is behind you, the driver has no hold over you, and $100 buys you nothing. So you act rationally in keeping it.
(6) So (4) and (5) cannot both be true; and since I cannot see how to give up (5), one of (1), (2), (3) has to go.

Here is why I think I have blundered rather than discovered something. Run the same three premises with a second driver who picks up only hitchhikers disposed to burn a $100 note on arrival: (2) is satisfied just as well by that disposition, since it too is worth $10,000 at the roadside and costs $100 later, so (1) and (3) entail that on arrival it is rational to set fire to your own money. That seems flatly false — a third party cannot manufacture reasons for you by conditioning a benefit on your having some disposition or other — so (1) looks like the culprit. My supervisor says giving up (1) is close to fatal: it appears to commit you to the view that reasons to be a certain kind of agent are never reasons to act, which would turn every argument for becoming the sort of person who does not re-maximize at every juncture, and every argument for sticking to a plan when you reach the later node, into a non sequitur. And she points out that I cannot reject (1) without giving up the thing that made the case matter to me: I spent years assuming that maximizing act by act settles everything, and I started this course precisely because this case shook that. I would like to keep (2) and (3) if I can.

So please: name the step where this goes wrong, and do it twice — once for the money-burning version and once for the original, in case they do not fail in the same place — and tell me what each rejection costs, including what it would cost me to hold on to (5) if (5) is really where the trouble is. At the end, separate the claims in your diagnosis that anyone who works on these questions would grant from the ones that are genuinely live disputes: I do not want to stand up in front of the group and present one side of an unsettled argument as the received view. No citations or reading list, please — I can find those myself. Just mark each substantial claim as near-unanimous, as contested-but-respected, or as your own preferred position, and say plainly if a step is one where competent people simply disagree.
