Here is the answer I'm stuck on, typed out more or less verbatim, typos and all:

"Parfit's non-identity problem rests on two claims. The first is that who a person is depends on the circumstances of their conception — which sperm, which egg, roughly when — so if the girl acts differently, a numerically different person exists later, not the same person with different properties. The second is that it is better for someone to have a life worth living than never to exist at all. So in the fourteen-year-old case, the child she has now could not have existed had she waited, and her life is worth living; the girl's act therefore makes no one worse off and wrongs no one. An act can be wrong only if there is someone it is worse for, so the act is not wrong. This shows that person-affecting views are simply refuted: no version of the restriction can survive, because any act that affects who exists will fail its test, and since nearly every act of ours shifts the timing of conceptions somewhere downstream, almost nothing we do could ever be wrong. Parfit's own response was to hold onto the intuitive verdict and give up the restriction instead, which is why he ends up saying that the outcome can be worse even though it is worse for no one.

The compensation variant doesn't change anything. If the problem is really about identity, then it doesn't matter that the girl can improve her own child's prospects rather than conceive a different one: the child is still not made worse off by being at 55 rather than 70, since at 55 she has a life worth living, and a person-affecting view has nothing to say about how well off someone is in absolute terms. And a view that said 'leave people above the level of a life worth living' is just a comparative view in disguise, because 'worth living' is itself a comparison between existing and not existing. So the supplement isn't required and the variant is the original case with extra steps.

For Part B, the different-number case is the same argument with more people in it. Under depletion, whoever exists later would not have existed under conservation, so again no one is made worse off, and again the restriction gives the wrong answer. A solution to the same-number case therefore automatically solves the different-number case: the structure is identical and only the number of people differs. And once you go impersonal, the number of people is irrelevant anyway — total utilitarianism handles both cases the same way, by adding up well-being."
