I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy training — what physics I know comes from weekend reading and from building a small rigid-body physics engine as a hobby project — and over the past year or so I've drifted into holding spacetime substantivalism: the view that spacetime, or the metric field, is a real thing with structure of its own, rather than a bookkeeping device for the distances and durations among material bodies. What pushed me there is the bucket case in its most stripped-down form: a universe containing nothing but one spinning sphere of fluid would still bulge at its equator, and the relationist seems forced to ask what it is rotating relative to, whereas I can just say it rotates relative to spacetime's own inertial structure. I also notice that in my engine the simplest working implementation stores absolute positions and velocities and derives relations afterward, and that every coordinate-free rewrite I've attempted ends up smuggling an equivalent background structure back in somewhere — which makes me suspect the relationist is describing my code rather than my physics. I'd like that suspicion stress-tested rather than confirmed.

So: play it straight. What would count against substantivalism, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? Start with the worry I can't shake myself. If spacetime points or regions are genuine individuals, then consider two worlds that differ only by a smooth sliding of all the matter and metric fields across an otherwise empty region — every body in the same relation to every other body, every possible observation identical, but the fields sitting at different points. Those look like two distinct possibilities for me and one possibility for my opponent, and I am not sure why that isn't fatal by the same standard that dispatched earlier absolute-space views. Is it? And if the escape is to keep the metric's structure real while denying that points have primitive identities — a view where geometry is real but there are no individuatable locations for it to be real at — does that still count as substantivalism, or have I quietly crossed over? I want to know whether that middle position is stable or whether it collapses into one side under pressure.

Then there's a scenario I made up to make the question sharp, and I want to know how much weight it should carry if it turned out to be true. Suppose future precision measurements showed that local inertial frames track the large-scale distribution of matter far more tightly than current theory requires: that in any region where the surrounding matter is roughly uniform, the non-rotating frame coincides almost exactly with the frame in which that matter is at rest, that this holds across wildly different matter densities, and that it still holds in regions where the density is extremely low. Call it the Machian coincidence. My instinct is that this wouldn't touch me — I'd say it's a contingent, perhaps lawlike fact about which matter distribution and which metric happen to co-exist in our universe, and that the empty counterfactual universe still has a definite fact about rotation. But I want to know whether that reply is principled or whether I've insulated myself: if no arrangement of matter could ever tell me anything about whether spacetime is doing the work, in what sense is my claim about spacetime doing explanatory work instead of just restating the physics in different words? Beyond those two, what else should I be exposed to — cases the view can't describe, consequences it's stuck with, results in nearby areas it stands or falls with — and which of these would a committed substantivalist concede as counting against them rather than dismiss as a confusion about representation? I'd personally find most convincing an argument that my view either does no work or collapses into its rival, so don't hold back on the theoretical defeaters; I don't need an experiment to be persuaded.
