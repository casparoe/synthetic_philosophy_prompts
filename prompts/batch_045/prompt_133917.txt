The question I set (20 points):

Leibniz claims that God creates the best of all possible worlds. In your answer: (a) explain why a possible world for Leibniz has to be a maximal set of compossible things, rather than just the collection of everything that is possible on its own; (b) say whether God's selecting this world is a matter of logical necessity or only moral necessity, and why Leibniz needs the distinction; (c) give one implication of the view that a critic would find hard to accept.

The answer I'm looking at:

Leibniz says that before creating anything God considers every way the world could be. A possible world can't just be everything possible thrown together, because some possibles rule each other out: the Adam who eats the apple and the Adam who doesn't can't both show up in the same history, since a world is a single interlocked story where every event fits with every other. So a possible world is a maximal compossible set, basically the biggest bundle of possibles that can all cohere. Since God is perfectly good and perfectly rational he picks the best of these bundles, and he isn't arbitrary about it: the best is the one with the greatest variety combined with the greatest order, meaning the most happiness and the least suffering compatible with there being creatures at all.

The choice is morally necessary, not logically necessary. God's goodness infallibly inclines him toward the best without forcing him, so there's no contradiction in supposing he had done otherwise, and that's what lets Leibniz keep the world contingent and pin moral evil on free creatures rather than on God. It also fits his claim that evil isn't a positive thing God made but a privation, the built-in limitation of anything created.

A hard implication: on this picture individuals look like ingredients rather than ends, since someone might have to suffer in order to raise the total. And there's a deeper problem behind that — if God is genuinely choosing between alternatives, the alternatives have to be real in their own right, so Leibniz seems stuck with something like Lewis's modal realism, a plurality of concrete worlds we just happen not to inhabit.
