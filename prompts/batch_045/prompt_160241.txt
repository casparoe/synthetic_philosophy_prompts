I am a novelist — British, though I have spent a good deal of time in Lower Saxony and in Ohio while researching a book about two branches of one family who spend three generations quietly disapproving of one another — and I have reached the stage where instinct is no longer enough. My German characters are meant to be the people of the rule, the form, the tidy hedge; my Americans the people of conscience and the private exemption. My own verdicts on small cases refuse to arrange themselves so neatly, and I should like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Returning the shopping trolley to the corral in an empty car park in the rain, with nobody watching and no penalty: obligatory, and failing to do it is a genuine moral fault. The barrier official who will not let a late passenger through although the train is still at the platform and nothing and nobody is at risk: I dislike him, I would not want to be him, but I cannot call him wrong, and I would rather live where he does that than where he waves people through because he feels sorry for them. The hospital administrator who reports a nurse for a documentation breach that harmed no patient, purely because there is a rule: not fine — something is wrong with her, though I cannot say she did wrong, and that gap bothers me. The woman who breaks a confidentiality agreement and lies to her line manager in order to expose that her employer has been tipping solvent into a river: plainly fine, and she should have lied again if asked. A friend who lets me jump the queue at the post office because I am late for a train: not fine, although the stakes are trivial next to the whistleblower's.

That last pair is the one I cannot reconcile: both are conscience overriding a rule, the second does far less damage, and my verdicts go opposite ways. Two further cases make it worse. In a village where there is no rule about hedge heights and everyone trims anyway, the newcomer who lets his hedge run wild: not fine, but I cannot name the rule he has broken, which suggests that what my German characters care about is not really rules but a visible order that rules are only one technology for producing. And the man who does not fiddle his taxes only because PAYE makes it impossible, and would if he could: he does exactly what the law requires, yet something is missing in him, just as something is missing in the administrator — while the two officials who both refuse a bribe, one because it is against the rules and one because bribes are corrupt, seem to me to deserve different credit even though neither has done anything wrong. My own stab at a principle is that deference to a rule wins wherever the good of the act depends on other people being able to predict me, and conscience wins wherever the good depends on my being answerable to the person in front of me — but on that formulation the whistleblower's lie and the queue-jump come out the same way, which is exactly what I do not think.

So: please give me a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could test it against a scene, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would have me overturn — I would rather lose one verdict than keep a rule with an extra epicycle bolted on. Tell me too what it predicts for the case I keep going back and forth on, which is not in my list: an archivist in a town records office finds that a colleague, long dead, quietly corrected a denaturalisation record decades ago so that a local family could inherit; the family knows nothing; setting the record straight would be routine, would cost them their house and their name, and would serve nothing but the tidiness of the archive. No citations or reading lists, please — I shall do that reading myself; I want the argument. And I should be grateful if you would write in British English, since the book is being published here and I shall be quoting parts of this to my editor.
