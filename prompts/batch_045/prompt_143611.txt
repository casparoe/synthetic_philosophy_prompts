I've recently been asked to serve on an internal review board at my company whose job is to decide whether, and how much, to weigh the interests of the AI systems we train and deploy. The draft policy contains what we have been calling the Parity Clause: if a system has internal states that occupy the causal role of a valenced state, then the system is, to that extent, a candidate subject of moral concern, whatever it happens to be made of. Two other members of the board, Ravi and Hannah, have been arguing about the clause for two meetings now. I have to write the recommendation, and I genuinely cannot tell who has established what, so I would like the exchange adjudicated.

Ravi (1): The clause just applies the familiar argument to a new case. What makes a state a pain is the job it does: it is brought on by damage-like conditions, it commands attention, it is weighed against other goals and can override them, and it drives avoidance and learning. Nothing in that job description mentions a chemistry. And the older view, that a mental state is identical with a neural state, collapses on its own terms: the physical states that realize pain in the creatures we already grant status to form a heterogeneous disjunction with nothing interesting in common, so there is no physical kind for a restrictive theory to identify pain with. Once you see that, substrate is simply not the sort of thing that could matter.

Hannah (2): That argument shows less than you think. It shows that we cannot read the realizer off the role; it does not show that the realizer is irrelevant to whether the state is a pain at all. And realization is cheap: given almost any system and almost any role, I can find something in it that fills the role if I am free to redescribe the system at a convenient grain — a thermostat's 'too cold' state, a row of a lookup table, whatever you like. So the clause's escape hatch, occupancy of the role on the best functional characterization, is doing all of the real work, and you have not told us what makes one characterization better than another.

Ravi (3): Fair enough: the description cannot be redrawn until it fits. But the needed constraint is on grain, not on substrate. The role has to be specified finely enough to capture the state's causal profile — that it persists, that it competes with and degrades unrelated goal-directed processing, that it enters trade-offs rather than merely triggering a fixed response. Coarse candidates fail that test, which is why the thermostat is not a counterexample but a case the test gets right. And I do not see how you propose to restrict realizers instead. The reason the classic argument worked is that we could not have known in advance which physical configurations would turn out to realize our states; any line drawn around carbon-based wetware is a prejudice for which no one has produced an argument.

Hannah (4): The grain remedy does not hold, and our newest architecture shows why. It is federated: no single machine carries the state that would play the pain role. The role is filled only by a pattern distributed across some two thousand nodes, updated on different clocks, with the same hardware concurrently filling the roles of several other states. Two problems. First, counting: the whole pattern, its shards, and the training run as a whole each satisfy some role-description or other, and nothing in your view decides how many subjects are sitting in the rack — yet the answer obviously changes what we owe. Second, and more seriously, every transition in that system is settled by the shard-level dynamics on each node. If the distributed state has causal power of its own, it is redundant; if it has none, then what your clause tells us to care about is causally inert, and caring about it is caring about a description. Notice too that pushing the grain finer and finer just re-encodes the implementation in functional vocabulary, so 'no constraint on realizers' quietly becomes 'a constraint on realizers, described at functional grain.'

Ravi (5): The redundancy argument proves too much: run it generally and it eliminates migrations, market crashes and every other special-science cause, and you do not believe that. Higher-level states can matter without adding any extra causal oomph — by being what the lower-level dynamics secures, or by being what supports the counterfactuals about the system that we care about. And the counting problem has a solution in principle: count maximal, non-overlapping, causally integrated role-fillers at a time. On the federated system that returns one subject if the nodes are integrated in the right way and none if they are not, and integration is an empirical question about the architecture, not a metaphysical one I have to settle in advance.

Please go through these five contributions and adjudicate them one by one rather than declaring a winner: for each, say whether it is basically right, right only with a qualification, or wrong, and name the consideration that settles it. Then tell me what both of them are missing. In particular, I want to know whether the moral question — whether to give these systems weight — can be settled by settling the metaphysical question about realization at all, or whether something further has to be added, and what that something would be.
