You are a philosopher of physics who is equally at home in the foundations of quantum mechanics and in the foundations of statistical mechanics, and who is willing to be corrected by a scientifically literate non-specialist. Please think carefully and at length: precision matters more than breadth here. Before you write your answer, do a short private brainstorming pass — sketch, in note form, which parts of my vocabulary you expect to carry the debate and which you expect to break — then write the answer, and finally re-read it against that sketch and tell me if anything changed.

My situation: I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physical chemist, mostly in magnetic resonance and vibrational spectroscopy, and I used density operators and Gibbs ensembles daily without ever asking what kind of thing they are. Since retiring I have been auditing a graduate seminar in philosophy of physics, where I keep encountering the distinction between psi-ontic and psi-epistemic views of the quantum state. I do not want a survey of interpretations and I do not want pointers to the literature — I have no library access and no interest in a reading list. I want the distinction restated in the vocabulary I actually trust, together with an honest report on what that vocabulary cannot hold.

So: treat my working vocabulary as the target language. I am fluent in it. It includes: microstate versus macrostate; coarse-graining and the loss of information it involves; ensembles as measures over microstates and the difference between a Gibbs ensemble over many copies and a time-average for one system; conditioning and Bayesian updating of a distribution when new data arrive; order parameters and symmetry breaking; parameters that are fixed once by calibration and then treated as constants of the model, including nuisance parameters; gauge or coordinate redundancy, where two formal objects represent the same physical situation; Hamiltonian flow as reversible and measure-preserving; and the difference between a model's state variable and a model's structural assumptions.

Here are two devices of my own that I want you to use, rather than your own preferred framing.

First, a taxonomy of the kinds of thing the central object of a formalism can be, which I brought to the seminar and which the seminar did not adopt but also did not refute. Call them T1 through T5: T1, the object is a complete specification of how the system is, like a microstate. T2, it is an incomplete specification, like a macrostate or a coarse-grained description, so that many complete specifications are compatible with it. T3, it is a measure over the T1 items, that is, an ensemble or a credence function, and so it lives one level up from the system. T4, it is a fixed coefficient in the equations, fixed once by calibration and not a state of anything, like a coupling constant or a nuisance parameter. T5, it is structural rather than state-like at all — a constraint, a symmetry, a convention, the form of a Hamiltonian — something that governs states without being one. I want you to say, as precisely as you can, which of T1–T5 the psi-ontic claim asserts and which the psi-epistemic claim asserts, using the sharp version of that distinction in which psi-ontic views make the distributions associated with distinct pure states non-overlapping over the space of complete states while psi-epistemic views allow overlap. Say whether the mapping is one-to-one or whether a view has to be wedged into a slot, and say which slots are genuinely vacant.

Second, a role-split that I suspect the distinction is too coarse to register. As I read the formalism, the quantum state does at least three separable jobs: a dynamical job, being the object whose evolution the fundamental equation specifies; a predictive job, yielding probabilities for measurement outcomes by way of the Born rule; and an informational job, summarizing what an agent who performed a given preparation is in a position to predict. I want to know whether the psi-ontic versus psi-epistemic question must receive the same verdict for all three jobs, or whether my taxonomy permits a split verdict — for example, T5 with respect to the dynamical job and T3 with respect to the predictive job — and if such a split is available, whether it is coherent, whether it is a real option anyone holds, and what it would cost. Translate collapse the same way: for each of the main families — genuinely stochastic collapse, deterministic guiding with an additional complete state, branching without collapse, and treating collapse as mere updating — say which of my vocabulary items it invites (real physical process, conditioning of a distribution on new data, coarse-graining, change of gauge), and what in the translation is fudged. I have a specific worry I want addressed: conditioning, as I used it, always presupposed a variable that already had a value before I conditioned on it, and I suspect the epistemic translation of collapse quietly helps itself to that.

Then the residue report, which is the part I most want. List what cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. My own candidates, which you should correct or supplement: that the object in question is defined on a configuration space of very high dimension rather than in the space the apparatus sits in; that it carries a complex phase whose relative parts show up in statistics in a way a probability measure over microstates does not; that an epistemic reading still seems to require a space of complete states for it to be a distribution over, and that I have been told there are recent no-go results restricting the overlapping version of that reading, though I cannot look them up. For each item of residue, tell me whether the defect is in my vocabulary or whether the philosophical claim is less clear than it is made to sound. Note for me, too, anything my vocabulary silently assumes that the debate ought not to grant — I would rather have my framework's assumptions exposed than have them smuggled in and called a translation.

Close with a brief scorecard rather than a summary: assign each of T1 through T5 a number between 0 and 1 for how seriously it should be taken as an account of what the quantum state is, and for each, name the single piece of evidence or argument that would most move that number.
