I write about science and statistics for a newspaper and for a radio programme, and I've been asked to give my newsroom's quarterly skills session: 75 minutes, about fourteen reporters and section editors, one screen and a handout I can print. The room's numeracy is mixed — two or three will be quietly hostile to numbers, one will know more than I do and will say so. The topic is Simpson's paradox.

I can state the paradox, and I've written about the two standard illustrations before (a university admissions comparison and a comparison of two kidney-stone treatments). What I want from you is not the explanation but a plan for the session, and I'd rather have my own instincts tested than confirmed.

First, the dependency structure. Tell me the order the pieces have to arrive in and why: what has to be in place before the reversal even registers as surprising, and which step collapses if I move it later. In particular, I want to know whether the arithmetic should come before or after the interpretive problem. My instinct is to have them see the reversal first and only then discover that the arithmetic is silent about what it means; but I can imagine the opposite case, that without the mechanism they'll walk away thinking they've been shown a trick.

Second, the case that carries the session. I don't want the two chestnuts above — the room has heard both, and hearing them again will confirm that this is a curiosity about other people's data. Give me one example to build the hour on and say what it has to contain and what it must avoid. Two constraints of my own: the numbers should be numbers a reader would actually act on, and there should be a detail I can change mid-session while leaving every cell of the table identical, such that the right conclusion flips. I'd also like your view on whether they should construct the reversal themselves — I have a half-formed plan in which they try and fail to build a reversal out of equal-sized groups, and thereby discover for themselves that unequal group sizes are doing all the work. If that's workable in the time, tell me how to set it up; if it's a trap, tell me why and what to do instead.

Third, the misconceptions, and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. The ones I expect: that the aggregate is a lie and the disaggregated numbers are the truth; that this is a small-sample or rounding artefact that more data would dissolve; that it is the same thing as the ecological fallacy or as ordinary confounding; that if the subgroup verdicts are unanimous one should plainly act on them; and — the journalist's temptation — that when the two summaries disagree the honest thing is to print both and let the reader decide. Add the ones I haven't listed. For each, say whether the defusing belongs before the case, during the reveal, or in the last ten minutes, and what damage is done by defusing it at the wrong moment.

Fourth, three things I'm unsure whether to include at all, and I'd like a verdict on each. Whether to raise the distinction between a grouping variable measured before the exposure and one produced by it: this is, I think, the deepest point available, and the one most likely to lose the hostile third of the room. Whether to tell them that you can keep subdividing and get a reversal back at a finer grain — I've heard this claimed as if it were always possible, I don't know whether it is, and I worry that telling a newsroom that any comparison can be reversed by partitioning it differently hands them a licence rather than a skill, since they will meet this move used in bad faith by people with an interest in the result. And whether the choice of summary matters — whether a reversal can appear for one way of stating the comparison and not for another — or whether that is a subtlety to leave out and openly say I'm leaving out.

My own one-sentence framing, which I want you to test against the session as you design it and repair or replace: that this is not a puzzle about numbers but a question about which partition you are entitled to hold fixed, and that the arithmetic cannot answer it. Tell me where that framing misleads a working reporter.

Finally, what they should be able to do at the end, and how I'll know. I want a check that doesn't test recall — I was going to hand them a paragraph of press-release prose containing a comparison and ask each of them to write a one-sentence lede, on the grounds that the lede forces the choice of summary into the open. Tell me what a correct lede has to contain, what the most tempting wrong lede will look like, and what a wrong lede tells me about which step failed. And give me the three or four questions I should put on a card for reading any study that reports a comparison.

Close by telling me which part of this plan is most likely to fail with this particular audience, which step you'd cut first if the session runs short, and what would lead you to sequence it differently — I'd rather have your reasoning exposed at the point where it's weakest than have it presented as settled. Don't send me to papers or reading lists; I have no access to journals from here and my colleagues won't read them.
