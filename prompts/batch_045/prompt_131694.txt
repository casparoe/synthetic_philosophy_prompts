I have just come out of a long, inconclusive argument with a friend — an economist, a card-carrying Bayesian — about the Allais paradox, and I want a case good enough to settle it, or at least to show him that the argument is not over. His position is that the Allais preferences are an arithmetic blunder plus a well-known psychological quirk, that anyone who is shown the two-urn presentation — the one where an initial draw either sends you to the high-probability branch, which pays the same thing whichever option you picked, or to the low-probability branch, where the options differ — feels the full force of the sure-thing principle and retracts. My position is that there are versions of the pattern in which the preference is not a slip at all but a genuine value: something like caring whether the worst outcome is off the table, or caring what kind of situation you are put in while the matter is unresolved, which no amount of redescription makes evaporate. So I need the case, and I need it built to order. Please act as a decision theorist and a philosopher of probability who has taught expected utility theory at graduate level: think carefully, and before committing to anything, brainstorm three or four candidate setups and discard the ones that are really just the money version in a costume, or that smuggle in diminishing marginal utility, or that work only because the agent is afraid of learning later what the forgone option would have given her. Then build the one that survives, and show that it survives.

The specifications. One paragraph, realistic, first-personal — a treatment decision, a care or custody arrangement, a job and a visa, something a normal person could actually face; no game shows, no trolleys. The stakes must not be money and must not be fungible: three outcome levels, call them good, better and worst, where the worst is not merely less of the good, and where no quantity of the good compensates for it. There must be two binary choices with exact, simple probabilities and a physically checkable randomizer (an urn of a hundred numbered balls, say), such that the agent takes the sure option in the first pair and the risky option in the second — the Allais pattern. The preferences must be reflectively stable: the agent has been told about the certainty effect, has seen the sequential two-urn presentation in which the shared high-probability branch pays identically either way, has been guaranteed she will never be told what the declined option would have yielded, and still prefers as specified. A case that works only because the agent is confused or has not thought about it is a failed case. And the pattern must not be explainable as risk aversion; show that it is not.

After the scenario I want five things. First, the decomposition: rewrite the risky option of the first pair as a compound lottery so that the common consequence is visible, apply reduction and independence step by step, and show that the second pair is what you get by scaling the non-shared branch down — that is, show that these really are one choice seen twice, and that what is violated here is the independence axiom specifically and not transitivity, reduction, or something else. Second, an explicit proof that no assignment of utilities to your three outcomes rationalizes both preferences: normalize the good and the worst, leave the better one as a free variable, and derive the contradiction. Third, name the feature of the case that does the work, and say what it is about the structure — not merely about the agent's psychology — that generates the flip. Fourth, an ablation: change exactly one thing (make the worst outcome survivable rather than catastrophic, or make the better outcome only marginally better, or make the shared branch itself slightly risky in one option only) and show precisely where the pattern collapses back into expected-utility behaviour and where it does not. Fifth, the strongest reply available to my friend: give the best version of the charge that you have misdescribed the outcome space, so that the real outcomes include facts about the choice situation — having turned down a sure thing, having lived through the wait, having held an option and lost it — and the agent is therefore an expected-utility maximizer over a finer partition; then say what is wrong with that reply, and what would have to be true for it to count as a defence rather than as a tautology.

One last thing, which fixes what counts as cheating. This is for a tutorial in which I want to hand students a case where violating independence looks like a value rather than a mistake, so the verdicts have to fall out of the structure and the constraints, not be stipulated: an agent who simply has an inconsistent preference is no use to me, and neither is one with exotic stakes, unmeasurable probabilities, or a plainly worse outcome she is said to prefer for no reason. Give the case a name, start with a short note on what you rejected in brainstorming and why, and keep the whole response under eight hundred words.
