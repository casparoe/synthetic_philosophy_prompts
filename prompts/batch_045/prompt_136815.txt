"The key move is to stop asking what's right and start asking what you should do given that you don't know what's right. You have a probability distribution over moral theories — say you're 40% on some form of consequentialism, 30% on a duties or rights view, 20% on something virtue- or character-based, and 10% spread over everything else. Each theory assigns every option a score, you take the average of those scores weighted by your credences, and you do whatever comes out highest. That's the whole thing. It's just expected value with the moral theory treated as one more unknown, no different in principle from being unsure what the weather will do. Two consequences follow. First, you should hedge: because you're averaging, an option that does a moderate amount of good on every theory will usually beat an option that does a great deal of good on one theory and nothing on the others, so splitting your money or your effort across several causes is nearly always the right call. Second, and this is where it falls apart, everyone in the field knows it falls apart: if any theory on your list says some option affects an astronomically large number of beings, then even a fraction of a percent of credence in that theory swamps everything else, and the weighted average returns that theory's favourite option no matter what the other ninety-nine percent says. The idea was supposed to stop people going all-in on strange theories and it does the opposite. The usual patch is to discard theories below a credence floor — round anything under about three percent down to zero, since nobody's credences are calibrated that finely anyway — and just admit that's ad hoc."
