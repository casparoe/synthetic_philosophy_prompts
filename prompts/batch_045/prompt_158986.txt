I'm putting together the final handout for an advanced seminar I co-teach on modality and semantic content, and I've decided the artifact should be a decision tree rather than yet another prose survey of the literature. My students have all been through the standard cases — the Hesperus/Phosphorus identity, water and Twin Earth, the standard meter in Paris, the number of planets, the zombie conceivability argument, and the behavior of indexicals in sentences about oneself and the present moment — and they can recite the labels well enough. But when I ask them what they actually take those cases to show, they blur together several views that I think should be kept apart. So the tree has to sort them by what they say about cases, not by which name they prefer.

The pipeline I have in mind is this: start every student from a shared commitment, namely that some identity statements are necessary yet knowable only a posteriori, and that a term can latch onto a referent even when most of what a competent speaker associates with the term is false of that referent. From there, each branch node should be a single question a student could answer without already knowing what view it leads to. I want at least these cuts represented: whether the a priori-accessible intension is a genuine component of semantic content or merely something we can know a priori about the reference; whether the two dimensions are two dimensions of modality, of context, or of epistemic status; whether the variety of necessity that is knowable a priori — sometimes called deep necessity, or necessity in the primary sense — is meant to do real philosophical work or is only a bookkeeping device for tracking which modal claims are a priori; whether essentially indexed expressions need their own separate apparatus or fall out of the same framework; and whether the framework's treatment of conceivability is load-bearing or is idle machinery that could be detached without loss. Leaves should carry a named position, or a neutral description if nobody holds it: the Kripkean direct-reference treatment of names and natural-kind terms, the epistemic two-dimensionalist picture associated with Chalmers, the metasemantic or contextualist picture associated with Stalnaker and with Kaplan's work on indexicals, descriptivist and reference-fixer variants that grant the cases but deny they are semantic, and the broadly skeptical line associated with Soames, according to which the two-dimensional apparatus misdescribes what is going on.

Three further constraints. First, the audience is advanced undergraduates who know the cases but have read almost no secondary literature, so no node may presuppose vocabulary beyond what I have listed above, and every question should be answerable from a concrete case rather than from a slogan. Second, where two leaves collapse — because no combination of available answers separates them, or because they differ only in rhetoric — say so explicitly rather than manufacturing a distinction; I would rather have an honest flag than a tidy tree. Third, I want the empty cells of the logical space mapped: for each combination of answers your tree permits but that no actual theorist occupies, give me one sentence on whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because nobody has gotten around to defending it. A colleague who runs a similar exercise in a philosophy of mind course has offered me her version, but it splits views only by how they answer the conceivability question, which merges several positions I want kept apart; so please keep the semantic question and the epistemological question on separate branches, and close with a short note telling me which of your questions do the real discriminating work and which turn out to be redundant.
