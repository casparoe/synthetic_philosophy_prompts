In debates about monetary policy, critics often invoke Hume's oil-and-wheels analogy from his essay on money: money, they say, is not one of the wheels of trade but the oil that makes the wheels move more smoothly. They use it to argue that expanding the money supply cannot durably increase real output or employment—adding oil does not add power to the machine—and that its only lasting effects are higher prices plus a temporary advantage in foreign wars and negotiations. Hume's text, though, complicates that picture: he allows that in the interval before prices fully adjust an influx of money can stimulate industry; he treats paper credit as a kind of counterfeit oil that foreigners will not accept and that artificially drives up prices; and he even suggests that the most advantageous bank would be one that locked up the money it received rather than returning it to circulation. Please audit this use of the oil analogy. Give the correspondence table—what correspond to the wheels, the oil, the engine, the friction, the load, and the speed?—and then say where the mapping breaks once the transitional stimulus, the specie-versus-paper distinction, and the possibility of too much lubricant are taken into account. Which disanalogies are fatal to the conclusion that monetary expansion is always neutral or harmful in the long run, and which are irrelevant? Does the oil framing implicitly assume that money's only economic function is to reduce transaction costs, or can it accommodate money as a temporary catalyst and as a stock of resources usable abroad? Finally, propose a better analogy for Hume's actual view, if there is one.
