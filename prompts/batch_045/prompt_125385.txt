I chair the investment and analytics group at a regional water utility. Three or four times a year my group is handed the same question in a new costume: before we commit to an expensive and hard-to-reverse capital decision — relining or replacing a stretch of trunk main, adding a treatment stage, relocating a pumping station — should we first buy more information? A condition survey, a short pilot, a hydraulic modelling study, a year of monitoring. Each costs real money, takes months, and freezes the decision while it runs, so the delay is itself part of the price.

My colleagues reason about this with a slogan I have come to distrust: information is never bad for you. They gesture at the value-of-information theorem usually credited to I. J. Good — roughly, that an agent who maximises expected utility and revises by conditionalisation cannot be made worse off in expectation by a costless observation, since such an agent can always disregard the observation and take whichever act had maximal expected utility beforehand. From this they conclude that any study with a non-zero chance of changing our minds is worth commissioning and that the only open question is whether it is cheap enough.

I do not think that conclusion follows, and I want a procedure rather than a debate. Please write a decision procedure my group can run on a proposal to buy information ahead of a capital decision, in the order the questions should be asked, with what counts as an adequate answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the question to a person because it is a question of values rather than fact. Constraints: my team has a good calibrated forecaster, one member who reliably over-reads a single striking data point, an engineering director who treats a pilot as a commitment to scale it up, and a board that must sign off and sometimes ignores what we give it. Nobody on the team will do algebra under time pressure, and there are usually no base rates for the specific asset under the specific street.

In building the procedure, please deal with at least the following, since these are the places I suspect the slogan is doing work the theorem does not license. First, what has to be true of us, as a decision-making body, for the guarantee to apply at all — in particular whether we can commit in advance to a rule linking each possible result to an action, and whether our actual revision practice is close enough to conditionalisation for the argument to go through. Second, the cases where the value is genuinely zero: results that leave the decision unchanged, but also results that can only bind us through channels that are not the decision, such as a finding that would trigger a regulatory duty or a publication obligation, where the option of simply ignoring what we learn is not available to us. Third, how to value information that is not free and that arrives late, in a form people without formal training can actually run, and what to do when the inputs cannot be estimated. Fourth, the cases where the study changes what we are choosing between or who gets to choose, and where the design of the study determines the answer it will return. Fifth, the fact that waiting is itself a way of acquiring information.

Then run the finished procedure on three proposals. One: a condition survey of an ageing trunk main before deciding whether to reline it or replace it outright. Two: a twelve-month pilot of a new treatment process before committing to full-scale construction, where the director will read the pilot as approval. Three, and this is the one I expect to dislike: a monitoring programme that would very likely detect a contaminant we cannot prevent and would then be legally obliged to remediate at great cost, where the study changes nothing about the physical outcome. If your procedure tells us not to commission that third study, I want to know whether that is the correct answer or whether the procedure is being used to license wilful ignorance, and I want the difference between expected value of information and whatever duty to inquire we might have stated explicitly.

Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do, and which steps are judgment wearing a lab coat.
