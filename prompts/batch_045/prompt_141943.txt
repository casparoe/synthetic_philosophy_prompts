Two people I work with got into it at a cookout this weekend and ended up asking me to settle it, which I honestly couldnt do. It started with a hypothetical Andre threw out:

Five years ago he legally rerouted an irrigation ditch on his own property to stop erosion that was eating away at his barn. It worked, but it also sped up the flow in a drainage canal, and tonight that canal is going to burst and flood a house where six people are asleep. His only option is to blow a small berm, which sends the surge into a trailer where one guy is sleeping — killing him instantly but leaving the house dry. If he does nothing, the six drown and the guy in the trailer is fine.

(1) Andre said the interesting part is that the case breaks the usual test. The standard move is that when you kill, you're the source of the threat, whereas when you let someone die there's already a threat going that you merely fail to stop. But here the threat is downstream of something *he* did — not something wrong or reckless, just something legal and reasonable at the time. So does him sitting still count as letting six people die, or as killing them?

(2) Lena said letting them die. Her reason: the "is the threat mine?" test was never really causal, it's normative. People cause threats permissibly all the time — a surgeon operates, foresees a fatal complication, the patient dies, and we don't say the surgeon killed him. What makes an omission start to look like killing isn't causation, it's whether the threat was created *wrongfully*. His ditch wasn't, so he's a bystander to his own five-year-old decision, and he's no more required to blow the berm then any stranger passing by would be.

(3) Andre came back with: that shifts the whole load onto "wrongfully," which was supposed to be the thing the doing/allowing distinction was explaining in the first place. If a threat being mine only matters when it's wrongfully mine, then wrongfulness is doing the real work and killing-vs-letting-die is just a symptom of it — so why keep the distinction around as a basic moral category instead of just talking about wrongful threat-creation directly?

(4) Lena said that's not a refutation, it's just the claim that the distinction is derived rather than fundamental, and plenty of useful distinctions are derived. Stealing vs. borrowing falls out of property rights, and nobody thinks that makes the distinction fake or useless.

(5) Andre said fine, here's the actual problem then. Suppose he'd known when he rerouted the ditch that there was a 5% chance of exactly this flood, and went ahead anyway because the numbers still came out ahead. Still permissible, so still "letting die" on her account. Now compare a doctor who gives the last ventilator to a different patient, foreseeing some chance that this patient dies. Also permissible, also letting die. But he thinks he's more on the hook than that doctor — he should warn the six, pay for their hotel, take real risk to fix the canal, and he'd owe them something after in a way the triage doctor wouldn't. If a two-box distinction can't represent any of that, it's just a label.

(6) Lena said she'd give him part of that: there's probably a graded notion of how implicated you are that a binary won't capture, but a distinction being coarse doesn't make it wrong, and law and everyday moral judgment need a fairly bright line even if what's underneath is a spectrum.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right about what — not just who wins overall? Rate each of the six moves somewhere from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say briefly why. I'd also like to know whether Andre's case actually does anything that the classic "let the trolley go straight" and "don't hook up the ventilator" cases don't, and what both of them are missing, because it felt to me like they were each answering a slightly different question and I couldnt put my finger on how.
