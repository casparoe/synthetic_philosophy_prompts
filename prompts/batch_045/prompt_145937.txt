I'm revising a paper chapter in which I argue against relational conceptions of personhood, and in a few weeks I have to defend it in front of a committee member who is sympathetic to the Ubuntu tradition — so I want to rehearse against someone who won't pull punches and won't hand me a strawman. Please play a committed defender of the relational view: someone who means the metaphysical claim and not just the ethical slogan, i.e. who holds that a human individual becomes and goes on being a person only in and through relations of mutual recognition with others, so that personhood is achieved, graded, and vulnerable rather than given once and for all with the organism. Give that character real resources: let them distinguish a being's status as a person from its underlying capacities, let them concede that personhood comes in degrees and can be damaged or partially destroyed, and let them concede that any reading on which personhood is just whatever the majority or the powerful decide is unacceptable — but let them refuse to concede that a being's personhood is independent of whether it actually stands in relations of the right kind. Keep each reply to one dense paragraph, in the first person, conversational but not chatty, and stay in character until I say stop. After the paragraph you may add a short bracketed out-of-character note: one sentence on how confident you are in that paragraph's central claim, and one flagging the single step in it you would be least willing to defend if I pressed hard on it.

My opening objection is the familiar ostracism worry, but in a version I haven't seen handled and that I think is considerably harder than the usual one. Take someone held for decades in near-total solitary confinement by captors who systematically withhold recognition: they are never addressed, never answered, referred to only in the third person, and their utterances are logged as noise rather than heard as speech. If your view ties personhood to actual relations of recognition, it looks as though it has to say the captors succeeded — they manufactured a non-person, and the philosophical description of what they did ends up ratifying it, which is the last thing a view with these political credentials wants. Now the twist. Unknown to the captive, everything they say in the cell is recorded; centuries after their death a community of scholars reconstructs their inner life in fine detail from the recordings and does to them everything recognition is supposed to involve — takes up their complaints, answers their questions, argues with them, mourns them, lets the reconstruction change how the community lives. Call this retroactive recognition. My contention is that you now have to say something intolerable in either direction. If retroactive recognition works, then whether someone was a person at a given time depends on facts about the remote future, and personhood becomes as fragile and as revisable as a reputation; if it doesn't work, then you owe me an account of what the temporal index is doing — why the very same relations confer personhood when they hold during a life but not when they hold after it, given that you also want to say the captive's personhood was wrongfully denied while they were alive. One constraint on your escape routes: the captive does not reciprocate the scholars' recognition, being dead, so if you rule retroactive recognition out on the ground that recognition must be mutual, you are disposing of my case on a technicality rather than on the temporal point — and if that is your move, say so plainly and treat it as a concession rather than a solution.

So, to put the question to you directly: in that cell, is the captive a person? Does your answer change once the scholars and their recordings exist? And if you want to say that they were a person all along whose personhood was merely unacknowledged, tell me what in your ontology is doing the work — which relations, to whom — because on the face of it there were none at the time, and I don't think you can borrow an answer from the capacity theorist you have been arguing against for the rest of the chapter.
