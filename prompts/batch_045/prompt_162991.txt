I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and last month at a workshop on machine-intelligence benchmarks I got into a dinner-table argument with a physicist friend about a corner of Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence (published in Mind) that almost never comes up: his treatment of extrasensory perception. My friend said Turing's remarks about ESP were a throwaway joke; I said that, whatever his personal beliefs, Turing took the objection seriously enough to treat it as a design problem for the imitation game. As I understand it, the objection runs like this: the interrogator is already confined to teletype communication with the contestants in separate rooms, but that separation is not enough, because a telepathic interrogator might read the mind of the human contestant, or a clairvoyant one might see the machinery, and thereby identify which is the machine; the obvious next fix would be to shield the rooms against such influences, except that we do not know what a genuine shield would have to be like, since we do not know the laws that any such faculty would obey. Before I started covering ideas, I spent six years as a computer-security reporter, so the framework I actually think in is threat modeling: attack surface, trusted versus untrusted components, side channels and covert channels, out-of-band leakage, shielding, and the difficulty of proving a negative about an unmodeled channel.

So here is what I need from you. Imagine I have a solid working knowledge of security engineering but only a general reader's familiarity with the 1950 paper. Restate Turing's handling of this ESP objection—the objection itself, the move of putting the interrogator or the contestants in a shielded room, and his worry that we cannot specify what the shielding would consist in—as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: attack surface, side channels, covert channels, out-of-band leakage, threat models, adversarial capability, and the impossibility of proving a negative about an unmodeled channel. I plan to use your answer to settle the dinner argument and perhaps to write a short column on what Turing was really doing when he worried about telepathy in the imitation game.

Then give me the residue report. I want to know which parts of Turing's discussion map cleanly onto a side-channel or threat-model analysis, which parts translate only if I distort them—for example, does talk of telepathy as an intentional act of a person map onto an adversary's capability, and does the difference between the interrogator's ESP and the machine's having ESP have any counterpart in a security model?—and which parts cannot be expressed in that vocabulary at all. Finally, tell me whether the residue points to something the security framework cannot say, perhaps about the point of the imitation game as a test of thinking rather than of indistinguishability under a fixed threat model, or whether it shows that Turing's remarks were less precise than they look when they are read as a piece of protocol design.
