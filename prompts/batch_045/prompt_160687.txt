I do a monthly "ideas" slot at the paper — 40 minutes in the newsroom next week, reporters and editors, no maths past percentages, one whiteboard — and I've volunteered to do a session on Dutch book arguments, mostly because I keep seeing "if your beliefs aren't probabilities you can be made to lose money for sure" dropped into pieces about AI forecasting and I don't think anyone writing that sentence could say what the argument actually is. Don't explain it to me; plan how I'd explain it to them. What has to be on the board before the word "Dutch book" even appears (my instinct: the price at which you'd take either side of a bet, before anything about degrees of belief), which single example should carry the whole thing and why not a horse race, and the misconceptions in the order they'll arrive — I'm guessing "so rationality just means not going broke" and "the bookie must be cheating or already know my numbers" and maybe "fine, but I don't gamble" — with the exact point in the sequence where I should defuse each. I also want them to leave knowing it's a template that gets run on other norms too, like updating by conditionalization or matching your confidence to a known base rate, rather than a fact about betting: where does that go without me opening a whole second can of worms? One question I can ask at the end that tells me they got it rather than just memorised the punchline, and one thing I should openly refuse to get into at this level.
