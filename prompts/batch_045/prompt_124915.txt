I work as a backend engineer and have no philosophy background beyond a freshman survey course, but I sit in on my company's informal "ethics and infrastructure" reading group, and last month we went through a chapter on social choice from an introductory textbook written for economics students. The chapter covered Sen's 1970 result — the so-called liberal or Paretian-liberal impossibility — and I came away unsure how much of its framing to trust. Paraphrased, the chapter's summary runs roughly like this: "Sen proved that liberalism is mathematically self-refuting. Give each person only 'minimal liberalism': a single pair of social alternatives over which that person's own preference settles the social ranking no matter what anyone else thinks. In his example Lewd settles whether Lewd reads the book or nobody reads it, and Prude settles whether Prude reads it or nobody reads it. Add the Pareto principle: if everyone prefers one outcome to another, society must rank it higher too. In the Lady Chatterley case everyone prefers that Prude do the reading to that Lewd do it, so Pareto gives Prude-reads above Lewd-reads, while liberalism gives Lewd-reads above nobody-reads and nobody-reads above Prude-reads — a cycle. So no society can be both liberal and efficient: any consistent social ranking either overrides someone's protected personal choice or flies in the face of a unanimous preference. Sen's own conclusion was that rights have to give way, which is why later defenders of rights have had to recast them as side-constraints that silence the Pareto principle instead of as preferences to be aggregated at all. And since the argument is really Arrow's theorem with non-dictatorship replaced by minimal liberalism, every real collective decision procedure, majority voting included, is infected by the same incoherence. Note also that the example only gets off the ground because Lewd wants to control what Prude does: the paradox is really a point about meddlesome preferences and externalities, not about rights."

Several things in that summary make me suspicious, and I can't tell which of them are my own confusions. First, the "right" here is defined as the power to fix the social ranking of a pair — but in the feature I'm actually building at work, a user's control over their own data is a permission to set a value, not a power to force our platform's global ordering, and those feel like different things to me even though the chapter treats them as the same. Second, "liberal" and "efficient" both seem to be doing more work than the technical conditions can bear: the chapter's final claim about real institutions seems too strong, since we don't try to produce a complete ranking over every conceivable configuration. Third, a colleague in the group said the meddlesome-preferences remark is a red herring, because whether a preference counts as meddlesome depends on how you individuate the alternatives, so you can't clean the paradox up by assuming people mind their own business. Fourth, someone sketched a fix I couldn't follow: instead of aggregating each person's unconditional preference over whole outcomes, you let each person's say range only over their own sphere, holding fixed what everyone else is doing, so that Lewd's desire that Prude read never enters the social ranking at all. One person in the group said this is a standard response that dissolves the paradox; another said it merely relocates it and quietly changes the subject from rights to something else.

I want an accuracy audit I can act on. Please go through the chapter's claims one at a time, in the order I've paraphrased them, and tell me what someone who works on this material would accept, what they'd call a defensible simplification, and what they'd say is a genuine misrepresentation — in particular, what Sen himself would object to in the claim that his conclusion was that rights must give way, and whether the word "efficient" is being used in its technical sense at all. Then apply the corrected version to my own case, which is a genuine design question and not a homework exercise: two engineers, Ash and Bo, each control whether their own telemetry is shared org-wide or not; the three configurations are Ash's shared, Bo's shared, and neither shared; the company rule is that if both engineers prefer one configuration to another, we adopt that ranking. Ash would most like Bo's telemetry shared, would settle for their own, and likes nothing shared least; Bo prefers nothing shared, but if something must be shared would rather share their own than have Ash's misleading data circulating. As far as I can tell this walks into the same cycle — but I want to know whether our system is really an instance of Sen's result or whether the cycle is an artifact of modeling choices we could legitimately make differently, and if so which one we should give up and why. Please take a definite position rather than laying out the options for me to choose between: tell me which parts of the chapter I should stop repeating, what you'd say to the two people arguing about the conditional-preference fix, and what you'd recommend for our feature. Where your verdict depends on an interpretive choice about what a "right" is, or on a technical point you'd want to check, say so plainly and tell me how much turns on it — but still give me your best answer.
