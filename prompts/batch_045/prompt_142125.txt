I have been unable to shake an argument a colleague made over lunch a few weeks ago, and I cannot locate the move I distrust. I will reconstruct it as faithfully as I can.

(1) Good's theorem on the value of information: for an agent who maximizes expected utility and updates by conditionalization, the ex ante expected value of a costless signal is never negative. The expected utility of the best policy that conditions on the signal is at least the expected utility of the best action chosen in ignorance of it.

(2) The proof places no restriction on the subject matter of the signal, on the agent's tastes, or on what she happens to care about. It requires only that the same actions remain available whether or not she looks, that she will in fact tailor her action to what she sees, and that her utility function is defined over outcomes and does not itself shift when she learns.

(3) (1) and (2) entail that declining costless information is never rational: to turn it down is to refuse something free that cannot, in expectation, from where the agent now stands, leave her worse off.

(4) And this holds for any signal whatever: the result of a predictive genetic test for a late-onset condition with no available treatment; the answer to whether one's partner has been unfaithful; the final chapter of a novel one is halfway through; the grade on an examination one could still resit; the contents of a sealed envelope that will say whether the enterprise one has spent five years on will fail.

(5) Yet calm and competent people decline precisely these. They do not get tested. They do not ask, or they ask and then ask to be told nothing. They leave the envelope on the hall table for a week. Some of them pay to avoid finding out, and would pay a great deal more to un-find-out.

(6) So either the theorem is false, or every one of these refusals is irrational, or — this was my colleague's own conclusion — the theorem is true and shows only that a large amount of ordinary conduct is irrational, which is no objection to a theorem.

I want to keep (1), and I want to keep the honesty of (5): I think at least some of these refusals are reasonable, and are not merely weakness of will dressed up in reasons. That leaves (2) and (3) as the places to look, and I have three candidates for what is doing the work there, which I cannot tell apart.

(a) The guarantee in (1) concerns the expectation taken before the signal arrives. What the person who refuses is often worried about is the signal she will actually receive, and that one can leave her worse off than she would otherwise have been, however favorable the average over all possible signals turns out to be. Is there any reading of rational on which ex ante optimality is not enough? And if there is one, does holding on to (1) commit me to rejecting it?

(b) The proof prices a signal only through the actions it lets the agent discriminate between. But some of the things people refuse seem to change the value of what happens next directly, and not through any action taken in response: the spoiler alters the experience of the remaining hundred pages, the test result alters the texture of the decade one then lives through, the answer alters a marriage in ways no subsequent choice can undo. If utility is allowed to take the signal itself as an argument, does the theorem survive? And if it survives only because one can always enlarge the space of outcomes until every such effect is re-described as something the signal was merely evidence about, has the theorem become unfalsifiable — a framework that can rationalize any refusal after the fact, rather than one that ever binds?

(c) Some refusals look like refusals to surrender an option, and not-knowing is a state with instrumental uses: the witness who can still testify honestly, the negotiator who cannot be squeezed for knowledge she does not have, the patient whose ignorance keeps her insurable, the parent who can still hope, the actor who must not know how the scene ends. Ignorance is sometimes the only commitment device available. And in settings containing other agents who observe and respond, better information can strictly harm its possessor, and can even destroy the risk-pooling that insurance-like arrangements depend on, leaving everyone worse off than before anyone knew anything. Is that a counterexample to (1)? Or is it only a reminder that (1) was proved for a single decision-maker facing nature, and that (3) generalizes illicitly from that case to all the others?

There is also a fourth thing my colleague says, which I find convenient and evasive in about equal measure: that if learning can change what an agent wants, then the theorem is being applied to someone who is no longer the same agent afterwards; that decision theory has no way of saying whether the earlier self should defer to the later one; and that refusals of the (b) kind are therefore neither rational nor irrational, but simply outside the theory's jurisdiction.

Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. If more than one of (a) through (c) is doing real work, say how they come apart — which refusals each explains and which it leaves untouched — and say whether (c) marks a genuine limit of the theorem or a misuse of it. I would rather keep (1) and (5) than (3), if I have to choose.
