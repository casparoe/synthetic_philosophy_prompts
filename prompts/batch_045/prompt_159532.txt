My friend and I got into a long, dumb argument last night that started with Sen's liberal paradox — the one where giving each person the final say over some tiny purely personal matter (whether the guy reads the racy novel, whether you sleep on your back or your belly) plus even a weak Pareto principle plus an unrestricted domain blows up into a cycle. He says the moral is that a private sphere is basically incoherent unless you also strike out everyone else's preferences about your personal business. I said that can't possibly be what actual institutions do, and then we started listing real rules and it got worse instead of better.

So here's the practice I want you to look at. Over your own body and your own life, a competent adult has the last word: you can refuse a blood transfusion that would save you, quit chemo, get a tattoo, smoke, refuse a shot. But the same legal systems also do all of this: you cannot consent to being killed or badly hurt by someone who is willing, and where assisted dying is legal it is fenced in with two doctors, waiting periods, a prognosis. You cannot sign away your future say — no self-sale into slavery, no enforcing a contract that says you can never quit, no consenting to be locked up. And your say simply disappears if a clinician says you lack capacity, or if what you want to do affects other people in a material way: quarantine, seatbelt and helmet laws, vaccination conditions on some jobs. We also punish some purely self-regarding conduct and ignore other conduct that is far more dangerous, which made my friend say the whole thing is just paternalism with a liberal fig leaf.

What bugs me is this: refusing a transfusion and asking for a lethal injection both end with you dead, but one is treated as yours and the other isn't. And the clauses where you are not allowed to give up your future say at all look strange if rights are about satisfying your present preferences — it looks like the institution is guarding your say later rather than respecting it now.

So: what philosophical commitments does this package of rules presuppose? What does "your business" have to mean for the pattern to come out roughly like this, and which parts of the practice strictly require that theory versus merely fit well with it? Where is it underdetermined, so that two or three quite different theories would license the same rules?

A few things I want you to actually take a stand on:

1. Is Sen's result even aimed at something like this? Real institutions aren't functions from preference profiles to social rankings, so does the cycle bite, or does the practice dodge it by treating rights as constraints on what may be considered rather than as wins in a pairwise comparison? Some guy at the bar told us the fix is to model a right as control over a menu of options instead of decisiveness over outcomes — does that dissolve the paradox or just move it somewhere else?

2. Does the practice have to presuppose that other people's preferences about your conduct are excluded from the ranking? Because they plainly aren't excluded everywhere. Is there a coherent version of "preferences about somebody else's personal business count less" that doesn't just reintroduce the cycle, and what does it presuppose about comparing or discounting preferences?

3. The irreversibility bit — you can't alienate your own future decisiveness. What does that presuppose about a person over time? Some diachronic or future-oriented view of autonomy, plain paternalism about regret, a capabilities thing, or just a rule of thumb? Say which, and say how you'd tell them apart from what the rules actually do.

4. My friend's constructive suggestion: your sphere is whatever you'd agree to keep under a veil of ignorance about which personal issue turns out to be yours. Does that generate this pattern, or does it generate something else entirely — and would it allow or forbid the irreversibility carve-out?

5. Finally, what would have to change if we took the irreversibility idea seriously? Should someone be able to waive in advance their right to refuse treatment, or sign an unbreakable contract, and what would the practice have to look like under each answer?

Please flag honestly which moves are forced by the theory and which are judgment calls, and don't pretend the pattern is tidier than it is. Write it in American English, and in plain prose — no headings-with-bullets lecture format.
