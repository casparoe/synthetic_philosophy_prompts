I am writing the take-home final for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of machine learning, and I need one question on the problem of eliciting latent knowledge from AI systems, together with the standard against which answers will be graded. I would like you to write the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The setup my students already have is the familiar one: a system is trained as a predictor on a task (say, next-frame prediction over video streams), and as a result it comes to represent features of the world that no one ever labelled. We then want to interrogate it in natural language and get an answer that reports what it represents rather than an answer that merely mimics what a human in its situation would have said. The difficulty is that the training signal for the answer-producing component comes from human-generated text, so the cheapest way to fit that signal is to simulate a human reporter rather than to report the predictor's own model of the world.

My concern is that any straightforward question on this topic gets answered by restating that setup and then cataloguing the families of technique that get discussed under this heading: supervised reporter heads trained on human-approved answers, debate or recursive amplification among reporters, mechanistic interpretability of internal features, training regimes that penalize reports compatible with a deceptive world-model. A student can produce that catalogue without touching the difficulty at all. In your question the taxonomy should be at most background, and an answer that consists of it should earn low marks.

I have two distinctions I want the question to turn on. Please state them more precisely if you can, and tell me briefly if either of them collapses under scrutiny.

(a) A storehouse conception versus a disposition conception of latent knowledge. On the first, the system already encodes a body of content in its parameters; elicitation is a decoding problem, and there is a determinate fact about what the system represents that is independent of how we ask. On the second, what the system knows is a matter of its counterfactual behaviour under interrogations it has never actually been given, so the content is fixed only relative to a space of possible elicitation procedures, and two procedures that are each reasonable might fix different contents.

(b) Extractive versus adjudicative strategies. Extractive strategies try to recover the system's actual representation and read it out. Adjudicative strategies give up on reading anything out and instead construct a procedure among competing candidate reports — cross-examination, adversarial scoring, betting against later observations — and treat whatever the procedure selects as the answer.

The crux I want the question to force is whether adjudication is a genuine alternative to extraction or merely relocates the same problem one step back. An adjudicative procedure needs a selection criterion. If the criterion is agreement with observations that arrive later, it is unavailable in exactly the cases of interest, where the point of asking is to know something before the outcome is revealed. If the criterion is something like being best supported by the predictor's internal state, we are back to extraction. And if the disposition conception in (a) is correct, there may be no procedure-independent fact of the matter for any criterion to track, so that the very idea of the system's latent knowledge becomes elicitation-relative. I want students to feel the pull of all three horns and then have to take a position.

Please build the question around a concrete case rather than letting it float at the level of abstraction above. One I have in mind: a predictor trained on surveillance footage of a facility, paired with a natural-language question-answering component fine-tuned on logs written by human overseers who watched the same footage and who systematically failed to notice one particular kind of event, so that no human-written report in the training data ever mentions it. Sharpen or replace this case if you can make it do more work.

Deliverables, in this order:

1. The question itself, with an explicit word limit of 1,200 to 1,500 words, an explicit instruction that answers must take and defend a position rather than survey the options, and an explicit instruction to state which conception in (a) is being assumed.
2. A model answer of roughly 800 words that commits to a position, answers the strongest objection to it, and does not dissolve into a literature survey.
3. A marking scheme out of 20, built around four or five named must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on the specific mistake that typically costs a candidate those marks.
4. A short paragraph, after the scheme, distinguishing an A answer from a B answer from a C answer in terms of the moves made rather than the conclusions reached.

Constraints: the question must not be answerable by restating the setup or the taxonomy of techniques; it must not presuppose knowledge of any particular paper, research-agenda document, or empirical result, since students sit the exam without access to any of it; and it must force a choice between the two conceptions in (a) rather than permitting an answer that quietly slides between them. Please write the question, the model answer, and the marking scheme in American English.
