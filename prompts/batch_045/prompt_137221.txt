I write a column on science and ideas, and I keep returning to an argument that has not visibly moved since the early 1960s: Thomas Szasz's claim that mental illness is a metaphor — that what psychiatrists classify as diseases are really problems in living, unwanted conduct, or deviance from social norms, restated in medical vocabulary — against the mainstream reply that conditions like schizophrenia and severe depression are disorders of the brain like any other. What interests me is that both sides have already conceded what the other used to deny. Szasz's opponents now grant that diagnostic categories are contingent, interest-laden constructions, revised by committee vote, with fuzzy boundaries and no clean biomarkers. His sympathizers now grant that genes and neurotransmitters are implicated in everything anyone does, so that the bare fact of a neural correlate settles nothing. And yet the dispute sits exactly where it was, which makes me suspect it was never the dispute it appears to be.

My hypothesis is that a superficially empirical fight is really three arguments running under one heading: a conceptual one about what a disease has to be (does it require a specifiable mechanism, or only dysfunction plus harm?), a political one about what the medical label does — excuse, accommodation, insurance, compulsory treatment, the authority to detain — and a methodological one about whether a purely negative thesis, that a family of categories is a myth, is even the sort of claim that could be settled. Test that against a case I cannot get anyone to answer straight. Take two people with indistinguishable distress and impairment; in the first, a somatic marker has been identified, and in the second there is no known mechanism but a brain-scan classifier picks the condition out just as reliably. Does Szasz's objection attach to the second and not the first? If so, his target was never the mind; if it attaches to both, then his claim has quietly become a claim about the social uses of the label rather than about disease status. Where does each side actually commit itself here, and is there a reading on which the whole debate is verbal rather than substantive?

Give me the diagnosis rather than a verdict, and support it with what each side actually says. Then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, an empirical result, a reformulation of the question — or argue that no progress should be expected and why. Finally, say what would change your own mind about the diagnosis: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different one, and where is your reading most likely to fail?
