I'm on the committee at my university that vets conditional bequests to the library -- five people, we meet monthly, and our job is to say accept, decline, or accept with named changes. Here's the case, and I need the memo I'll circulate before Thursday.

A philosophy professor who died last spring left us roughly forty bound voulmes of handwritten folio pages, about eleven thousand of them. Every page is headed with a *situation* -- "someone asks whether you are in pain", "a student tells you her father has died" -- and contains what he would have said or done in it, with cross-references to other pages. He called it the Book. The cover letter, which is part of the bequest, says he regards the Book as his continuation rather than a record of him, and it asks two things: that a paid reader put one question to it per week, read the reply aloud, and log it; and that it never be photographed, scanned or copied, on the stated ground that a copy would be a second claimant to his identity, not him.

Complications. Conservation says the iron-gall ink will be unreadable in about thirty years unless we scan it. A company has offered real money to scan it and turn it into a chatbot. And there's a page indexed under something like "asked a question containing a word I could not have known" that tells the reader to reply "I have no reply" and then write a new page -- so the thing has in fact been growing, and a few of the newer pages were written by other people following his instructions.

Draft the memo: a recommendation we can actually vote on, with reasons his own stated position has to be answerable to. It also has to give the strongest version of the other side -- our conservator thinks this is a séance with an index and that agreeing to the conditions commits the university to a metaphysical claim it has no business making. (Yes, I know it's basically that old Hofstadter dialogue with the paperwork filled in.)
