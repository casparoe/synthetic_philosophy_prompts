Here's what's been bugging me. I'm teaching a graduate seminar on formal epistemology this term and we spent last week on the accuracy-first vindication of probabilism: credences are graded beliefs, the sole epistemic good is closeness to the truth, we measure inaccuracy with a strictly proper scoring rule (Brier, log, whatever), and then the dominance argument shows that any incoherent credence function is beaten, world by world, by some coherent one. Fine. But on Thursday one of my students, who is annoyingly good at this, pointed out something I haven't been able to shake: the whole apparatus only gets off the ground once you've fixed an agenda — the set of propositions, or the partition, over which you score the agent. And agents differ in their agendas, and it looks a lot like the agent can game the score by choosing the agenda. Suppose I form a credence of 0.7 that it rains tomorrow. My inaccuracy is then scored over {rains, doesn't rain}. But I could instead have had opinions about how many millimeters fall, or about the exact pattern of droplet trajectories, or — in the other direction — I could simply decline to have any view about tomorrow's weather at all and spend my credal energy on nothing whatsoever. The finer my agenda, the more inaccuracy I seem to rack up for exactly the same evidence; the coarser it is, the closer to the truth I look, for free. That is, veritism as stated seems to recommend a kind of strategic coarseness, or even intellectual laziness — have as few opinions as possible, and never aquire a new distinction. Worse, it makes cross-personal and diachronic comparisons of epistemic rationality ill-defined: is the inquisitive scientist who forms credences over hundreds of fine-grained hypotheses less rational than the couch potato with three opinions, just because the scientist has many more chances to be wrong? And note that this looks like it sneaks a *pragmatic* element back into what was advertised as a purely epistemic, non-pragmatic vindication: choosing your agenda to manage your expected score is exactly the sort of gaming the accuracy-firsters were proud to have avoided.

So: write an essay on what the accuracy-firster should say about the relativity of accuracy to an agenda. Don't just catalog options — defend one, and argue against the best version of the alternatives. To get you started, here are four lines I've been kicking around, though you should feel free to argue that I've misdescribed them or that the real fix is something else entirely. (1) Normalize: compare agents by *average* inaccuracy per proposition rather than total, so having more opinions isn't automatically a liability — but then ask yourself what stops me from padding my agenda with a thousand logical truths I'm certain of, or a thousand near-tautologies, and thereby manufacturing a spectacular average. (2) Let the agenda be fixed by contextually salient questions, so that what gets scored is what's actually at issue in the agent's situation — but then say honestly whether that is still a non-pragmatic epistemology, and how salience avoids being gerrymandered the same way the agenda was. (3) Quietism: epistemic rationality never told you *which* questions to have attitudes about, only how the attitudes you have must hang together, so the couch potato is guilty of nothing and the problem is a pseudo-problem generated by asking a norm to do work it never claimed to do. (4) Score over the finest partition the agent's own conceptual repertoire makes available, so you can't dodge inaccuracy by refusing to draw distinctions you could have drawn — but then say what happens to the norm's satisfiability, and to the comparisons, when the agent's conceptual repertoire is itself something she can shrink. Along the way I'd like you to be explicit about whether the scoring-rule choice (Brier vs. logarithmic, and the fact that the log score can blow up) makes any of this better or worse, and to close by answering the big question: is agenda relativity a fatal problem for accuracy-first epistemology, a manageable technical annoyance, or a problem that any veritist view — and maybe any view with a purely epistemic notion of rationality — inherits anyway? Commit to something.
