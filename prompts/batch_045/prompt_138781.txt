I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or history, and I have been asked to run a 90-minute internal strategy session at the company where I work. We are roughly 200 people; we build tooling on top of other organisations' frontier models rather than training them ourselves. The session has to be organised around a single historical analogy for transformative AI, and I have to defend the choice to a leadership group that will push back on it. I need help picking and defending, not a survey of options.

The three candidates I have been given are: nuclear weapons, meaning the Manhattan Project and the years immediately after it; the printing press; and the Industrial Revolution. Rather than a discussion of what each does and does not capture, I want you to score them, because I have to actually choose one, and I would rather have a ranking I can argue with than a balanced treatment I cannot act on.

Score each of the three from 0 to 10 on each of the following axes. I defined them for my own purposes and you should feel free to criticise or replace any of them if you think it is confused.

1. Timing resolution. Does the analogy say anything specific about the shape of the transition — gradual, threshold-like, or something that looks gradual until it suddenly does not — as opposed to saying something only about the destination?

2. Who decides. Does it tell us anything about whether the outcome gets set by a small number of actors, such as a handful of labs or a handful of states, or by diffuse adoption across many unrelated users?

3. Legibility to the people living through it. Was it obvious at the time that something large was happening, and if not, how long was the lag between the underlying change and general recognition of it? Score this about the participants' own experience of the period, not about how clear the case looks to us in hindsight.

4. Lock-in. Did the historical case contain a point after which reversal was no longer available, and did anyone in a position to act get a genuine chance to take a different path before that point?

5. Action guidance for someone at my level. This is the axis I care most about. Does the analogy imply anything that I or my team should do differently in the next two years — in what we build, what we depend on, or how we hire — given that we are neither a frontier lab nor a government?

Two distinctions I have been working with, which I offer in case they are useful and which you should reject if you think they are muddled:

First, the contrast between analogies of arrival and analogies of the run-up. Most of the discussion I have encountered uses these cases to describe the world after the technology has landed. What I actually need is a frame for the period in which the technology is credibly coming but not yet here, because that is where we are and where our decisions have to be made. My suspicion is that the printing press does considerably better on this than its reputation suggests and that the bomb does worse, but I cannot make that case rigorously.

Second, what I have been calling the Monday test. An analogy earns its place only if it generates at least one expectation that both of the default views — capabilities keep improving at roughly the current rate, and nothing much changes — would not generate on their own. If an analogy only restates what we already believe in more vivid language, it scores zero for me however evocative it is.

Alongside the scores and a one-sentence justification for each, please give me:

- An overall weighting across the three analogies summing to 100, representing how much of the session's 90 minutes you would build on each, with any ties broken rather than left as ties.
- The one analogy you would drop from consideration entirely, and the strongest reason someone would give for keeping it.
- The single most serious objection to your top-ranked choice, and what kind of evidence or argument would move it out of first place.
- Explicit flags anywhere your scoring depends on a historical detail you are not confident about. I have heard it claimed, for instance, that the gap between the spread of steam power and any measurable improvement in ordinary workers' living standards ran to several decades; treat that as hearsay and correct it if it is wrong, and tell me how much of the score rides on it either way.

Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind — I will not be able to follow them up. Work from what is broadly known about these three cases, and where a load-bearing fact is genuinely uncertain, say that it is uncertain and how much it matters rather than filling it in. Assume I will be reading this aloud to people who know the history better than I do.
