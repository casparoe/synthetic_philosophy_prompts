I am a PhD student in philosophy and the grader for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility (mostly juniors and seniors, many of them philosophy majors). Below is one of the final exam questions, together with a student's answer, reproduced verbatim apart from a little trimming of hedging at the margins. I have deliberately not marked it yet: I would like an independent read before I settle on a grade, so please evaluate it on its own terms rather than trying to reconstruct what you take my view to be.

The exam was written in class, 45 minutes per question, and students were told to aim for 600 to 800 words. No citations were expected or given. For this question the only relevant reading on the syllabus was Galen Strawson's paper setting out the Basic Argument; the seminar had also covered the consequence argument, agent-causal libertarianism, and P. F. Strawson's reactive-attitudes discussion, and students were told they could draw on any of it.

The question (20 points):

State Strawson's Basic Argument as exactly as you can. Then address the following three points.

(a) Is the argument's conclusion hostage to the truth of determinism? Explain why Strawson says it is not, or explain why you think in fact it is.

(b) Strawson says that the impossibility of true moral responsibility is no cause for panic. Say precisely what, on his view, is lost and what is retained, and explain how retaining it is supposed to be compatible with giving up retributive desert.

(c) Consider the graded self-authorship reply, which I will call the asymptote reply. Grant Strawson that no one can be truly responsible for her initial nature, and grant that no one can complete an infinite series of self-shaping acts. Still, an agent might shape her character over a long life by successive choices, each made in light of a character she has herself partly shaped, so that her responsibility for her character grows without bound and approaches, without ever reaching, full self-authorship. If such a thing were possible, would it answer Strawson's argument? Why or why not?

The student's answer:

Strawson's Basic Argument has four steps. First, you do what you do, in any given situation, because of the way you are. Second, so if you are to be truly, or ultimately, morally responsible for what you do, you must be truly responsible for the way you are, at least in certain crucial mental respects. Third, you cannot be truly responsible for the way you are in any respect at all, because to be truly responsible for the way you are you would have to have brought it about that you are the way you are, and to do that you would already have had to be a certain way in light of which you made yourself the way you now are, and that prior way would in turn have to have been something you brought about, and so on. Since a genuinely infinite series of acts cannot be completed by a finite agent, the series can never get started, and so you cannot be truly responsible for the way you are. Fourth, therefore, no one is ever truly morally responsible for anything they do.

The argument is not an argument for determinism, and Strawson is explicit that it does not presuppose determinism; he holds that free will is impossible whether determinism is true or false, and he is not a hard determinist. Nor is it an empirical claim about how character is acquired.

(a) The first premise is nevertheless a determinist-looking claim about action: it says that what you do is fully explained by, and indeed necessitated by, your character at the time of action. That puts the Basic Argument in the same family as the consequence argument, and it means the argument rests on an answer to the very dispute it is supposed to settle. Strawson has not, as far as I can see, addressed the agent-causal libertarian, who holds that some choices are not explained by the agent's prior character at all but are the agent's origination of a new causal chain. If the libertarian is right, the first premise is false for at least some actions, and the regress has nothing to bite on: the character the agent has after such an originary choice is not simply inherited but chosen, so the regress stops and true responsibility is possible after all. The argument is therefore weaker than Strawson makes it sound.

(b) What is lost, if Strawson is right, is retributive desert. True moral responsibility is the only notion on which anyone could deserve to suffer simply because she did wrong, and the Basic Argument shows that no one has it. What is retained is ordinary, everyday moral responsibility: we can still praise and blame one another, hold one another to expectations, and take responsibility for what we do, because these practices do their real work by coordinating behavior, expressing our reactive attitudes, and encouraging improvement, none of which requires that anyone be the ultimate source of her own character. Strawson's official view, then, is that apart from the institution of punishment, nothing in our ordinary responsibility practices has to change. That is what the no-panic claim amounts to.

(c) The asymptote reply shows that Strawson overstates his conclusion. Strawson has only ruled out complete self-creation, that is, being the cause of oneself from nothing, and getting the series started. The regress shows at most that we cannot have total responsibility for our characters; it does not show that we cannot have partial responsibility, and partial responsibility that grows without bound, which is what the reply describes, is all that our practices of praise and blame really require. Since Strawson himself says, in the no-panic part of the paper, that we can and should go on ascribing moral responsibility in the ordinary way, he is in effect committed to saying that the graded, partial self-authorship described in the reply is enough. So the reply succeeds, and Strawson's position turns out to be a version of compatibilism: moral responsibility is a matter of degree, and it is compatible with the truth of the Basic Argument that we are responsible to a very high degree. The argument is valid and it refutes the inflated ideal of ultimate self-creation, but it has no skeptical upshot for us.
