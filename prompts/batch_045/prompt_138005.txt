On a recent thread about whether it makes sense to quote probabilities on unproved mathematical claims — I had argued that a computationally bounded agent ought to price bets rather than refuse them, something like 0.1 on the claim that the 10^100th digit of π is 7, and something middling on Goldbach's conjecture — a commenter replied with nothing but "2 + 2 = 4", as though that settled it. I take the intended point to be roughly: mathematics is the one domain where we have real certainty, so importing hedging into it is a category error. But the line cannot be doing that much work as stated, so I would like the menu instead of the gesture.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses that "2 + 2 = 4" could be standing for in that exchange, ordered roughly from weakest to strongest. My own candidates, to be corrected, split, or replaced:

1. Mathematical claims have determinate truth values: bivalence holds, there is no indeterminacy in the subject matter.
2. Some mathematical claims can be settled in a way empirical claims cannot — by proof rather than by evidence.
3. Whatever an agent knows to be entailed by what it already accepts should get probability 1, i.e. rational credence is closed under known entailment.
4. A fully rational agent is logically omniscient: probability 1 on every tautology, and no uncertainty anywhere in mathematics.
5. All apparent uncertainty about mathematics is really empirical uncertainty in disguise — about your memory, your hardware, whether your proof search terminates, what the mathematical community will eventually accept.
6. Any non-extreme probability on a decidable arithmetic claim is strictly irrational, however little compute the agent has to settle it.

For each reading: state it precisely enough that it could be true or false; classify it as trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive and contested, or straightforwardly false; and say whether it is a claim about the world, about ideal agents, or about bounded ones.

Then the part I actually care about: which of these readings would support the commenter's dismissal, and which would only support a much weaker conclusion that I already accept?

Two constraints on the answer. First, I do not think readings that fail on grounds of boundedness help here: if reading 6 is right, then a bounded agent asked to price bets on the π digit must either refuse to quote odds or take what is effectively a sure loss to a bookie with more compute, and I want to hear what the slogan's advocate should say about that case, not a gesture at ideals. Second, here is a case I have not seen handled, which I will call "equivalence arbitrage": a bookie with more compute than me offers me simultaneous bets on two formulations of the same conjecture, phrased so that I would quote 0.7 on one and 0.9 on the other, and is able to prove the equivalence while I am not. Does any reading on the menu tell me what my two probabilities ought to be before I have seen that proof — and if it does, is the prescription satisfiable by any agent that cannot yet prove the equivalence? If not, say so plainly; I would rather be told that no reading addresses this than be told a story about ideal agents.

Finally: which reading do you guess the commenter actually meant, and which single reading should I concede to them at the outset?
