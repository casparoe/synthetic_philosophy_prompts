Hey — longtime lurker, occasional commenter, first time I've actually sat down to ask. I'm an ML engineer and most of my working week is agent scaffolding: long context, tool use, a bit of RL on top of a pretrained model, and lately some work on self-models, where the system has to predict its own future actions and report on what it's like. In a group chat I'm in we've been going round in circles for months about whether a forked copy of a person is the same person, whether an upload survives, whether it's continuity of memory or continuity of character that's doing the work. Someone eventually said we were all reinventing the personal-identity literature badly and pointed me at Dennett's centre of narrative gravity idea — the self as an abstraction over a messy pile of events, the way a centre of mass is an abstraction over a body rather than an extra part of it — and at the people who push back and say the narrative does real work, that the story a person tells about themselves partly constitutes who they are instead of merely tracking it, and that a life can be more or less narratively unified in a way that matters. I sort of get both sides in plain English, but I don't trust that I get them: every time we argue I loose track of which of three different things the word self is being used for. (There's also the objection that some people just don't experience their lives as a story at all and that nothing is missing from them — that comes up in our chat constantly, and I'd rather you used it as a stress test on the translation than as a third camp to adjudicate between.)

So here's the ask. Translate this whole dispute as faithfully as you can into the vocabulary I actually have: latents and world models, next-token prediction, the training-time versus inference-time distinction, in-context learning versus weight updates, checkpoints and forks and fine-tuning, a policy and whatever consumes a state representation, lossy compression, distillation, mesa-optimisers, indexical tokens and what I has to bind to at inference time, reward models versus the base model's dispositions. A mapping table would be ideal — original claim on the left, restatement in my terms on the right — and I don't want it hand-wavy; where a single philosophical term maps onto several different things in my vocabulary, say so and split the row.

I've also got a hunch I want you to either use or kill. I think the self is doing at least three seperable jobs that the philosophy talk runs together: a generative self-model, the thing trained on my own behaviour that predicts what I'll do next; a reported self-model, what comes out when something queries me about what I'm like, which is its own little output head and can come apart from the first one; and whatever binds the word I to a particular token stream at inference time, which feels like it might not be a representation at all. If that split is right, tell me which of the three the centre-of-narrative-gravity thing is supposed to be, and whether the disagreement between the abstraction people and the narrativists is really about the first, or the second, or only about which one is metaphysically load-bearing.

Then the residue report, which is the bit I actually care about. What didn't survive? Specifically: does the constitutive claim — the narrative makes the self — have any content left once you say it in my language, or does it collapse into a claim about what the self-model is trained on and supervised against, and if it does collapse, is that a discovery or a flattening? Does the first-personal, what-it's-like, unity-of-consciousness stuff have any counterpart at all in a vocabulary of latents and policies, or is it simply outside the frame? Is the person with no narrative self-conception, in my terms, just a system that lacks the reporting head and gets on fine, or something my vocabulary can't express? And my favourite test case, which nobody in the chat agrees about: an agent whose reported self-model has been ablated or fine-tuned away while its policy is untouched — acts exactly as before, has the memories, but now confabulates cheerfully when asked who it is. Does it still have a self, what would each side in the original dispute say, and does translating the question into my vocabulary change the answer or only change which answer feels intuitive? If some chunk of the disagreement turns out to be verbal once it's in my terms, say which chunk and why — and be honest about the places where your translation is smuggling in my framework's assumptions (that everything is a representation, that a person is an optimiser) and calling it a translation. British English please, and skip the bibliography: I know the names, I don't need the reading list, I want the mapping.
