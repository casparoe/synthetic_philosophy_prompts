I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about a decade, and every time the value of originals comes up the thread runs on the same three cached moves, so I want to build the spec before I build the post.

You are a professional philosopher of art with training in analytic metaphysics who has also advised a museum's collection committee on deaccession, conservation triage, and whether to display facsimiles. Think carefully, and work in two passes: first brainstorm a long candidate list of requirements together with the cases that motivate each, then prune hard and keep only requirements that are doing real work. Taboo the word authentic except where you define it.

Here is the deliverable. Write the desiderata that a theory of what is lost when a perfect substitute replaces an original must meet to be adequate for two jobs: to tell an institution what it is buying when it insists on the original (conservation budgets, insurance, whether to keep showing something once it is known to be a fake), and to tell an individual whether it is rational to care that the object they have loved for years turns out to be a forgery. Adequacy is relative to those two purposes, so say when a requirement is demanded by one and not the other.

For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Say which of the things people demand here are genuine constraints and which are prejudices of a particular approach. Flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and name the single requirement that knocks out the most otherwise-popular theories.

The cases it must get right: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a unique painting secretly swapped into its frame forty years ago and admired ever since; a canvas whose underdrawing is by the master but whose paint layer is mostly by pupils working from his designs, catalogued as his for three centuries; a genuine work demoted to a pupil after reattribution, with the object and its actual history unchanged; a painter who repaints an exact copy of her own destroyed painting; a piece that is just instructions plus a certificate, executed by whoever holds it; a file whose copies are byte-identical, where only the transaction record differs; a found canvas with no documentation that stylometry puts at high but not certain probability of being by a known hand; and destruction of an original in storage that nobody would ever have seen again, with an indistinguishable replacement installed the same day.

Separate at least the aesthetic or experiential, the art-historical, and the scarcity or economic registers, and say which of them the word better is tracking in ordinary claims about originals. One candidate of my own: that a theory must explain why the backroom destruction above is a loss at all. Is that a real desideratum or my own prejudice — and if it is real, which theories fail it?

A short scorecard of two or three contenders against the list is fine at the end, but the criteria are what I'm paying for.
