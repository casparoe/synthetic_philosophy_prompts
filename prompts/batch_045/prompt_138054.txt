I help put together the quarterly internal newsletter of the Ontology Compliance Group at Meridian, a long-running persistent virtual world, and for the next issue I want a satirical centerpiece rather than another policy summary. Background: after a long internal review the platform formally adopted what it calls the realist line — the view that in-world objects are genuine objects, computational entities that persist, collide, resist and are tracked by the physics engine; that they are not to be confused with merely fictional objects (there is no such sword, but there really is this sword-instance); and that an object is one thing while its rendering, which varies with hardware, draw distance and a user's settings, is another. This was sold to us as a tidy way to settle moderation disputes. It has instead produced an enormous amount of work, because once we say the objects are real we owe our users a principled story about what it is to damage, destroy, delete, duplicate, restore or steal one, and what any of those are worth.

So: write the Adjudication Memorandum in which the Group announces its revised Schedule of Compensable Events. Form is a numbered internal document — clauses, sub-clauses, a short definitions section, two or three worked examples in boxes — about 800 words, in a deadpan bureaucratic register that has clearly been drafted by people who take their own ontology seriously. The target is the realist line itself, together with the genres it is being forced to wear: insurance tariff, moderation policy, actuarial guidance. Please put the following on the table and have the Board rule on each without hedging: the you-cannot-steal-a-number problem (if an object is a multiply instantiable pattern, in what does theft consist, and is restoring from last night's snapshot a resurrection or a replacement?); the orphaned-object problem (procedurally generated terrain and entities in regions no player ever visited, unowned and unperceived, deleted in the weekly maintenance window — what is the tariff for that, and what is the correct name for a scheduled recurring event of that kind?); the recursion question (a fully working VR rig operated inside Meridian by a user's avatar, containing its own world with its own objects: the Board must either apply a depth discount of some percentage per level of nesting or explain why no such discount is available to it, and it should notice which option its own official statements about computational structure commit it to); avatar injury (harm to the user, or harm to an entity with interests of its own) and the offline case (a beloved item destroyed while its owner is asleep, so that no one's experience changed — whether the tariff recognizes object-directed harm at all, and what that does to the pricing schedule); and, in an appendix, how a broken wrist sustained in physical reality while reaching for a real table is to be classified in this scheme.

Constraints, and they matter more than the jokes. Stay inside the position: nobody in the document may suggest that virtual objects are not really real, and no character may wink, break register or say in so many words that this has all gone too far — the reductio has to be carried entirely by the Board following its own commitments where they lead, which means the reader should recoil at a conclusion the Board itself treats as routine paperwork. At the same time the piece should be fair: include at least one clause where the realist's actual motivation is stated well enough that a philosopher sympathetic to it would not feel strawmanned, and if you include a dissenting footnote let it be a dispute about the depth discount or the backup question rather than an escape from realism. Invent whatever names, case numbers and internal committees you need; please do not cite, quote or name any real author or real work, since our readers will not have a library to hand.
