I host a fortnightly evening group called Decisions, Bets and Games in the back room of a pub: twelve to sixteen adults, mostly friends of friends, ranging from about twenty to sixty years old, with very uneven comfort with numbers. I get one 90-minute session with a short break in the middle, no projector, but I can print cards and handouts and I have counters, dice and a whiteboard. Any stakes have to be trivial and non-monetary — choosing the next game, a chocolate bar, a round of applause, something of that kind. I left school at eighteen and have been working through this material on my own from a couple of books and a great deal of graph paper, so please assume I know the elementary vocabulary (payoff matrix, expected value, dominated strategy) and unpack everything past that.

The topic for next month is the following kind of argument: you cross out one of your options because some *mixture* of your other options does better against everything the other side could possibly do, even though no single one of your options does; then, once that option is gone for you, something opens up for the other side; and so on, in a cascade, until the process stops. I would like you to design a session that makes a room full of non-specialists *feel* that argument by playing it, rather than by being told it.

Some design requirements, in rough order of importance:

1. The first game should contain an option that no single alternative beats — the elimination only becomes available once you consider splitting your play between two others. It should be genuinely surprising to the room, and the mixing weights should not be an obvious half-and-half; ideally the hedge leans heavily on an option that looks weak or silly on its own, so that people resist the crossing-out and then discover it is forced.

2. The cascade must be real: at least three rounds of elimination, and at least one option that survives when the full set of options is on the table but dies later, once something else has been removed. I want participants to hear themselves say, in effect, that they could not have seen it at the start.

3. I want a moment where the hedge is not advice. That is: in one of the games, the mixture that does the eliminating should be a bad thing to actually play, given how people in the room in fact behave — please show the expected-value arithmetic against a realistic or empirical guess about actual play — so that the room has to confront the difference between *this option can never be part of a guaranteed-better plan* and *this mixture is what you should do*. This distinction is the thing I keep getting stuck on in my own reading, so please build the activity around it rather than around the technique.

4. Near the end, if time allows, a ten-minute coda on what goes wrong when you delete options that only *weakly* lose: two tiny matrices where the order in which you delete changes what survives, with both orders worked through. I do not need this to be a game; a whiteboard demonstration with volunteers is fine, but it should land as a warning rather than as a footnote.

5. Optionally, and only if you think it can be done honestly in the time available: a short three-player stage designed to expose that the mixing argument quietly assumes the other players' choices are uncorrelated — that a hedge can look like it protects you against everything only because it treats two opponents as varying independently. If you think this cannot be staged fairly in the time left, please say so plainly and explain in a couple of sentences what the difficulty is; I would rather hear that than get an activity that fudges the point.

What I need from you: the rules and materials; the payoff tables with the exact mixtures and the inequalities written out so that I can verify every elimination by hand with a pencil; a minute-by-minute timeline; the handout text as I would actually give it to people (it must be playable without anyone being told in advance what a mixed strategy is — the vocabulary should arrive after the experience, not before); and a debrief that ties specific moments in the play to specific features of the argument, including answers to the two objections I expect to get: why would I ever randomize, I am not trying to be unpredictable, and why should I assume the other person is reasoning the way I am. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — including what happens if someone plays an option that was supposed to be eliminated, since that will certainly occur — and be frank about which part of the argument the activity cannot produce and which I will simply have to state myself at the end.
