My office book club has been working slowly through a short introduction to epistemology, and we have stalled on the Gettier problem. One member of the group defends a line I will paraphrase: knowledge, she says, is like a clock showing the correct time. A stopped clock happens to show the right time twice a day, but it does not know the time; a clock that shows the right time because its mechanism works does. So, she concludes, the Gettier cases only show that justified true belief needs one further condition — that the belief have been produced by a process of a kind that generally gets things right — and once that condition is added, the problem more or less dissolves.

I suspect the clock picture is doing the work of her conclusion, and I have been pushing an analogy from my day job instead: knowledge is like a green build in continuous integration. A build can be green while the code is broken — two bugs that cancel, a test whose assertion was silently weakened, an alert that stays quiet because the metrics pipeline died — so a passing suite is strong evidence that the code is correct, never a guarantee.

I am an engineer with no philosophy training, so please define any term of art you use. What I want is an audit of both analogies, in British English, in under 700 words including any table:

1. A mapping table. For each of the clock picture and the CI picture, say what plays the role of the world, the belief, truth, the subject's evidence or justification, the process that produces the belief, and luck.
2. Which failure modes each picture can represent and which it cannot. I care about three: a build that is green because two bugs cancel; a build that is green because the assertion passed for a reason unconnected to the code; and a build whose green light is a flake. My sense is that only the second is genuinely a Gettier case, and that the clock picture cannot see any difference between the three. Check that.
3. The disanalogies, split into the ones that matter for the conclusion she wants and the ones that do not. The clock has a designer, a purpose, and a convention against which its output is judged; the build has an explicit specification, an author of the tests, and — the part I think may be fatal — a ground truth we can inspect directly afterwards, which we cannot do with our evidence about the world.
4. Whether her conclusion survives. In particular: does the CI picture support a global condition (the process is usually right) or a local one (this build would not have been green if the assertion had been false), and does it say anything at all about whether the steps in the reasoning itself have to be sound? If one of those is what the analogy is really motivating, is it enough to close the Gettier gap, or does something still slip through?
5. If there is a better analogy for knowledge-as-opposed-to-merely-true-belief than either of ours, give it in one or two sentences.
