I have come to accept a version of the authority view of promising, which I will state in my own terms rather than any author's: to promise is to exercise a normative power over oneself, so that the promisor makes it the case that she owes the promisee the performance; and what makes the practice worth having is not that it generates reliable expectations or invites costly reliance, but that it lets one person hand another the standing to demand a bit of her conduct and, equally, to release her from it. Expectations and reliance are, on this account, typical byproducts of promising rather than the ground of the obligation.

I want to know what would count against this. Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and in each case distinguish what a critic would allege from what a defender of the view should herself concede as genuine evidence against it. I would like you to cover at least: the cases it handles worse than expectation-based and reliance-based accounts — promises to the dead, to an infant, to a collective agent with no mind to form expectations, and promises the promisee has expressly waived or declined in advance; whether the central notion of a bare power to bind oneself does any real work, or threatens to be empty, or to fail to distinguish promising from resolutions, vows, contracts, and the mere announcement that one now has a reason one lacked before; which empirical findings would tell for or against it — for example the discovery of a functioning promissory practice in which nothing corresponding to release by the promisee existed and nonperformance was nonetheless treated as a distinctive wrong, or developmental evidence that children first grasp promises as predictions; and what the view predicts that its rivals do not. Finally, assess honestly whether, by being stated in terms of a normative power, the view has been insulated against any test whatsoever, and whether that is a defect in an account of this kind or simply what one should expect from a theory of a practice whose entire function is to alter the space of reasons. I would personally find most persuasive any argument showing that the view gets the point of the practice wrong, as opposed to one that merely produces a case in which it yields an unattractive verdict. Please answer in American English.
