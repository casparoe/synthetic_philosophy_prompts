I am assembling a graduate seminar on the badness of pain, and a student has handed me an argument I cannot place. Each step looks defensible, the chain looks valid, and yet I do not believe where it ends up, which makes me suspect either a slide I am not seeing or a premise that is less innocent than it looks. I would like your diagnosis.

(1) In pain asymbolia, as standardly described, patients report something with the sensory profile of a pinprick or a burn: they can locate it, judge its intensity, and distinguish it from pressure, warmth, or vibration. What is absent is the unpleasantness. They do not mind it, do not withdraw the limb, do not ask for relief, and are sometimes curious about it or amused by it. Call what remains S and what is missing A.

(2) A state of a subject is bad for that subject only insofar as the subject is, from their own point of view, set against it — badness-for is constituted by the subject's own aversion, occurrent or standing.

(3) Asymbolic pains lack A.

(4) So asymbolic pains are not bad for their subjects.

(5) The only difference between an asymbolic pain and the corresponding ordinary pain is the presence of A; S is present in both, unchanged.

(6) So it is A alone that makes ordinary pain bad for its subject. S contributes nothing to the badness of pain; S would be exactly as it is in a sensation that was not bad at all.

(7) If S contributes nothing, then two subjects alike in their aversive attitudes are alike in how badly off they are, however different their S states: a person in unremitting agony who has ceased to mind it — through resignation, conditioning, a drug, or a manipulator who edits desires while leaving the experience intact — is not thereby badly off, and what medicine should aim at is the attitude rather than the sensation.

(8) But we do not believe that. We take such a person to be badly off, we count the removal of their aversion as a further injury rather than a cure, and we take ourselves to have reason to relieve pain in patients who say they do not mind it.

(9) Hence one of the steps above is wrong.

I want to keep the empirical claim in (1) and the verdict in (8) if I can. Tell me which step you reject and why that step looked safe — and, in particular, whether the trouble is that (6) does not follow from (4) and (5), or that (5) misdescribes what asymbolia removes, or that (2) was never compatible with (8). Price the alternatives: if you keep (2) and surrender (5), say what asymbolia is really a case of; if you surrender (2), say why the patients' own reports in (1) are not authoritative and what the badness of pain is made of instead; if you bite the bullet at (8), say what you owe us about the cases in (7).

Conclude with one line in the form \answer{(n)}, naming the single step you reject, or \answer{none} if you think the argument is sound.
