The situation: I run a monthly evening reading group of about fourteen people — mostly software engineers, a GP, a retired maths teacher — who know effective altruism mainly from podcasts and blog posts, and who have had no formal philosophy or economics. We have ninety minutes. Last month we attempted the earning-to-give debate and it went badly: the group spent almost the whole session arguing about whether high-earning donors are hypocrites, and they left with the impression that every critique of effective altruism is really a critique of motives. So this month I want to do the institutional critique properly, as a critique of a method rather than of anyone's character.

My gloss on it, so that we are working with the same thing: effective altruism's standard method takes the menu of existing interventions as given, assesses each candidate use of a marginal pound by asking what would have happened otherwise — including whether the intervention has room for more funding — and ranks options by expected benefit per pound. The institutional critique, as I understand it, holds that this procedure is structurally blind to actions whose return comes from changing the menu itself: founding organisations, shifting professional or legal norms, building funding pipelines, seeding a research field. Those returns are threshold-shaped, path-dependent, and attributable to no single marginal pound, so a method built to compare marginal contributions to going concerns will systematically underrate them. That is roughly what I want the group to see, and I want them to see why it is a problem for a way of reasoning rather than a reason to feel sulky about donors.
