I have no training in philosophy, but I have been working on one question by myself: whether lying is morally different from deliberately misleading someone, and if so where the difference is supposed to sit. A friend thinks the difference is nearly the whole story, and I used to agree. Then I noticed what seems to follow from it, and I have ended up with an argument whose conclusion I am sure is false. I would rather be told which step to drop than keep going round in circles.

(1) You lie only if you say something you believe to be false. Deceiving on purpose by any other route — hinting, staged evidence, calculated silence, a true sentence chosen for what it suggests — is misleading, not lying.

(2) What a speaker says is settled by what they mean, not by the plain meaning of the sentence they utter. Otherwise every slip of the tongue, joke, and metaphor would be a lie.

(3) Meaning something is an intentional act, so what you mean is up to you: if you intend your words to express proposition X, then X is what you say.

(4) So there is an escape hatch, which I have started calling laundering: take any true proposition that the sentence you want to utter could be used to express, privately intend that one, and you have said something true. You can then deceive as thoroughly as you like and never lie — even when the reading you intend is one no hearer would ever arrive at.

(5) If there is a moral difference between lying and misleading, it has to be a difference in what the speaker did with their words; that is the only place the two cases differ.

(6) By (3) and (4), what a speaker does with their words is settled by the speaker's own intention.

(7) So which of the two categories a deceptive speaker falls into — and therefore how wrong their deception is, or whether it is wrong at all — is something the speaker can settle by an act of will.

That has to be wrong. It makes verbal ingenuity a moral excuse and ranks the careful, artful deceiver as less blameworthy than the clumsy one, which is backwards; I do not think anyone who took the trouble to make every sentence literally true is thereby the better person.

One thing I noticed but cannot place: laundering feels different when the true reading is one the hearer could in principle work out — a shared joke, a private code, technical jargon — from when it is one only the speaker could reach. I am not sure what to do with that.

Please tell me which step you would reject, why that step looked safe, and what each of the other options would cost me — including the option of concluding that the lying/misleading distinction does no moral work and that we should only ever talk about deception. I would like to keep (1) and (2) if I can.
