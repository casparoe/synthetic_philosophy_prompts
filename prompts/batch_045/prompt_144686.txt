The object to audit is the loaf-of-bread picture of the block universe, as it was used at my philosophy-of-time reading group last night. In the version my friend gave: spacetime is a four-dimensional loaf; a simultaneity slice is everything that exists *now* for some observer; the angle of the knife is that observer's inertial frame; different observers carve the same loaf into different stacks of slices and no carving is privileged; and — the load-bearing part — the whole loaf is there whether or not anyone is cutting it, in the way every slice of a real loaf is there even though you only ever see one cut face at a time. Hence, he concluded, all events are equally real and the future is *already there*, the way a distant city is already there.

He put it to two distinct jobs.

First, to carry the Rietdijk–Putnam argument in its Andromeda form: two people strolling past each other in opposite directions carve simultaneity slices that, at the distance of a nearby galaxy, come apart by days — so an event that is in your future is already in someone else's present, and if their present is real, your future is real too.

Second, and this is where I want the pressure applied, to reach a moral conclusion in a discussion about how much to weight the very long-term future: because the future is already in the block, distance in time is supposed to be as morally irrelevant as distance in space, so discounting future benefits is as arbitrary as discounting the welfare of people on another continent. On his telling the far future is not a set of mere possibilities but a distant country already populated by actual people.

What I want from you:

1. A correspondence table. For each element of the source — the loaf, a slice, the angle of the knife, the fact that slicing is a choice, the fact that all the slices are in the loaf at once, the crumbs, the knife itself — say what it is meant to stand for in the target, and flag any element of the source that has no counterpart and any feature of the target the picture leaves unrepresented.

2. The disanalogies, explicitly ranked into the ones that are cosmetic for his purposes and the ones that bite. Please assess at least these: that a knife can be set at any angle in space while boosts are bounded by the light cone, so not every tilting yields a legitimate time slice and causal order is invariant; that time is not a fourth spatial direction and the metric signature is not a detail; that our epistemic relation to the two temporal directions is utterly unlike our relation to left and right; and that the loaf picture quietly presupposes a globally sliceable spacetime, which general relativity does not guarantee — there are solutions with no global slicing at all and solutions with closed timelike curves, and I want to know whether the analogy is even available there, or whether it smuggles in a cosmology-neutral structure that the physics neither gives nor rules out.

3. Whether the Rietdijk–Putnam move actually forces eternalism or merely restates the analogy with extra steps. Say what a presentist, a defender of a privileged foliation, and a growing-block theorist can each reply, and be specific about which premise of the argument each one rejects and what that rejection costs them elsewhere. I especially want to know whether the step from *no observer-independent simultaneity* to *no objective simultaneity* needs an extra premise, and what that premise is.

4. Whether his moral conclusion survives, and this is the part I could not settle at the table. Two questions, please. Does *all events are equally real* lend any support at all to temporal neutrality in the weighting of benefits, or is that principle defensible and criticisable on grounds entirely independent of the metaphysics of time, so that the block is decoration? And relatedly: if the whole block is there, in what sense do we change anything? Does the picture that makes the far future *already there* also make *we ought to act differently so that it goes better* hard to state — so that the analogy establishes the metaphysics and then quietly eats the conclusion it was brought in to support?

5. One complication he raised when I pressed him, which I could not answer and which I think is where the real work is. He said the block being real does not entail that the future is *fixed*: a block can contain branching structure, so indeterminism is compatible with all events being real. Grant him that. Then ask what happens to the distant-country-full-of-actual-people picture under branching: if many incompatible branches are equally real, are the future persons definite individuals to whom duties are owed, or merely candidate persons, and if the latter, has the analogy broken precisely at the point where it was doing the moral work? Conversely, if only one branch is actual, what in the picture tells us that?

6. Finally: if the loaf is the wrong picture for this job, propose a better one and say, briefly and honestly, what it would and would not support. Is there an analogy that motivates eternalism while staying neutral on determinacy and on fixity?
