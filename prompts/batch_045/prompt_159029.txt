I have been going back and forth with a friend who works in rational choice theory, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict on the conclusion, because I cannot find where the argument breaks and I am no longer sure it does.

His argument, as I have reconstructed it:

(1) A person's preferences are diachronically incoherent if and only if there is a money pump for them: a possible sequence of trades such that (a) at each step the person would, by the lights of their own preferences at that step, choose to accept the trade, and (b) were the sequence to be run, they would end up with an outcome they themselves rank below the one they began with, having also parted with money.

(2) If a person strictly prefers X to Y at a given time, and nothing else hangs on it, then there is some small sum they would pay at that time to have X rather than Y. A preference that disposed you to pay nothing for its object would be sentiment, not preference.

(3) Take someone whose ranking of two options, A and B, reverses between Monday and Tuesday: on Monday A is ranked above B, on Tuesday B above A. By (2), on Monday she pays a small sum to swap B for A; on Tuesday she pays a small sum to swap A for B. Anyone who offers both trades nets the difference, and she ends where she began, poorer. By (1), she is diachronically irrational.

(4) Nothing in (3) turns on why the reversal happens. The construction uses only the fact that the ordering at one time ranks A above B and the ordering at another time ranks B above A. So it covers every reversal, including: (i) someone who reverses her ranking of two treatments after reading a decisive trial; (ii) someone who acquires a taste for olives after a month of eating them; (iii) someone whose ranking of a holiday this week and a holiday next week flips as the date draws near; (iv) someone whose preference for curry this evening depends on whether she had curry last night, and whose ranking over whole sequences of dinners is perfectly transitive.

(5) But (4)(i) is a plain description of learning, and (4)(iv) is a person whose preferences over the only objects she has preferences about are entirely in order. So the argument has proved too much.

What I want to keep: (2); the verdict that the person in (4)(iii) really is open to some complaint while the people in (4)(i) and (4)(iv) are not; and the thought that (1) captures something real, namely that being reliably exploitable is a defect in an agent rather than merely bad luck about who she meets. I would rather give up almost anything else than those.

Something slips between (3) and (4), or perhaps inside (1), but I cannot locate it, and every tightening I try either drains the argument of all force or leaves its force intact along with the absurdity: if we read the acceptance condition so that a person who foresees the sequence refuses the first trade, then no one who can count to two is ever pumped, and (1) looks empty; if we read it so that she takes each trade on its own merits, then (4) stands and learning is irrational. It also worries me that (1) is stated as an equivalence when the argument seems only to need one direction, and that it makes incoherence a matter of what would happen were someone to turn up offering trades that in fact no one offers.

Which step do you reject, why did that step look sound, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, please price the options I can see rather than just pick one: rejecting (1) outright and needing some other account of what is wrong with cyclic or time-inconsistent preferences; keeping (1) but only in the direction the argument actually uses; restricting (2) so that preference and willingness to pay can come apart; and finding a principled distinction among the four reversals in (4) that does not simply re-describe them from the inside. And say what each of the three standard stances costs — accepting each step as it comes, refusing the early trades because you can see what is coming, and binding yourself or sticking to a plan adopted earlier — given that my friend's reply throughout has been that the second of these is enough and that if it makes (1) nearly vacuous, so much the worse for (1).
