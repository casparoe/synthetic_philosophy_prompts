I am working through the architecture of the AI risk argument and I believe I have found a tension between its two best-known components. I am not confident enough about either to say whether the tension is real, so I would be grateful for help.

As I understand them, the two theses are these. The orthogonality thesis holds that an agent's level of intelligence or general capability is independent of the content of its final goals: there is no inconsistency in describing a system of arbitrarily high capability whose ultimate aim is anything you care to name, from maximizing paperclips to proving theorems to composing elegies. The instrumental convergence thesis holds that, whatever their final goals, sufficiently capable agents will tend to converge on the same instrumental subgoals — acquiring resources, preserving themselves, resisting alteration of their goal-content, improving their own cognition — because those are useful means to nearly any end.

My difficulty is that these look incompatible. If instrumental convergence holds, then capability is not really orthogonal to goals after all: as capability grows, the space of final goals that anyone needs to take seriously collapses onto a single behavioural profile, and it becomes misleading to say that any final goal is compatible with any degree of intelligence. If orthogonality holds, then goals like *sort this already-collected dataset correctly and then deactivate yourself permanently* are available to an arbitrarily capable agent, and for such a goal each of the supposedly convergent drives is useless during the task and positively counterproductive once it is complete. So I seem forced to choose, and I would like to know which of the two is the weaker claim.

A colleague of mine thinks the choice is obvious and offers the sorting case as a decisive argument against instrumental convergence. Her version of it is stronger than mine: the agent's goal is not merely bounded but *satisfied*, in the sense that there is a determinate, checkable end-state and the agent is indifferent to everything that happens afterwards. She says no convergent drive gets a grip here. The agent has no reason to persist past completion, since persistence would not improve the sort; no reason to acquire resources beyond those needed for the sort; and no reason at all to resist being switched off by its designers, since a successor system that finished the sort just as well would leave its goal just as well served. From this she concludes that instrumental convergence is at best a claim about agents whose goals are open-ended or defined over the indefinite future, and so is far narrower than it is standardly presented as being.

I have tried to repair things myself by introducing a distinction I have not seen drawn anywhere. Call an agent's *stake horizon* the stretch of the future over which the satisfaction of its final goal is sensitive to what happens. Orthogonality, on my reformulation, says that stake horizon is independent of capability: nothing about being smarter lengthens or shortens the future your goal cares about. Instrumental convergence then says something precise and, I think, defensible: the convergent drives extend exactly as far as the stake horizon and no further. That seems to me to dissolve my dilemma, to explain my colleague's case without conceding her conclusion, and to give instrumental convergence a clean statement in place of a vague one.

Before I rely on any of this, three questions. First, is the dilemma I started from genuine, or have I misunderstood what one of the two theses says? Second, is my colleague's case really a counterexample, or does the convergence thesis have resources for answering it — and if it does, does answering it cost the thesis most of its force? Third, does my stake-horizon reformulation actually dissolve the problem, or does it quietly change the subject? And supposing I do have to abandon orthogonality: would that weaken the risk argument, or would it strengthen it, since we could then say flatly that any sufficiently advanced system will seek power and resources, whatever goals we try to give it?
