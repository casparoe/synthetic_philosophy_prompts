Write a poem to close the final session of a graduate seminar I have been teaching on cyclical and linear theories of history — Polybius and his cycle of constitutions, Ibn Khaldun on group feeling and the dynasty that spends it within a few generations, Spengler's civilizations as organisms, Toynbee's creative minorities turning into dominant ones. The room will include two political scientists sympathetic to cliodynamics, a historian of the early modern Mediterranean who regards the whole tradition as metaphor wearing a wristwatch, and several philosophers who kept asking what would have to be true for a cycle's second turn to count as the same turn as the first. The poem must work read aloud to that mixture: no in-jokes, no lecture in verse, nothing that depends on the reader already agreeing.

The form is a full sestina: six unrhymed stanzas of six lines, then a three-line envoi gathering all six end-words, two to a line, in whatever order the sense demands. The six end-words should be the six phases of the constitutional cycle in order — kingship decaying into tyranny, aristocracy into oligarchy, democracy into rule by the mob, and so round again — either named outright or rendered as concrete emblems, provided that a reader who knows the scheme can map each word to its phase without being told. Let the machinery of the form do the arguing. The rotation brings every word back six times, each time in a different structural slot, and the last stanza's closing line lands on the word the poem's opening line began with. The poem should arrive back at that word having made it impossible to hear it as it was first heard.

Two distinctions from my own work should be embodied in the verse and never named in it.

The restoration discount. A fallen order that is restored — the heir returns, the council is reconvened, the old titles are struck again — never commands what the original did, and the shortfall is structural rather than unlucky: a form legible as a restoration is read as a quotation, and quotations are obeyed less readily than institutions. So each later appearance of a phase should feel thinner than the first even where it looks identical.

The archive effect. A polity that owns a written record of its own previous cycle cannot enter that cycle innocently. Its elites imitate the phase they take themselves to be in — staging the founders' hardiness, turning careerism into elite overproduction, performing decadence on schedule — and the imitation accelerates the transition it imitates. Recurrence and acceleration are one fact seen twice. So the later stanzas should tighten: shorter clauses, less patience in the syntax, the same images arriving before the poem has prepared for them.

Hold both readings to the end. Something irreversible does accumulate each turn, which is the linearists' complaint, and the wheel does come round, which is the cyclicalists'. Let the envoi and that closing word carry both without adjudicating by assertion, and without the shrug that says we were always doomed. No footnotes, no apparatus, no social-science vocabulary, no modern proper nouns; it should be a poem Polybius could follow in translation even though it knows something he did not. Then add a note of at most 120 words: which end-word is which phase, and where the drift is registered.
