I work in clinical education for a regional ambulance service — about two thousand operational staff, mixed roles from emergency dispatchers to paramedics to newly qualified technicians — and several times a year I have to turn a new or revised clinical protocol into training that crews will actually follow at three in the morning, under time pressure, with a patient in front of them. The constraints are unglamorous: I get ninety-minute slots in the rota, a couple of high-fidelity simulation days a year, no ongoing coaching budget, and every package has to be signed off by the clinical director and, where it touches a scope-of-practice question, by staff-side. Last weekend, coming back from a two-day conference, I spent about three hours in a hotel bar arguing with an old colleague who now lectures in paramedic science. She said our protocols, mnemonics and handover templates produce crews who are competent without comprehending anything, and that what we should be doing is teaching people to reason; I said the mnemonics *are* the reasoning, offloaded into a tool, and that no amount of "understanding the physiology" survives adrenaline and sleep loss. We ended up misusing, I suspect, a four-level scheme for kinds of minds — as I half-remember it: creatures hard-wired by evolution to do a small fixed repertoire; creatures that can learn within a lifetime by being reinforced for what they actually try out in the world; creatures that can pre-screen candidate actions internally, so that bad options can be discarded in the head instead of in the field; and creatures whose heads have been re-equipped with imported thinking tools — words, diagrams, checklists, formulae — with that last grade named after some psychologist called Gregory. My colleague used the ladder to argue that almost everything we do is pitched one rung too low; I used the same ladder to argue that our checklists are the highest rung, not the lowest. Neither of us could say how you would actually decide, item by item, which rung a given behaviour needs, and that is the argument I need settled in a form my trainers can use.

What I want is a triage procedure my team can run on each discrete behaviour in a protocol — the questions in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where it stops with a "no, this cannot be trained in the time you have", and where it hands the decision to a person because it is a question of authority or values rather than of learning. It has to be operable by an instructor who has never heard any of this theory, on a single page, in ten minutes per item. Please build it around distinctions that survive that simplification and say which ones you are dropping and what it costs. Some things I have been playing with, which you should use, fix, or throw away as you see fit: the *trial cost* of an item — how bad it is if the learner finds out by actually trying it on a patient, which is what pushes something from on-the-job shaping to simulation or to a rule that filters options before action; the *transfer distance* between the training room and the context where the behaviour has to appear, on a scale from identical through similar to genuinely novel; the *installation depth* of any tool you want crews to use, meaning what sub-tools must already be in place before a mnemonic or threshold value does any work, and what it takes to install them; the *empty-room test*, my name for checking whether a behaviour shows up when no instructor and no assessment is in the room, which I think is what separates something that has really been learned from something the crew has merely learned to perform for us; and *Darwinian residue*, the parts of a failure that are hard-wired rather than trained — startle, tunnel vision, degraded hearing under stress, fatigue — and which therefore have to be designed around rather than educated away. I also want the interference question handled explicitly: crews arrive with years of reinforcement history that rewards speed and seniority, and I suspect some of our training fails not because it is pitched at the wrong rung but because the payoffs in the system still favour the old behaviour, which would make it a management decision and not a training one.

Then run the finished procedure on three items and show your working, including one where I will not like the answer. First: getting crews to complete a new sepsis screening tool and make a pre-alert call on every eligible patient, when the current failure mode is that experienced crews eyeball the patient and skip the form. Second: a rarely used, high-stakes invasive skill with a catastrophic trial cost — paediatric intraosseous access in a moving vehicle will do — where I cannot afford more than a single simulation session per crew. Third, and this is the one I expect to resent: our flagship ninety-minute module on the physiological reasoning behind the new chest-pain pathway, which my colleague would call the only genuinely higher-rung thing we do and which I have defended for two redesigns. Tell me honestly whether the procedure says that module is doing work it cannot do, whether it should be cut, shortened, or moved somewhere it bites, and what I would put in its place. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot tell me: I need to know which of these calls it has no authority over, where it will confidently mislead me, and whether there is any way to use it after an incident to tell a misclassification from a botched implementation. If the four-level scheme is the wrong frame for any of this, or if it is being stretched past what it was built for, I would rather be told that than handed a tidy procedure built on it.
