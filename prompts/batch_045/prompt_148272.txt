I spent forty years in experimental physics, magnetism mostly: hysteresis loops, calibration curves, the standing argument about whether a feature in the data is above the resolution of the instrument. In the three years since I retired I have been sitting in on the ethics reading group in the philosophy department, and I have come to think that the argument the group keeps having about how much morality can demand of a single agent is being conducted at the wrong level. Everyone brings a theory and defends it against three stock examples; nobody first says what a theory of this kind would have to be able to do in order to count as adequate. So before I read any more of them I want the specification.

Let me fix the purpose so that adequacy has a determinate sense. I am seventy-one, in decent health, with a modest but sufficient pension, a paid-off house, and a great deal of unscheduled time. I want to know what is required of me with what is left of my working life, in a form I could defend without embarrassment both to the reading group and to my granddaughter. And I want an answer that does not fall apart if the cost-effectiveness estimates that the effective-giving people rely on are wrong by an order of magnitude, because after a career spent looking at error bars I do not trust them to better than that.

So: write the desiderata that any adequate account of the limits of moral demand must meet, each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference, and each stated so that I could test a candidate theory against it.

The cases I want the specification to be answerable to, with variants that I think are harder than the ones in circulation:

1. The pond case with eleven bystanders, where the child can be rescued by any two of them and the marginal contribution of a twelfth is zero. Whether a theory's demands are indexed to what others actually do strikes me as the deepest fault line here, and I want to know whether that indexation is a defect or a feature.
2. The retiree: low income, substantial assets, and a large stock of free time. A theory that measures sacrifice in currency misclassifies me entirely. Does a theory owe me a common numeraire across money, time, risk, bodily integrity, and my relationships, or may it leave the currencies lexically ordered and unordered among themselves?
3. The null result. A researcher spends twenty years on a programme that had, by the best ex ante estimate, a real chance of a very large benefit, and it comes to nothing. Is the demand assessed prospectively or retrospectively, and does sacrifice that turns out to have bought nothing still count as sacrifice?
4. The second career: someone who could, with effort and some cost, raise their capacity to do good substantially, by training for a higher-earning or higher-impact occupation. Must a theory say whether the baseline of capacity is given or chosen?
5. Hysteresis. Two agents identical in every respect today, one of whom has a long history of sacrifice behind her and one of whom has never given anything. May a theory give them different verdicts on the strength of that history alone, and if it may, what does that do to the idea that requirements supervene on the agent's present situation?
6. The moving baseline. Someone who has trained himself to want very little, or who has come to enjoy the work of aiding others, so that what would once have been a large sacrifice is now none. Is the size of a sacrifice measured against the agent's actual desires, against the desires he could have reshaped, or against some third thing? In my old field this was the question of what you calibrate against, and a theory that left the reference level free was not a theory.
7. Risk to the agent. A certain modest sacrifice with a certain modest good, versus a ten-year commitment with one chance in a hundred of an enormous good and ninety-nine chances of nothing much. May the theory be risk-averse about my sacrifice while remaining risk-neutral about the good?
8. Universal compliance. What does the theory demand of everyone when everyone else already complies and the marginal impact of one more person is negligible? A theory that quietly presumes partial compliance and then generalises has, I suspect, a hidden failure of scale invariance.

Alongside these I want the distinctions a theory cannot collapse: what is required versus what there is most reason to do versus what is merely permitted versus what is praiseworthy if done; how much is demanded versus how many departments of a life the demand reaches; whether the limit is on the demand itself or only on the blame for failing to meet it; individual versus collective demands; and whether the account is meant to be action-guiding at the moment of choice or only to be true.

Three further things, which are the part I most want.

First, mark each desideratum as non-negotiable or as the preference of a camp, and tell me honestly which of my own demands are imported prejudices from a working life in the laboratory. I have in mind particularly the demand that a theory deliver a verdict at a stated resolution rather than an interval or a shrug; I suspect that is a physicist's taste rather than a requirement, but I want the argument either way, and I want the same treatment for the demand that the theory not be gerrymandered to reproduce verdicts we antecedently like.

Second, identify any pair or triple of desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which one you would drop. If the list itself has a structure that forces a choice, that is the result I am after.

Third, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive candidates, and whether it does so for a good reason.

Only after that, and briefly: a short scorecard, a paragraph each and no more, for the families of view the reading group keeps returning to — scalar and satisficing consequentialisms, views that grant the agent a personal prerogative or a sphere of permission, views that limit how much impartial morality may ask for by appealing to the shape of human motivation, and views that simply deny there is a limit to be had. I can check your scoring against the papers myself; it is the criteria I cannot get from them.

Two constraints, please. Where a case needs a number, invent a plausible one and label it as invented rather than leaving it schematic. And do not spend your answer on the layout of the literature: I am not asking who said what, I am asking what any of them would have to be able to do.
