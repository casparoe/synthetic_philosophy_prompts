I am working on forecasting from the past — reference classes, base rates, the inside/outside view as applied to empires — and I want a single case worked in depth rather than another defense of the method. I know the general argument for why base rates beat causal storytelling about the case at hand, and I don't need it repeated. What I don't have is a case where the mapping is done carefully enough to show where the apparatus actually bites and where it is only decoration. I want the Suez Crisis of 1956.

Take as the object of analysis the forecast made by the British and French governments in the autumn of 1956: Nasser nationalized the canal in July, the Israelis attacked into Sinai at the end of October, London and Paris issued their ultimatum and began air operations, troops went into Port Said in early November, and within about a week there was a ceasefire under American, Soviet and United Nations pressure, followed by withdrawal and by Eden's fall from office a couple of months later. On the standard telling, the cabinet was forecasting something like this: that Anglo-French military action would bring Nasser down and produce a compliant Egyptian government within days to weeks, and that British and French forces would then hold and reopen the canal on acceptable terms. That is the forecast I want analyzed. If you think the documentary record supports a weaker, vaguer, or simply different forecast, say so at the start and analyze that one instead — the mapping matters more to me than the attribution.

Please do the following.

First, fix the target variable. State precisely what was being predicted, over what time window, with what implied confidence, and — the part that is usually skipped — what outcome would have counted as vindicating it. A claim like "Nasser will fall" is not checkable as it stands; say what it would have had to mean in order to be scored, and what the historical record can actually score it against.

Second, recover the reference class the cabinet was in fact using. They were not, I take it, reasoning from no class at all: the 1930s were doing a great deal of work, with Nasser cast in the role of the dictator who grabs and then folds when finally faced with force. Work out what class that is, what base rate it would have suggested, and in which direction it was biased. Then tell me what this does to the usual diagnosis. If the failure is not that they ignored base rates but that they selected a class engineered to flatter the action they had already decided on, then "base rate neglect" is the wrong name for it, and I want to know what the right name is and whether the outside view has any internal resource against that failure, or whether it is purely a discipline the forecaster has to bring from outside.

Third, construct the class you would defend, using only what was reasonably knowable in 1956 — the other end-of-empire coercions, the recent cases of a great power using limited force against a nationalist government, and so on. Give me the base rate you would have put on the target variable, but be explicit that it is a judgment and say how much your answer moves if the class is widened or narrowed one notch. Any case sits in endlessly many classes with different base rates, and I want to see how much of your verdict survives that fact rather than a number presented as if the class had chosen itself.

Fourth, test an idea of mine against the case. I have been distinguishing the prospective outside view — the one available before acting, from a class you could have written down in advance and a base rate you adjust — from the retrospective outside view — the one you have after the fact, when you know the outcome and can see which class the case "really" belonged to. My suspicion is that these two almost never coincide, and that the gap is structural rather than a matter of error: the very features that make a case memorable enough to enter anyone's reference class are the features that made it hard to forecast in advance. Call this hindsight contamination of the reference class. Say whether Suez supports that suspicion or is a counterexample to it — is Suez, precisely because it is now the textbook case of imperial miscalculation, a case where we can no longer tell what a 1956 forecaster could honestly have known? Then, if the suspicion holds, can the prospective outside view be repaired at all? Consider at least two repairs: restricting admissible classes to attributes knowable before the outcome, and deliberately taking the broadest admissible class and shrinking toward its mean. Say whether either survives this case, and what each one costs in usefulness.

Fifth, tell me where the framework is silent at Suez, and be strict about the difference between silence and misapplication. Three candidates occur to me. One: the intervention was undone chiefly by a party that was not in the plan — American financial and diplomatic pressure, the United Nations, the two superpowers briefly acting together. Is that an unmodeled variable, or a structural blind spot, given that a base rate taken over a class of interventions says nothing about whether a party outside the plan holds a veto? Two: part of what was forecast was the forecasters' own future resolve, since the government had publicly committed to not being blackmailed. Can a base rate about other people's resolve be applied to your own, or does self-reference break the move? Three: some of "Nasser will fall" functioned less as a prediction than as a thing said in order to hold a coalition together and to bring about its own content. For each, tell me whether it is a limit of the outside view or merely a variable nobody counted, and do not force the framework to speak where it cannot.

Finally, give me the verdict both directions. Is Suez a confirmation of the outside view, an anomaly for it, or a kind of case the method was never built for? And what, concretely, would the outside view have licensed the cabinet to do — change what they believed, change what they planned, or change what they checked before committing? I am especially interested in the minister who says that this case is special: is he making a statistical mistake, or is he doing something else that the framework has no vocabulary for?

Constraints: no citations, bibliographies or reading lists, and please flag any historical claim you are not confident about rather than asserting it. Make the mapping explicit throughout — which actors are the cases, what the options and outcomes are, what counts as evidence, what the numbers are and how you got them. Continuous prose, somewhere around a thousand to thirteen hundred words.
