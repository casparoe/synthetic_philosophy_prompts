I'm on a committee that is about to rewrite the choice architecture for several decisions made by members of my university: automatic enrolment in a supplementary pension for casual teaching staff, the organ-donor question on the annual registration form, and the electricity tariff applied in university-managed housing. Before I argue for any of it I want my own position audited, because I suspect the pieces do not all fit. Here is where I think I stand.

(1) Paternalistic interference is permissible only if the person affected would endorse it were she fully informed and deliberating well. That is an idealized-endorsement test; actual consent is not required, and the fact that someone objects here and now does not settle anything.

(2) *Means* paternalism is far easier to justify than *ends* paternalism. Helping people get what they already want is one thing; steering them toward a conception of a good life they do not share is another, and much harder to defend.

(3) Nudges are presumptively permissible in a way that mandates and bans are not, because they leave the agent's options in place and so leave her free to choose otherwise.

(4) I support automatic enrolment and presumed consent *because* defaults are sticky. I would oppose reforms designed to make opting out easier — a one-click exit, an annual reminder, a neutral active-choice screen that forces a yes/no before anyone can proceed — even though such reforms leave people's options at least as intact as the default does.

(5) I reject the complaint that a nudge is objectionable merely because it works through inattention, framing or inertia. Every arrangement of options influences somebody, and there is no influence-free arrangement to compare against.

(6) I do, however, hold that each intervention must be publicly avowable: permissible only if we could tell the people affected what we are doing and why, and have it survive the telling. I do not think we are required actually to go and tell them.

(7) Most of the defaults I would be voting on were installed years ago by people acting on reasons nobody now holds. Call these *legacy defaults*. I think we may permissibly leave one in place without producing a live justification for it, provided it is not now actively misleading anyone.

(8) I am also willing to nudge for other people's benefit — the green tariff, say — even when those nudged do not much care about that benefit, so long as they are not made worse off by their own lights.

Please audit this set. Tell me which commitments are in genuine tension with which, and which pairs only appear to be; I am fairly confident that at least one of the apparent conflicts dissolves on inspection, but I cannot tell which. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally serious, and for each one say which commitment would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set. If any single commitment is doing two different jobs under an ambiguous phrase, say which reading I need and what that reading commits me to. Finally, name the one revision that would clean up the most damage at the least cost.
