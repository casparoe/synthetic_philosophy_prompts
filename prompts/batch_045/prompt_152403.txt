Write a dialogue between two decision theorists who disagree about how to evaluate rules in a variant of the absent-minded driver problem. The prompt is the variant itself, so set it out carefully before the characters speak.

The case. A driver is driving home late at night along a road with two indistinguishable intersections, X (first) and Y (second). At each one he can EXIT or CONTINUE. Payoffs: exiting at X yields 0; exiting at Y yields 4 (home); continuing past Y yields 1 (he ends up on the motorway and has to pay for a hotel). He is absent-minded: at every intersection his evidence is exactly what it was on any previous visit. He cannot tell X from Y, and he does not remember whether he has already made a decision. His strategy is therefore a single number: continue with probability p at every intersection.

The twist. In the familiar version of the problem his randomizations at the two intersections are independent. Here they are not. With probability δ = 1/3 he is in a perseverative frame of mind at the second intersection and simply repeats whatever he did at the first; with probability 2/3 he chooses afresh, continuing with probability p independently of what he did before. He cannot tell, at the time, which frame of mind he is in; and, a stipulation that does real work, he has no introspective access to the outcome of his randomization before he acts on it. Both characters accept the standard self-locating bookkeeping: the expected numbers of visits to X and to Y stand in the ratio 1 : p, so the driver's credence at an intersection that he is at Y is p/(1+p). The dispute is not about that bookkeeping.

The two characters. Give each a name and make them equally competent; neither is a foil.

The planner holds that rationality is a property of the policy adopted ex ante: the right rule is the one that maximises expected payoff from the standpoint of the departure, given that the driver knows in advance that he will face this very deliberation twice and that his two acts will be correlated in the way described.

The sequencer holds that rationality is a property of what the agent does with the evidence he has when he acts: a rule is admissible only if the agent at the intersection, with credence p/(1+p) that he is at Y and with the continuation structure the rule itself induces, has no reason to depart from it, i.e. only if the rule is a fixed point of the intersection-level best response.

What the dialogue has to accomplish.

1. Begin with the uncorrelated case, δ = 0. Have both characters derive, in their own words and showing the algebra, that the ex ante value is 4p − 3p², so the planning optimum is p = 2/3 with value 4/3, and that the intersection-level continue-minus-exit difference, (4 − 6p)/(1 + p), vanishes at exactly the same point. They should register this agreement as something that stands in need of an explanation rather than as a coincidence to be passed over in a sentence.

2. Turn to δ = 1/3. The arithmetic they should arrive at: the probability of continuing at Y given that he continued at X is r = 1/3 + (2/3)p, so the ex ante value is p(4 − 3r) = 3p − 2p², maximised at p = 3/4 with value 9/8; while the intersection-level difference is (3 − 5p)/(1 + p), vanishing at p = 3/5, where the value is 27/25. So the planning optimum is one the agent who must execute it rejects (at p = 3/4, exiting is worth 12/7 to him and continuing 9/7), while the only rule that endorses itself at the intersection is ex ante dominated (27/25 against 9/8), and the pure best-response dynamics do not settle anywhere: below p = 3/5 the best response is to continue for sure, above it to exit for sure. If the algebra comes out differently for you, follow your own algebra and say so explicitly.

3. Then give each side its strongest turn, and make each respond to what the other has actually said rather than to a prepared statement. The sequencer should press at least this: that the planner's rule asks the driver, at the moment of action, to do what is by his own lights the worse thing; that the planner's marginal calculation contains a term, the effect of p on the act at Y through the perseveration channel, which cannot appear as a reason for acting now; and that ex ante value is computed from a standpoint the agent no longer occupies, so appealing to it looks like handing authority over the later self to the earlier one. The planner should press at least this: that when the two acts are correlated, a departure from the rule at one intersection is not a local departure, since it propagates to the other through the perseveration channel, so the fixed-point condition the sequencer relies on is not the equilibrium condition it resembles; that acting on the intersection-level preference is self-defeating, since always exiting returns 0, the pure dynamics cycle, and the self-endorsing rule returns 27/25 against 9/8; and that the sequencer's question, what is best here holding fixed what I do elsewhere, is malformed when there is no way to hold that fixed.

4. Close by locating the crux precisely rather than resolving it: state what would have to be true for the planning optimum to be the answer and what would have to be true for the fixed-point rule to be the answer, and identify the one substantive proposition the two views disagree about, for instance whether the agent's own later act may be treated as part of the environment when evaluating a present option. No concession speeches, and no verdict imposed from outside; the disagreement should still be live at the end, but a reader should be able to say what kind of argument or case would settle it. If they want a final stress test, let them consider total perseveration, δ = 1, where the planning optimum is p = 1 with value 1 and the fixed point is p = 1/3 with value 1/3.

Constraints: 1,000 to 1,400 words; no citations, references, footnotes, or named papers, since the argument has to stand on its own arithmetic; no stage directions beyond what is needed to keep the exchange legible; and keep the register that of two professionals who have been through this many times and are now trying to find out where exactly they differ.
