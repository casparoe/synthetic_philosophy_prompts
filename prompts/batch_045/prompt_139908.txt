Hi — hoping for a bit of help with a campus mess that I suspect is also a problem in social epistemology, though I can't quite say which one.

About three years ago roughly twenty of us from across campus started an email list to push back on the administration's plan to hand our online degree programs to a private provider. I'm one of the philosophy people on it. The campaign is going to the faculty senate in a few weeks and I've got a bad feeling about it, but not the usual bad feeling.

Here's what puzzles me. Nothing about this group looks closed. Nobody has ever been kicked off or shouted down, new people get added whenever they ask, and we circulate the other side's material constantly: the provost's blog posts, the consultancy's white papers, op-eds from the higher-ed press. But every time, the organizer forwards it with a short framing note and a pre-written reply, and the discussion then ratifies the reply. Two members who drifted away and came back with actual data — completion rates at a university that did something similar, that kind of thing — got accounted for rather than answered: one had taken a small consulting gig, the other was shortlisted for a deanship, and that became the explanation for why his evidence didn't count. And last month we learned that the advocacy report most of our talking points come from has been partly walked back by its own authors; the list's response was that the authors had been lent on by the administration. Which might even be true. That's the thing — an explanation is always available.

I've started calling a related thing template immunity: we've spent three years rehearsing why each type of argument for outsourcing is wrong, so any new argument gets recognised as an instance of a type we've already handled and never really gets assessed on its merits. I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real phenomenon or something I made up to feel clever in my own head.

Meanwhile I'm one of two or three people on the list whose posts reliably get agreed with, and I've noticed that when I hedge about something the group accommodates the hedging rather than updating. I've drafted a memo with the strongest version of the other side's case — I think at least one emprical claim in our motion is false, and I think part of the administration's argument is right. My department chair says either quit the list or get two people from the other side onto it. Both feel like they miss whatever is actually going on.

So: should I send the memo, and what exactly am I doing if I do — is trading on my standing with these people a way of getting them to think, or just a softer way of managing them? If some distinction in the epistemology literature is doing the work here, please use it; I'd much rather you named the structure than told me to keep an open mind. And be explicit about which parts of what you're saying are settled and which are live disputes among people who work on this — don't smooth a real disagreement into a consensus. No need to name papers or studies, I want the shape of the argument, not a reading list.
