I am teaching a first-year seminar on moral psychology next term and am pairing selections from Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments with Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, and I want a reading of the novel as a sustained case study in what happens to the impartial spectator when the social machinery that is supposed to produce it is deformed. I have the texts and my students will have read Smith on sympathy, on the pleasure of mutual sympathy, on our division of ourselves into examiner and examined, and on the distinction between the love of praise and the love of praiseworthiness; so please do not summarize the plot or the theory, and assume I can supply whatever textual references I need.

Three threads interest me, and I would like each of them pressed as far as it goes. First, a calibration worry. On Smith's account the inner judge is not an imported standpoint but something tuned by repeated exchanges of sympathy with the people around us, by the felt concord or dissonance between their sentiments and our own. Stevens's working life is one of near-total deference asymmetry: he has no peers, only superiors and subordinates, and the person whose sentiments he is most practiced at reconstructing is also the person whose approval he most needs. Call it the calibration problem. I want to know whether Smith's own account predicts this outcome, or whether he has resources — the independence of the imagined spectator from any actual spectator, the general rules of morality, the requirement that the spectator be well-informed — that let him say Stevens's judge was never really impartial in the relevant sense. Second, the sufficiency of self-division. Smith's mechanism is the imagination splitting a person into two; Stevens is a virtuoso of exactly this, since the professional ideal of never being taken unawares is self-division formalized as a craft. I take the novel to show the machinery working perfectly while impartiality is entirely absent, which suggests the machinery plus imagination is not sufficient and that something further is doing the work — perhaps a social condition of reciprocity among equals, perhaps an informational one, since Stevens declines to read, declines to inquire, and takes the view that a butler is not to have opinions. I would like your view on which condition Smith needs and whether he has it.

Third, I want the praise-versus-praiseworthiness distinction tested rather than applied. It looks like Smith's bulwark against just this failure: we want to deserve approval, not merely to receive it. But Stevens's test of desert is internal to the service he has chosen, and if Smith explains how we learn what is praiseworthy partly by exposure to the actual approval of those around us, the distinction may re-create the dependence it was introduced to escape. Is that a real vulnerability in the theory, or am I misreading the order of explanation?

Point me to the scenes that carry the reading — I am thinking of the dismissal of the two housemaids, the night his father dies upstairs while Stevens serves below, the parlour scene over the book he is reading, the exchange in the pub about what dignity consists in, and the bench on the pier — and tell me, in each case, what the scene shows and where my reading forces it. I especially want the places where the novel resists: where Ishiguro is doing something Smith's vocabulary does not reach, where Stevens is not simply self-deceived but afraid of his own interior, and where an honest critic should say the parallel breaks down. Close with what you think the novel contributes to the Smithian debate that the arguments alone do not.
