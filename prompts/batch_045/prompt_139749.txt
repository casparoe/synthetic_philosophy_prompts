Hey — I'm a novelist, and I've hit something that's probably more philosophy than craft, so I'm hoping you can help.

I'm about 200 pages into a book about a woman who, after a nasty accident, starts hearing a running commentary on her own conduct. Not a hallucination, more like her conscience has grown a voice. The conceit I care about is that this voice isn't private and isn't infallible: in my notes I call it the gallery, and it's made out of internalized other people. Early in the book it's vicious and shaming because she's surrounded by people who shame her. Later, with a different crowd, it gets kind, and I want the reader to feel that her moral improvement and her moral collapse are the same mechanism running in different weather — that she has no way, from the inside, to tell which one she's in. A friend pointed out that this is basically Adam Smith's impartial spectator with the plumbing showing, and I've been chewing on it since. I don't care whether Austen ever read Smith, by the way; I care whether the idea is true to what the book does.

So: read Pride and Prejudice through the impartial spectator. I mean the business where you split into the person who acts and the person who watches, and where the goal isn't just to be praised but to be the sort of person praise would actually be owed to — you want the applause, but you also want to deserve it, and the second desire is the one that's supposed to keep the first honest.

Concretely, here's what's occured to me and where I'd like you to push back. Elizabeth seems like a showcase character: she's constantly examining herself, and the letter at Hunsford is a violent recalibration of her internal spectator, which is roughly what I want for my narrator. But the interesting thing, for my purposes, is that her spectator was mis-set long before that — Wickham's charm, her family's constant ridicule, her own pride in being the one who sees through people. If her inner judge was built out of the same social materials that misled her, then Smith's spectator isn't a cure for bias, it's a delivery mechanism for it. Is that a fair reading, or am I bending the novel?

And what about the characters who don't fit: Mr. Collins, who wants the praise without bothering about the desert; Darcy, whose fault seems less vanity than a failure to imagine being seen from below; Wickham, who reads like a man with no interior at all; Jane, who refuses to condemn anyone and might be the more interesting failure — a spectator so charitable it can't do its job.

Then the thing I actually need for my own book: does Elizabeth's arc vindicate the idea or quietly dismantle it? Part of me thinks Austen is showing that the spectator is never impartial, just variably colonized by your neighbourhood and your class, and that Elizabeth gets a better one mostly by luck — a letter, a house, a housekeeper's testimonial. If that's right, my gallery is a faithful version of Smith rather than a parody, and I should stop worrying. If it's wrong, I'm building a whole novel on a misreading.

Last bit: what does the fiction give you here that the argument doesn't? I have a hunch it's that a novel can show a spectator being recalibrated in real time, over hundreds of pages of social life, whereas Smith mostly hands you the finished mechanism — but say if that's self-serving.
