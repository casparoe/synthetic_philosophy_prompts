I volunteer a couple of Saturdays a month with a local land trust doing invasive-plant pulls, and last spring the ecologist who runs them gave our group a description instead of a specimen: the one we were after, she said, looks a lot like bamboo — hollow, segmented canes — except the stems are reddish and speckled and the leaves come off the stem in a zig-zag, one per node. I had never knowingly seen either plant in my life. Two weeks later I'm walking my dog past a vacant lot, I see something that matches, I pull a bit of it and send a photo to the group chat, and it was definately the invasive one. I came away from that feeling like I'd learned what the plant is.

My flatmate, who did a module on classical Indian epistemology last year and hasn't shut up about it since, says nothing philosophically interesting happened. Her line is that I got a sentence from an expert (fine: trusting people), stored it, saw something with those visible features, and then either inferred it — experts say the invasive looks like this, this looks like this, so this is the invasive — or just recognised it the way I'd recognise my own bike in a bike rack. Either way, she says, there is nothing in my story that isn't already perception, inference, memory and testimony, and adding a fifth item to the list of "sources of knowledge" is just being impressed by how the experience felt. She also says the comparison one on that list is the embarrassing entry, and that the fight over whether each entry does any real work was basically won by the people who wanted to keep only perception and inference. I can't quite put my finger on why, but her inference version feels like it skips a step or sneaks one in, and I'd like to know the strongest form of her argument before I try to answer her. Then a second thing happened: another volunteer followed the same description and confidently pulled what turned out to be a native shrub with reddish stems whose leaves come off in opposite pairs — same route, wrong result — and my flatmate says that settles it, because a genuine source of knowledge couldn't hand you a falsehood; when I said eyesight misfires in bad light too, she said that's precisely my problem, since if I let it misfire I owe her an account of what separates the misfire from the real thing, and if the answer is just "whether you happened to be right" then calling it a source does no work at all.

So here's where I'm stuck, and I'd rather not just assert something she'll shoot down. Is she right that the whole thing is reducible, or is there something the resemblance route gives me that perception, inference, memory and testimony can't produce between them — and if there is, what's the best version of her reduction and where exactly does it break? How should I handle the volunteer who pulled the wrong plant: does that case refute the route, or only show it can go wrong, and what would have to be true for each of those answers? And one last wrinkle I can't sort out: if the ecologist had been standing beside me in that lot, pointed at the plant and said "that's the one," I'd have ended up with the very same belief — is that the same route as mine or a different one, and should either of us care? (My dog, for what it's worth, seems to acquire "squirrel" from about one exposure, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's the same phenomenon or none of my business.)
