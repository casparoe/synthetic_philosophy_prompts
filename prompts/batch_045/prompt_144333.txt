I am on a small committee revising the practices of a learned society — among them double-blind reviewing, the expectation that referees work unpaid, the order in which senior and junior members are called on at panels, and a lingering practice of not citing another member's unpublished conference paper without asking first. We keep calling these things 'conventions' and 'norms' interchangeably, and I have come to think the word is doing no work in our deliberations. So before I classify any of them, I want the specification.

The purpose fixes what adequacy means here, so let me state it. The theory I need is one a reformer can use from the inside, in advance of acting, and without survey data, to answer three questions: whether a given practice is a convention at all rather than a mere regularity, an artefact of an external constraint, or a norm of some other sort; if it is a convention, what is actually holding it up; and which of the instruments really available to us — setting a precedent, changing what is observable to whom, a public announcement, attaching a cost to deviation, changing who the population is — has any chance of moving it, and which is certain not to. A theory that only lets a philosopher classify a practice correctly once the history is known is no use to me, and a theory that classifies correctly but tells me nothing about levers is only half a theory.

Write the desiderata such a theory has to meet. For each one, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference, and tell me whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a prejudice of one camp. The classifications I most need it to get right, and which I would use as test cases: a practice everyone complies with and everyone knows everyone complies with, while each privately believes — falsely — that most others endorse it, and which survives a public announcement of that fact; a practice that looks conventional but is really a by-product of an external constraint, such as a funder's reporting requirement, so that repealing the constraint dissolves it without anyone changing their mind; a practice nobody in the organisation can state, which nevertheless survives near-total turnover of its members; a practice that is inefficient and known by all to be inefficient, and yet stable for decades; and a practice that collapses within a year with no change in sanctions, population, or anyone's private convictions. Anything your list cannot sort from its neighbours is a gap in the list.

Two further things matter more to me than completeness. First, flag any subset of your desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied: I would rather have a short list with a proved tension in it than a long list that quietly presupposes a theory that cannot exist. My own suspicion is that the demand that a theory explain stability and the demand that it explain endogenous change are met by theories of incompatible shapes — one wants an equilibrium concept, the other wants a dynamics defined largely off the equilibrium — and that the second of these is where the Lewisian picture is thinnest. If something like that is right, say which desideratum should give way given my purpose, and what I lose by dropping it. Second, tell me which single requirement on your list excludes the most candidates, and whether it is a genuine requirement or a preference you happen to share — I would rather know which of your demands is doing the real work.

If the honest answer is that 'convention' is a folk term that should be split into two or three kinds before any desiderata are stated, say so and give me the taxonomy the design purpose actually needs, rather than analysing a word.

Then, briefly — a short paragraph each, and no references, since I know the literature and will supply citations myself — how the Lewisian coordination account, broadly evolutionary and dynamic accounts in the spirit of Skyrms, and normative-expectations accounts in the spirit of Bicchieri do against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than on the scoring.

Mark your confidence on each desideratum you call non-negotiable, and for each, state what would make you drop it: which case, or which argument, would change your verdict. Say too where you suspect your list has been shaped by what is easy to model rather than by the phenomenon itself — that is where I most expect it to be wrong.
