I'm in a reading group on philosophy of language and last week one of the PhD students leading it gave us a summary of Russell's theory of descriptions that I typed into my notes more or less as he said it. (The reading was Russell's 1905 paper, "On Denoting", I think.) Here's the gloss, roughly verbatim:

"Russell's theory of descriptions is basically his fix for the problem of empty names — sentences that look like they're about things that aren't there. The key move is that a phrase like 'the present King of France' doesn't refer to anything and doesn't even really have a meaning on its own; it's just logical scaffolding. What the sentence 'The present King of France is bald' actually asserts is three things rolled into one: there is a present King of France, there's only one, and he's bald. Since France has no king, the sentence simply comes out false, and we never have to bring in some shadowy nonexistent object for it to be about. Russell then extended the same treatment to proper names: 'Aristotle' is really just shorthand for some description like 'the teacher of Alexander the Great'. And that extension is what let Kripke kill the theory off in the 70s — a name keeps pointing at the same individual across possible worlds, but a description like that could easily have been satisfied by somebody else. Notice also the payoff for negation: 'The present King of France is not bald' comes out true, because the negation just denies the third of the three claims. Oh, and he said the primary/secondary occurrence distinction Russell draws is just Donnellan's referential/attributive distinction under another name. The other big objection was Strawson's, which was the reverse: he said sentences like that are neither true nor false, since there's nothing there for them to be about."
