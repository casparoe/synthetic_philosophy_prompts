I am writing a seminar paper on how a historian can claim knowledge of events she did not witness. My foil is Herodotus' stated practice of recording what people report while withholding commitment to its truth; my case study is Thucydides' methodological preface, which is supposed to be where that practice gets replaced by something stricter. I have come to a stop on one sentence of that preface, and I can no longer tell whether I am mistranslating the Greek or whether the Greek is genuinely trying to do two incompatible things.

The passage, in a nineteenth-century English translation by Richard Crawley (1.22.1, 1.22.2, 1.22.4):

[1.22.1] "With reference to the speeches in this history, some were delivered before the war began, others while it was going on; some I heard myself, others I got from various quarters; it was in all cases difficult to carry them word for word in one's memory, so my habit has been to make the speakers say what was in my opinion demanded of them by the various occasions, of course adhering as closely as possible to the general sense of what they really said."

[1.22.2] "And with reference to the narrative of events, far from permitting myself to derive it from the first source that came to hand, I did not even trust my own impressions, but it rests partly on what I saw myself, partly on what others saw for me, the accuracy of the report being always tried by the most severe and detailed tests possible."

[1.22.4] "The absence of romance in my history will, I fear, detract somewhat from its interest; but if it be judged useful by those inquirers who desire an exact knowledge of the past as an aid to the interpretation of the future, which in the course of human things must resemble if it does not reflect it, I shall be content."

The sentence I cannot resolve is this one: ὡς δ᾽ ἂν ἐδόκουν ἐμοὶ ἕκαστοι περὶ τῶν αἰεὶ παρόντων τὰ δέοντα μάλιστ᾽ εἰπεῖν, ἐχομένῳ ὅτι ἐγγύτατα τῆς ξυμπάσης γνώμης τῶν ἀληθῶς λεχθέντων, οὕτως εἴρηται. Crawley takes τὰ δέοντα μάλιστα as "what was in my opinion demanded of them by the various occasions" and τῆς ξυμπάσης γνώμης τῶν ἀληθῶς λεχθέντων as "the general sense of what they really said." An early modern English version I also looked at has Thucydides delivering the speeches as he judged the speakers would have been most likely to have spoken them, which does not seem to me to be the same claim at all, and I cannot tell which of the two is a paraphrase of the other.

Here is a taxonomy I have built myself of the standards the sentence might be invoking; I suspect this is where I am going wrong, so please treat it as provisional.

A. Verbatim fidelity: reproduce the words actually uttered. Thucydides plainly says this was impossible, so A is out as a standard — but note that he says it was impossible for memory, not that it is undesirable.
B. Gist fidelity: reproduce the content of what was actually said — its claims, arguments and commitments — while freely rearranging words.
C. Occasional fitness: produce what the situation required a speaker of that character and those aims to say, whether or not it was said.
D. Composite fidelity: ξύμπασα is additive, so that what is preserved is not the gist of a single speech but the purport of the whole body of things said on an occasion, by the speaker and by others, compressed into one speech.
E. Dialectical adequacy: produce the strongest available articulation of the position actually taken, so that the reader understands the debate rather than the man.

My tentative reading is that the δέοντα clause governs framing, selection and emphasis only, while the ξυμπάσης γνώμης clause is the binding fidelity constraint, so that Thucydides is claiming B (or D) with C-level freedom in arrangement. But I cannot construct a test that discriminates B or D from C using the text we have, and 1.22.2's "most severe and detailed tests" and 1.22.4's exact knowledge of the past sit badly with any reading that permits C at all.

To force the question, I have been using an invented analogue. A closed-door negotiating session in 1962 leaves no transcript. What survives is a minute of the decisions taken, a contemporaneous diary entry summarising one intervention in a sentence, and four memoirs written between eight and twenty years later whose accounts diverge along lines of later political allegiance. A historian writes the session as a set of opposed speeches, each internally coherent and each responsive to the others, and footnotes only the minute. Under my taxonomy, which standard is she meeting, and is she thereby reporting something or making something? Does calling her practice B rather than C depend on anything a reader could check?

What I would like from you:

1. A close paraphrase of what the Greek sentence commits Thucydides to, word-group by word-group, and a verdict on whether the two old renderings are in fact rival interpretations or the same interpretation in different idioms.
2. An adjudication among A–E, or a better taxonomy if mine conflates distinctions that matter. If the Greek is genuinely undecidable between two of them, say so plainly, and tell me what each resolution would cost my argument.
3. A discriminating test: what feature of the speeches in the rest of the work, or what feature of the sentence, would count as evidence for one reading over another, and would that test be usable by someone who cannot verify anything against an independent record?
4. A reply to my supervisor, who says that asking whether Thucydides' speeches are accurate is anachronistic, that ancient historiography had no norm of verbatim report to violate, and that I am criticising him for failing to do something he never undertook. My worry is that the severe tests of 1.22.2 and the predictive utility claimed in 1.22.4 commit him to more than the genre does. Tell me what I should concede here and what I can hold.

I do not need references or suggestions for further reading. I need the argument: where the interpretive weight actually falls in that sentence, and what I can responsibly claim in a paper about knowing the distant past.
