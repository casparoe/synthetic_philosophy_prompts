I'm putting together the "stakes" week of my grad proseminar on knowledge and action, and I want to stress-test pragmatic encroachment before I stand up in front of twelve people who've read everything twice — I was talking it over with a colleague in the law school who does torts, and she convinced me the view has a structural problem that I can't quite put my finger on, so I want to try it out on someone who actually holds it. Please play a committed pragmatic encroacher: someone who genuinely thinks that how much is practically riding on a question for a subject partly fixes whether that subject knows the answer, and who thinks the contextualist alternative gets the phenomenon right but the mechanism wrong. Be sharp rather than a strawman — give up small points freely, never the core claim, and tell me plainly when you think one of my cases smuggles something in. Stay in character until I say "stop", keep each turn to roughly a paragraph so it feels like an argument at a whiteboard rather than an essay, and if anything in my setup is underspecified just make the most reasonable assumption and say it in character. Also, start each reply with your bottom line — one sentence, no preamble, no restating my question — and only then give me the reasoning. And please don't reach for the literature: no papers, no names, no footnotes. I want your own machinery, not a reading list.

Here's my opening. Take Dana, the engineer who signs off on the load calculations for a footbridge. The liability rule is perverse in a way that I don't think is unusual at all: if she knew the calculations were wrong when she signed, it's a criminal matter and she loses her licence; if she was simply wrong without knowing, it's a small fine and some paperwork. So the cost of being wrong is much, much higher if she knows. Her evidence is decent but not conclusive — put it wherever you like on a scale, say comfortably above the bar when stakes are low and comfortably below it when stakes are high. Now run your theory. If she knows, the stakes are severe, so the bar rises, so she doesn't know; but if she doesn't know, the stakes are trivial, so the bar falls, so she knows; so she doesn't; and so on. There's no resting point — the theory returns no verdict at all. Second case, same engineer, inverted incentive: she's personally on the hook for a bad sign-off only if she did not know, on a should-have-checked theory of negligence, while a sign-off made knowingly with the risk disclosed on the form is protected. Now, at that same middling level of evidence, both verdicts are self-consistent: if she knows, stakes are low, bar stays down, she knows; if she doesn't know, stakes are high, bar goes up, she doesn't know. Two fixed points and nothing in the view that picks between them. My claim is that these aren't exotic — negligence standards, should-have-known clauses, fiduciary duties, willful-blindness doctrine, and about half of professional-regulation law are built out of exactly this dependence, where the stakes are a function of the subject's own epistemic status, and that's precisely the territory pragmatic encroachment was supposed to explain rather than fall silent on. So you owe me one of two things: a fixed-point or reflective-equilibrium clause, plus a principled rule for choosing among competing equilibria when there is more than one; or an argument that the relevant stakes must be measured in some way that is independent of whether the subject knows — in which case I'll argue that the independence move quietly costs you the very bank-and-airport style cases that made the view attractive in the first place. Which one is it, and why should I believe the costs are lower than I think?
