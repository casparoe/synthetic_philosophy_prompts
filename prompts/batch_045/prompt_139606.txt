Hey — I'm on a small platform team and we keep having the same dumb argument about which of the things we're building actually count as "agents" as opposed to just tools with extra steps. Marketing of course wants to call everything an agent. I realized I have pretty firm gut feelings about specific systems, and they don't track the stakes at all, so I'm hoping you can dig out the principle I'm implicitly using — or tell me it's a mess and what a cleaner one would cost me.

Here's my list, with how I actually feel (I've tried to make it a fair spread, and I've left out anything where I think I'd just be parroting a headline):

1. A chess engine that ranks moves and I click the one I want. Tool, obviously fine.
2. An LLM answering a question while calling a calculator, running some Python, and fetching a page mid-answer. Tool, fine — even though it's literally doing things in the world, it doesn't feel agent-y to me at all.
3. Autocomplete that finishes whole functions, or whole paragraphs in my voice, as I type. Tool, fine.
4. A coding agent handed a ticket, working in a scratch branch for three hours, running the test suite over and over, opening a PR at the end; I read the diff before anything touches main. This one feels like an agent, and I'm fine with it.
5. The same coding agent, but it merges to main when CI is green and I find out from the changelog. Not fine — and it's not just "bugs might slip through", because I'd also be uneasy if I was confident the code was good. Something about it having acted rather than proposed is what bugs me.
6. A trading bot executing small trades inside a written mandate (position limits, asset list), nobody approves individual trades, humans see daily P&L. Fine. Doesn't bother me even slightly, though real money moves and no human is in the loop.
7. An assistant that replies to routine emails in my name — "got it", "Thursday works", that kind of thing — and shows me a log at the end of the day. Not fine. Makes me uncomfortable in a way #6 doesn't, and I can't pretend it's about the size of the consequences, because obviously it isn't.
8. A support bot that can issue refunds up to a hundred bucks and email the customer about it, with humans auditing a random 5% afterwards. Not fine, and I notice it's the same shape as #7.
9. A spam filter that silently deletes mail. Fine, obviously — even though deleting is about as hard to take back as sending.
10. An NPC or dungeon master in a game that lies to the player. Fine, obviously.

The principle I keep half-forming is something like an "unsend" test: a tool produces proposals that only bind when a human takes them up, an agent produces things that are already out in the world and cost something to take back. But that test gets #6 and #9 wrong by my own lights, so it can't be the whole thing. I also tried "could a competent user have predicted the output in advance" and that one dies immediately on #4. My other thought is that #6 is fine because the bot isn't speaking for anyone, while #7 and #8 are me being represented by something that isn't me — but I don't know how to state that without it collapsing into "acting in someone's name", which sounds too narrow to be the real line.

So: give me the principle, or two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it, with the clause-by-clause note of which of my cases motivates each clause — and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop (I'd rather give up one verdict than carry a principle with an epicycle glued on just for #9). Please also run it on some cases I haven't listed: an agent that hires and pays human freelancers for microtasks inside a budget I set, an AI tutor that decides on its own whether a student is ready to move to the next topic, an assistant that bids for me in an online auction up to a limit, and one that files my taxes. And settle, if you can, the two I go back and forth on: a voice agent that phones the dentist and the garage to book and move appointments from a script and hands the call to me the moment anything off-script happens, and an assistant that declines meeting invitations using rules I wrote down once. A small table at the end with your tool/agent call and your fine/not-fine call for each of my cases would be great, and please finish with a last line of exactly this form:

Verdict: [your single best principle, in one sentence]
