I'm a senior taking an elective called Justice and Public Policy, and instead of a paper my group has to present a case to the class next week — twelve minutes, no slides, and we're slotted right after lunch, so it has to grab people immediately. The unit is on the question "equality of what?" We've been given three answers to work with: one says a fair distribution equalizes how well people's lives actually go for them, taking each person's own preferences and experiences as the measure (I'll call it the welfare view); one says it equalizes the external things people have to work with, while holding people responsible for the tastes and ambitions they chose to form (the resources view); and one says it equalizes what people are genuinely able to do and be — the real freedoms they have, not just their holdings or how satisfied they feel (the capabilities view). We've already done the stock examples, the person with a cultivated taste for something absurdly expensive and the person who needs equipment to get around, and my teacher says those are played out: everyone in the room now knows which lever to pull for each view, and the discussion just replays the same three moves about choice, adaptation, and conversion. So she wants a case that our classmates can't diagnose from the surface.

That's what I need from you. Please construct one short case — a single situation with a fixed set of people and one thing being divided — meeting all of the following. First, three or four people, and the thing being divided should not be cash and should not be evenly divisible, so that "split it equally" isn't available as a way out. Second, the three views must yield genuinely different recommendations about who gets how much, not three differently worded descriptions of the same distribution. Third, the description should be view-opaque: a classmate who hasn't thought carefully shouldn't be able to tell which recommendation comes from which view, so please don't build it around the usual tell-tale figures — no cultivated expensive taste, no disability, no one who is miserable and doesn't realize it. Fourth, at least one person's preferences should have been shaped by their expectations about how the distribution would be made: they adjusted what they want, or trained themselves to enjoy what they could get, partly in light of the rule they expected to face. I want the views to have to take a stand on what to do with that adjustment, not merely notice it. Fifth, at least one person's welfare should be partly comparative, so that part of what makes their life go well is how their share stands relative to the others — which means the welfare view can't just tally satisfactions person by person in isolation. Sixth, keep the case clean: everyone in it is honest and well informed about their own good, and there are no uncertainties or measurement problems to hide behind, so whatever disagreement remains is purely about which currency is the right one.

After the case, give me three things. (a) What each of the three views would say about the distribution, written in the same plain terms and at roughly the same length, so my classmates can vote on them without spotting which theory is speaking. (b) For each of the three, the objection I should expect from the room and the reply that actually works, as opposed to the reply that merely sounds good — my teacher is going to press us on exactly that difference. (c) An account of the single feature of the case that all three views are reacting to differently, the hinge that makes them diverge, with each divergence traced back to what that view takes the point of equality to be. Please don't name or cite any philosophers or texts; I need the case and the reasoning, not references. End your answer with a final line of exactly the form \answer{...} containing a phrase of at most twelve words naming that hinge.
