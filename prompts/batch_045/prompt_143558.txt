Hey — I've been chewing on Pascal's stuff about justice and force for a while now and I feel like I keep losing track of a distinction, so I want you to help me untangle it rather than give me an overview of it.

Here's the material as I understand it (correct me if I have it wrong, but please don't go hunting for fragment or section numbers, I really don't care about that part). The core move: it's fitting that the just should be obeyed and it's necessary that the strongest should be obeyed; justice that can't back itself up is useless, and force that admits it's only force is just tyranny; and since we couldn't figure out how to give justice any teeth, we ended up calling whatever has teeth just. He pairs this with a claim that there is something like a real natural justice or natural law, but that custom becomes a kind of second nature that buries the first one, so nobody can any longer tell which parts of their local law are natural and which are just habit. He also thinks the whole arrangement only works if it stays hidden — force has to show up dressed as justice, and it matters that nobody lets on. And he runs the same move on property: people tell themselves a story about titles and original grants rather than admitting that holdings trace back to whoever got there first plus whatever law happens to exist.

I've been playing with three readings of what he's actually claiming, and I can't tell whether they're really different:

- The labeling reading: the word just, as it actually functions in a Pascalian society, doesn't pick out anything over and above whatever force has settled on. It's an honorific that winners get to hand out, and its job is to make compliance feel like duty. On this reading his famous line is a semantic claim, not just a causal one.
- The epistemic reading: real justice is out there and the word really does refer to it, but custom has wrecked our ability to tell what it requires, and open dispute about it is destructive, so force gets pressed into service as a decision procedure — a coordination point everyone can see. Force is a proxy, not a constituent.
- The derivation reading: there's no duty to obey the strongest as such, but there is a genuine and prior duty not to throw everything into dispute and disorder, and the duty to comply with whoever can actually settle a question falls out of that. So the obligation is inherited rather than manufactured — you really owe it, just not to the person, and just is a slight mislabel for what is really a peace-keeping duty.

The thing that got me stuck: the third reading looks like it just turns Pascal into Hobbes with better prose. Order beats justice, the rest is decoration. The distinction I've been using to save him is the hiddenness requirement, which I think comes in two flavors that people run together:

a. Doxastic: the machine needs people to actually believe the justice story. Kill the belief and compliance goes with it.
b. Discursive: what matters is that the justice story is what gets said in public — in law, edicts, official speech — no matter what people privately know. Common knowledge is fine on this version; what breaks things is avowal.

If it's the second one, Pascal isn't doing psychology at all. He's making a claim about the vocabulary that public power is required to speak in, which puts him somewhere much stranger, closer to a public-justification view than to a debunking genealogy. It also seems to fit better with the part in the discourse addressed to the young nobleman where he's told not to disabuse the people about what their deference is really based on: the instruction is about what to say, not about what to believe.

So here's what I actually want. Explain how the three readings differ in a way that makes clear what turns on the difference, then commit to one and defend it — don't hand the options back to me as a list. Tell me how much work the hiddenness requirement is really doing versus how much I'm reading into it, and whether Hobbes has his own version of it and I'm inventing a difference. And a narrower thing I can't get straight: in the mouth of an ordinary person in a Pascalian society, what does just refer to — nothing at all, the real property via a broken causal mechanism, or the real property plus a false story about why it binds? Those feel like three different answers and they don't line up cleanly with the three readings above.

Last bit, which I think might be the most interesting one: is the account self-undermining in a way Pascal would have to own? Once you've read it you know, and knowing looks like it should dissolve either the belief (on the doxastic version) or at least the ability to say it with a straight face (on the discursive one). He seems weirdly fine with that. Does that push him toward the discursive reading, or does he have a separate reply — something like the disorder coming from contesting power rather than from knowing the truth about it?
