I got into this at a barbeque with my brother-in-law, who's into the "give effectively" crowd, and I've been going in circles on it for a week. Here's his argument, written out as cleanly as I can manage:

(1) If a creature can suffer, that suffering matters, and it matters just as much as the same amount of suffering in anyone else — which species you belong to doesn't get a vote.

(2) When we're not sure whether a creature can suffer, we should still go by expected value: a 1% chance of some amount of suffering is a hundredth as urgent as being certain of that amount.

(3) There are absurdly many insects. I half remember a figure with eighteen zeroes in it, and something like millions of insects for every human being. And if they can suffer at all, the typical wild insect's life is short and ends badly: starved, eaten alive from the inside by a wasp larva, frozen solid.

(4) So even at 1% odds that insects are sentient, the expected amount of insect suffering in a single year dwarfs everything humans do to chickens and pigs put together. Give the insects a millions-to-one head start and then knock 99% off for the uncertainty, and they still win by a mile.

(5) So insect suffering belongs near the very top of any decent person's to-do list — far above the things people actually spend money trying to fix.

(6) And if you follow it through: a meadow holds vastly more insects than a parking lot of the same size. So converting wild land into something sterile looks like it reduces suffering, and a world with fewer meadows and more asphalt would be, other things equal, a better world. Also, the same move works one level down. Mites, springtails, nematodes — there are even more of those, so you can set the odds at 0.001% and they swamp the insects too. You can keep playing that game with worse and worse odds forever, and the crowd always wins, because there's always a bigger crowd.

I can't accept (6), but I can't find the bad step either. (1) and (2) look like the load-bearing ones, and if I have to drop something I'd much rather drop (2) than (1). So: is this thing valid and I just have to swallow the conclusion, or is there a switch somewhere between (2) and (6)? Tell me which premise you'd give up and what giving it up costs me — does it cost me the ability to take low-probability-but-huge risks seriously at all?

One more thing I can't sort out. Someone told me the 1% in (4) isn't really a per-insect probability: either insects as a group can suffer or they can't, so it's one shared bet rather than a million separate 1% bets. I couldn't tell whether that was a deep point or a dodge. Does it change anything?
