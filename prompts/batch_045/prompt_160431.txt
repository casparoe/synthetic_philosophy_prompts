I'm a senior at a small high school, and over winter break I got into an argument in our philosophy club that I haven't been able to let go of. I ended up defending a broadly Humean line about the laws of nature: there is nothing in the world over and above the totality of particular events — no primitive relation of necessitation, no relation of one state of affairs producing or governing another — and what we call a law is just whatever earns a place in the simplest yet most informative overall description of that totality. I like the view because it makes laws answerable to what actually happens, and because it explains why we should expect there to be findable laws at all. What I want to know is how much I'm risking by holding it.

So: what would count against this view? I don't just want the standard complaint that mere regularities can't govern — I want to know what would have to be true for that complaint to bite, and which version of it the view's own defenders concede as counting against them rather than wave off as a brute intuition they're happy to explain away. Three places where I feel most exposed. First, explanation: on my account a law looks like a summary of its instances, so it seems able at best to unify them and not to explain them; is there a kind of explanation I'm thereby giving up, and is it one we actually seem to have? Second, a case I made up that I can't get a stable verdict on: a universe containing exactly one event of a certain kind — say, one particle whose entire trajectory is captured by an extremely elegant equation that nothing else in that universe ever satisfies again. The simplicity-versus-informativeness trade-off in my account seems to have no principled way to say whether that equation is a law of that universe or a coincidence; whichever verdict it gives, tell me why the verdict isn't ad hoc, and tell me whether the case is even coherent. Third, chance: our own best description of the world appears to be probabilistic, and I keep worrying about a history in which some process looks chancy with a stable chance early on, but the total run of outcomes over all of time is skewed enough that the best description of the whole of history assigns that process a very different chance, or none at all — so that what the chance was at the time seems to depend on, and get revised by, events that hadn't happened yet. Is that a genuine cost, or just an oddity I should learn to live with?

Please rank what you find from fatal to merely awkward, say which of it a committed Humean would themselves concede as counting against the view, and be honest with me about where I've insulated myself. My friend's accusation is that I've defined laws as whatever the best description says and then left "best" loose enough that no possible history could refute me — that the view survives any evidence by quietly reweighting simplicity against informativeness. Is that fair, and if it is, is it a defect in a view of this kind, or just what a metaphysical claim about what laws are ought to look like? For what it's worth, the thing I'd personally find most persuasive is a case where the view can't even state a distinction we plainly make: I care much less about governing as a metaphor than about whether I lose the difference between a law and an accident.
