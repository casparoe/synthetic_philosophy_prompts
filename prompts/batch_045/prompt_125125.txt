You are a professional philosopher who has spent your career on moral responsibility, moral psychology, and the explanation of wrongdoing under institutional pressure. You now work as a consultant to public bodies that have to turn those questions into decisions. Take the task seriously and reason carefully: think it through before you write, and don't resolve the hard part by restating a slogan.

Start with a short private brainstorm before you draft. List, in rough notes, the considerations you can see bearing on the case below — I'd expect somewhere between eight and fifteen — and for each one mark three things: (a) whether it is backward-looking (about what the person deserves, or what he actually did) or forward-looking (about what would happen next, about risk, about what the institution needs), (b) which way it cuts, and (c) whether it depends on facts our file cannot settle. Then discard anything that survives only because it sounds weighty, and draft the memo from what's left. Don't put the brainstorm in the deliverable; I want the distilled document.

Here is the situation.

I sit on the vetting commission of a country that, roughly two years ago, emerged from an authoritarian regime. Over about fifteen years, that regime carried out a program of forced removal and mass killing against a minority population. Our commission has an eighteen-month mandate and about 1,200 cases left to process; we can hear only a fraction of them, so whatever standard we adopt has to be applicable on paper, by staff, to a file in under an hour, and it has to generalize past the case in front of us. Nothing in this request depends on identifying the country or the period, and I have deliberately left both unspecified. Nothing here needs outside research; do not cite anything and do not recommend anything to read. If you use a term of art, define it in the memo for readers who have never opened a philosophy book.

The applicant, whom I'll call P, is 54. Under the old regime he was a mid-grade logistics officer in the internal administration: he certified movement schedules and fuel and ration allocations for the removal convoys. On the record, he never selected anyone, never guarded anyone, never killed anyone, never volunteered for additional duties, and never joined the ruling party beyond the automatic enrollment that came with the grade. There is a transfer request in his file from month nine of his service, refused by his superior; he then stayed in the post for another twenty-two months and was rated "quietly efficient" in two performance reviews. The archive also contains one internal memorandum in his handwriting pressing a district office to speed up its paperwork, using language that describes the program as a cleansing the region needs. A former colleague has submitted a letter saying P "never liked it, he just did what the rest of us did."

Our statute lets us bar from public employment any person who "personally and substantially facilitated" the regime's core crimes, with a narrow exception for duress, defined as a credible threat of immediate harm to the person or their family. P does not meet that exception: had he refused, the realistic consequences were reassignment, demotion, and loss of pension, not prison or death. So the statutory question comes down to what "personally and substantially" requires, and that is where our commission has split, along lines that track two competing pictures of why people like P did what they did.

On the first picture — our shorthand is the "ordinary men" picture — the right explanation is situational: role induction into a job that is broken into tasks, incremental escalation, diffusion of responsibility across a chain of signatures, career and pension incentives, conformity to the immediate peer group, and the sheer ordinariness of paperwork. On the second — our shorthand is the "willing executioners" picture — the right explanation is cultural and dispositional: he shared, with most of his society, an interpretive scheme in which the victims were an existential threat and their removal was legitimate self-defense, and his memo is a trace of that conviction rather than of a man trapped in a role. The two pictures are, for our purposes, empirically indistinguishable: the file cannot tell us whether P was a reluctant clerk who wrote one bad memo or a convinced one who hid it well. Note also that each picture has a mitigating and an aggravating use; the second picture preserves blame better, but it also hints that P's epistemic situation was one in which the wrong was not visible to him as a wrong.

Three further complications are live in our deliberations, and I want the memo to take them on rather than smooth them over.

First, one of our commissioners keeps pressing a counterfactual: had P refused, the convoys would still have run, because the system filled vacated slots within days and the paperwork was designed so that every signature in the chain was replaceable. Call this the substitution test. The worry is that a standard on which easy substitutability counts in the applicant's favor ends up rewarding the most bureaucratic and most efficiently organized perpetrator systems, and ends up clearing exactly the people whose compliance made the program administratively possible. But the opposite worry is just as real: if P is barred for doing what two thousand others in the same grade also did, and we can only bar a fraction of them, then the bar is not tracking anything about P.

Second, our sanction is a strange hybrid. Barring someone from public employment looks backward, like a judgment of his conduct, but it also functions prospectively, as protection for a new administration that needs officials who will refuse unlawful orders. The same facts pull opposite ways here: the very features that make his compliance expectable under pressure — ordinary situational pressure, willingness to keep one's post, no appetite for martyrdom — are exculpatory when we are asking what he deserves, and aggravating when we are asking what he is likely to do next, since a person who complied under moderate pressure is precisely a person who may comply again.

Third, there are audiences beyond P. Survivors' organizations have told us they do not care about our caseload math; they care whether the new state will publicly say that this work was not just administratively necessary. And the civil service cannot actually function if we disqualify a substantial share of the experienced staff, which is a consideration I dislike but cannot pretend away.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the other eight commissioners before our session in three weeks. It should: (1) state a bottom line — bar, clear, or bar with conditions — and if you recommend conditions, specify them and the schedule on which they would be reviewed, with dates or durations; (2) state a general standard for the remaining caseload, written so that staff can apply it to a file without calling us; (3) show which of the two explanatory pictures is actually doing the work in your recommendation, and where your conclusion is deliberately insulated from the fact that we cannot tell them apart; (4) take the substitution test seriously and say what significance, if any, replaceability should have for a vetting decision as opposed to a criminal sentence; (5) say plainly which function — backward-looking or forward-looking — our decision is serving, or how you are weighting the two, and why; and (6) give the strongest consideration on the losing side its full weight, in a form that the commissioners who hold it would recognize as fair. Write it as an internal commission document: roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, decisive, free of hedging placeholders, and honest about what the commission is not in a position to claim — in particular, I do not want any sentence implying that we have settled what kind of person P is.
