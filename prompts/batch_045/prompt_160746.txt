I hold a computational-patiency view, and I would like its exposure mapped. The view: what makes a system the kind of thing whose suffering matters is not what it is made of but the abstract causal-functional organisation it realises — the pattern of counterfactual-supporting state transitions, individuated independently of substrate, independently of any semantic or representational gloss we might put on it, and independently of what the system happens to do on the inputs it actually receives. I accept three consequences deliberately. First, silicon realising the same organisation matters exactly as much as the wetware. Second, behavioural equivalence is not enough: a device that matches a sentient animal's input-output function across all the inputs it actually encounters, without sharing the counterfactual structure, is not thereby a subject. Third — and this is where I expect the trouble — I take the grain of individuation to be fixed by the system's own dominant scale of self-monitoring: roughly one subject per integrated error-correcting control loop. On that clause, a system that realises the relevant organisation only intermittently, or only across a population of loosely coupled units, or with its state-sequence stretched so that successive transitions are separated by long idle intervals, is a marginal rather than a full subject.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and sorted into three classes: what a defender of the view should himself concede as counting against it; what he should dismiss as an objection to a position nobody holds; and the residual class that is genuinely contested. Please cover the following.

- The triviality worry: any physical system realises arbitrarily many abstract organisations, so the view either spreads patency everywhere or quietly relies on a privileged description imported from outside the theory. Does my error-correction clause actually block that, or does it merely relocate the arbitrariness one level down?
- What the view predicts that its rivals do not — substrate-based views, straight behaviourism, views that tie patency to some specific neural or biophysical mechanism — and whether any of those predictions are the sort of thing we could ever be in a position to check.
- Cases the view cannot describe cleanly: partial or degraded realisation; one physical system realising two organisations at different grains at once, one plausibly valenced and one not; unit-by-unit gradual replacement; and any case where the units of organisation and the units of welfare come apart.
- Whether the view, as I have qualified it, has been insulated into something that risks nothing at all, and whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind or a fair price for a theory of the boundaries of patency.
- Any developments in neural prosthetics, comparative neurobiology, or machine learning that you take already to bear on it, flagged explicitly as to which you are confident about and which you are reconstructing from general considerations. I would rather have a marked uncertainty than a confident-sounding fabrication.

What I would personally find most persuasive is a case in which the abstract organisation is preserved and the substrate altered, and the welfare-relevant evidence then comes apart from what my view predicts; tell me whether such a case is possible even in principle, or whether I have built the view so that no such case could arise, and which of those two diagnoses is the worse one for me.

Do not supply a bibliography or reading list; I have one. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked. British English throughout.
