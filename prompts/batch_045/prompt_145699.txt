I got pulled into an argument in a group chat a few weeks ago and I still can't tell who's supposed to be embarrassed here. Some context: I give about 3% of my income to a charity a friend recommended, and someone else in the chat said that if I actually believed my own reasons I should be giving way more, because of what he called astronomical stakes. Then he wrote out an argument, which I'll try to reproduce fair. He calls the conclusion "expected value fanaticism," says it's uncomfortable, and says you should just bite the bullet.

(1) When you're choosing between actions, rationality requires ranking them by expected value -- the probability-weighted sum of how good each outcome is -- whenever the numbers involved are finite and well-defined. (2) How good an outcome is doesn't top out: saving 10^30 lives is more than a trillion times better than saving 10^18, which is more than a trillion times better than saving 10^6, and so on with no ceiling. He phrased it as "value is unbounded and roughly linear in lives saved, at least once the numbers get big." (3) If value is unbounded, then for any probability p greater than zero, no matter how tiny, and any finite good g, no matter how big, there's some number of lives N large enough that a p chance of saving N lives beats g for sure. (4) You can't wriggle out by setting p to zero for claims that sound crazy, because there's no principled number to put there instead: if a guy tells you he's running 10^40 simulated people who will be tortured for a year unless you send him fifty dollars, your credence in that should be small but not zero, and given (3), "small but not zero" is not small enough. (5) And the fact that a story sounds ridiculous isn't evidence on its own, since he can always make the number bigger until it outweighs however implausible the story sounds. (6) So a rational person sends the fifty dollars. And more generally, every dollar spent on an ordinary well-evidenced thing -- bednets, whatever -- is a dollar not spent on some long shot with a bigger number behind it, so a rational person puts everything into long shots and nothing into the things we actually have evidence for.

I said that can't be right and he said I was refusing to follow principles I already accept. But what actually bugs me is that I think his argument proves more than even he wants. If (4) and (5) are right, then for any action I might take there's also some crazy story on which that action causes an astronomical amount of suffering -- maybe the simulation runs fine if I don't pay and collapses if I do. Then every option has astronomically huge expected value in both directions, the comparison stops being a well-defined number at all, and expected value doesn't tell me to pay the mugger, it just goes silent, or tells me everything is equally good and equally bad. That's a worse ending than the one he was aiming for, and I can't locate which step does it.

The bits I'd most hate to give up are (1) and (2). The "multiply the probability by the value and add it up" thing is what got me giving money in the first place, and "saving more lives is better" is the one part I'm confident about. So I keep hoping the problem is in (4) or (5), but I can't say what's wrong with either: "there's no principled cutoff" sounds right to me, and "you can always make the number bigger" seems obviously true. Where does this actually go wrong? If the honest answer is that I have to drop (1) or (2), what does that cost me, and is there any version of "just use expected value" that survives?
