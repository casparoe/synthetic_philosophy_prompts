Something I keep running into. Any time a bureaucracy or a company automates a decision that lands badly on an individual person -- cut off a benefit, flag a claim as probably fraudulent, score someones loan or their risk of reoffending -- the rule that comes back is that a human has to be in the loop, and the guidelines all call it meaningful human oversight. But the way these things actually get built, the human sees the machine's recommendation on a screen, has a couple of minutes, and is expected to sign off; in the ones Ive read about, overrides are in the low single digits and a reviewer who deviates too often gets pulled aside for a chat. We're also completely fine letting spam filters and ad targeting run with nobody watching, even though they touch way more people.

What does that rule actually presuppose? It can't just be accuracy -- the case where we insist hardest is exactly the one where the machine beats a tired person, and the workflow is deliberately built so that deferring is the rational move. So what's really being protected: a place for blame to land, or the idea that someone is owed a decision made by a person? Pull it apart for me -- what does the practice strictly require, what does it only gesture at, and do those hang together given that the human usually has no real power to disagree and never meets the person affected? Is this still work in whatever sense matters, or has the job quietly turned into absorbing blame, and is that function worth keeping? If we dropped the requirement, what would we actually lose?
