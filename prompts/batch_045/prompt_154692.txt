The setting: I'm in a graduate reading group on Moore's paradox — we started from Moore's original remark about having gone to the pictures and not believing it, and have been working through why the present-tense first-person conjunction is absurd while the past-tense, third-person and negated versions are not. Last week a visiting speaker got impatient with a question of mine and closed the discussion with the line "belief aims at truth — that's really all Moore's paradox amounts to." He was using it to do a specific piece of work: to rule out, in advance, a whole family of *non-normative* explanations of the absurdity. I had been pressing the suggestion that the oddness might be pragmatic rather than normative — an effect of what one can felicitously avow, or an artefact of the way we settle the question "do you believe P?" by settling the question "P?", or even a limitation on what a single cognitive system can represent about itself — and he took the slogan to show that any such explanation is beside the point. I suspect the slogan is doing far less work than he thinks, but I can't get clear on what it would have to mean in order to do the work he wants.

To help, here is a battery of cases I've been collecting, which any proposed precisification has to give a verdict on, and which I suspect the slogan handles unevenly: (i) the drugged perceiver, who can see and hear rain, knows she was given a hallucinogen twenty minutes ago, and says "It's raining, but I don't believe it" — this strikes me as markedly less absurd than the standard case, and perhaps not absurd at all; (ii) the diachronic version, "It rained all through the night, but I never believed it," said the following morning about a period of fever; (iii) the plural version, "It's raining, but we don't believe it," compared with "It's raining, but the department doesn't believe it," which is perfectly fine; (iv) the embedded version, "If it's raining and I don't believe it, the seedlings will get watered anyway," which seems unproblematic; and (v) the cross-attitude neighbours — "It's raining, but I don't want it to be," "It's raining, but I refuse to believe it," "It's raining, but I don't know it."
