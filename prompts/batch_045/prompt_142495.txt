hey — I'm a section leader for my prof's intro philosophy of mind course (18 students, one 75-minute section, normal classroom: whiteboard, movable chairs, thats it). It's just me running it, no co-facilitator, so anything needing two leaders or a computer lab is out. Last week we did the careenium/simmballs bit: the idea being there's this hypothetical brain goo that literally has the property of caring (or of being the kind of stuff that has experiences), and then there are "simmballs" which reproduce exactly what a blob of careenium does — same inputs in, same outputs out, same place in the whole causal network — but in a way that only imitates the caring rather than doing it. And the question the reading sort of leaves hanging is whether the symbol-level description is actually pushing the physical substrate around, or whether all the real causal work is done by the goo and the description is just a description riding along on top.

I want an activity where they *feel* that pull rather than hear it. Ideally no mini-lecture at the top, and nobody (including me) says "functionalism" or "substrate" out loud until the last ten minutes — they should run into the disagreement themselves and only then get the vocabulary for it. What I want them to come away seeing is something like: the fight isn't really stuff-vs-pattern, it's about whether the pattern is what does the causal work or whether it merely tracks work that the stuff is doing.

A few cases I'd like baked into the rules somewhere, though you can rework them if you've got better ones: (1) the swap done neuron-by-neuron, one at a time, vs (2) the swap done in one go, where a whole region is pulled out and replaced by a single black box guaranteed to have the same input-output function — same total amount of goo removed either way, but my hunch is they'll sail through (1) and balk at (2), which would show they're sensitive to the *grain* of the replacement rather than to how much organic stuff is left. (3) the "careenium paste" case: exactly as much careenium as a healthy brain, but ground up and spread evenly so it isn't doing any organized work at all — if they say that subject doesn't care, they've already conceded that organization matters and not just the quantity of the right stuff. And (4) a distinction I want them to stumble on: a simmball that's only been *tested* to match careenium on every input that's actually come up so far, versus one that matches on all physically possible inputs because it implements the same function — if they treat these differently, the thing doing the work in their judgment is counterfactual robustness, not substrate.

So: rules, materials, a timeline, and a payoff scheme that uses points rather than real money (I can offer a small stake, like candy or a bonus participation credit). Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto the parts of the idea — including whatever discovery the activity would actually produce if my hunch about grain-sensitivity is wrong. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I've got a bunch of CS and engineering majors who'll say "obviously it's just the function, who cares what it's made of", at least one person who'll dig in on the goo, and probably someone who refuses to play because the premise is silly) and how to handle each. And be honest about which part of the question the activity just can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself in the last ten minutes.
