Someone on my team ends every design debate with "the best is the enemy of the good" — last week he used it to argue we should ship option B without scoring it against the other two candidates, and also that we dont need to agree up front on what "good" even means. I think the line is doing two different jobs and maybe neither. Can you seperate out the precise claims that sentence could be making and tell me which hold up? The ones I can half-formulate: (i) plain optimal stopping — search costs something, so past some point looking harder loses in expectation; (ii) there is no unique best at all, because the options are multidimensional and there's no single scale to rank them on, so a threshold on what matters is the only coherent target; (iii) the strong one I actually suspect him of meaning — when your model of the options is misspecified, maximizing against that model can leave you worse off in reality than someone who just clears a bar, so satisficing isn't a concession to being computationally limited at all but the right answer under severe uncertainty; (iv) a treadmill reading — aiming at the best keeps ratcheting your aspiration level upward, so you end up worse off in satisfaction terms even as outcomes improve. Add any readings I've missed, especially ones from the bounded-rationality literature that aren't reducible to these. For each one: is it trivial, substantive and defensible, or just false? And for each: does it actually support his two moves in the meeting — skipping the comparison, and skipping the definition of "good" — or does it quietly depend on a premise nobody checked, like that we've already searched enough, or that the bar is calibrated rather than just inherited from whoever spoke last? Give it to me as a list I can take into the next meeting.
