I direct the variety development programme at a mid-sized seed company, and I need an allocation procedure my team can run every cycle, not a one-off recommendation. Roughly sixty candidate lines are screened each year; we advance six to ten of them to Stage 2 field trials; there is one main decision cycle in the spring and a smaller top-up round in the autumn. The stakes are not abstract: our base is currently £18m, and if it falls below £12m corporate places the programme in a restructuring review — of the three programmes in this company's history that hit that trigger, two were closed within eighteen months, and a closure costs about fifty-five jobs. My team is two breeders and one analyst with no formal training in decision theory, so anything I hand them has to run in a day on a spreadsheet. Everything above a certain size goes to the firm's investment committee, which includes a CFO who thinks in expected value and internal rate of return and who will ask, immediately, why we are not simply funding the highest-expected-value projects in descending order until the money runs out.

The mechanics, so you don't have to guess at them:

- There are no external top-ups. The programme lives on what its releases earn. Uncommitted capital carries over between years and earns roughly zero in real terms, so idle money is not compounding for us even though it is still the firm's money.
- A Stage 2 advancement costs between £0.6m and £2.4m, spread over three years. A line that is dropped is a total loss of that spend. A line that becomes a commercial variety returns royalties over the following five years; historically 60% of successes have come back at three to six times the spend, and a handful at ten to forty times, which is what makes the arithmetic mean of the whole distribution look so much better than the typical outcome.
- Funding is close to lumpy. At about 70% of the full Stage 2 budget the historical success rate for a class is roughly 80% of its full-funding rate and the payoff scales roughly proportionally; below 70% it collapses, because under-resourced trials produce data nobody can interpret. So the practical choice per project is usually zero, 70%, or 100%.
- The projects are not independent. A bad season at the trial network — drought, or a disease that moves through the sites — has historically hit about one year in eight, and in those years the cohort success rate has run near 8% against a baseline near 35%. Failure is common across a cohort in exactly the years that hurt most.
- They are not independent in a second way either. Our stations and staff can carry about eight concurrent Stage 2 projects at full quality; past that, our own rough estimate is that per-project success probability falls by something like 10% relative for each project above eight, and falls hardest for the weakest projects in the cohort.
- Our probability estimates are thin and probably optimistic. They come from three to eight analogue lines per project, and the deeper reference class consists of advancement decisions *we ourselves chose to make*, which means it is selected by our own past enthusiasm. We have two years of scored forecasts against outcomes and nothing before that, so I do not know whether our breeders are calibrated, and I suspect they are not.
- No borrowing and no external co-investment. Corporate also assigns us one mandated project per cycle (resistance-management and regulatory work) with a negative expected return, which we cannot decline.

Here is what I think I know, and I would rather be corrected than flattered: sizing each commitment to maximise the expected logarithm of the bankroll gives the largest long-run growth rate, and this differs from maximising expected value because the expected value of a high-variance strategy is carried by rare enormous outcomes that a single entity like ours mostly does not live to collect. I take it this is why the log rule looks timid to a CFO and why the timidity is not a mistake. I am much less sure how much of that carries over to us, given that our horizon is not infinite, our bets are lumpy and correlated, our probabilities are guesses, and our bankroll is a budget line inside a firm that can absorb variance we cannot.

What I want from you:

1. A procedure, in the order my team would actually run it: the questions to ask, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure returns a hard no, and where it stops and hands the decision to a person or to the committee because the question is one of governance or values rather than of fact. It must include how to set the *number* of bets for a cycle, how to size each one given that they interact and share a common shock, how to haircut for thin and self-selected probability estimates with an actual rule rather than an instruction to be cautious, how to treat the mandated project, and a barrier test that asks what happens to the base if this cohort fails together.
2. An honest paragraph I can put in front of the CFO answering "why not just take the highest-expected-value projects first", together with your own verdict on how much of that paragraph is a real argument and how much is a change of framing.
3. The procedure run on three cases, with the arithmetic shown in whatever units you think are the right ones.

Case A — the flagship long-shot. A novel trait cross championed by the breeder who founded the programme: £2.2m at full funding, a claimed forty-times upside, estimated success probability of 8% from its champion and between 1.5% and 3% from our analyst using the wider reference class. It is indivisible at anything like the size the sizing rule would want. I should tell you that I have been tempted to carve out a small "exploration" sub-budget — funded from the base, but not counted as part of the compounding bankroll — precisely so that bets like this do not have to pass the sizing test. Tell me whether that move is coherent or whether it is just a way of hiding a bad bet from the criterion, and if it is coherent, what discipline it needs: how large it may be, how it is replenished, who is allowed to spend it, and what happens when the exploration book is empty and the flagship project is still there.

Case B — the dull one. An elite backcross for disease resistance: £1.9m at full funding, scored base rate 0.6 across fourteen analogues, realised multiples on success between two and three-and-a-half times with a median near 2.4x. The analyst points out that at 70% funding the historical success rate for this class is about 0.5 with the payoff scaling roughly proportionally, that 70% funding has a slightly higher expected multiple per pound spent, and that it has a materially lower chance of any success at all. What does the procedure say, and does the answer change if two projects of this class are available this cycle and the base cannot comfortably carry both at full funding?

Case C — the near-duplicates. Two breeders each propose a project introgressing the same resistance gene into different backgrounds. The analyst scores them independently at 0.4 each and wants to fund both. They would very likely fail together in a network year, and if both succeeded they would cannibalise each other commercially, so the second success is worth far less than the first. I want to see how the procedure handles payoffs that are not additive across bets.

Finally, the part I care most about. Tell me what the procedure cannot do, and be explicit about what would change your mind about the procedure itself. As I see it, the two load-bearing assumptions are that our capital really does behave like a multiplicative bankroll behind a near-absorbing barrier, and that our probability estimates mean anything once they have been shrunk. Tell me which one is doing more of the work in the recommendation you are making, what observation over the next two years would tell me it is false, and what I could cheaply measure to find out. Mark the places where you are extrapolating past what the growth-rate argument strictly establishes — in particular what survives, and what does not, once the horizon is a corporate review cycle rather than infinity and there is no recapitalisation after a bad year. And flag the ways you would expect this procedure to misfire that we would not notice until they had already cost us money. Please do not give me citations, links, or a reading list; I have a librarian for that. I want the reasoning and its breaking points.
