I write long-form pieces on science and ideas, and I'm currently working on a feature about Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds. Last month I sat in on a small two-day workshop on early modern optimism, and I came away convinced that the interesting version of the dispute is not the Voltaire-cartoon one but a technical one about what kind of comparison is even being made: whether best here means a genuine maximum, better than every alternative, or merely an unexcelled element in a partial order, a package no other available package beats. I'd like you to write me a scripted dialogue I could excerpt in the piece, between two people who are equally good at this: Nadia Farouk, a historian of early modern philosophy who thinks the thesis survives once you read perfection as metaphysical rather than as welfare — the greatest variety of being compatible with the greatest order — and Peter Ahlgren, an analytically trained philosopher of religion who thinks that move buys survival at the price of content. Set it on the second evening, walking back along the Leine from the Leibniz library to the conference hotel, and open it with Peter saying he is no longer sure he knows what the claim asserts.

The things I'd want them to actually fight over, and to answer rather than skate past, are these. First, the subtraction worry: if this world contains a great deal of pointless suffering, isn't the world that is just like it minus that suffering better, and doesn't that alone refute the thesis — to which Nadia should give the real Leibnizian reply that worlds are not assembled event by event, that everything hangs together, so the seemingly nearby alternative may not be available at all, and Peter should then press on whether that connection is a discovery or a promissory note. Second, a saturation argument one speaker raised that I haven't been able to shake: for any world containing finite goods there seems to be a better one with more of them, so there is no maximum and a deity who must create the best could not create at all. Third, and this is the bit I most want dramatized, a hypothetical I'll supply: imagine a small research group posts a preprint estimating that, on any remotely plausible account of invertebrate sentience, the total suffering in Earth's biosphere exceeds the total of enjoyment by many orders of magnitude, and that this would hold for almost any world in which mind-bearing creatures arise by evolution through natural selection, since selection runs on differential death. Nadia can use this to show that her thesis was never a forecast about hedonic totals; Peter should use it to ask what, if anything, her thesis rules out, and whether a God who could have produced the same variety of minds without the selection mechanism would have had any reason not to. Let each of them concede at least one substantial point, let neither be impressed by their own vocabulary, and close with each of them owing the other something specific rather than with a verdict — Nadia owing an account of what would count as evidence against her reading, Peter owing an account of what a non-welfare notion of perfection could still be for.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or stage directions, and no screenplay formatting — work the speakers into the sentences themselves, so that it reads as a description of a conversation I could lift a few paragraphs from, somewhere in the region of 1,200 words.
