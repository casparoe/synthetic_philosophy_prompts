I'm TAing an intro ethics course this term and we did torture last week, and afterwards a couple of us got into an argument about the ticking time bomb scenario that I've been chewing on ever since. Someone brought up a real German case and I want to see it run through the hypothetical properly. Roughly as I remember it: in 2002 an eleven-year-old boy from a wealthy Frankfurt family was kidnapped for ransom, the police arrested the suspect after he picked up the money, the boy wasn't with him and they were worried he'd been left somewhere to die of thirst or exposure, the deputy police chief told a subordinate to threaten the suspect with serious pain unless he said where the boy was, the suspect talked and told them where the body was, and the boy turned out to have been dead since before the arrest. The officer got a suspended fine in the low five figures, his subordinate a smaller one, and the kidnapper later won a few thousand euros in damages. Don't bother fact-checking any of that and don't worry about the exact court rulings; just take the facts as I've given them. What I want is the case read through the ticking bomb scenario in both directions.

Three distinctions to impose on the way in, because I think the scenario quietly hides all of them. Call the first legibility: the ticking bomb case as usually stated assumes you know you're in a ticking bomb case — you have the device, the timer, the person who planted it. Here the police had a strong suspicion, a missing child, a suspect who was definately lying, and no confirmation the child was alive. Call the second latency: the window in which the information is worth anything. In the hypothetical the window is fixed and known; here they believed they had hours and actually had none. So tell me what the ticking bomb reasoning delivers when legibility and latency are both low — does it degrade smoothly or collapse, and around what values? Third, call it the sham case: no pain was ever inflicted, only threatened, and I want to know whether the threat-only version is the same moral object as the one the scenario describes. If a threat the interrogator never intends to carry out works just as well, is that a vindication of the ticking bomb intuition or a reductio of it, a sign that what was doing the work in the intuition was never the pain itself but something else — the licence, the suspension of the normal rules, the fear? And separately: the torture worked in the thin sense that the information was true and they recovered the body, and failed in the sense that there was nobody left to save. Does the justification track the rescue, the information, or only the expected value at the moment of acting?

Then the other direction. Say what each of the live positions — absolutism about the prohibition, threshold deontology, lesser-evil or dirty-hands views, whatever else you think deserves a seat — says the officer should have done, what each predicts about how he should be treated afterwards, and how the institutional version of the question goes: advance legal authorization and a paper trail versus an intact ban with punishment and mercy applied after the fact. Then be explicit about where the scenario goes silent or actively misdescribes this case, and then tell me what the case does to the scenario: is Frankfurt 2002 the ticking bomb finally happening in real life, a case the scenario structurally cannot describe, or evidence that the scenario is doing something other than what it advertises itself as doing in the argument? Pick one and defend it. Finish with the bit I still can't get straight — the outcome reads as if the moral bookkeeping came out backwards, with the man who ordered the threat fined roughly the price of a used car while the murderer collected damages — and tell me whether a ticking bomb framework even has the resources to speak to that, or whether it's just an artefact of how the German courts handled it and philosophically inert.
