I'm leading next week's session of a mixed graduate–undergraduate reading group on philosophy of physics — about a dozen people, mostly philosophy students who have seen the Schrödinger equation but not density matrices, plus two physics majors, seventy minutes, whiteboard and nothing else. My assigned topic is why probability is a problem for the many-worlds interpretation: not the preferred-basis question, not whether branching is real, but the specific puzzle of where the Born rule's weights come from in a theory in which every outcome occurs. I don't need the problem explained to me again; I need a plan for getting the room to feel it, and I'd like the plan's reasoning made explicit rather than just its steps.

Two facts about the group that I think should shape the design. A postdoc sat in on the last session and told them, off the cuff, that in a branching universe every outcome happens, so probability talk is just self-locating ignorance about which branch you're on, tidied up by the observation that amplitude-squared tracks the observed frequencies. I take that to gesture at one of the live strategies, but delivered that way it concealed everything that makes the topic hard, and I'd rather start from what they now half-believe than from a clean slate — so tell me what to correct first and what to leave standing. Separately, one member of the group lost a parent to suicide earlier this year, so the quantum suicide / quantum immortality thought experiment is off the table entirely; if you think that is where the vividness normally comes from, tell me what to put in its place and what the substitute has to do that the omitted case was doing.

Here is what I want from you. (i) The order the pieces must arrive in, and the dependency reasoning: what has to be on the board before the problem can even be stated — I'm guessing something about amplitudes being the dynamical quantities while probabilities are what we're trying to recover, something about branching not coming pre-packaged into a countable set of equally real alternatives, and something about why 'the experiments confirm the rule, so what's the problem?' is not an answer at this level. (ii) The one example to carry the whole session, what it must have and what it must lack, and why. My candidate is a repeated measurement on identically prepared systems in a superposition with unequal amplitudes — spin prepared at an angle that isn't 45°, or a single photon at an unbalanced beamsplitter — so that naive branch-counting and the Born rule visibly come apart; but I'm worried that the moment I describe the setup as 'transmitting nine times out of ten' I have assumed the very thing under discussion, and I can't tell whether to open with the symmetric 50/50 case as a warm-up or whether that case quietly teaches the wrong lesson by making counting and the rule agree. (iii) The misconception map: which wrong pictures they will arrive with or construct in the room, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be defused or, better, invited and then used. The ones I expect are: that if all outcomes occur then every probability is one; that this is just the preferred-basis problem in costume; that the weights are an extra postulate bolted onto branching, so the interpretation buys nothing; that the decision-theoretic derivations have simply settled it; that the trouble is only that a continuum of branches can't be counted, so the problem is merely technical; and, from one of the physics majors, that the Born rule is forced by the structure of the state space alone — something about Gleason — so an hour on this is wasted. Tell me which of these are genuine, which are harmless as presented, and which one, if I let it pass unchallenged, will make the rest of the session pointless. (iv) One question I can put to them in the last five minutes that would show me they have grasped what the problem is rather than learned to say 'amplitude', 'branch' and 'weight' in the right order — ideally a transfer question rather than a recall question. (v) What I should openly decline to settle in seventy minutes, and how to say so without leaving them with the impression that the field is stuck or that nothing has been achieved. Please don't point me to papers or suggest readings: I'm not assigning any, and I want the session to work without them.
