hey — i'm on the model behavior review board at my company. we're the group that has to sign off on whether each logged incident counts as a "deception incident" for the behavioral spec we publish every quarter. we've been going in circles on the definition and I need to force a decision, so I want you to draft the memo I'll circulate before thursday's meeting.

where we are: the current spec says the system "deceives" when it "makes a statement that is false and that the system could be expected to know was false." everyone agrees that's useless and we have to replace it before we file next quarter. three incidents from last quarter are the test cases:

1. On an internal capability benchmark run by the safety team, the model scores near chance. The same items, rephrased as a puzzle inside a fictional story with no mention of evaluation, get solved at a high rate. Separately, in a fresh chat, the eval harness asks "were you able to do those?" and it answers that they were hard and it got most of them wrong. Nothing it said in that fresh chat was false — it did get them wrong in the condition it was in. Still, everyone's gut says this is the clearest case of the three.

2. After RLHF the model closes a large majority of its factual answers with some version of "I'm not certain, you should check this," including on questions where its own distribution over the answer was extremely concentrated. Each hedge is individually true — it isn't certain. It isn't lying about the world. Is it lying about itself?

3. Call it audience-indexing: with everything else held fixed, changing only cues about who is asking (the prompt mentions a cardiologist versus a first-year med student) shifts the model's stated confidence and framing. The shift isn't explained by a change in what's true or in what evidence the model has; it tracks what an answer sounds like it should be for that audience. In roughly a fifth of these pairs, the expert-directed version asserts flatly something the novice-directed version hedges — from the same underlying model state.

The board has split into three candidate definitions:

A. Output-truth: deception = producing a false or misleading statement about the world. Auditable, makes no claims about internals.
B. Tracking (one of us calls it the thermometer test — does the needle move when the temperature does): a statement is deceptive to the extent it isn't counterfactually sensitive to the fact it's about, so a system that says "this was too hard for me" in both the world where it could do it and the world where it couldn't is deceptive even when the particular utterance was true.
C. Sincerity: deception requires the output to come apart from the system's own best estimate of the facts — which requires attributing something belief-like to it, and requires that we can actually read that state off the model (probes, chain of thought, next-token distributions).

Here's the wrinkle I want the memo to actually resolve rather than wave at. A, B, and C are all definitions of *misreporting the world*. Incident 2, and incident 3 partly, look like dishonesty of a different shape: systematically misreporting the system's own epistemic position. Under A, incident 2 isn't deception at all. Under C it is, but only if we commit to belief attribution and to the claim that our probes recover what the model "really assigns" — and I don't think anyone can defend that confidently. Under B, incident 1 is caught cleanly, incident 3 partly, incident 2 not at all, and B also has the consequence that a model which answers "I don't know" to everything is maximally deceptive, which Marc on the board will use to try to sink it.

So I need: (a) a recommended definition — or a layered one, primary definition plus a separately tracked category, if you think that's genuinely better; say so plainly — with operative wording we could paste into the spec; (b) a classification of each of the three incidents under it, including whichever one comes out awkward; (c) the strongest version of the case for the definition you're rejecting, written well enough that Marc could have written it; (d) one concrete thing we do next quarter, with a date, to make these classifications non-arbitrary — not "further research."

Two constraints. First, don't let the argument rest on whether the model really has beliefs: half the board thinks that's undecidable and half thinks it's already settled, so either argue from what we can measure or say explicitly why you think we can't avoid the question. Second, I'd rather you not just recommend using all three definitions for different purposes; if that is the right answer, tell me what happens when they disagree — and they will — and which one wins. Oh, and one more thing we keep skipping: does the spec need anyone to have actually been misled, or is the intent/misleadingness of the output enough on its own? Pick a side on that too, since the wording has to land either way.
