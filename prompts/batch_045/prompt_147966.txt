I convene a small graduate reading group on moral responsibility, and this term we spent eight weeks on Strawson's essay *Freedom and Resentment* alongside a stack of incompatibilist replies to it. Two of our members have now reached the point where they are, I think, re-running the same argument past each other: one of them has been defending her reading for a decade and has largely stopped finding his objections intelligible; he, in turn, is convinced she has quietly changed the subject. Her position, as she would state it: to be morally responsible is to be a fitting target of the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, hurt feelings, forgiveness; these attitudes are partly constitutive of ordinary interpersonal relationships rather than optional additions to them; what makes them fitting is the quality of will an agent's conduct manifests, not the causal history of that will; and so the truth of determinism has no tendency to show that no one is ever responsible. His position, as he would state it: blame and punishment are deserved only if the agent is the ultimate source of her action in a way that causal determinism excludes, and in a way that any merely reasons-responsive mechanism she did not make excludes too; the reactive attitudes are real psychological phenomena with real social uses; and a notion of responsibility defined as fittingness to our attitudes can be satisfied by someone who deserves nothing at all, which is why it cannot be what we were arguing about.

What I want from you is the joint statement these two should have produced instead of their eighth argument: a document both could sign. Give me, first, a numbered section of agreed claims — including what they agree about the reactive attitudes themselves (their phenomenology, their role in relationships, the way excuses that deny bad will operate), about what a satisfactory account would have to deliver, and about what the disagreement is *not* (neither takes determinism to have been established, neither holds the reactive attitudes to be pointless or optional in everyday life, and both accept Strawson's point that the participant stance and the objective stance are genuine alternatives we actually move between in particular cases). Then a table of the residual disagreements, one row each, with three columns: a neutral formulation, her preferred formulation, and his — each of which its own side would sign without wincing, and none of which is allowed to be vacuous or to win by insinuation. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them, and for each crux at least one fully specified case both would accept as bearing on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance, plus a note on what each side would count as a genuine cost to their own view if it went the other way.

Three cruxes I suspect are doing the work, but add or substitute as you see fit. First, the desert question: does her fittingness carry anything beyond a claim about what it is coherent or useful for us to feel, and if it does, what in the account bears that weight? I suspect part of this crux is verbal and part is not — I want you to say which part is which and why, and to say plainly anywhere a formulation that looks like a compromise is actually empty. Second, the manipulation cases; please handle both of mine, which I have specified so that nobody needs to look anything up. In the Calibration study, a device is implanted in an agent without her knowledge and tuned so that her deliberations track the moral reasons better than they did before; she notices nothing, deliberates as she always has, and a year later reliably acts well for the reasons there are and is disposed to feel guilty when she does not. In the Vale study, a cohort of children is raised by caregivers trained to respond to conduct only in the objective-attitude mode — no resentment, no blame, no praise, no guilt-induction, only causal explanation, repair, and management — and, by hypothesis, the resulting adults are statistically indistinguishable from controls in their ability to recognize and act on moral reasons and in their rates of helping and rule-following, yet report no guilt, resentment, or gratitude, and no sense of their own conduct as morally up to them. For each: what does her account imply, and is Strawson's own claim about how deeply rooted the participant stance is hostage to an empirical prediction — what does she predict the Vale adults would do under stress, or inside a close relationship, or when genuinely wronged? What does his account imply, and if the Vale adults turn out to be perfectly decent people, does that tell against him or only against something he never claimed? Third, scope: how much of the disagreement survives if it is restricted to the hard cases — psychopathy, severe deprivation, covert manipulation — and does either position need the reactive attitudes to be universal, or only to be ours?

Please work from the arguments as you understand them: I do not want citations, page references, or a reading list, and I would rather you did not spend the answer telling me which authors have defended which version — I want the formulations themselves, and if you introduce a refinement of either view that is not part of the standard package, flag in your own words that you have introduced it. If you think one of my two cases is rigged, underdescribed, or uninformative, say which and why, and add the case you would put in its place. Close with a short note on how confident you are in each of the agreed claims, and single out the one claim or formulation you are least sure of, with a sentence on what would settle it. The answer should be in American English.
