**Task.** Write a dialogue of roughly 900–1,200 words between two philosophers of mind who disagree about what happens to the unity of consciousness in a patient with a complete callosotomy. The setting is concrete and low-key: the two of them have just come out of a lab demonstration of the case described below and are arguing about what it shows. Keep the exchange dramatic but disciplined—no appeals to authority, no references to published literature or named experiments beyond the case as stipulated, no footnotes or citations. The argument must stand on its own.

**The case.** The patient, R., had the corpus callosum fully sectioned years ago as treatment for intractable epilepsy. Standard testing yields the familiar pattern: items shown briefly in the right visual field are named and described; items shown briefly in the left visual field cannot be named, though R. can select the matching object with the left hand. When conflicting words are presented to the two visual fields simultaneously, the speaking hemisphere reports only its own word and, when pressed about the other, supplies a fluent, confident invention rather than admitting ignorance.

The new protocol is what they have just watched. Wearing goggles that use eye tracking to hold each of two unrelated silent films locked to a single visual field, R. watches both films continuously and simultaneously for several minutes. Three findings are on the table:

1. *Dual tracking with a bottleneck.* The right hand tracks events in film A and the left hand tracks events in film B, both accurately and without cross-contamination, for minutes at a stretch.
2. *Spontaneous alternation.* At unpredictable intervals of a few seconds to a minute, the spoken report switches: mid-session R. begins narrating film B, and when questioned afterwards treats earlier episodes of film B as things he saw. Just before and just after each switch, the manual tracking of the other film becomes unreliable for two or three seconds and then recovers.
3. *Fine-grained shared affect.* Skin conductance, pupil dilation and spontaneous facial expression track emotionally salient events in *both* films, including events in whichever film is not currently being reported. The responses are content-specific: they track the emotional shape of the unreported film rather than producing a uniform startle.

Afterwards R. insists he watched a single film and saw all of it; he reports no gaps, no double vision, and no sense of having missed anything.

**The two positions.**

*Serial duality.* At any moment there is exactly one stream of consciousness in R., but which of the two hemispheric systems realizes it alternates; each system is a fully integrated phenomenal field, and consciousness is drawn serially from one and then the other. On this view the verbal denials, the confabulations, the absence of cross-report and the alternations are all surface readings of a single underlying fact, and the shared affective responses are subpersonal—arousal and autonomic response coordinated by subcortical structures, of a kind that can occur without the eliciting event being conscious at all.

*Graded co-consciousness.* R. is a single subject whose consciousness is *partially* unified: the two content-streams are co-conscious but not fully phenomenally unified, bound together at the level of arousal, gist and affect but not at the level of specific content. Unity is therefore a matter of degree, and the intact brain sits at the top of a scale rather than on the other side of a boundary. On this view the restriction to one reported stream is a bottleneck in report, memory consolidation and verbal access, not a restriction in what is experienced, and confabulation is something the reporting system does, not something the experiential stream is like.

**Requirements for the exchange.**

- Both speakers must be equally competent and equally charitably presented. Each should state the other's position in a form the other would accept before criticizing it, and each should concede at least one genuine difficulty in their own position rather than dismissing it.
- The disagreement must be joined, not merely asserted. In particular the dialogue must work through the following, in whatever order arises naturally:
  (a) whether phenomenal unity can intelligibly come in degrees at all, or whether being co-conscious is an all-or-nothing relation, so that any "partial" unity collapses into either full unity or outright duality;
  (b) what would distinguish shared *experience* of an event from merely shared subpersonal response to it, and whether finding (3) settles that question in either direction;
  (c) the bearing of the case on the normal, intact subject: whether the intact brain differs in kind or only in degree, and whether a sufficiently fast alternation would be distinguishable from genuine co-consciousness even in principle, or whether that remaining disagreement is merely verbal;
  (d) what experiment each would regard as decisive, given the protocol already available.
- Neither speaker should win by fiat. Close the dialogue with each naming the single further finding that would move them to the other side, and let those two findings be different.
