Okay, this has been bugging me since my uncle was declared brain dead last year and we had about a day to decide whether to donate. The package of things the hospital did just doesn't add up to one clear idea of what death is, and I want to know what has to be true for it to make sense.

Here's what actually happened and what I found out afterwards:

1. They declared him dead on neurological criteria — no blood flow to the brain, no brainstem reflexes, apnea test, the whole checklist — while his heart was beating, his chest was warm and moving, his incisions were healing, and they had him on pressors and a hormone drip to keep his organs in good shape. If he's dead, what exactly is the hormone drip for, and who is it helping?

2. They were completely sure he was dead, and then they asked us for permission to take the organs, and made it clear that if we'd said no, they'd have stopped. So there was still something of his to defer to, or at least something of ours.

3. They kept repeating the dead donor rule: nothing they do will kill the donor. But on the other track — when treatment is withdrawn from someone who isn't brain dead and the organs are taken after the heart stops — the whole thing hinges on a wait of a few minutes after circulation ceases, and everyone I've read agrees the number is a convention that different hospitals set differently. And in the brain-death track, the heart is literally still beating when the chest is opened.

4. A declared-brain-dead pregnant woman can be kept on a ventilator for weeks so the fetus becomes viable, and this has happened more than once. A body that gestates is doing the thing living organisms do.

5. In a few places the criterion itself is opt-out-able: New Jersey won't let death be declared on neurological criteria if the physician has reason to think it would violate the person's religious beliefs. So whether you are dead depends partly on what you believe, which makes death look less like a fact we discover and more like something we decide or register.

A friend who does some philosophy gave me a distinction that seemed to unlock part of it: irreversible versus merely permanent. Irreversible means it cannot be reversed by anything; permanent means it won't be reversed because nobody is going to try. On that reading, circulatory death is a prediction plus a decision, not an observation, and the two criteria are doing completely different kinds of work under the same word.

So my question: what theory of what a human being is, and of what it is for one to end, does this whole package of practices actually presuppose? I care less about which theory is true than about which ones the practice quietly rules out. Does it commit us to thinking of ourselves as organisms whose death is the loss of the capacity to integrate and sustain themselves, with the brain as the thing doing the integrating? Or does it, despite appearances, depend on some personhood or consciousness view? Can it be read as a view on which death is partly a conventional or legal status rather than an event in the body? Where is the package genuinely underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would all license the same hospital behavior? And where are the parts actually in tension — does the pregnancy case or the religious exemption break the organism story, or does it just show that we are running two different concepts of death under one word?

And then the practical half: if we swapped the implicit theory for a straight higher-brain criterion, or for the view that death is a decision we make rather than a fact we find out, what would concretely have to change in how the hospital acts? Also how much of the current set-up is really there for reasons that have nothing to do with the metaphysics — organ supply, family grief, liability, the fact that nobody wants the conversation — because I suspect that's most of it and I want to know which parts of the practice are load-bearing for the theory and which are just institutional inertia.

I don't want a reading list, just the argument. And tell me if the question itself is confused.
