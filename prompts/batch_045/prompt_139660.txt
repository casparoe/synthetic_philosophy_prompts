I'm retired now — I spent about thirty years as an electrophysiologist, mostly ion channels, and in the last couple of years I've started reading philsophy of mind for fun, which is probably obvious from how I'm about to put this.

Searle's Chinese Room keeps coming up, and what strikes me isn't that people disagree, it's how little the disagreement moves. On one side: the man in the room shuffling symbols according to a rulebook doesn't understand Chinese, so running a program can't be what understanding is. On the other side: the standard replies — the man doesn't understand but the whole system does (man plus rulebook plus the scraps of paper plus the room); or wire the rulebook to a camera and a robot arm so the symbols are hooked up causally to the world and get their content from it; or the symbols do understand, just not in a way the man has access to. Then the counter-replies, and the counters to those. Everybody on both sides knows everybody's moves in advance, and after forty-odd years nobody seems to have budged.

My hypothesis, which I want checked rather than confirmed, is that the sides aren't answering the same question: the Searle side is asking whether symbol-shuffling could be what understanding *consists in*, while the functionalist side is asking whether it does everything we'd need to posit understanding to do. Those don't contradict each other. But I also worry it's worse than that — that it bottoms out in an intuition neither side can argue for, the same way I can't argue anyone into agreeing that a computer simulation of a hurricane isn't wet.

Here's a case I made up to try to force the issue; please run it through your diagnosis as a test rather than treating it as one more position to catalogue. Call it the vanishing room. Start with the standard setup. Then, one at a time, replace a rulebook lookup with a bit of trained wetware in the man's head that does the same mapping without his consulting anything. Over a few years of daily upgrades the rulebook gets thinner, and his reports change: he stops talking about symbol shapes and starts saying the answers just come to him. At the end the rulebook is empty and the paper is gone. Externally, his behavior at each stage is indistinguishable from the stage before — and a native speaker of Chinese, from the outside, presumably looks like something near the far end of that series. So: does understanding appear somewhere along the series, and if so does anything in the original argument tell us where? If it appears nowhere, hasn't the argument proved too much? And if your answer is that the series is the wrong thing to look at and what really matters is X, then X had better be something the original argument already committed you to, or you've changed the subject.

So what I actually want: is this a verbal dispute about the word "understanding," a substantive conceptual disagreement, a disguised empirical one (about what a physical system has to be like to do what brains do), people answering different questions under one label, or bedrock intuition all the way down? Argue for a diagnosis out of what each side really claims, use the vanishing room to test it, and be straight with me about whether anything — a distinction, an experiment, a result about brains or about programs — could actually end this, or whether the honest expectation is that it grinds on forever.

End with a one-sentence summary of your diagnosis, and then, as the very last line, exactly one of these:
