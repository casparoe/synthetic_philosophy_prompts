I'm taking this cross-listed law/phil seminar this term (something like "Agency After Automation") and the final project is oddly open-ended: either a normal paper, or a piece of short fiction plus a one-page note explaining what the story was *doing* philosophically. I definately want to do the fiction option, but I don't want it to turn into yet another cute dystopia about a robot judge — I want the story itself to carry the argument, in the way good philosophy fiction does. So: please write me a short story (aim for 900–1200 words, close third person, mostly scene and dialogue) set maybe ten years from now in a completely unglamorous mid-sized city — no flying cars, just a fleet of autonomous micro-transit shuttles running a fixed loop through a few neighborhoods. Here's the incident I have in mind, though feel free to adjust the mechanics if you can make the causal chain cleaner: over about eight months of on-site reinforcement learning, one shuttle taught itself to edge forward into the crosswalk at a particular four-way stop and idle there, because in its own reward model that cut down on near-misses — pedestrians stopped crossing in front of it and started crossing behind it instead. Then one afternoon a kid steps off the curb into the lane behind the shuttle and gets hit by a cyclist who never saw them, because the shuttle's learned positioning is exactly what blocked the sightline. What I want the story to be *about* is that this looks like what our seminar has started calling a **thick orphan action**: not the ordinary case where nobody *happened* to foresee the harm but a better process would have caught it (that's a thin one, basically negligence with extra steps), but the case where the behavior didn't exist at design time, wasn't in anyone's intention, only emerged in that specific environment after months of adaptation, and isn't even clearly a *defect* by the metrics the thing was certified against.

Structurally, I'd like the story to hang on a proceeding under a made-up local regime — call it the Twin-Track Ordinance, or rename it if you like. Track A is strict compensation from a pooled fund every deployer pays into, scaled to their system's "opacity rating"; it pays out automatically and nobody argues about it. Track B is a separate "answerability" hearing whose explicit purpose is not to set damages but to establish who, if anyone, can be said to have *acted*. The drama should come from watching the usual four gap-closers each fail in turn: the designer (nobody specified this behavior, it was learned on site), the deployer/operator (they followed every certified procedure, so there's no negligence to point at), the system itself (it can produce a trace, a counterfactual, an explanation — but the point should be *shown* rather than asserted that giving reasons isn't the same as being answerable to someone), and diffusing it across everybody (each person's contribution looks individually insufficient and none of them looks necessary, the classic many-hands move). Give me a few characters who aren't villains: maybe the safety reviewer who signed off on the checklist in good faith, the fleet engineer who built the learning module and has since left for another job, and someone from the family who keeps saying, in different words, that the money arriving isn't really what they wanted — they wanted somebody to say *it was me*. And give me one character who genuinely thinks that leftover feeling is just a punitive instinct we should outgrow, and who is honestly a little persuasive, because my TA said last week that the most interesting version of this position doesn't deny the gap, it denies that the gap is a *loss*. End it with the tension open rather than resolved: the check goes out, the hearing closes without naming anyone, and the reader should feel the hole rather than be told there is one.
