I have been trying to settle whether I could have come from a different sperm and a different egg, and I have ended up with an argument whose conclusion neither I nor the friend I built it with is willing to accept. Define the target view, origin essentialism (OE): necessarily, a person exists at a world only if that person originated at that world from the very gamete pair they actually originated from. Our argument against it runs:

(1) A gamete is not the sort of thing a block of wood is. A sperm is the outcome of one meiosis: a single draw, settled by molecular accident, from an enormous space of possible recombinations of my father's genome, plus fresh mutations, and it survives for hours. There seems to be no determinate fact about whether a sperm whose genome differs from the actual one by a single base pair is the same sperm described differently or a different sperm altogether.

(2) Tolerance. If variation of that size is allowed anywhere, it is allowed at the origin: had I originated from a gamete pair differing from the actual pair by one crossover, or one base substitution, I would still have been me. The alternative is that whether I ever exist at all turns on one unobservable molecular toss-up, which seems absurd. Besides, I have survived enormous change since conception; most of the matter I was made of has been turned over repeatedly. If identity can tolerate that much change, it can tolerate one nucleotide's worth of difference at the start.

(3) Chaining. Tolerance has no natural stopping point. For any pair from which I could have originated, a minimally different pair is also one from which I could have originated. Such minimal differences can be strung along a continuous path from the actual pair to a sperm from a different man and an egg from a different woman, and identity is transitive.

(4) So I could have originated from any gamete pair whatsoever — which does not merely refute (OE) but empties the question it was meant to answer. And the same reasoning applies to the familiar artifact case: chain single-molecule differences in the wood and the table turns out to have been makeable from any block anywhere.

My friend concludes that origins simply carry no modal weight and we should learn to live with that. I think something has gone wrong, but I cannot say what, and each step looks sound when I take it on its own. The two claims I most want to keep are precisely the ones the argument squeezes together: the block-of-wood intuition, and the thought that my existing should not be hostage to a single unobservable molecular event.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If the chaining step is the culprit, I want to know exactly what it assumes about could have — whether the intermediate variants must be possible together, within a single world, and why my friend's chain fails to establish what he takes it to establish. If there is no flaw and a premise has to go, tell me which is cheapest to surrender: in particular, what giving up (2) commits me to about the fragility of my own existence, whether the argument is equivocating between change over a life and difference across possible origins, and whether the gamete case and the artifact case can come apart at all or whether any view that saves the table is also a view that saves (OE) for persons. One further datum I cannot place: since one zygote can divide, the very same gamete pair can apparently yield two people, so sameness of origin looks insufficient for sameness of person as well, and I am no longer sure what work origin is doing in any of this.
