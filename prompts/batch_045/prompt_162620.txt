teaching the metaphysics of time module again and Thursday is our last seminar, presentism vs eternalism, and I want to close it by reading a poem out loud instead of doing the usual "so where does that leave us" slide. Can you write me one?

Form: a sestina. Six sestets, then the three-line envoi, standard end-word rotation, all six words back in the envoi. I have opinions about the words but not strong ones, so: start with now / then / always / gone / remains / still, and swap at most two. The constraint that matters is that at least two of the six should be able to do double duty — a verb in one stanza and an adverb or noun in another, or pointing forward in one place and back in another — because I want the rotation itself to smuggle in the A/B-series point: the same word means something different depending on where it lands, and nothing about the words changes, only where "now" has got to. Don't save "now" for the last stanza; let it turn up in a different line each time.

Scene: I want something concrete, not a voice musing in a void. My instinct is a grad student alone at an observatory at 4am, looking at light that left a star long before she was born, but take it wherever. Whoever the speaker is, they should have thought about this professionally and be unable to stop noticing it.

Content things I'd want a specialist to nod at rather than wince at:

- Both views get a real hearing, and the poem shouldn't be a stealth argument for one of them. The eternalist gets the indexical analogy — past and future are real the way other places are, "now" works like "here" — and the presentist gets to push back that the disagreement is about what exists, not about where you're standing.
- Somewhere around stanzas three or four, the relativity wrinkle: what's simultaneous with you depends on how you're moving, so a presentist owes you an answer. The presentist I want voiced is one who bites the bullet and says there is one privileged slicing of things into nows, a single true simultaneity, even though no experiment could ever tell you which one it is. Get that move right and don't name anybody for it.
- Around stanza five, the uncomfortable thought that the whole dispute might not cash out in anything you'd notice. Everyone in the room agrees you will die, that grief is grief, that the dead are unreachable in the ordinary sense. Have the speaker feel the pull of "so what's the difference" and then either resist it or fail to — I genuinely don't know which way it should land, so leave it open.
- There's a third option I've been using in section and I'd like it in the poem somewhere. I made it up to be clear rather than to be original, so don't go looking for it: call it the ledger. On the ledger view the future isn't unreal, it's unsettled — entries posted but not yet closed — the past is entries closed and not revisable, and becoming is the posting rather than the creating. Nothing comes into being, it gets reclassified. It's supposed to be a middle: it hands the presentist a real asymmetry between past and future (settled vs open) while handing the eternalist everything that exists. Give it two stanzas' worth of air, and let the form be the judge of whether it's a genuine third option or just a relabeling — but don't say which in so many words.

Please don't lean on the glowing four-dimensional block picture; in my experience that image is doing more work than the theory does. No names, no citations, no glossary afterwards — if the poem needs footnotes it hasn't worked. And no verdict in the envoi; I'd love the last line to be a question the poem hasn't already answered, but I'll take a good image instead.

It gets read aloud in about two minutes, so keep the lines speakable, and someone who's never done any metaphysics should still be able to follow it and feel it.
