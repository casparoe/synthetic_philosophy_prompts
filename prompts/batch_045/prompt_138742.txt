A friend sent me a blog post explaining updateless decision theory and I can't tell how much of it I should actually believe. Roughly, it says this:

UDT says you should never update on your evidence -- a UDT agent ignores what it observes and instead asks which policy it would have wanted to commit to from behind the veil of ignorance, before it knew anything at all, and then mechanically does that. That's why it one-boxes in Newcomb's problem: not because one-boxing is evidence that the million is there, but because "always one-box" is the policy you'd have picked in advance, so that's what it executes. This matters because UDT wins the cases where causal decision theory loses money, like Newcomb's problem and Parfit's hitchhiker. But it also means a UDT agent pays up in counterfactual mugging, handing over money on the strength of a branch of the coin that didn't happen, and since nobody sane would pay there the theory is refuted -- it's a curiosity about perfect predictors and brain copies with no bearing on real decisions. (He also keeps calling it "functional decision theory, as people usually say now", as if that's just a rename.)

I'm fairly sure the "never updates on anything" bit can't be right -- obviously the thing has to react to what it sees -- but I cant say what goes in its place, and the rest of it sounds plausible to me. So: which parts would someone who actually works on this accept, which are distortions, and is the counterfactual mugging point really the refutation he takes it for?
