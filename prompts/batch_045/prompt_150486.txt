Hey. I write a science-and-ideas column and I'm finally doing the piece on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument that I've been dodging for about a year. I've drifted into thinking it's basically right, and I want somebody to talk me out of it before I file, so please don't flatter me.

The version I'm working from, in my own words: you do what you do because of the way you are; to be genuinely morally responsible you'd have to be responsible for the way you are; the only way to be responsible for the way you are is to have brought it about by some act of self-formation; but any such act already comes out of a way-you-are that you didn't choose; so you need an endless chain of self-creating acts; we're finite, so the chain never gets started. And I take the point that's supposed to make it escape-proof: it doesn't matter whether determinism is true, because an undetermined choice just injects luck rather than authorship.

So — what would count against it? I want defeaters ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, and I want to know which of them someone who actually defends this argument would concede as counting against it, as opposed to which ones they'd dismiss as quietly redefining "responsible" into something weaker that nobody was arguing about.

Three things I've been chewing on, which I'd like you to engage with rather then bat away:

1. Convergent regress. The argument leans on the idea that the chain has to be completed step by step, and sure, a finite being can't perform infinitely many acts. But what if the steps aren't independent — suppose each act of self-shaping is the kind of thing that narrows in, so that the sequence of characters converges on a fixed point the agent would arrive at from any nearby starting condition. Could responsibility attach to the limit rather than to any step in the series? Is "responsible in the limit" coherent at all, or does it just re-inherit the original problem at every step, forever?

2. Closed loops. Skip the infinite chain entirely: suppose a case where the agent's later choice to become the sort of person who made the earlier choice is itself brought about by that earlier choice — a self-consistent causal loop, in the style people use for time-travel stories. Now the character really is its own product with no regress. Does that deliver self-creation, or is it still just luck that the loop exists and that the agent can't bilk it?

3. Global properties. The argument seems to want an originating act. But lots of properties belong to a whole temporally extended thing while belonging to no moment of it — a melody isn't at rest at any instant, a life has a shape that no single episode has. Why can't responsibility be like that: a feature of the entire trajectory of a person, needing no first cause? And if the defender answers that this is a different and weaker property than the one at stake, is the whole dispute really just a fight about which property we were after?

Then the bit I actually care about for the piece: has this argument been insulated to the point where nothing could count against it? It seems to survive every objection by redescribing the objection as not being about real responsibility, and it blocks the indeterminism escape route by construction. If that's right, is it a virtue — a valid argument is allowed to bite bullets — or a sign that we've drifted out of the space of claims that can be tested at all? What would the argument's own friends say it would take to make them drop it?

And what would move me personally: I think I'd be moved less by a clever counterexample than by being shown that the argument slides between two senses of "responsible for the way you are" — tell me whether there's a genuine equivocation hiding there or whether that's just critics sounding deep.

Keep it under 700 words. Ranked defeaters, one line each, tagged fatal / serious / awkward / doesn't touch it, then two or three sentences on the unfalsifiability worry at the end. If one of my three is the strongest, say which and why.
