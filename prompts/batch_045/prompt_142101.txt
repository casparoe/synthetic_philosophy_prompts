I am trying to work out what I actually think about whether the size of a brain has any moral relevance, and I want to get past slogans on both sides. I mean by moral status two things together: how much weight an individual's interests carry when weighed against the comparable interests of another individual, and what may be done to that individual for the sake of others. And I want to distinguish four candidate quantities from the start, because I suspect I treat them differently: total mass or volume, total neuron count, the share of the body's resting energy budget that the organ consumes, and the richness of the conscious repertoire the organ actually supports.

Below are my verdicts. Every empirical claim in them is stipulated, so please take the biology as given rather than disputing it: I am not asking for facts, I am asking what principle my reactions add up to.

A. A typical adult human, brain roughly three times the mass of a typical adult chimpanzee's. My verdict: the human has the higher status, and I would permit, for a modest benefit to humans, research on the chimpanzee that I would not permit on the human. But I would not say the chimpanzee's interests are negligible, and I am less comfortable with this than I used to be.

B. A species whose adults have brains the size of a pea and a repertoire much like a fish's. Verdict: much lower status than a human's, and this verdict costs me nothing to give.

C. A whale whose brain is six times the mass of a human's but has a thinner cortex, fewer cortical neurons than a human's, and a repertoire that contains nothing above the human range. Verdict: no additional status over a human. It is not bigger-than-human status, and I do not think I am being inconsistent with A in saying so.

D. An alien species whose behavioural repertoire, learning curves and social complexity match ours, but whose brains are one twentieth the volume, with denser and faster neurons, drawing a much smaller share of the body's energy budget. Verdict: equal status to a human.

E. Two species with identical behavioural repertoires and identical injury-free lives, except that members of the second have twice the neurons, the surplus forming a dormant reserve recruited only after brain injury. Verdict: no difference in status. Unused capacity does not seem to count.

F. A human infant versus an adult chimpanzee whose brain is three times the mass of the infant's. Verdict: the infant's status is at least as high, probably higher. Potential seems to count here, in a way it did not in E.

G. An adult human born with severe microcephaly: brain mass and neuron count inside the chimpanzee range, repertoire roughly that of a human two-year-old. Verdict: full human-level status. I would not enroll such a person in a terminal experiment of the kind I permitted for the chimpanzee in A.

H. A chimpanzee whose lineage has been modified so that its brain is human-sized and whose cognitive and emotional repertoire is in the human range. Verdict: full human-level status.

I. Two versions of me with a brain half the size. In the first, a disease halves my brain volume over two years with no change whatever in my psychology, memory or preferences. In the second, the same reduction happens in the womb, and the resulting adult has the repertoire of a mouse. Verdict: no loss in the first case; substantial reduction in the second.

J. An adult human with advanced dementia whose remaining functional neural mass is small and whose current repertoire is roughly that of a dog, alongside a dog with the same current repertoire. Verdict: the human still has the higher status, although I cannot tell whether that is status or something in the vicinity of it.

The pairs I cannot reconcile are G against H, G against the second half of I, and E against F. G and H each say that one of species membership and realised capacity trumps the other. G and the second half of I are both low-capacity humans and I give them opposite verdicts. E and F both involve unused potential, and I discard it in one and lean on it in the other.

My own tentative principle is this: size is never morally relevant in itself; the only thing that matters intrinsically is the richness of the conscious repertoire the individual actually realises, or would realise but for reversible interference; and size, neuron count and energy share matter only as fallible evidence for that. I think this handles A, B, C, D, E and the first half of I, and that it either fails on G and the second half of I or needs a clause about the individual's history that I suspect is ad hoc. I would rather repair it than be handed something unrelated, but I would also rather be told plainly if it cannot be repaired.

Please deliver the following.

1. The best principle you can state, in numbered parts, with the case letter that forces each part, and kept short enough to be quoted back at me as a single test.
2. A table of my cases A through J: for each, whether your principle agrees with my verdict, and where it does not, what it says instead and what it costs to accept.
3. Verdicts of your principle on cases I have not listed: a colony of a million small-brained animals that genuinely constitutes one collective mind of human-grade sophistication while no individual rises above insect grade; a neuron-by-neuron software emulation of one particular living human brain, running without a body; an adult human who has had one cerebral hemisphere removed and has only mild deficits; a species with ten times the human cortical neuron count whose behaviour and reported experience stay inside the human range; a human in a coma from which full recovery is expected within a month; and an engineered pig with a pig-sized brain and a human-grade repertoire.
4. Which of my three irreconcilable pairs is a genuine conflict and which is only about words, and, for the genuine conflicts, which verdict you would overturn and why that one rather than its partner. If no single principle saves all ten verdicts, tell me what a principle that saves all ten would have to look like, and how much complexity it would buy.
5. Which of my verdicts you think are not really about status but about something else: the effects on third parties, the expressive or symbolic meaning of an action, epistemic caution under uncertainty, or the special duties of family and caregivers. Mark these clearly, because I want to know how much of my discomfort is status and how much is everything around it.

Reason from the cases and the arguments; do not cite or recommend literature. Please write the answer in British English.
