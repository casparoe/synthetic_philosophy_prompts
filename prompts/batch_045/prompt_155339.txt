I'm writing an essay for a consciousness seminar and I want to use Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day as a case, but the reading I've got feels too smooth, so I want someone to stress-test it before I commit to it.

The reading: Stevens spends most of the book in first-order states he won't acknowledge — grief when his father is dying upstairs while he keeps serving dinner downstairs and then goes back to his duties, something like love in those scenes where he keeps drifting into the parlour on fake errands while Miss Kenton is reading, and loyalty to Lord Darlington that he later flatly denies to people he meets on his drive west. On a higher-order view (Rosenthal's thought version, or the Armstrong/Lycan inner-sense version) those states aren't conscious until there's some higher-order representation of them, and the book's whole shape is him forming one very slowly, maybe only really at the end on the pier when the old man on the bench says the evening is the best part of the day.

What I actually want to know is whether this is a good case for higher-order theories or whether it quietly assumes the conclusion. Concretely:

- The thought version needs concept possession: Stevens has to think of himself as grieving, not just register himself as being in some state. The inner-sense version doesn't. Which fits what the book shows? My instinct is that Ishiguro gives us a man whose inner sense has been professionally deafened by thirty years of training, so even the perceptual version calls him unconscious, and the concept version just gets there more cheaply and more plausibly. Is that right, or am I misdescribing the theories?
- At the other end: could a self-representationalist — the view that a conscious state represents itself and no distinct second-order state is needed — claim the novel as their case instead? Stevens' grief is phenomenally present to the reader the whole time even though it isn't present to him; the pathos depends on us supplying what he withholds.
- I want to push that last point into an argument, and I need to know if it survives: that what the novel really adds to the debate is the possibility of a *delegated* or *readerly* higher-order representation, one located outside the subject. That's either a reductio of higher-order theories (surely consciousness can't be outsourced to an audience) or a discovery about how dramatic irony works that the theories should be able to shrug off. Tell me which, and why.

And please don't let me get away with the reading — I'd rather find out now where it breaks. Two worries I've already got: (a) first-person retrospective narration is itself a higher-order representation, so on the face of it every state Stevens narrates is conscious by construction and the reading collapses into a tautology; (b) self-deception is exactly the case where higher-order theories and their first-order rivals (Dretske, Tye) fight to a draw, so leaning on it may just import the stalemate rather than break it. Say which of these is fatal, which can be answered, and whether the fix is to take the subject of the higher-order state to be the Stevens inside the scene rather than the Stevens doing the narrating. Point me at the two or three scenes you'd actually build the argument on, and be honest where the book resists being read this way.
