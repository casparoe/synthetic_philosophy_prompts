I am putting together a handout for a final-year reading group on the value problem that Plato's *Meno* bequeathed to epistemology: granted that knowledge and mere true belief can agree on the truth of the proposition and can issue in exactly the same action, why should we care about the difference? I do not want a survey article. I want a decision tree that takes a student from the starting commitment below to a named position, built from the logical space rather than from a list of -isms: the questions first, then the combinations of answers they permit, then the actual views located in that space.

The starting commitment, which every branch must respect: we compare knowledge with mere true belief in a single case, at a time, holding fixed the truth of the proposition believed and, so far as the case allows, its practical upshot. I also want the tree to keep apart three questions the literature distinguishes, since a view can answer one and not the others: the primary question (why knowledge beats mere true belief), the secondary question (why knowledge beats any lesser epistemic standing, such as justified belief or a Gettiered justified true belief), and the tertiary question (why knowledge is *qualitatively* or distinctively better than what falls just short of it, rather than merely one more point on a continuum).

Constraints on the questions. Five to seven branch nodes, no more than twelve leaves. Every node must be phrased as a question about one of the cases below or about a claim, answerable by someone who has never heard the labels — nothing of the form *do you think reliabilism is right?* Each leaf carries the name of the position (or *unnamed* if nobody defends it), one sentence on the view, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate rather than pretending to separate them, and list the unoccupied combinations with a one-clause diagnosis of why nobody is there (incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored).

The cases, which I want used as the diagnostics:

1. The road to Larissa: a true belief about the way there gets you there just as well as knowledge does.
2. The coffee machine: a good cup produced by a machine that reliably produces good cups — has the reliability added anything to *this* cup? And the reply: the machine will go on producing good cups tomorrow, so the reliability is worth something after all. Does that reply rescue the view it was meant to rescue, or does it relocate the value from the belief to the faculty that produced it?
3. The loving grandmother: she truly believes her grandson innocent on evidence no epistemologist would credit, the belief serves every practical purpose knowledge would serve, and she seems beyond criticism.
4. The detective and the lucky tipster: the same true belief about who did it, one inferred from the evidence, one handed over by an informant with no track record.
5. Barn-façade county: a true belief produced by a process that is reliable where the subject actually is but fails in nearby cases she cannot tell apart.
6. Two students who both truly believe the theorem; one can derive it, say why the steps force the conclusion, and answer what-if-we-drop-this-assumption questions.
7. The good informant: what a small community needs is not a belief-level property but someone who can be relied on to pass the truth on and to speak up — is the valued item a status of persons?
8. Stakes: one and the same true belief suffices for the low-stakes decision but not the high-stakes one; is that stakes-sensitivity part of the value story, or a rival to it?

Leaves that must appear somewhere, under these names or better ones: the dissolution (no value difference to explain, or knowledge is just true belief), the practical-guidance answer in the spirit of Plato's own (knowledge is tied down and so survives perturbation), process reliabilism with a swamping reply, the faculty or dispositional version of reliabilism, the credit or virtue-theoretic answer (truth attributable to the agent's cognitive agency), the anti-luck-plus-ability hybrid, the knowledge-first refusal to explain knowledge in terms of its parts, the view that knowledge is the norm of practical reasoning (with or without stakes-sensitivity), the social or genealogical view on which the concept flags good informants, and the view that the value problem is genuine but insoluble for knowledge because the good we were after is understanding. Add any further leaf the space allows, and mark unnamed ones as such.
