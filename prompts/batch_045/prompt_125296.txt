I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and I sit in on a lunchtime reading group at the hospital where my partner works. We did a session last month on naturalism versus normativism about health and disease, and one of the residents built an argument that I've been turning over ever since. It seems to prove far too much, but I can't find where it leaks, and I'd like someone to name the step and price the alternatives. Here it is, cleaned up from my notes: (1) Any usable account of disease has to return a verdict on individual cases — for this patient and this condition, yes or no. (2) Naturalism says: a condition is a disease just in case some part or process in the person is performing below the statistically normal level of function for that person's reference class. (3) A reference class is just a partition of a population; biology may fix the species, but nothing in biology fixes how fine to go inside it. (4) Refine far enough — ancestry, genome, diet, occupation, medication, altitude — and every real patient comes out average: given everything true of them, their numbers are exactly what you'd predict. So, on (2), nothing is a disease. (5) Coarsen far enough — all humans, all ages, all states — and perfectly ordinary conditions (being three years old, being pregnant, being asleep) fall below normal function. So everything is a disease. (6) The theory itself puts no constraint on how fine or coarse to go, so (2) can be made to return whichever verdict you want in any actual case. (7) When a verdict flips with a choice the theory leaves open, the theory isn't reporting a fact about the patient; the choice is being made for us, because of which differences we happen to care about clinically. (8) So the value-free core isn't value-free: our interests are doing the work at exactly the point where the reference class gets fixed. (9) So normativism is true: at bottom, calling something a disease is a value judgement about it. (10) But then whatever a society disvalues can qualify as a disease, and medicine ends up pathologising whatever the people in charge dislike. (11) So there is no stable fact of the matter: the question 'is it a disease?' is always a negotiation, and the three specialists who gave my daughter three different answers weren't disagreeing about a fact at all.

I don't accept (11). The two claims I'd most like to keep are: (i) there is a fact of the matter about whether my daughter's condition is a disease — she's seven, she's been labelled with idiopathic fatigue syndrome after two years of tests that keep coming back in the normal range, and three specialists have given three answers — even if nobody currently knows what that fact is; and (ii) whether it's a disease isn't settled by what anyone wishes were true. As a stress test I made up a case: call it congenital hypoalgesia type V. Roughly one person in three hundred is born with it; they report about a third of typical pain, they have no other measurable difference, their wounds heal faster, and in my imaginary cohort data they have slightly higher completed fertility than matched controls — yet they score below the third percentile on pain-threshold testing for their age and sex. It looks like a disease on one reading of (2) and not on another, and I'd like to know what that tells me.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? Walk my numbering, tell me why the step you reject looked airtight before I looked closely at it, and price the main alternatives — including defining function by selection history instead of by current statistics, and the hybrid view that a disease is a dysfunction plus a harm — and say what each option does with my invented hypoalgesia case and with my daughter's. Open with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating of my question. British English, please.
