**Question 4 (25 marks).** Read the following case, which I'll call *Compensating Instruments*.

Vera is a technician in a water-testing laboratory. The lab's protocol for lead concentration requires two independently calibrated probes, North and South, to be placed in the sample; the technician averages the two readings and records the average. The protocol has an excellent track record, and both probes were calibrated against a standard that morning and passed. Vera runs a reservoir sample: North reads 12.3 ppb, South reads 11.7 ppb. She averages them, gets 12.0, and believes that the sample's lead concentration is 12.0 ppb. Her belief is true — the concentration is exactly 12.0 ppb. But both probes have drifted since calibration: North now reads 0.3 ppb high and South now reads 0.3 ppb low, and the two errors happen to cancel. A discrepancy of that size is well within what the protocol treats as ordinary measurement noise. Vera's evidence does not distinguish the actual situation from one in which the concentration is 11.7 ppb and the drifts are +0.5 and +0.1 (readings of 12.2 and 11.8), in which the same procedure, applied to the same evidence, would again have delivered 12.0. She has no way of detecting the drift from anything available to her, and no reason to suspect it.

Three candidate necessary conditions on knowledge:

(C1) *No false lemma.* S knows that p only if S's justification for p proceeds through no false statement and is not defeated by any truth.
(C2) *Appropriate causation.* S knows that p only if S's belief that p is caused, in the way processes of that kind normally operate, by the state of affairs that makes p true.
(C3) *Safety.* S knows that p only if, in nearly all nearby possible worlds in which S believes p formed in the same way as in the actual world, p is true.

(a) Does Vera know? Answer yes or no, then name the single feature of the case that carries the most weight for you. [4 marks]
(b) For each of C1, C2 and C3: say whether Vera's belief satisfies the condition; say whether the verdict that condition delivers matches your verdict in (a); and say what the case shows about the condition — whether it handles the case successfully, is merely unhelpful here, or is refuted by it. [5 marks each]
(c) A classmate says: this is the fake-barn case in a lab coat — the causal chain is intact, the truth of the belief is a matter of luck, and there is no new moral. Is that right? State precisely what *Compensating Instruments* shares with the barn case and what, if anything, it does that the barn case does not — in particular, whether it discriminates between any two responses that the barn case does not discriminate between. [6 marks]

**The student's answer.**

*(a)* No, Vera does not know. Her belief is true and she is justified: she followed the lab protocol exactly, the calibration records are clean, and there is nothing available to her that would indicate a malfunction. So this is a Gettier-style case — justified true belief that falls short of knowledge. The feature doing the work is that the accuracy of her belief depends on a coincidence. Two independent errors cancel exactly, and had either drift been slightly larger or slightly smaller, the same procedure applied to the same evidence would have delivered the same figure of 12.0 for a sample whose real concentration was different. Her evidence does not discriminate between the actual situation and those nearby ones, so relative to her epistemic position it is luck that she landed on the truth.

*(b)* **C1.** Vera's justification rests on no false statement. Both readings are correctly reported, the arithmetic is correct, and the procedure she follows is the lab's validated one. So C1 is satisfied and delivers the wrong verdict. This is the familiar pattern: the no-false-lemma condition was built for Gettier's original cases, where the subject reasons through a false disjunctive premise, and it has no grip on a case like this one, where nothing false appears anywhere in the subject's reasoning. Nor can it be repaired. Any fourth condition strong enough to exclude a case in which every premise the subject relies on is true would have to require that her justification guarantee the truth, and then no contingent empirical belief would ever count as knowledge. The failure is therefore not specific to C1: it shows that the project of adding a fourth condition to the classical analysis is hopeless.

**C2.** The causal condition is satisfied too, and this is a more interesting failure. The concentration of 12.0 ppb is a cause of Vera's belief: it causally affects both probes, the readings cause her to apply the protocol, and the protocol delivers her belief. Nothing exogenous intervenes, so there is no deviant causal chain of the kind a lesion or an accident would produce. So C2 predicts knowledge, which is the wrong verdict, and the case is a genuine counterexample to the causal theory, just as the fake-barn case was. The contrast is instructive: in the barn case the chain is intact and the landscape is full of facades, whereas here the setting is an ordinary lab and the defect lies inside the method itself.

**C3.** The safety condition is also satisfied. Vera's belief is sensitive: had the concentration been different — 12.1 ppb, say — then, with the drifts as they are, the procedure would have delivered 12.1 and she would have believed that instead. So she would not have believed that the concentration was 12.0 if it had not been 12.0, and that is exactly what the anti-luck condition asks for — that the belief track the truth across nearby cases. Since safety is the leading anti-luck condition and it is satisfied here, safety theorists are committed to saying that Vera knows, which is the wrong verdict. The case therefore shows that no modal anti-luck condition captures what is missing in Gettier cases.

*(c)* The classmate is half right. The two cases do share the structure he points to: in both, the causal condition is met, the belief is true and justified, and its truth is not creditable to the subject. But he is wrong that there is no new moral. In the barn case the defect is in the environment, and that leaves room for a relevant-alternatives reply — Barney could in principle have checked whether he was in fake-barn country. Here the defect is internal to the method, and there is nothing Vera could have checked that would have revealed the cancellation: recalibration is not required by the protocol and she has no reason to want it. So the case is immune to the "the subject should have ruled out the alternative" reply in a way the barn case arguably is not. Where the classmate is right is on the modal profile: in both cases the subject's belief is insensitive and unsafe, so the two cases fail exactly the same conditions and the theoretical moral really is the same one.
