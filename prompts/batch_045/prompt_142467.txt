Two other novelists at the residency I'm at got into it over dinner and I somehow ended up as referee, which I'm definately not qualified for. Someone had brought up the longtermist thing — that the far future might hold astronomical numbers of lives, so it dwarfs everything else in expected value, so it should dominate what we do now.

Mara said the simulation argument deflates all of that. If we're in a simulation, then the future isn't open-ended in the way the fanatics need: it ends whenever the people upstairs stop paying for the run, and its ceiling is their compute budget, not the lifetime of stars. Its like characters in one of my novels worrying about their pensions — there is no page 400.

Jonah said it does the opposite. The reason to take the simulation argument seriously in the first place is that simulations would be cheap and numerous, so most minds would be inside them. That means more at stake, not less — and even if this particular run gets switched off, the other sims go on, so shutdown isn't the end of value.

Mara said he'd swapped the question. Fanaticism isn't about how much value exists, it's about what we can steer from here. You can't reach the other sims and you can't reach the base layer, so your leverage is confined to one run with an unknown expiry date. Big distant payoffs you can't causally touch don't generate reasons just by being big.

Then it got stranger. Jonah pointed out that if they run the same sim many times with variations — the cheap thing to do — then either your choice is repeated across all those runs, which multiplies your leverage, or your decision-theoretic weight is split between them, which divides it, and he couldn't tell which. Mara added that if sims run sims, most observers are in the deepest, cheapest, most resource-starved layer, so conditional on being simulated you should expect a short cramped future, which she took as support for her side. Jonah's last move was that the whole argument eats its own inputs: estimates of how many sims get run rest on assumptions about physics and computing in base reality, but if you're in a sim then all your evidence about physics is evidence about how the sim was coded, so you can't use your physics to estimate the number of sims in either direction. Mara said fine, but then you also can't use it to say how long the sim lasts.

I really can't tell who's winning. Can you go through it move by move — which specific points are right, which are confused — and say what both of them are missing, especially about whether any of this should actually change what somebody does on a tuesday morning?
