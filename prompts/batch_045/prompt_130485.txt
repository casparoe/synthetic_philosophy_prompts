Hey — I need a workshop design, and this has stopped being hypothetical for me.

I do a couple of shifts a week on the front desk at a small community clinic in a neighbourhood where maybe a dozen or so first languages get spoken, and we're redoing our take-home cards (how to use the spacer with your inhaler, when to come back, what to do if the dizziness doesn't go away) as pictures only, because the translated versions are permanantly out of date and half the time the language we need isn't in the folder. One of our board members keeps saying pictures don't need translating, everyone understands a picture. I went looking for where that idea actually comes from and landed on Otto Neurath — the Vienna Circle guy who spent years building a picture language for teaching social and economic facts to adults, on the view that images stick in memory and don't have to be translated, and that you end up needing explicit rules for things like repeating a figure to mean more of it. I dont need the history nailed down and I'm not writing this up, so no footnotes or reading list.

What I want: next month I'm running a 90-minute training session for our volunteers — about 14 of them, mixed ages, none of them philosophers, some of them the people who actually hand the cards out. I want them to feel for themselves why pictures don't need translating is half true and where exactly it stops being true, before anyone says the word convention. No mini-lecture at the front. It has to be something I can run on a Tuesday night in a church hall with printer paper and markers, and whatever wins the last round I'm genuinely going to print and try on real patients — so small stakes, but real ones.

Two things I want built in, because they're what we keep failing at. First: our cards constantly need to say things like about 1 in 20 people get a rash, and if you're also on the blood thinner, don't — quantity, probability, conditionals, prohibition — and my hunch is that's exactly where pictures stop working and people start smuggling in shared conventions without noticing. Second: we argued last week about whether a photo would be more universal than a line drawing, and nobody could say why; if your design can make that disagreement show up as something they do rather than something I announce, great.

I'd also like the readers in the exercise to get no chance to ask questions — one-way, like real signage — and at least one round where the card has to say something about itself (that it's out of date, that you should ask a nurse instead of following it), because I suspect that's the place where the whole idea really breaks.

Sections please, in this order:

Setup and materials — about 150 words. Who's in the room, what you hand out, what's banned (letters and numerals obviously, and I'd also ban the tick, the cross and traffic-light colours — tell me if that's a mistake).

The rounds — the main bit, about 600 words, with a small table: round, what the designers have to encode, what you've rigged to go wrong, what they'll probably do instead. One-way communication throughout, different reader groups per round if that helps.

Scoring — about 150 words. Something that rewards being understood rather than being clever or pretty; no prizes, nothing that costs money.

Debrief — about 400 words. Map specific moments in the rounds onto specific bits of the idea: similarity versus convention, showing a situation versus saying that it holds, why adding rules starts turning pictures back into writing, and the photo-versus-drawing fight. Say where I should finally name Neurath's own conclusion — that pictures work as an aid inside a system with rules and words around them, not as a self-sufficient language — and what to do if the group lands on pictures are just ambiguous and stops there.

What will go wrong — about 250 words, five or six bullets. I especially want the version where all my volunteers share English so the exercise succeeds for the wrong reason, and the version where one confident person designs everything.

What the game can't do — about 150 words. Be straight with me about which part of the idea I'll have to say out loud myself, because no amount of markers and paper will get them there.
