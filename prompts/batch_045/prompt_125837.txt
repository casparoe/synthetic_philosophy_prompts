I am writing a seminar paper for a graduate reading group on the philosophy of biology, on whether life is the sort of thing that ought to be defined at all, and I want to hang the paper on one episode rather than on the usual parade of viruses, prions, mules and fires. The episode is the arsenic-life affair of 2010 and its aftermath: a strain of bacterium isolated from Mono Lake, a hypersaline and alkaline lake with an unusually high natural arsenic concentration, reported in a 2010 paper by Felisa Wolfe-Simon and colleagues and announced at a NASA press conference that December, with speculation about a shadow biosphere and about biochemistry that does not need phosphorus. As I understand the facts, the claim was that when starved of phosphorus the organism could substitute arsenic for a small fraction of its phosphorus and keep growing; the medium described as phosphorus-free in fact contained a small residue of phosphate from impure reagents; the DNA was never purified away from everything else before the elemental analysis; the chemical objection was that arsenate esters hydrolyse far too quickly in water to sustain a stable backbone; independent work in 2012 found no detectable arsenate in the DNA and established the strain as arsenate-resistant but phosphate-dependent; and Science retracted the paper in 2025. I also take it that the strain sits, by its ribosomal RNA, in an unremarkable place among ordinary salt-loving bacteria. I know the standard story about this episode, the premature press conference and the peer review that happened on blogs and the lesson about hype, and I do not want it. What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions between the episode and the theory.

The two accounts I want it run through are these. The first is the working definition NASA has used since the 1990s, roughly: a self-sustaining chemical system able to undergo Darwinian evolution. The second is a proposal of my own, developed with a friend in the group, which adds two clauses to any functional definition of that shape. The hereditary-vehicle clause says that before the question whether a system is alive is even well posed, one must be able to point to the physical component that carries heritable information in it; the question whether arsenic gets into the DNA is ill posed until you have identified what, in that system, plays the DNA role, and in a candidate shadow biosphere you have no right to assume it is nucleic acid at all. The single-sample clause says that an adequate definition must come with a test that could in principle return a verdict on one isolated specimen, without waiting for generations and without prior genealogical information; the capacity for Darwinian evolution looks like it fails this, since it is a property of lineages over time rather than of a specimen in a dish.

So here is the analysis I want. First, under each account, reconstruct what the claim that this was a new form of life would have had to mean, what the experiment would have had to show to establish it, and what the measurements actually taken could and could not have established; I care most about the gap between the operational test and the definitional claim. Second, say which features of the episode each account explains: the shape of the excitement, the fact that the decisive criticism landed on chemistry and on method rather than on the concept of life, the significance of the strain being genealogically ordinary, and whether the episode was ever really about life rather than about biochemistry or about independent origin. My own suspicion is that it conflates two questions, novel chemistry and second genesis, and I want to know which account keeps them apart and which lets them collapse together. Third, run it the other way: is the episode a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the account never anticipated? Does it show that definitions with chemical content do real work, with the instability of arsenate esters functioning as an exclusion rather than as a discovery, or does it support the Chyba and Cleland line that the definitional project rests on a misunderstanding and that what we need is a general theory of living systems? Notice also that nobody ever doubted the organism was alive, so say plainly whether this is a borderline case at all, and what it shows that the debate reaches for borderline cases the way it does. Where one of the accounts has nothing to say about some feature, say so rather than forcing it. Assume the background and do not summarise the episode or explain what Darwinian evolution is. Give me no citations, references or reading suggestions; I have library access of my own, and I want your own reasoning. Write the whole thing as continuous prose of roughly nine hundred to twelve hundred words, in paragraphs, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables, the way you would write it in an email to a colleague; and if I have got some fact above wrong, correct it in a single sentence at the point where it matters and carry on.
