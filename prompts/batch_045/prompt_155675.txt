A small foundation I do occasional consulting for is drawing up a call for proposals for a three-year, roughly five-person program on cohort kinds — the question of whether "Baby Boomers," "Millennials," "Gen Z" and their successors pick out anything real, or whether they are artifacts of decade-rounding, of the publishing industry's need for marketable labels, and of the human appetite for tidy stories about history. I sat next to a demographer at a conference dinner last month who said she thought the whole vocabulary should be retired, and next to a social historian who said the labels were indispensable precisely because they are partly constitutive — people organize their self-understanding around them — and I have been unable to decide which of them is closer to right. The program has a hard deliverable, which is what makes this an agenda-setting question rather than a seminar topic: by the end of year three it must hand something usable to two audiences, namely policy people who keep reaching for "generational equity" as a framing in pension, housing and climate debates, and editors who want a house rule for when generational language is legitimate. So I do not want a survey of the field or a reading list; I want a defended research agenda, with an ordering and the reasoning behind it.

Here is my provisional menu, with the labels I have been using to keep them straight. (i) Shear versus drift: call a shear a genuine discontinuity in a birth-year trend — one that survives controlling for age and period and that stands out against year-to-year noise — and call drift the smooth monotone change where every cut is as arbitrary as deciding where orange stops and yellow starts; how much of what gets reported as a generational difference is shear, and how much is drift discretized after the fact? (ii) The boring-cohorts problem: the most clearly real cohorts may be administrative ones — school-entry birth-date cutoffs, draft lotteries, a vaccine mandate with a date threshold — which are causally potent and utterly lacking in the cultural personality that makes generational talk attractive; if those are the paradigm real cohorts, what does that imply for the interesting ones? (iii) Indexicality: generational terms appear to be relative to a population, usually implicitly the United States, so that "Millennial" may be a property of a person-in-a-population rather than of a person; what would correct cross-national translation even consist in? (iv) Label feedback and retrospective redrawing: cohort traits are widely publicized, may thereby be partly produced, and the boundaries get quietly revised once a label catches on — does that make the kinds real in the way social kinds are said to be, or does it make them artifacts? (v) Projective cohorts: grant me, without my defending it here, that unlike "Baby Boomer," which named an already-observed fertility spike, the most recent cohort names entered wide circulation while their members were still young children, so that the group's defining experiences had not yet occurred; what is the ontological status of a kind named in advance of the events meant to constitute it? (vi) The identification problem: cohort, age and period effects are hard to separate, and it is not obvious whether the residual that gets labeled "cohort" is a cause or a bookkeeping remainder. (vii) Eliminability: do cohort variables explain anything over and above age, period, region, class and education, or is generational explanation always shorthand for a more boring decomposition? (viii) The normative question the policy people actually care about: can cohorts be units of justice at all, given that they are fuzzy-edged, population-relative, and composed of people who are also, simultaneously, members of overlapping age groups with conflicting interests?

Rank these, or replace them — I suspect several are the same question in different clothes, and I would like to be told which ones collapse into each other and which ones I have misdescribed. What I want from you is the ordering and its defense: which questions are upstream in the sense that other questions cannot be answered until they are settled, which are tractable in three years for a group of five with no new data collection beyond reanalysis of existing panels, which are neglected relative to how much turns on them, and which I should drop as overrated because the answer would not change what either audience does. Tell me explicitly where you are trading importance against tractability, and give me the signs over the next three years — a finding, a data release, a methodological result — that should move a question up or down the list; also tell me where you think my whole framing is wrong, since it is my framing and not yours that will end up in the call.
