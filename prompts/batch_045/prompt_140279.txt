I am putting together a graduate seminar handout on whether the truth of a knowledge attribution moves with the stakes, and I want its centrepiece to be a decision tree rather than a survey of views. Please build the tree from the logical space instead of from a list of names: begin with the questions, show which combinations of answers are coherent, then place the actual positions in the occupied cells and say, for each empty cell, whether nobody is there because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Constraints on the nodes. Six to eight branch nodes, eight to ten leaves. Every node must be a question about a case or about a normative role — whether knowledge is what licenses action, what licenses assertion, or neither — phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing what the answers lead to; I do not want any node asking whether knowledge depends on context, or otherwise inviting the student to name their theory. At least one node must force a choice about whose situation fixes the threshold: the subject's, the attributor's, the assessor's at the time of assessment, or a third party's. At least one must address whether merely raising an error possibility in conversation, without changing anyone's practical situation, moves the threshold.

The diagnostic work should be done by these cases rather than by the standard two-line bank vignette: (1) the subject's evidence is ordinary and nothing of hers is at stake, but a stranger's entire career depends on the proposition and he will act on her word alone; (2) the stakes are enormous but the subject neither knows nor has reason to suspect them, because they were created by an unopened will; (3) an enormous stake attaches to the proposition only after the subject has already acted, and the question is asked retrospectively; (4) the subject stands to gain a fortune if p and lose a fortune if not-p, with the two stakes equal and opposite; (5) the subject has trivial stakes and says that she knows, while an eavesdropper whose life depends on the same proposition says that this is false; (6) the evidence is the same as in the ordinary case and nothing prudential turns on p, but believing p falsely would itself be a serious moral failing, such as a betrayal of trust.

Leaves that must appear: classical invariantism that explains the stakes effect as a matter of what is conversationally conveyed rather than what is said; a debunking view on which the intuitions track something other than knowledge; subject-sensitive invariantism of the Stanley/Fantl–McGrath kind; contextualism of the DeRose/Cohen kind, on which the attributor's context sets the standard; contrastivism of the Schaffer kind; a relativism on which the truth of an attribution varies with the standards of whoever assesses it, so that a later assessment can reverse an earlier verdict; a view on which it is the moral cost of a false belief, rather than the prudential cost of a wrong action, that raises the bar; and, wherever you think it belongs, the relevant-alternatives-style treatment of error possibilities. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Finally, tell me which pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate and why — I expect trouble between contextualism and assessor-relativism, and between subject-sensitive invariantism and contextualism whenever the attributor's context happens to track the subject's stakes — and tell me which of my six cases is doing real work and which one collapses into another.

Lead with a single sentence stating your bottom line: which distinction actually does the separating, and how many of the questions turned out to be redundant. Then give the tree and the notes, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked for.
