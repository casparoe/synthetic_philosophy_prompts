Could you take on the role of a professional philosopher of technology — or an STS person who has actually been in this fight — and think it through properly, ideally doing a bit of private brainstorming about candidate diagnoses before you commit to one? I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist who has started sitting in on a graduate reading group in philosophy of technology, and we've been going in circles for about six weeks now on the artifacts-have-politics question. You know the set-up: Winner's Do Artifacts Have Politics? in Daedalus back in 1980, with the example everyone always drags out — the low overpasses on the Long Island parkways, supposedly too low for buses to get through, so that people without cars couldn't reach Jones Beach — and then the mechanical tomato harvester that reorganised a whole agricultural labour market. (I gather the history of the bridge case is contested, and that someone — Bernward Joerges, I believe — has pushed back on it; I'd rather you not lean on that example's factual accuracy either way.) One half of our group says the concrete really is doing political work, that the arrangement of stuff settles who gets what and who gets to do what, and does it durably and without anyone having to keep re-deciding. The other half says that's sloppy: the artifact is a means, the politics is in the intentions, the laws, the ownership, the people who maintain and enforce, and if you took the social arrangement away the object would just sit there being a low bridge. Neither side has moved by a millimetre and it's starting to feel like my old departmental arguments about interpretation of quantum mechanics.

Being an experimentalist I keep trying to turn it into tests, and that's partly why I'm suspicious of both sides. Here's my first attempt, tell me where it fails: (1) the reversal or ratchet test — in my world we'd call it hysteresis — the claim that the interesting thing about built stuff is the asymmetry between what it takes to install it and what it takes to undo it, so that a one-off decision becomes a standing condition that only sustained collective action can reverse; (2) the substitution test — if you removed the overpass and put up a sign saying no buses, with a fine and someone to enforce it, would the normative situation change? If the answer is barely, then the politics may be about the cost and reliability of enforcement rather than about physical form; (3) the invariance test — whether the artifact produces the same distribution of benefits and burdens across a wide range of surrounding social arrangements, or only in ours. My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that this is barely an empirical dispute at all: I think the sides are quietly answering different questions — one about blame and malleability and what we could have done otherwise, one about durable effects — and that the word 'technology' is sliding between an artifact, a system, and an infrastructure, while 'politics' slides between distributive effects, coercion, legitimation, and the shaping of what people can even want. But I also wonder whether there is a genuine hidden empirical question in there about the invariance test that nobody has done the work on.

So please: diagnose what kind of stalemate this is, using what each side actually says rather than a summary of positions. Is it verbal, is it a clash about which cases count as data or about what counts as evidence, is it bedrock intuitions about responsibility that neither side can argue for, or are they just solving different problems under one label? And then, given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction I'm missing, a formal result, an empirical project, a different question — or is this one where no progress should be expected, and why?
