I want a decomposed credence rather than a survey of positions, so please reason from the characterizations given below rather than from anything you might recall about specific authors, papers, or experiments: no citations, no reading suggestions, no appeals to what has or has not been shown in the literature. Treat the empirical patterns I stipulate as hypothetical.

The proposition I want a number on is this: in human subjects, phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness are distinct properties that can come apart in at least one direction. By phenomenal consciousness I mean the property in virtue of which there is something it is like for a subject; by access consciousness I mean a representation's content being globally available — poised for verbal report, for deliberate reasoning, for voluntary action, and for coordination across sensory and motor systems. Global workspace theory, in the form I have in mind, holds that what consciousness science studies just is the event of a representation's winning access to a capacity-limited workspace and being broadcast from there to a wide population of consumer systems, with a distinctive neural profile: relatively late onset, sustained and recurrent activity, long-range fronto-parietal involvement, and widespread information sharing across otherwise modular processors. Call that profile the broadcast signature.

Three hypothetical result patterns, to be used below:

1. Cross-modal rescue. A masked visual stimulus is not reportable and has no effect on any visual task, but when the same shape is delivered as a tactile pattern shortly afterward, subjects reliably recover the visual shape and say they were visually aware of it the whole time while being unable to act on it when it occurred.
2. Orphan broadcast. A stimulus produces the full broadcast signature, and subjects report a vivid experience of it, but the content is demonstrably unavailable: it influences no voluntary response, no reasoning, no cross-modal task, and cannot be reported at the time.
3. Silent channel. A neural interface routes content into the workspace bypassing the usual sensory pathways, so that the content becomes fully available — reportable, usable in reasoning, effective in action, accompanied by the broadcast signature — but subjects deny having any experience of it and deny it even when the same content arrives through the ordinary pathway and is experienced.

Please give me a single probability for the main proposition, and build it out of at least these components, each with its own number and one line of justification:

- that phenomenality can overflow availability in humans;
