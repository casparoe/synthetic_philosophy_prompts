I'm working on a piece about quantum-computing hype and trying not to embarrass myself, so I'm checking the pop-sci book I'm cribbing from. It sums up the Church-Turing thesis in a single paragraph:

"In 1936 Church and Turing independently established that every mechanical procedure — and so every computer anyone will ever build — computes exactly the functions a Turing machine computes, which is why one general-purpose machine can do anything a special-purpose one can. Since 'effectively computable' is an informal notion, the thesis isn't the kind of thing that can be proved; but nothing hangs on that, because its real content is just the mathematical fact that Turing machines, lambda calculus and recursive functions all pick out the same class of functions, so it isn't an empirical claim about the world either. Quantum computers will show it was too strong. There are problems a quantum machine can settle that no classical machine ever could — that's what quantum supremacy means — and that same gap is the reason a human mind might still be able to do things no machine can."

I suspect at least two things are off here: I think quantum speedups are a problem for how fast, not for what's computable in principle, and I think the bit about universal machines is a different result wearing the same name. But a logician friend also mutters that people have tried to prove versions of the thesis from axioms about what a mechanism can do, which sounds like it contradicts the "can't be proved" line, and now I don't trust any of my instincts.

Can you go through it roughly claim by claim: what someone who actually works on computability would accept, what they'd call a conflation or an overstatement rather than a blunder, and whether the book's closing moves — the quantum argument and the one about the mind — survive once the rest is fixed?
