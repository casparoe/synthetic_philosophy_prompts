Eight months ago my father had a brainstem stroke. He is awake and alert, follows everything that is said in the room, and can signal yes and no with his eyelids, which is slow and unreliable and fails entirely when he is tired. The clinical team told us to treat him as fully present, and I have never had any difficulty doing so: it does not seem to me that his mind is remotely in question, even though there is very little behavior left to point at.

For unrelated reasons, my working life now consists of building and evaluating conversational AI systems, and I am regularly asked, by colleagues, by reviewers, and by journalists, whether the things we build understand anything. I notice that I want one account of what licenses attributing a mind, covering both situations, and I have a nagging sense that the things I say in the two settings are not the same account. So I have tried to write down where I have landed, as neutrally as I can manage:

(1) The only evidence anyone can ever have for a mind other than their own is behavior. Appeals to what a thing is like on the inside cannot do evidential work, because we have no access to them and never will.

(2) Turing was right to replace the question with an operational one. Questions about minds that no possible observation could settle are not substantive questions, and pretending otherwise is how the topic stays stuck.

(3) Passing the imitation game in a strong sense would settle it for a machine: open-ended, adversarial, extended conversation, indistinguishable from a human's, where the responses are genuinely novel rather than retrieved or recombined. A system like that I would count as minded.

(4) A system whose answers are produced by selecting and recombining fragments of human-authored text according to statistical similarity would not have a mind, however indistinguishable its output, because it is relaying rather than thinking. This is not a point about how good the output is; it is a point about what is going on inside.

(5) Substrate is irrelevant. A criterion that turns on carbon rather than silicon, or on having a certain neural anatomy, is chauvinist, and I do not want any part of it.

(6) Mind-attribution is not really a yes-or-no matter. It comes in degrees, and it is partly a pragmatic decision about how we are going to treat something, not a discovery of a sharp fact.

(7) My father is conscious and present, and that judgment is not uncertain, not provisional, and not a pragmatic policy decision.

Please audit this set rather than lecture me on the topic. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, ordered from most severe to least, and for each, what exactly would have to be given up and what that costs me elsewhere in the list? Which of the apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and why? Which single revision would clean up the most? I have not decided which commitment I would sacrifice, and I would rather you told me than that I rigged the question.

Two things I especially want addressed. First, in my father's case, what seems to be doing the work is his neurology rather than anything he does, which looks like exactly the kind of criterion (5) forbids; say what happens to (5) if (7) stays. Second, for patients who produce no outward response at all but whose measurable brain activity can be voluntarily modulated to answer yes-or-no questions, does (1) reach them if behavior is read widely enough to include neural activity, and if it does, what does that wide reading do to the rest of the package?
