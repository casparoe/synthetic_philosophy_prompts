I'm a fourth-year PhD student in a psychology department that adopted a pre-registration requirement about two years ago, and our chair now wants the same thinking written into the dissertation guidelines as an explicit grading scheme for evidence. I sit in on a philosophy of science reading group, where we recently ran into what people call the problem of old evidence: if you already know that E, then your credence in E is 1, so conditioning on E cannot raise your credence in any hypothesis, and yet theories plainly do get confirmed by evidence everyone already had. I would like to understand what our department's actual practice presupposes about confirmation, because the parts of it seem to me to point in opposite directions, and I would rather reconstruct the implicit theory than be told that the rubric is merely sloppy.

Here is the practice, as concretely as I can state it. The scheme has three tiers. A hypothesis written down and time-stamped before the data were collected gets the top tier. A hypothesis formulated without consulting a dataset that already exists in the lab, and then tested against it, gets the middle tier. A hypothesis read off a dataset the researcher has already looked at gets the bottom tier, is dismissed as post hoc, and counts for almost nothing — no matter how well it fits or how large the effect is. But the same department also does two things that seem to cut against this. First, it treats a successful replication as confirmatory, even though the replicating team knows the original result and deliberately designs its study around reproducing it: the hypothesis there is as post hoc as anything, and nobody minds. Second, the results our subfield actually celebrates — the ones people give keynotes about — are cases where a theory accounted for an anomaly that everyone in the field had known about for years and that the reigning theory had conspicuously failed to explain; those are treated as stronger evidence than any pre-registered prediction, not weaker. A friend in the physics department says his field is the same way: the memorable cases are known recalcitrant data being accounted for, and he keeps using the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion as his example. He adds that his field sometimes institutionalises the worry in the opposite direction, by doing a blind analysis: the group fixes the entire analysis pipeline using simulated data and control regions of the real data, and only afterwards looks at the region where the new effect would show up, so that nobody can be accused of having tuned anything to the answer.

So my question is what view of confirmation this pattern of credit presupposes. Is novelty of prediction doing the real work, or is it only a proxy for something else — that the data were not available for tuning, that the theory had no spare degrees of freedom, that the evidence discriminates against a live class of rivals? Does the celebration of the known anomaly force the practice into a comparative or contrastive account, on which confirmation is a three-place relation between a hypothesis, evidence, and an alternative, rather than a boost to a hypothesis on its own? Does it also force confirmation to be indexed to an agent's epistemic situation at a time, so that whether something confirms depends on what you already knew and whether you helped yourself to the data when you built the theory? I especially want to know which parts the practice strictly presupposes, which parts merely sit well with it, and where it is underdetermined: could someone who keeps a broadly Bayesian framework and handles old evidence by some counterfactual or likelihood-ratio route endorse the rubric exactly as written, and would the blind-analysis practice look rational to them? Finally, if we replaced the implicit view with a straightforward comparative-likelihood view, what would have to change in the rubric — would the three tiers collapse into one, would the credit for explaining old anomalies survive, and would blind analysis become pointless?

Please answer in under 700 words, and don't cite or recommend any readings.
