I'm in the lower sixth and help edit my school's creative writing magazine, which is putting together a themed issue on the self this term; I'd like to contribute a story rather than an essay, but I want the story to actually do some work rather than just wear an idea as a badge. The thing I keep circling round is something my philosophy teacher sketched when I asked him about free will: that consciousness may not be the control room at all but a simplified dashboard — a summary the brain shows itself, in the way a computer desktop shows you icons rather than voltages — so that what I experience as weighing the reasons and deciding is really a press release issued after a great deal of sub-personal negotiation has already settled the matter. What I'd like you to write is a story that makes a reader feel the pull of that picture without ever stating it.

The setup I have in mind: a near-future private clinic offering, for a fee, a temporary intervention it markets under some bland corporate name like a "transparency window", which dials down the normal compression and lets a person watch, in real time, the parallel bargaining among the subsystems that produce her actions — the competing urges, the shifting coalitions, the moment the outcome is settled something like half a second before she feels herself choose. Make the protagonist a sixteen-year-old girl, Nadia, who isn't a curious volunteer but has a stake in the answer: she is on the brink of something she badly wants to have authored (refusing her father, or confessing to something that will cost her a friendship — your call), and she books the session because she wants to be sure the decision will be hers. Set the ordinary parts of it somewhere drab and specific — a night bus, a swimming pool at six in the morning, a sixth-form common room with the radiators clanking — and give me one scene in the clinic where the technician's reassuring jargon is clearly doing commercial work.

The part I care most about is that the story stay genuinely ambiguous between three readings I can't choose between myself: that the dashboard is a lie and the real agency is somewhere else entirely; that it is merely compressed rather than false, a faithful summary the way a good map is faithful, so that nothing has actually been debunked; and that the readout itself is only another dashboard, so the regress never bottoms out and "seeing the machinery" is just getting a sharper picture of it. I'd also like there to be a moment where all that extra information makes Nadia noticeably worse at something she is normally good at, because I suspect the compression is load-bearing for action rather than merely deceptive, and I want the reader to feel that. Please end on an image rather than a verdict, don't let anyone deliver a paragraph explaining the idea, and above all don't resolve it by having her simply decide to embrace the illusion, or by revealing at the last minute that it was all a dream or a simulation — I find that move a cheat. Aim for roughly a thousand to fourteen hundred words, third person, British English. Then, in a short note after the story that isn't part of the fiction, tell me which of the three readings you were trying to make vivid, and where you think a reader might reasonably draw the opposite moral — I'd like to know where the story leaks.
