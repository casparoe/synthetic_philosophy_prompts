**The Outside View Needs a Fixed Date**

*1. The promise.* How long will a given large polity last? The outside view answers: don't build a story out of the target's peculiar features — the current ruler's competence, the loyalty of the armies, the state of the treasury — because such stories are mostly noise dressed as insight. Instead, pick a class of comparable cases, read off a base rate, and adjust away from it only where you can explicitly justify doing so. The method buys discipline at the price of content, and its defenders think that is a good trade.

*2. The reference class problem arrives at once.* The outside view tells you to use a base rate, but not which one. All empires; land empires with literate administrations; empires that have survived at least one contested succession; empires at their greatest territorial extent; empires possessing a standing professional bureaucracy and a codified succession ritual — each is defensible, and their base rates differ by more than the entire spread a forecaster cares about. Nor is the choice itself an outside-view judgment: selecting the class embeds a causal theory about which features are prognostic, smuggled in behind a statistical routine. So the outside view looks like the inside view with one layer peeled off, and peeling further does not obviously terminate.

*3. Two proposed constraints.* I want to argue that part of this is fixable by two rules.

*(a) Contemporaneous identifiability.* A class is admissible only if a well-informed observer at the forecast date could have determined membership without knowing what happened later. This excludes classes defined retrospectively — polities that lasted at least two centuries, polities that were followed by a recognisable successor state, polities whose collapse was peaceful. Such classes are the ones a hindsight-soaked mind reaches for first, and their base rates are the most flattering, because membership is itself a partial report on the outcome.

*(b) Fixation at entry.* The class must be specified once, at the moment the target first qualifies for the broadest admissible class, and never revised thereafter. This is meant to block the *founding-date legerdemain*: because the starting point of a polity is conventional — a conquest, a dynastic change, a refounding, a change of constitutional form — an advocate can reset the clock, and with it the reference class, whenever the current class becomes inconvenient.

*4. The drift fallacy.* Here is the part I am least sure of. Suppose a forecaster works in units of total lifetime: at each date she selects a class of historical analogues that resemble the target as it is now, computes their mean *completed* duration, and reports that as the prediction for the target's total duration. Because resemblance is partly a matter of having survived — bureaucracies are built, succession rituals codified, provinces pacified over time — the class narrows as the target ages, and its mean completed duration rises. The forecast therefore slides toward optimism year by year without a single new piece of causal evidence. My claim is that this is a genuine artefact and not a discovery, and that it dissolves if one reframes the question in terms of the *hazard rate* rather than the total duration: what we want is the expected remaining life conditional on survival to now, and the shape of the hazard does all the work. Under an approximately constant hazard the age-conditioned forecast is flat, so the apparent improvement is pure drift; under senescence it should fall; under institutional consolidation it should rise.

*5. The sting.* If §4 is right, the outside view's verdict on an aged polity is entirely a bet on the shape of the hazard, and the outside view does not supply the shape. A dozen or so comparable cases cannot distinguish constant hazard from mild consolidation from mild senescence. And the founding-date convention contaminates even that estimate: every refounding resets the clock and manufactures the appearance of renewal. So the honest output of the method is not a number but a range across admissible classes, plus a declared founding convention, plus a pre-commitment made in advance about which class will be used. The outside view's real product is disclosure and pre-commitment; the number is decoration.

*6. The residual worry.* If the honest output is somewhere between a decade and a millennium, the outside view has purchased humility at the cost of content. I don't know whether to treat that as a defect, as the whole point, or as a sign that the method is being asked a question it cannot answer.
