Long time lurker here, finally posting because this has been bouncing around my head for a week and I can't tell which parts of it I actually believe. Context: I work in commercial admin for a mid-sized firm that bids for local council contracts, which means I am the guy who maintains the gifts and hospitality register. There's a £25 limit above which you have to log things, and I have never once been able to articulate why that number is where it is. A supplier sending us a box of Diwali sweets feels obviously fine — it's relationship maintenance, you'd be insulted to refuse it. A £40 bottle of wine from the same supplier gets flagged as a red flag. But the sweets are also, you know, from a supplier who wants us to keep buying from them. I've tried tabooing the word and it doesn't help; I end up drawing the line in the same arbitrary place.

So I went looking for something better and ended up reading this very long blog post (one of those 10k-word pop-dev-econ things, I think it was by someone at an NGO, I'd have to dig the tab back up) that takes the hard line that the whole modern concept of corruption is basically a Western export and the universalists are doing imperialism with a spreadsheet. I'm reconstructing from memory so maybe I'm the one mangling it, but the argument as I recieved it went roughly like this:

- The modern sense of "corruption" is a 1990s institutional invention — the word originally just meant rotting meat or the decay of a body, so there's no deep concept here that we're being unfaithful to. It was essentially coined by Western development agencies to describe behaviour in countries they were already disposed to look down on.
- The working definition everybody uses, abuse of public office for private gain, is either vacuous or circular. Every official gains privately from her salary, so the definition only does any work if you've already smuggled in a Weberian picture of an impersonal bureaucracy with rule-bound offices — i.e. you've assumed the conclusion about which norms are the right ones.
- In most of the world, what we'd call a bribe is a gift. Guanxi, wasta, blat, turning up at the hospital with an envelope — the line between gift and bribe is drawn by culture, not by morality, and it's the same behaviour we call networking or relationship-building when someone at a hedge fund does it.
- There's no such thing as a free gift anyway. Anthropologists have been saying since Mauss that every gift creates an obligation to reciprocate, so the gift/bribe distinction collapses and a gift is just a bribe with better PR.
- Clientelism is the same thing with extra steps: a patron hands out favours, clients hand over votes or loyalty, everyone knows the price. It's corruption with a relationship bolted onto it.
- Conclusion: "corruption" just names whatever the people with power want to prohibit. There's no non-question-begging standpoint from which to criticise any of this, and the anti-corruption industry is Western preferences with a spreadsheet attached.

I'm maybe 60% sympathetic to the gift stuff — my in-laws are from a country where you definately do not turn up at a government office empty-handed, and it genuinely isn't experienced there the way my compliance software thinks it is. But the conclusion smells like a motte-and-bailey to me, and I can't locate where the swap happens. Which is where I'd like help.

Can you go through that list claim by claim and tell me what a defender of the universalist side would just concede, what's a fair hit that's been overstated, what's an actual conflation, and where a real objection is being presented as a knockdown? I'm especially unsure about the Mauss point: it feels like it's doing something sneaky, because if it works it seems to make *every* gift a bribe including birthday presents, and if that's where it ends up then it can't be supporting the relativist conclusion it's being used for. And I'd like to know what the people who actually do ethnography on this stuff would themselves reject in that summary — my sense is that someone who's spent two years in a village is not going to say "it's all just power", but I don't know what the more careful version is. Also: does the conclusion follow from the parts that are true, or does it need a step that isn't in there?

The other thing. The only non-circular test I've been able to come up with myself is something like: an exchange is corrupt if it predictably leaves some identifiable third party worse off than they'd be if the official did what their role requires — call it the "who gets bumped" test. A bribe bumps whoever was ahead in the queue or the better bidder. But I'm worried it over-catches: the sweets-supplier is surely making his competitors marginally worse off, and every bit of relationship-building disadvantages whoever didn't do the relationship-building. And I'm worried it under-catches things like campaign finance or regulatory capture, where nobody identifiable gets bumped in any particular decision but the institution ends up serving something other than what it's for. Is that test salvageable, is it just the private-gain definition with extra machinery, or is there a version of it that handles clientelism — where there often isn't a queue and the person benefiting isn't obviously bumping anyone — better than the standard definitions do?
