Please act as a professor of philosophy who specializes in modal epistemology — the part of philosophy that asks how, if at all, we can find out what is possible and what is necessary. Work in stages and show your reasoning as you go: I would like a short planning paragraph first, then the instrument itself. Do not reach for a bibliography or for citations; I want your own analysis, and nothing here should require looking anything up.

I run a philosophy club at my high school, and we are making a one-page poster called a sorting flowchart: a reader starts at the top, answers a handful of questions about concrete cases, and ends up at a named position on the question of whether our ability to imagine or conceive something is any guide at all to whether it is really possible. The point of the poster is that it should sort people by what they are willing to say about cases, not by which slogan or which philosopher they already like. My classmates will be able to follow careful reasoning, but they have no technical background, so I need the cases in plain language and the technical vocabulary confined to the labels and the notes.

Here is what I need.

The instrument. A decision tree with five branch questions. Each branch question must be a short concrete case with two or three answer options, written so that a reader can answer it without knowing what position it leads to. Please keep every case to three sentences or fewer, with no symbolism and no jargon in the question itself. A hard rule: no node may ask, in the abstract, whether conceivability is evidence of possibility, or whether such-and-such is possible, or whether the reader accepts any named view. The sorting has to happen through case judgments only.

The questions I want included.

One must be the classical case of the two names for the same object: a competent speaker who has learned one name for the bright object seen in the evening and another name for the bright object seen before sunrise, and who has never checked that they are the same body. Give the reader options along these lines: yes, I can perfectly well imagine a world in which these turn out to be two different objects, and that seems like a real possibility; I can imagine it, but it is not really possible; I cannot really imagine it once I think it through properly; I have no way of telling whether it is possible.

One must be the case of a molecule-for-molecule physical duplicate of a conscious human being who has no conscious experience at all, with the same style of options.

One must test the negative direction of the test: a case where something is very hard or impossible to conceive, and the question is whether that failure is any sign that the thing is impossible. I would like this case to involve a claim whose truth nobody in the story is in a position to know the answer to — I was imagining a hard unsolved mathematical claim that no one has settled, though if you think a different sort of example does this work better, use it and tell me in one sentence why.

One must be an agglomeration test: two claims such that a subject can imagine each of them obtaining, but cannot imagine them obtaining together, where the combination is not ruled out merely by the meanings of the words. Ask the reader whether they treat the ability-to-imagine as something that survives putting two cases together. Then, in your notes, tell me whether the failure here, if there is one, is a real problem for using conceivability as a test or just an artifact of the example you chose.

The fifth question I invented myself, and I am not sure it is fair, so I want you to use it but also to police it. Call it the second-order test: can the reader imagine a situation in which their own capacity to imagine is systematically misleading about possibility — a case where they have a vivid, stable, fully coherent seeming-conception of some claim, they have reflected on it as hard and as long as they like, and yet the claim is impossible? This question should only be asked on the branches of the tree where the reader has already credited at least one of the earlier conceivings as genuine, so the tree will not be a uniform grid, and that is fine. If you think the question is rigged — if it merely restates the dispute, or if it begs the question against one of the leaves, or if every leaf has to give the same answer — say so plainly in a sentence or two and propose a repair.

The leaves. Seven to nine of them, including at least these: the view that conceiving something is genuine but defeasible evidence that it is possible, evidence that can be outweighed; the view associated with the two-dimensional framework, on which a suitably idealized and positive conceiving really does entail a kind of possibility; the view that some truths are necessary but knowable only through investigation of the world, so that the test reliably breaks down for those; the skeptical view that we have no dependable grip on modality here at all and that conceivability tells us little or nothing; the view that denies we can genuinely conceive the duplicate case in the first place; and a view I will call conceivability localism, on which the test is trustworthy in some subject matters and untrustworthy in others — you should say which domains a localist would pick and why, and mark the leaf as unnamed if in fact nobody holds it. Where you are confident that a position has a standard name or is standardly associated with a philosopher, label it that way; where you are not, write unnamed. Each leaf should get one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Build it from the logical space, not from the list of names. Before you draw the tree, list the answer combinations your questions permit and mark which cells the positions above actually occupy. For the combinations nobody occupies, give a single line each on why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Then flag the pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate. I suspect the idealized-conceivability theorist and the defeasible-evidence theorist will collapse together over some region of the space, and that the skeptic and the localist may be harder to pry apart than they look. For each such pair, say whether some further case would separate them and, if so, what it would have to be like; if nothing would, say that instead of inventing a distinction.

Finally, give me a scoring key: a table from answer patterns to leaves, plus a rule for what my club should do with a reader who gives an inconsistent pattern — for instance someone who treats the duplicate case as a genuine possibility while insisting that the two-names case is not possible after all. Tell me whether to call that a tie, a new leaf, or a mistake, and why.

One last constraint on length and format, since this is going on a poster: the whole thing should stay under about eight hundred words plus the tree itself, the tree may be drawn as an indented outline or in plain ASCII, and after the instrument please add a two-sentence note on which of my five questions is doing most of the work and which one I could drop without merging any leaves.
