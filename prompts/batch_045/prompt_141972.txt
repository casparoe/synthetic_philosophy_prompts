I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy-of-psychiatry seminar, and instead of a final paper our instructor has asked each of us to submit a piece of short fiction (1,200–1,500 words) that dramatizes one of the positions we read this term, followed by a brief structured note. I want mine to be about the skeptical view of mental illness that occupied the first three weeks of the course: the view that illness language is literal only where a condition can be located in the body as a lesion or a demonstrable physiological fault, that everywhere else it is a metaphor for difficulties in conduct, character, and circumstance, and that because the metaphor is a medical one it silently converts moral and political questions about what we may do to one another into technical questions about what a doctor may do to a patient.

The instructor's rule is that the story has to make the view work rather than knock it down. The character who holds it should be the most intelligent person in the room, and by the last page the view should have changed in some way — hardened into a sharper version of itself, revised so that it draws a different line than it used to, or dissolved into some other question entirely — but it must not be refuted by a dim opponent, by a coincidence, or by a plot twist.

Here is the situation I have in mind. A jurisdiction has recently replaced its civil-commitment law with a statute I'll call the Somatic Criterion Act. Under the Act, the state may confine, treat, or excuse a person against their stated will only where a condition satisfies two clauses: (a) it is a deviation from species-typical functioning, and (b) it can be identified by a competent observer without relying on the person's own account of what they take themselves to have reason to do. Clause (b) is the skeptical clause. It was written into the statute precisely to keep problems of living, choice, and character out of the clinic and to leave them where the drafters thought they belonged, in the domain of responsibility.

Write the story of the first hearing held under this Act, subject to the following:

- Third-person limited, past tense, from inside the head of a clinician in her fifties who helped draft clause (b) and who now sits on the panel that has to apply it.
- The petition before the panel is to detain and feed, for fourteen days, a man in his thirties who is not violent, not suicidal, and not confused: he is failing to eat and to attend to his affairs, and he declines all offers of help. The old law would have called this grave disability; under the new law it has to pass both clauses.
- Only three people in the room: the clinician, the man, and a lay member of the board — someone with no theory at all, a retired transit worker or the like — who asks the question that breaks the clause.
- The man is articulate and entirely unphilosophical. He reports that he still sees his reasons perfectly well: he can list them, rank them, and explain why they are good reasons. What has stopped is that seeing them no longer moves him. He is not exactly sad. He is not indifferent to what happens to him. He simply does not act, and he cannot say why, and he is not asking to be excused.
- The required turning point: the lay member asks about something the Act does not define — whether someone who can state a reason but cannot act on it is unable in the sense the statute means — and the clinician realizes that clause (b) is satisfied, so that the test she designed to keep the will out of medicine lets this man in.
- Taboo resolutions. No lesion is discovered during the hearing or afterwards; the man is not malingering and does not recover; there is no pharmaceutical villain, no act of violence, no death, and the clinician does not resign in protest. Whatever happens has to happen in the argument.
- The skeptical view is never named on the page. No character says that mental illness is a myth, and no character uses the word myth at all. The position has to be carried by what happens in the room.
- The narrator should not attach any real diagnostic category to the man, though the clinicians may use a coinage of their own.

After the story, give me a single valid JSON object, with no commentary before or after it, using exactly these fields:

- "characters": an array of objects, each with "name", "role", and "relation_to_the_view" (how this character stands to the skeptical position)

- "clinician_before": her understanding of the view at the opening of the story
- "clinician_after": her understanding on the final page
- "kind_of_change": exactly one of "hardened", "revised", or "dissolved"
- "best_line_for_the_skeptic": the single strongest thing said in the story on behalf of the skeptical view
- "best_line_for_the_patient": the single strongest thing the man says
