I've been reading rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days and I keep watching the same thing happen: heritability gets invoked, someone points out that it doesn't mean immutable and doesn't license between-group inferences, everyone nods, and three threads later the identical move shows up again. I more or less know the textbook defintion — it's a ratio of variance components, estimated within a defined population in a defined environment, not a measure of how genetic a trait is in any absolute sense, and it carries no direct claim about how a trait responds to intervention. What I've never seen is it doing honest work on an actual case, and the case I want it put against is the Flynn effect.

Here's the episode as I have it, and correct me where I'm wrong since I'm going from memory: through most of the twentieth century raw IQ scores rose in essentially every country that was testing, on the order of three points a decade, continuously and roughly linearly up to somewhere around the 1990s; the gains are largest on the abstract/fluid stuff like Raven's matrices and much smaller on vocabulary, arithmetic and general knowledge; and there's some evidence of a plateau or mild reversal in a few developed countries afterwards, including Nordic military conscript data. Take that shape as given even if my numbers are off. Now do the mapping properly. Take a heritability estimate for IQ in adults in a rich country — call it somewhere between 0.5 and 0.8 — and say exactly what quantity it is about: what the trait is, what the population is, what the environment is, and what the Flynn effect is a change in. Then say whether that estimate has anything to say, even in principle, about a shift in the mean of an entire distribution, or whether it is silent by construction. My own suspicion, which I want checked rather than flattered, is that it is silent by construction and that the standard cautions undersell how complete the silence is: heritability is estimated from differences between individuals, so a cause that moves everyone in a cohort by roughly the same amount is invisible to it no matter how large the effect, and the whole research program is blind to level shifts in a way nobody spells out. Is that right, and does that kind of blindness have a name?

Then run it the other direction and tell me what the case does to the concept — and I want actual arguments here, not gestures. First: if the gains are concentrated on fluid subtests and nearly absent on crystallized ones, then either what rose isn't the latent trait whose heritability was estimated, or the tests aren't measuring the same construct across cohorts; on either reading, does the trait in the estimate stay fixed enough for the number to mean anything across a fifty-year gap? Second: the gains look biggest at the bottom of the distribution, which on a variance-components picture should compress environmental variance and push within-cohort heritability up; is that a genuine prediction the concept makes, is it the kind of thing that could actually be checked, and if the honest answer is that nobody knows or that it isn't checkable, say that instead of inventing a result. Third: is there any coherent reading on which you could ask for the heritability of the Flynn effect itself, or the heritability of the difference between two cohorts, or is that a flat category error — and if it is one, why does the move keep feeling tempting? And finally the thing I actually want out of this: is the Flynn effect a confirmation of the nothing-about-mutability line, an anomaly for it, or a case the concept was never built to see, which leaves it looking worse than its defenders usually admit? Say precisely where the concept should refuse to speak, and if some other framing — reaction norms, measurement invariance, whatever you think fits — does better on this case, say what it buys.
