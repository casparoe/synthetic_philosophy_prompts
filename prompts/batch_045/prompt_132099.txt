we were talking in my government class about how soldiers on both sides of a war get treated as basically the same: a guy fighting for the side everyone thinks is in the wrong isn't a murderer for shooting enemy soldiers, and if he gets captured he gets prisoner-of-war treatment and goes home after instead of being put on trial. My teacher called this the moral equality of combatants or something like that. What I actually want to know is what a setup like that presupposes — does it have to assume that putting on a uniform cancels your responsibility for what you do, or can it rest on the idea that being blameworthy and being liable to be attacked are two different things, or is it really just that nobody can tell whos side is just until the war is over so we agree to treat both the same?

And here's the part I find wierd: most countries will punish a soldier who refuses to deploy, but the same countries pay full veterans benefits and pensions to soldiers who fought in — even volunteered for — wars that basically everyone later agrees were unjust, and nobody ever talks about taking any of it back. What does that combination of practices presuppose about responsibility? Does it strictly need the philosophical view people normally use to defend it, or could several different views explain it, and which views does it quietly rule out? I care way more about the reasoning than about a summary of the debate, so please don't just hand me a reading list.

One more thing I invented: suppose a country paid compensation to the families of enemy soldiers its own soldiers killed, on the grounds that the killing was allowed but was still a real harm to the person and the people who depended on him. Would that fit together with the rest of the stuff above, or does it break some assumption the rest of it is leaning on?
