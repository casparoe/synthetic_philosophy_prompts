I have been trying to work out how the two main cosmological arguments relate to one another, and I keep arriving at a conclusion that does not seem to be shared by anything else I have read on the subject, which makes me suspect I have misunderstood something fairly basic.

My understanding is this. The kalam argument and the argument from contingency are standardly presented as members of a single family: both begin from some general feature of the cosmos, both infer a cause or ground of it lying outside the cosmos, and both are said to arrive at God. They appear to differ only in their starting point. The kalam argument requires that the universe began to exist, and it tries to support that premise in two ways: empirically, by appealing to Big Bang cosmology, and a priori, by arguing that a completed infinite series of past events is impossible. The argument from contingency is presented as the more cautious of the two, because it does not need a beginning at all. An everlasting universe would still be contingent, would still contain within itself no reason why it exists rather than not, and so would still require a necessary ground.

From this I have drawn two conclusions. First, that the contingency argument is the more general argument, and the kalam argument is what you get when you bolt onto it one additional premise — a first moment of time — that is both metaphysically contested and hostage to whatever cosmology turns out to be true. Second, that the two arguments are rivals for the same job. Both are routes to the same conclusion, so the only live question is which route is better supported, and a successful defence of either would render the other redundant. The steady production, in contemporary cosmology, of models in which our expanding universe is a local episode within a larger and possibly beginningless reality looks to me like it simply settles the rivalry in favour of contingency.

I have tried to test this with a case. Imagine a cosmos consisting of an endless series of temporal stages, each produced by the stage before it, with nothing outside the series acting within it; and stipulate further that there are no facts about the series as a whole beyond the facts about the individual stages and the causal relations among them — call this the no-further-fact stipulation. As I see it, this case is precisely the one the contingency argument was designed for and precisely the one the kalam argument cannot handle, which confirms that kalam is the special case. Yet I am stuck on why anyone would still prefer kalam. Its defenders say it is the metaphysically cheaper argument, since it assumes only that whatever begins to exist has a cause, and not the strong principle of sufficient reason that the contingency argument needs in order to demand an explanation of the cosmos as a whole. But if every stage in my imagined series has a causal explanation, and the stipulation rules out any further fact about the series for such a principle to latch onto, what is left for the contingency argument to explain? And why would the principle that every member of a series is explained fail to explain the series itself?

Am I right that contingency is the more general argument and that kalam is a dispensable special case of it? Is my no-further-fact stipulation a fair way to press the point, or does it assume something it should not? And what is the honest reply to the defender who insists that his argument gets to the same place with fewer metaphysical commitments?
