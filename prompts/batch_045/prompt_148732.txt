I teach intro ethics — big lecture, ~280 students, twice a year, and the discussion sections are run by TAs who are mostly grad students from other departments and who get one 90-minute training session from me, total. Every time we do the Republic book 2 material — Glaucon, the ring, why be just when being just costs you and nobody is watching — I make them write a 500-word paper answering him. That's ~280 papers a semester that somebody has to get through in about ten minutes each, with a comment that's actually worth reading, and students can appeal to me if they think the TA got it wrong.

What I want from you is a procedure my TAs can run on a paper, in this order, that decides one question: does this paper actually answer Glaucon, or does it quietly answer a near neighbour instead? Not "is the argument good" — I read for that when it matters. Just: whatever this thing is arguing, is it an answer to the challenge, or an answer to a question Glaucon already took off the table? (I tell them in lecture that he's stripped away reputation, consequences, and the wages of justice, and that he's set it up so the just person gets the reputation of injustice and the unjust person gets the reputation of justice — so "justice pays eventually" is precisely what he asked you not to say.) For each step tell me what counts as a pass, where the procedure stops with a hard no, and where it hands the paper to me because the question there is one of judgement rather than fact. And be honest about which steps are really just a TA's gut in a flowchart costume — I'd rather know then pretend.

Constraint: the checks have to be worded so someone with no philosophy can run them. If a step requires recognising a reductio or an internal reason, it's useless to me.

One wrinkle. For the last two years I've been running my own variant along side the original: in my version the ring doesn't only make you invisible, it also means that what you do while wearing it leaves no trace on you — no memory of it, no habit formed, no change in what you later want, no guilt, no self-knowledge, nothing to be proud or ashamed of afterwards. I use it because it kills three families of answer at once: the you'll-get-found-out family, the you-become-a-bad-person family, and the you'd-betray-your-own-deepest-projects-and-lose-your-integrity family. I want to see whether anyone can produce a reason that doesn't run through one of those channels. So the procedure has to work on papers answering my version too. And seperately: tell me whether my variant is a fair sharpening of Glaucon or whether I've rigged the question so that nothing could possibly answer it. I genuinely can't tell anymore.

Then run the procedure on three papers I get versions of every semester. (1) In a world where everyone had a ring we'd all be worse off, so we agree to be just, and I keep my agreement even with the ring on. (2) The self is constituted by its choices, so there's no me left over who'd get to enjoy the loot — the person who comes out of the ring just is someone else. (3) A paper that is genuinely beautiful and is my favourite every single time — you'd have to live with yourself afterwards, you'd know what you'd done, and that knowledge would eat you alive, so you're still worse off. I suspect the procedure is going to tell me (3) is a dodge, and I'd like to see whether it can say that without also saying something stupid.

Hard cases the procedure has to handle: a paper that rejects the framing of the challenge outright in a way that is itself a real philosophical move; and a paper that answers a version of the question Glaucon wasn't asking but does it brilliantly. Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do — I don't want to hand a TA something that makes her think the hard part is finished.
