Me and my flatmate have been going back and forth on something for weeks and it's gone stale, so I want to hear two people who actually know what they're talking about argue it properly. Bit of context first.

I never went to university — I drive a van for a bakery and listen to philosophy podcasts on my shift, so I probably get some of the terminology wrong. The thing I got snagged on was this complaint by some British philosopher (Williams? I think that's the name) about a man who can save either his wife or a stranger: the point is supposed to be that if he stands there and adds the extra thought that saving his wife is what morality permits, or is what the best impartial theory recommends, then he's had one thought too many — the healthy case is just that he saves his wife, and bolting the justification on top somehow makes things worse rather than better. And it generalises: a morality that is always asking you to step outside your own attachments and weigh them against everyone elses is asking you to stop being the person who has those attachments, which is the bit people call the integrity objection. I'm half convinced and half not. Half convinced because yeah, it does feel gross to think about your own kid in those terms. Half not because if your deepest commitments are never allowed to be questioned, you're just running on whatever your upbringing installed, and that seems like an accident rather than a justification.

So here's the bit I made up, which is really why I'm writing. What if you split the job in two? Once a year you sit down for an afternoon with a spreadsheet and decide, as impartially as you can, what share of your money and hours and loyalty goes to your own people versus to strangers, and you build your rules of thumb out of that. Then for the rest of the year you never run it again — you just love your daughter, finish your album, whatever, no calculating in the moment, no one thought too many. I call it the once-a-year ledger, and it feels like it should give the impartial person everything they want and the integrity person everything they want, which is what makes me suspicious of it. And then a nastier version: what if you don't do the audit at all, and instead an app does it for you and just hands you your numbers — this month, 8 hours and £200 to strangers — and you follow it without ever knowing the reasoning. Now you definately don't have the corrupting thought. But it feels worse somehow and I can't say why.

Could you write me a dialogue between two friends arguing about all this? Lets say they've just finished a shift volunteering at a food bank and they're washing up in the kitchen afterwards, they've had this argument before and they respect each other. One of them — call her Marta, she's a nurse, she's actually had to decide who gets her attention when there isn't enough of her to go round — takes the impartial line. The other — call him Jules, he's a carpenter who plays in a band, and he's the one who brought up the one thought too many thing — takes the integrity line. Both of them should be sharp, neither should be a pushover or a strawman, and they should actually answer what the other one just said instead of delivering speeches past each other. They can interrupt each other and swear a bit.

Things I'd want them to really get into:

- Is the complaint actually about the thought you have in the moment, or about what ultimately justifies what you do? The once-a-year ledger looks fine on the first reading and useless on the second, or the other way round, I can't tell which.
- Whether the ledger is just consequentialism with better bookkeeping, or whether deciding in advance that your daughter gets some percentage of you has already done the damage.
- The app version. If outsourcing the calculation gets rid of the bad thought, why does it feel worse? Is it worse?
- Whether you can actually turn the dial. If the audit says you're overspending on your family, can you love them less on purpose? If you can't, is the audit pointless, or is it a standard you're simply failing to meet?
- Whether someone without deep attachments — a bit of a drifter, nobody they'd especially miss — even gets to use this objection, or whether for them it collapses into a fancy way of saying I'd rather not.
- Whether that other complaint people mush in with this one — that you're on the hook for all the bad stuff you failed to prevent, not just the stuff you did — is secretly the same argument or a completely different one.

And one more: where the impartial calculation tells you to do something you find horrible. Not just skipping a night out and donating the money, but something like taking a job you'd hate for twenty years, or hurting one person to stop twenty others being hurt. Does the one thought too many thing even show up there, or does it turn into something else in that case?

Make it a proper long one, 1200 words or more. By the end I want both of them to have given ground on something real, and I want them to have got clear about where the actual disagreement between them lives — but don't tie it up in a bow, and don't let either of them simply win. And keep them talking like actual people: no name-dropping of books or authors, no some philosophers say, no footnotes, just them and the argument.
