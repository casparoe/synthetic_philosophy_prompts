Setup: binary state of the world (a new drug either works or doesn't), a long queue of agents, each gets a private signal of some fixed accuracy strictly between a coin flip and certainty, each sees the whole history of earlier actions but no one else's signal, then each picks accept or reject and is paid for matching the state. (1) Every agent maximizes expected payoff conditional on everything they have: the public history plus their own private signal. (2) Since signals are informative and independent of the history, each agent's posterior given history-plus-signal strictly differs from the posterior given the history alone — their own evidence really does move them. (3) So each agent decides on the basis of information nobody else has; their action is a response to genuinely new evidence, not a copy of what came before. (4) If each action is a response to genuinely new evidence, then each action tells later agents something they didn't already know, so the chain of actions can't go informationally dead. (5) If information keeps flowing down the chain, the group is effectively pooling more and more independent evidence, and pooled independent evidence drives posterior to the truth — so as the queue gets long the probability the group settles on the wrong action should go to zero. (6) But the textbook model says the opposite: there's some fixed probability, not shrinking with group size, that the first couple of agents both get misleading signals, a wrong run starts, everyone after them piles on, and the group is wrong forever. (7) So either the model's agents aren't rational or rationality is incompatible with herding — which is absurd, since explaining herding as rational is the whole point of the model.

I want to keep (1) and (2): they're the model's own assumptions and (2) is close to just Bayes. But (3) to (4) and (4) to (5) both look fine to me too, which is why I think I'm missing something.
