I have a ninety-minute seminar next month with a graduate reading group, and I need a plan for how to explain one idea — not the explanation itself.

The audience: cognitive-science and vision-science PhD students. They are fluent in signal detection theory, Bayesian cue integration, and the inattentional-blindness literature; none of them has any Indian philosophy, and they are reflexively suspicious of anything that looks like a taxonomy.

The idea: within the Nyāya framework of pramāṇas (perception, inference, comparison, and testimony — the four the school standardly recognises), knowledge of an absence is a problem case. Knowing that the cat is not in the hall does not look like perception, because there is nothing there to be perceived, and assimilating it to inference looks like smuggling in a universal generalisation that no one has actually established. One way out is to admit a further pramāṇa of non-cognition, of the kind several other schools accept; another is to argue that such knowledge is really perception or inference in disguise. I want the group to feel the pull of both resolutions before I tell them anything about how the dispute went.

What I want from you:

1. The dependency structure. What has to be in place before "how can you come to know that something is not there?" even registers as a hard question for people who already have a perfectly good story about failed detection? My assumption is that they first need some grip on what a pramāṇa is for — a route to a successful cognition with a normative dimension, not a justification and not a decision rule — and on the conditions a genuine cognition has to meet (novel, undefeated, about things as they are). Which parts of that are genuinely load-bearing for the negative case, and which can I merely gesture at? I also plan to introduce the Sanskrit term only once the phenomenon is on the table; tell me if that order is wrong.

2. The carrying example. I want one case, varied along a single dimension, to carry the whole session. My candidate: you come home, open the front door, and conclude that the cat is not in the hall, because she always comes into the hall when that door opens and you neither see nor hear her. Is this the right case, and what must it have and lack? I think it needs (a) an explicit eligibility condition — the cat is the sort of thing that would have been perceived by you under the conditions actually obtaining; and (b) a matched failure case, e.g. the same reasoning while you are in another city and cannot check. I think it must not rest on an explicitly formulated generalisation, or on a probabilistic judgement, or on a threshold applied to an internal signal, since any of those lets the group file the whole problem under machinery they already have and stop thinking.

3. The misconception map: the misunderstandings in the order this audience will actually produce them, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be defused. The ones I anticipate: (i) this is just inference from the absence of a signal; (ii) this is the argument from ignorance with a Sanskrit name; (iii) non-cognition is not a pramāṇa at all, it is a label for a gap in the taxonomy; (iv) once they grant that absence can be known, the slide to "so absence must be some kind of thing" — the realist treatment of absence, which I would rather they arrive at as a consequence than swallow as a premise; and (v) a wholesale redescription in terms of sensitivity, criterion and miss rate, which quietly changes the subject from warrant to decision policy.

4. One check at the end that distinguishes "they can now classify cases" from "they see why the negative case matters for a theory of the sources of knowledge" — ideally a fresh case with a new wrinkle, for instance an eligible object together with an unnoticed sensory defect.

5. What I should openly refuse to cover in ninety minutes with this audience, and say so out loud. My list: the fourfold classification of absence (prior absence, destruction, absolute absence, mutual absence); the later Navya-Nyāya technical vocabulary; postulation as a further pramāṇa; and the cross-school disputes with the Buddhists. Flag any item on that list whose absence would make the session collapse.
