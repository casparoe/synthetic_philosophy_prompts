I'm teaching a first-year pragmatics seminar and using Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day as a running case study (I'll also screen the Merchant-Ivory film, so the film version works just as well for this). It's narrated by a butler who means things all the way through that he would never admit to meaning, which looks like the case Grice's machinery is least built for: speaker meaning is supposed to be an overt intention the hearer is meant to recognise, and implicature is supposed to be calculable and cancellable. So the question I want to put to the group: can there be implicature the speaker doesn't intend to convey and would sincerely disown — and if so, does Grice's recognition condition have to be relaxed, or is the better move to seperate Ishiguro, the older narrating Stevens, and the younger Stevens inside the scene, and locate the implicature at one level rather than another? Could you actually run the cancellability test on two or three scenes (the houseguest at the conference quizzing him on trade policy while his father is dying upstairs, his exchanges with Miss Kenton on the West Country trip, the talk with the stranger on the pier at the end) and say where it gives a clean verdict and where it's plainly the wrong tool? Be straight with me too about where the reading collapses — where the novel just isn't doing the saying-versus-meaning thing and I'd be forcing it. And if the novel shows something about implicature that the standard textbook examples don't, tell me what, because that's what I want to build the last session around.
