I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I'm the technical lead on a project I no longer trust myself to evaluate. We are five years and roughly forty million dollars into a rewrite of our billing system — about seventy percent done, thirty engineers — and the alternative on the table is to stop and go back to maintaining the old system, whose upkeep gets more expensive every year. Our new CFO says continuing is a textbook sunk cost fallacy: the five years are gone, only remaining costs and remaining benefits count, and the fact that we have come this far is not a reason.

What makes this hard is that I can point at three things and cannot tell which of them are reasons and which are rationalizations. First, an asymmetry of reversibility: stopping is close to irreversible, because the team disperses within a quarter and the knowledge that is not in the artifact leaves with them, whereas continuing is reversible in the sense that we can always stop next quarter — so even if the expected value of finishing is slightly negative, continuing preserves an option that stopping destroys. An economist friend used the phrase quasi-option value for this; argue with the idea, not the label. Second, history-dependence: I want to claim that two companies identical in every currently measurable respect — same artifact, same people, same contracts, same roadmap — would warrant different decisions if in one of them those people had spent five years building that artifact together and in the other they had been assembled last week. Third, and against me: the standard counterfactual test seems to cut the other way, because if the artifact and the team could be conjured for free tomorrow I do not think I would choose to finish, and I notice that this looks like evidence that what is actually moving me is the spending itself.

So give me your credence, as a number, that there is something here: that in cases of this shape the sunkness itself can do genuine decision-theoretic work which does not reduce, without residue, to forward-looking costs and benefits. My own credence that continuing is rational is around 0.6 and the CFO's is under 0.05. Please give me no reading list, no citations, and no survey of who has argued what — I have no library access and will not chase references. If part of my disagreement with him is only about words, tell me which part. And if you think two of my three points are really one, or that I have misstated the standard argument, say so in whichever item it belongs to.

Answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no preamble and no closing summary:

1. Your headline number, together with the sentence that fixes how you are construing the proposition, so I can see whether we are betting on the same thing.
2. Your probability that my history-dependence claim is true: that holding fixed everything currently measurable, the bare difference between having spent and not having spent can itself warrant a different decision.
3. Your probability that the reversibility asymmetry is a genuine forward-looking reason rather than sunk-cost reasoning in disguise, or at least a reason whose force itself depends on the past expenditure having happened.
4. Your probability that such residue, if it exists, is large enough in an organisation like mine — and is tracked closely enough by what people actually say, as opposed to loss aversion, escalation of commitment and face-saving — to change what a real recommendation should be.
5. How your second, third and fourth numbers combine into your first; which of them my 0.6 and the CFO's 0.05 most likely disagree about; and the single thing I could check about my own project that would move your headline number the most.
