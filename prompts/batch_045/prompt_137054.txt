I am giving a sixty-minute guest session next term to a second-year game-design cohort: roughly twenty-five students, no philosophy beyond a single ethics module, a projector and a whiteboard available, and a room in which a good half of the students have themselves built levels organised around killing non-player characters. Several of them will arrive assuming that the question is a prelude to an accusation, so the sequence has to disarm that without flattering them.

The session's target is the question whether video-game characters matter morally. I do not need the arguments; I have them. What I need is the plan: the order in which the components have to be introduced, and what depends on what. As I see it, the question bundles at least three things that my students will run together unless I prise them apart deliberately: whether a virtual or fictional entity can itself have moral status, that is, can be wronged or have interests; whether an act directed at such an entity can be morally significant for reasons that do not run through its status at all — what the act expresses, what it cultivates in the agent, what the player thereby becomes a party to, what it does to other players who share the world; and, given whatever we conclude, what follows for design and for criticism, which is a further question rather than the same one. Tell me which of these must be on the table before the others can even be intelligibly raised, and what it costs me if I get that order wrong. My own guess is that unless the second question is separated from the first at the outset, any consideration that does not involve harming a mind will be dismissed as irrelevant, and unless the third is explicitly deferred, the hour will collapse into an argument about regulation. Correct me if either guess is wrong.

On the carrying example: I have three candidates. The nameless non-player character killed for loot; the gamer's dilemma as Luck formulated it in 2009, used as a diagnostic rather than as a conclusion; and a hypothetical I have been experimenting with, in which a companion character's dialogue is generated at runtime by a language model, which retains memory of what the player did to it across sessions, and whose repertoire of pleading, flinching and register-shift was shaped by training on recordings of human distress. Which one should carry the session, and what must it have and what must it lack in order to do that work? I am unsure whether the third case advances the discussion or smuggles in a separate debate about machine minds and thereby loses the room. A related question: does it matter whether the distress behaviour was hand-authored by a writer rather than learned from human distress data, and if that distinction is worth drawing, where in the hour should it appear, and should I raise it at all?

The misconceptions I expect, in the order I expect them: that nothing real is harmed and so there is nothing to discuss; that if the character has no moral status, that settles every version of the question; that the appeal to what the act does to the agent is the empirical claim that violent games make people violent, reacted to accordingly; that the gamer's dilemma must be answered by locating a morally relevant difference between the two acts rather than by suspicion of the intuitions themselves, or else that it forces both acts into the same verdict; that wrong slides seamlessly into should not be made available. With the runtime-generated companion I additionally expect two opposed errors: treating the distress behaviour as though it settled the question whether there is a mind there, and treating the fact that it is a machine as though it settled everything in advance. For each, indicate the point in the sequence at which it should be headed off, and whether any of them is better left to surface and be answered in the room rather than pre-empted.

Finally, two things. Give me one check I can run in the last ten minutes that would show the distinctions have landed rather than that the students have merely been made defensive; my own candidate is to ask them to construct one case in which an in-game act is impermissible although nothing with moral status is affected and no one is harmed, and one case in which the character does have moral status but the act is permissible, but I would rather have a better test if you have one. And tell me what I should openly refuse to cover: I am inclined to name the empirical literature on media effects, the metaphysics of fictional entities, whether an artificial system could be conscious, and the legal question, as out of scope, and to say so at the start; tell me whether that is the right list of refusals, whether any of those four actually needs five minutes rather than exclusion, and what I have omitted from it.
