I'm in a third-year epistemology seminar, and last week's session was on the value problem in the Meno — the question of why knowledge is supposed to be worth more than mere true belief, given that if what you want is to get things right, true belief seems to give you everything. My tutor leaned very heavily on Socrates' own image: true beliefs are like the statues of Daedalus, beautiful to look at but liable to run away in the night unless you chain them up, and chaining them up is working out the reason why. She used it to do real argumentative work: she said that once you take the image seriously the value question just becomes the question why a chained statue is better than a loose one, and that the answer is obvious — the loose one won't be there when you come back for it. It went down smoothly, but I want to know whether the image was doing the work or whether the smoothness was.

Two things I've been chewing on since. First, a variant a classmate raised afterwards: suppose you can rent a chain — accept someone else's explanation on testimony, not grasping it yourself — and suppose it holds the statue just as securely as a chain forged out of your own understanding. On the statue picture a rented chain seems to do the job perfectly, but most of us think borrowed understanding gives you something less than knowledge, or something less valuable than it. Second, most of the true beliefs we care about are needed exactly once (which bus goes to the station, whether the café on the corner opens at eight), and for a one-shot need the chaining accomplishes nothing at all: the statue wanders off afterwards and you never wanted it back. My tutor's reply was that knowledge is for creatures with futures, which struck me as conceding that the image only delivers value given a fairly specific kind of agent with fairly specific needs.

So: could you audit the image properly? I'd like the correspondence table laid out — what plays the statue, the chain, the running away, the owner, the workshop — and then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually matter for the job the image is being asked to do and the ones that don't (statues have legs and motives and beliefs do not; a chained statue is no better a likeness than a loose one, so the chain adds permanence and not truth; chaining leaves the statue unchanged while holding a belief for a reason arguably changes what the belief is). Then the part I really can't settle: does the image say anything about what I've seen called the swamping worry — once truth is held fixed, does the extra reliability or stability add any value at all, or does the image merely relocate that worry to future occasions and nearby cases? Does it quietly import a reliabilist answer when the question was supposed to be open between reliability, credit to the agent, and understanding? Does my classmate's rented-chain case break it, or just force a choice about what the chain represents? And if the image can't carry the obvious answer after all, what would a better analogy look like, and what would it have to make salient that the statues don't? Please argue it out rather than telling me who has said what about it — our library's catalogue is down this week and I need something I can reconstruct on my own in an essay.
