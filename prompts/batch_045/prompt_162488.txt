I am writing a paper on precommitment and its limits, and I want the implicit theory of a body of existing practice reconstructed rather than adjudicated. The practice is a pattern that recurs across constitutional law, private law, and medical law, and to me it looks internally inconsistent. I want to know what theory of the agent — and of the authority of an earlier decision over a later one — it presupposes.

Take the following as stipulations about the pattern, since I am interested in the structure rather than in the detail of any one jurisdiction.

1. Constitutional entrenchment. A founding or amending supermajority can make later change harder than ordinary change: in the United States an amendment needs two-thirds of both Houses to propose and three-fourths of the states to ratify. Several postwar constitutions go further and place a small core of provisions beyond amendment altogether — in Germany by explicit text, in India by judicial doctrine holding that amendments may not damage the constitution's basic structure.

2. The denial of entrenchment elsewhere. The same legal orders generally hold that an ordinary legislature cannot bind its successors: a statute carried by ninety percent can be repealed by a later fifty-one percent. Even in systems that officially deny entrenchment outright, courts have sometimes enforced procedural requirements about the manner and form of future legislation while refusing to let a legislature freeze substance.

3. The strong and weak dead hand in private law. A will takes effect only at death and lets a testator dispose of property in ways no living person could, sometimes for generations through trusts. Yet the same systems limit dead-hand control through rules against perpetuities, against restraints on alienation, and against restraints on marriage; and no one may contract away their own future autonomy — no specific performance of personal service, no enforceable undertaking to obey, and in most common-law systems clauses whose function is to deter breach rather than compensate it are disallowed as penalties.

4. Ulysses contracts in medical law. A competent person may bind a future incapacitated self: advance directives, including psychiatric ones, are honored once capacity is lost. But while competent, the person may revoke such a directive orally, for no reason, even when the instrument declares itself irrevocable.

5. The escalation asymmetry. Across these areas, precommitments that make future action costlier but still possible — a supermajority, a cooling-off period, a second vote, a mandatory deliberative stage — are honored. Precommitments that fix an outcome permanently are not. Item 1 is the conspicuous exception.

I want you to work with two distinctions I will introduce. Call a precommitment capacity-triggered when its authority is supposed to derive from some deficit in the later self — impaired judgment, duress, a situation the earlier self foresaw and the later self cannot be trusted to handle. Call it continuity-triggered when its authority is supposed to derive from the later agent's being the same agent who made the promise, notwithstanding full competence. Second: distinguish holding a power as an owner from holding it as a trustee, where an owner may give the power away or use it up and a trustee may not.

Please give me:

(i) The single theory of precommitment's authority that makes the largest number of items 1 through 5 look rational, stated precisely enough that I could test it. For each item, say whether the theory strictly entails it or merely accommodates it.

(ii) A placement of each of the five items on the capacity-triggered/continuity-triggered axis and on the owner/trustee axis. My suspicion is that a capacity condition explains items 2, 3, and 4 but does not explain item 1, so that the practice needs a second and independent principle that sits awkwardly beside the first. If that is right, state the second principle and say whether the two are genuinely in tension or only appear to be.

(iii) The places where the pattern is philosophically underdetermined — the items that two or more rival theories would license equally well — with the rival theories named and the item each would explain differently. If you think some item is better explained as historical accident than as theory, say which and why.

(iv) A ruling on three cases, none of which any actual court has decided. First, the phantom veto-holder: a clause provides that it may be amended only with the consent of a second chamber, and that chamber is later abolished and its functions merged into the first. Does the clause become unamendable, become amendable by the successor body, or lapse? What does your theory say, what would each rival say, and would the answer change if the required consent were that of a state legislature and the state were dissolved? Second, the delayed double-key clause: a provision is adopted by eighty percent in a referendum and specifies that it can be repealed only by seventy percent at two successive general elections at least five years apart, the second following a mandatory citizens' assembly report. Twenty-five years on, a seventy-four percent majority at a single election seeks repeal; most of the current electorate was not enfranchised at enactment; the triggering shock is one the enactors expressly considered and rejected as a ground for repeal. Does the constraint hold? Third, the competent-but-tempted majority: a debt brake is adopted in the explicit knowledge that later voters, fully competent and fully informed, will want to borrow. On a capacity-triggered account this is the case where precommitment should be least authoritative, yet constitutions are full of it. Does this show that constitutional precommitment is not a Ulysses contract at all but a different institution, and if so, which?

(v) Finally, what would concretely have to change if the practice adopted either of the pure views: that no generation may bind another beyond a fixed term, or that any supermajority may entrench whatever it likes, forever. Name the items that would have to go.

Constraints: reconstruct rather than cite — no cases, statutes, or authors, and no appeal to how some particular jurisdiction happens to handle these questions; treat my five stipulations as given. About 1,000 to 1,300 words, in prose with the structure you think the material calls for.
