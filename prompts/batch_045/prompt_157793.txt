im in a philosophy of religion seminar this term and we just spent two weeks on Molinism and middle knowledge and i feel like i understood it and then stopped understanding it. our lecturer used two analogies back to back and i think they might be pulling in opposite directions, but im not confident enough to say that out loud in class.

Background as i have it: middle knowledge is supposed to sit between God's knowledge of mere possibilities (what any free creature could do) and his knowledge of what he's actually decreed will happen, and it's the bit about what any free creature *would* do in any circumstance. The whole point of the doctrine as i understood it is that it lets you keep libertarian free will and still get really fine-grained providence — prophecy, who ends up responding to grace, that sort of thing.

Analogy one (the lecturer's): middle knowledge isn't exotic, we have some of it too. Her example: you know your roommate well enough to know that if she found a wallet full of cash on the street she'd try to return it — and you know that without having watched that exact situation, and knowing it doesn't commit you to saying she's *forced* to return it. God's middle knowledge is just this but for every creature, every circumstance, with no guessing. She used it to defuse the grounding objection — the worry that there's nothing in reality for a counterfactual of creaturely freedom to be *about*, since the creature's free decision doesn't exist yet and God hasn't decided anything yet. Her move was: counterfactuals about free actions plainly have truth values, we rely on them constantly, so there is something there for God to know.

Analogy two (from a guy in the seminar who leans Calvinist, used as an objection): Molinism makes God like a film director with a warehouse of audition tapes. Every possible actor has already recorded how they'd play every possible scene; the director just picks which scene to shoot, and once he's picked, the performance is fixed. What he was using it for: to argue that Molinism gives God no real providence — on this picture God doesn't determine what anyone does, he only selects from a menu he didn't author, so "providence" collapses into shopping. The reply was that Molinists distinguish strong and weak actualization: God strongly actualizes whatever he causes directly (the circumstances, the offer of grace, whatever) and only weakly actualizes free creaturely responses, by placing creatures in circumstances where he knows how they'd respond — and weak actualization is supposedly enough for the providence the doctrine was invented to secure.

- Where does each break, and — this is the part i keep loosing the thread on — which of those breaks actually matter for the job each analogy is being asked to do, and which are noise? Candidate disanalogies i came up with, tell me which ones do work and which i should drop: (a) my knowledge of my roommate is inferred from her actual behavior in the actual world, but God's is supposed to be prior to there being any actual world, so there's no track record to infer from; (b) God's is non-inferential, complete, and covers infinitely many cases at once, while mine is a fallible generalization from a handful; (c) in the director case the tapes already exist and God didn't make them, whereas creatures only exist because God creates them, so the menu isn't independent of the chooser; (d) my knowledge seems to track a settled character, and if character settles the choice then it isn't the libertarian freedom Molinists want — so the analogy either smuggles in determinism about the will, or my "knowledge" is really just a guess and the analogy leaves God with nothing to know; (e) Molinists apparently hold that God has no middle knowledge of what *he himself* would freely do, which feels like it should cause trouble for the analogy in a way i cant quite articulate.
