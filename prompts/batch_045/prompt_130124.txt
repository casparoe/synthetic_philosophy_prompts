Putting together where I've landed on the St. Petersburg game for a decision theory reading group, and I want it poked at before I present. My position:

(1) The expected monetary value of the one-shot game — payoff doubles with each tails, so you get 2^n if the first heads is on flip n — really is infinite. The math is fine; the puzzle is only about what it's rational to pay to play.
(2) The fix is Bernoulli: you maximize expected utility, not expected money, and utility in money is concave, so the fair entry fee is small (a couple of dollars, more if you're rich).
(3) Utility is unbounded above: there's no point after which more money or more welfare stops making things better.
(4) Some of what's left over is explained by the many-plays version — the fair price for n plays only grows like n log n, so intuition is tracking the repeated case rather than the single play.
(5) I'm also inclined to say a small chance at an infinite good is worth paying any finite amount for.

Someone told me (2) and (3) can't both be true, but I dont see it. I do worry that a nastier variant where payoffs grow like 2^(2^n) breaks (2) no matter which utility curve I pick, and that (4) might not even be the same kind of explanation as (2) — maybe it's doing something else entirely. Where are the actual tensions, how bad is each one, and what's the cheapest thing I'd have to give up for each? Rank them rather than just flagging them all.
