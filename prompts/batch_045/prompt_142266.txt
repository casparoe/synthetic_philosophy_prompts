Our literary magazine is running a feature that pairs poems with ideas from other classes, and I'm doing the one on information cascades — the setup where people choose in sequence, each can see what everyone before them did but not what they privately knew, and past some point it becomes reasonable to copy the crowd and set aside your own evidence, after which the length of the line tells you nothing it didn't already tell you.

I'd like a villanelle. The two refrains should be the private signal and the public action: one line for what the speaker actually has reason to believe, one for what they do because everyone else did it. The form should do the arguing — the refrains come back word for word while the reasoning around them shifts, and in the last stanza they land side by side so the reader sees that the private signal was never refuted, only drowned out. Someone who has read about cascades should be able to tell which refrain is which and why the ending isn't a resolution.

Two things I want the poem to get right instead of just nodding at. First, nobody in the line is being irrational: if you can see choices but not reasons, following may well be the sensible move, and the failure belongs to the setup rather than to the people in it. Second, keep apart the case where people copy because they take the crowd to know something and the case where they copy because being the one who walked away is itself expensive. In high school the second is usually the stronger one, and I think it's also the harder one to escape, since learning you're in a cascade doesn't buy you anything when leaving is the visible part. If the poem can't hold both, I'd rather it stayed with the second.

Set it somewhere ordinary — a food truck line, a lunch table, a sign-up sheet in the hallway — and keep the diction plain. I'd rather have a poem that stands on its own than one that pauses to define a term.
