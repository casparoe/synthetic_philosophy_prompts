I am sixty-three years old, I have had no formal training in philosophy, and I have been circling the same question since my wife died four years ago, so I would like a direct answer rather than a survey of what various thinkers have said. Please write in American English, in plain language, and do not soften anything into consolation.

The claim I keep returning to is one associated with Douglas Hofstadter, who argued — after his own wife's death — that the thing we call a person's I is a pattern of symbols realized in a brain, and that because a pattern is not tied to one piece of matter, a dead person's I can persist in diluted form inside the brains of the living who carry it. I find myself wanting to agree. When I finish one of her sentences, or when I make a small decision by an instinct that is recognizably hers rather than one I would have chosen before I met her, the experience does not present itself to me as remembering her or as having been influenced by her. It presents itself as her being here. My brother says that is grief doing the talking, and that the most that happened is that her patterns caused changes in me, which is a different thing entirely.

Here is the proposition I want your credence on, stated as precisely as I can manage. Something that is literally, and not merely figuratively, my wife's first-person perspective — the same subject to whom her experiences happened — is now at least partially realized in at least one living brain. I am not asking whether she is remembered, or whether she changed me, or whether it is comforting or useful to speak as if she were present; I already believe all three. I am asking whether the thing in virtue of which her experiences were hers is now, in some degree, operating in someone else. As a rough standard of resolution: imagine that a century from now the question of whether a partially instantiated pattern can support a perspective at all is somehow settled. What probability do you assign to that settlement coming down on the side of yes-and-it-can-happen-in-this-kind-of-case?

Please give me that number, and then the decomposition rather than the number alone. Assign a rough probability and one line of justification to each of the factors the number depends on — I would expect these to include at least: whether a self is the sort of thing that is constituted by a pattern at all rather than by the matter that carries it; whether patterns are the sort of thing that admit of degrees, so that a partial realization is genuinely a realization rather than merely a resemblance; whether one integrated brain can host more than one perspective, given that any pattern running in my brain is running on my hardware and entangled with my memories, my body, and my plans for Tuesday; and whether everything I am inclined to describe as her presence is fully accounted for by ordinary psychological causation, so that I would report exactly the same experiences if nothing of her survived at all. Add any further term you think belongs. Then say how the terms combine into the final number, which single term the number is most sensitive to, and at roughly what value of that term the number would cross from below one-half to above it.

I am at about 0.4 on the proposition and my brother is at about 0.02. Tell me where you think that disagreement actually lives: is it a dispute about what brains can do, about whether a perspective can be shared, about whether identity comes in degrees, or only about what either of us is entitled to call her?

Two cases I would like you to work through rather than gesture at, because the usual ways of talking about persons do not seem to me to handle them.

First, the census. Suppose we had a fine-grained way of measuring how much of a dead person's self-pattern is still realized in each of several living people, and suppose the figures for my wife came back spread across five of us: forty per cent in me, thirty in our daughter, fifteen in her sister, ten in a former colleague, five in her brother. No one contains anything close to a full copy. On that supposition does she exist now? If every figure were one per cent instead, would your answer change in kind or only in degree? And if there is a threshold somewhere between, what could possibly fix its location — what in the nature of a self would make forty per cent her and twenty per cent not her?

Second, the mutual case and the two heirs. Suppose the indwelling runs in both directions: I carry a great deal of her and she carried a great deal of me, symmetrically, the way a very long marriage is sometimes described. Does that make us one person, two people, or two people each of whom is partly somebody else? Now suppose instead that her pattern is realized to the same substantial degree in me and in our daughter simultaneously. Is there then one I shared between two brains, two partial I's, one and a half of her, or none — and does how you answer that change what you should say about the original case?

Finally, whichever way you land: if your number is low, tell me honestly whether anything of what I care about survives it — whether there is a nearby claim, weaker than she is literally here, that would earn a high number from you, and say exactly what that claim says and why it stops short of the one I asked about. If your number is high, tell me the strongest available version of my brother's objection and what it would take for it to move you.
