I'm in the lower sixth, and I'm writing my Extended Project on whether attachment to one's own country is a virtue or a vice. While reading and arguing about it over the past few months I've noticed a pattern in how people talk, and I can't work out whether it is a recognised phenomenon with an established name, or whether I'm inventing a spurious unity.

It usually shows up like this. After a national sports victory, or at a Remembrance assembly, or when some long-dead figure from our own country is praised for a discovery, people slide very easily into "we": they say "we" achieved it, and they seem to feel a genuine, personal pride about it, even though they had no hand in it, would have opposed it at the time, and in some cases weren't born when it happened. But the moment the story is an unflattering one — the slave trade, the empire, some wartime atrocity — the very same people retreat to the strictest possible account of individual agency: they weren't there, they didn't do it, their family wasn't even in the country, and so no share of it attaches to them. What struck me most is that the boundary of the group itself seems to flex. A scientist born two centuries ago in the next city is "ours" when he is being celebrated, but a merchant from the same period who made his money from slavery is treated as belonging to a country that was somehow a different entity then. And it sometimes runs the other way as well: I have friends who are extremely critical of the country, who would never permit themselves a moment of patriotic pride, and who nevertheless insist that we owe apologies or reparations for historical wrongs — so they seem to accept a share in the guilt while refusing the share in the credit.

I don't think what I'm describing is just the ordinary business of people taking credit for their own successes and blaming circumstances for their own failures, since none of this is about anybody's own actions at all, and each individual move seems perfectly defensible in isolation — it does sound strange to say you feel guilty about something done in 1830. I'm also not sure it is simply a fallacy to be exposed, because there may be a principled story on which a community can be a source of pride without being a source of responsibility, or vice versa. I'd be grateful if you could tell me whether this pattern — the asymmetry, and the way the group's edges stretch for credit and shrink for blame — has an established name in philosophy or in social psychology; if the nearest concepts only fit it partly, which ones they are, how my case differs from each, and which comes closest; whether what I've described is really one phenomenon or two or three that I've wrongly lumped together; and whether there is a defensible version of the move rather than only a defective one. I'm after the carving rather than a label, so please don't just hand me a term. Please write your answer in British English.
