I'm writing a novel and I think I've accidentally painted myself into a metaphysical corner, so before I commit to any of it in the final draft I'd like someone to check whether the set of views I've ended up with actually hangs together.

The setup: a sculptor spends a week making a statue — call it The Diver — out of one single lump of clay. Three plot points matter. Her assistant drops a sandbag on it and squashes it flat into a disc. The museum that owns it later takes it apart into two hundred crates, ships it across the country and rebuilds it exactly as it was. And the sculptor's will leaves "the lump of clay" to her daughter and "the statue" to her gallerist — and the daughter is the one who does the squashing.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. The statue and the lump are two different things, not one thing under two descriptions. The lump existed on Monday and the statue didn't; the lump could have survived the squashing and the statue couldn't.
2. At every moment when they both exist they have exactly the same parts, the same shape, the same mass, the same location, and exactly the same causal powers. That's the whole reason I need (1): they differ only in modal and historical properties, and by Leibniz's law things with different properties aren't identical.
3. It's a real difference in the world, not a decision about how we talk. I dont want to be told that there's nothing more to it than which conventions we happen to pick.
4. But when philosophers argue about whether there's one thing on the plinth or two, I think that argument is basically verbal — everyone agrees about the matter, the shape, the history and the causal powers, and they're just fighting over "thing", "exists" and "identical".
5. Modal facts about artefacts have to be grounded in non-modal facts. "The statue couldn't have survived being squashed" has to be made true by something about the matter and how it's arranged, not by a brute essentialist fact about statues as such.
6. There's exactly one statue on that plinth. No teeming: no "statue-minus-its-thumb", no "clay-plus-half-a-thumb", no thousands of near-enough candidates with equal claims to being it.
7. I want it to be genuinely indeterminate when the statue began to exist — was it the last pass of the thumb on the cheek, or the one before that? — and I want that to be vagueness in the world and not just in our words, including at the point where it's supposedly up in the air whether what's sitting on the plinth is one thing or two.
8. Verdicts I'm stuck with for plot reasons: the squashing destroyed the statue and left the lump; the crate-by-crate disassembly and rebuild did not destroy the statue; and the daughter, by squashing it, really has taken something from the gallerist, even though the only matter involved was hers.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I have a feeling (3) and (4) are odd bedfellows but I think that one's fine, and apart from that I definately can't locate the problem. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging all of them as equally bad; for each one say what would have to give and what it would cost me as a novelist, since some of these I need for the plot to work; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. And if any of the verdicts in (8) is one my own principles can't actually deliver, say which one and why.
