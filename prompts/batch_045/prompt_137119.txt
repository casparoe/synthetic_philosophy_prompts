i'm giving a work-in-progress talk next week to our legal theory reading group on free will skepticism and punishment, and my supervisor basically dared me to try to state my own position in one page instead of hiding behind the Caruso/Dennett back-and-forth. so here's the list, and i want to know whether it hangs together.

(1) No one is morally responsible in the basic-desert sense. not the psychopath, and not the rest of us either: the luck and manipulation arguments are as good against ordinary agents as against the wired-up one.

(2) So the state's only justification for punishment is forward-looking. public-health model, quarantine rather than retribution, and proportionality read as: the least restrictive measure sufficient to neutralize the threat, and nothing more than that.

(3) But i want to keep the completion discount. attempts should be punished substantially less than completed crimes, including near-miss cases where the only difference between attempt and completion was a bit of luck that arrived after the agent had already done everything — the gun jams, the victim turns out to have died of an unrelated clot ten minutes earlier, whatever.

(4) I'm not an abolitionist about the reactive attitudes. resentment and moral anger are often genuinely fitting, and "he had it coming" is sometimes a real reason for a grim satisfaction, not just a useful fiction i've decided to endorse from the outside.

(5) Punishing the innocent and retroactive criminalization strike me as unjust in a way that isn't just inefficiency or bad consequences downstream. i want that as a hard side-constraint, not a rule of thumb.

(6) The Dennett-ish thought i keep coming back to: the practice of holding people responsible partly makes people more responsible over time, it's self-fulfilling in a way that seems to matter, and i've been calling whatever status it generates P-desert (practice-relative desert) in the draft. i want P-desert to be enough to keep desert talk in the law while still denying that anyone has basic desert.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? i think (5) is fine on rights grounds — using someone as a mere means and all that — but i may be fooling myself, and honestly i can't locate the real problem: every time i write a paragraph i think the trouble is somewhere else than it was last time. So go through the pairs, say which tensions are real and which only look like tensions, rank the real ones by severity, and for each give the cheapest fix. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.

Please commit to something: tell me which one you'd actually drop and why, and what it costs me, rather than laying out the live options and leaving the choice to me — i can get that from the literature. And keep it as plain prose, no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, the way you'd write it in an email to a colleague who asked. thanks.
