The Methods and Standards Subcommittee I serve on has to send the Programme Board of the National Brain and Mind Research Programme a one-page recommendation by Friday, and I would like you to draft it.

Background: the Programme is about to issue its second call for proposals, and its stated success metric is a validated, clinically usable measure of conscious experience. Two factions have asked us to settle how first-person reports may figure in funded projects. One wants the Programme to adopt heterophenomenology (Dennett's method) as a standing rule: subjects' utterances are to be recorded as texts — dispositions to speak, to be explained by third-person data — and never taken as authoritative evidence about what the subject experienced. The other wants trained first-person methods (structured explication interviews, micro-phenomenological descriptions) admitted as evidence about experience itself, and argues that the rule would demote the Programme's own target to a footnote.

Two live applications make it concrete. The first is a blindsight study whose patients give mutually inconsistent reports about whether they saw anything. The second proposes to bypass reports altogether, using no-report paradigms and post-decision confidence measures as markers of awareness.

Please address three things. First, the calibration dilemma: any candidate neural or behavioral marker has to be calibrated against something, and the only available calibrators appear to be reports — so a rule that demotes reports may make the Programme's own success metric unreachable. Second, a distinction I think we have been collapsing: heterophenomenology as a methodological scruple (do not take reports at face value; they are question-dependent and partly an artifact of how they were elicited) versus heterophenomenology as an ontological thesis (reports tell us only about a theorist's construct, the subject's heterophenomenological world, and never about experience itself). Say which of these we would actually be voting on, and whether one can be adopted without the other. Third, if reports are admitted as evidence, what standard should they meet as a measurement instrument — what counts as a validated elicitation procedure?

The memo should recommend one of the following: adopt the rule, adopt the scruple only, adopt it with an explicit carve-out for trained first-person methods, or decline to legislate methodology at all — with reasons applicants could act on, and an honest statement of the strongest consideration on whichever side you reject. Keep separate what is settled among people who work on these questions from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Write in American English, and close with the recommendation encoded on its own final line as \answer{...} using one of these labels: Adopt-Rule, Adopt-Scruple-Only, Adopt-With-Carve-Out, Decline-To-Legislate.
