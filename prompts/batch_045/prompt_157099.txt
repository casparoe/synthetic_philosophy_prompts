Two people at drinks after a philosophy department talk last week spent about an hour going at each other over higher-order theories of consciousness, and since I was sitting between them I got drafted as referee, which I was useless at. Maya was defending a Rosenthal-ish actualist higher-order-thought view; Theo was attacking it. I scribbled down roughly how it went:

(1) Theo opened with the rock objection: if a mental state's being conscious just consists in there being a higher-order thought about it, then a HOT somehow attached to a rock would make the rock's states conscious, and that's absurd. Maya said that misses two constraints already built into the view — the thought has to represent the state as one of the subject's *own* mental states, and it has to arise non-inferentially from whatever does the monitoring — so the rock case can't be constructed in the first place.

(2) Theo: fine, drop the rock. Suppose we cross-wire the monitor, so that when your visual system is in the state normally caused by green light you get a HOT with the content *I am now seeing red*. On your account you are now consciously seeing red. But nothing red is going on anywhere in you. So the HOT isn't making an existing state conscious, it's manufacturing a phenomenology out of nothing — the opposite of what the theory promised.

(3) Maya: then I'll add a matching condition. The HOT confers consciousness only when its content matches the type of the first-order state. When it doesn't, you get an introspective seeming with nothing behind it: you'd sincerely say *I seem to be seeing red* while not consciously seeing red at all.

(4) Theo: that condition is suicide. If matching is what does the work, the first-order state's own properties are doing the explaining and the HOT is decoration — you might as well say the state is conscious in virtue of being the kind of state it is. And you've re-imported the regress: something has to settle whether HOT and first-order state match, and if that's another representation we're off to the races.

(5) Maya: two replies. The regress charge is unfair, and the self-representationalist picture you'd rather have is worse: either a state that represents itself has infinitely many layers of content, which nothing finite could instantiate, or its self-representation is just an intrinsic structural feature — in which case you've given up the explanatory project and you're back at plain first-order representationalism. Also the matching condition isn't ad hoc: we need a monitor anyway to make sense of dissociations where discrimination survives but experience doesn't, and a monitor worth the name reliably tracks what it monitors, so write that in as a clause and the mismatch case is ruled out twice over.

(6) Theo: the reliability clause is worse than the matching one. If the monitor has to be reliable, then in every normal case the HOT and the first-order state covary exactly, so nothing observable seperates your view from the first-order view *plus* "and there is a monitor." You've made your theory empirically equivalent to its rival and you still can't say what the HOT adds. Which connects to the old worry you haven't answered: you say HOTs are themselves unconscious. An unconscious thought that I'm in pain doesn't seem to make my pain feel like anything — so how does an unconscious state hand consciousness over to another one?

(7) Maya: that last bit conflates two things. Being conscious *of* something isn't the same as being a conscious state, and what the HOT needs to be is the first, not the second — otherwise we get an infinite regress. Theo: then you've relocated the mystery into the "conscious of" notion, which is precisely what the theory was supposed to analyze.

(8) Maya then floated two fallbacks. First, take the higher-order perception / inner-sense version, where an inner sense scans first-order states and borrows their qualitative character, so the theory can say what the experience is like and not merely that it's conscious. Second, loosen the timing: the HOT needn't be simultaneous with its target, it can arrive within a short window and confer consciousness on a state that has already ceased. Theo: the first needs an organ and a property for that organ to detect, and if the property detected is the state's qualitative character then you've presupposed what you were meant to explain; the second has a state becoming conscious after it's gone, which is a backwards influence smuggled into a theory whose whole selling point was being naturalistically respectable.

I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning, and they asked me to settle it. So: can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what, where each of them quietly slid past something, and what both of them are missing?
