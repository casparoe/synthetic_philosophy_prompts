I'm about two thirds through a novel set in a psychiatric clinic during a revision of the diagnostic manual, and I've gotten stuck on something my characters keep arguing about: which of the categories they're fighting over are real discoveries and which are the kind of thing that makes itself up as it gets named. I've been reading a bit about looping effects — the idea that a human kind and the people falling under it co-evolve, so the label feeds back into the experiences and behaviour of the labelled — and my writing group says it's too academic to put in a book, but it's genuinely how my protagonist thinks, so I need to get straight what I believe before I let her say it. Here's where I land on cases. A nineteenth-century epidemic of people wandering off and turning up in a distant city with no memory of who they were: textbook looping, and I'm comfortable saying the kind was mostly made up, since it ballooned and then essentially vanished. A syndrome defined by a specific genetic deletion that produces a recognisable cluster of features: discovery, no looping at all, the label just tracks something that was already there. A childhood attention-and-impulse category that later gets extended to adults, with prevalence climbing sharply once stimulants and exam accommodations are attached to it: also looping, and here's where I'm inconsistent — I refuse to say it's made up, I want to say it has a real core that the looping exaggerates. A disruptive-behaviour category where what visibly changes is not the children but the tolerance and reporting thresholds of parents and teachers: I count that as looping too, even though the feedback runs through other people. Retrospectively diagnosing a historical figure: not looping, just anachronism, wrong in a different way. And the one I invented for a previous book — a made-up syndrome I gave a character, which readers then took to their own clinicians — strikes me as the purest case of all, but I can't say why it feels different from the clinic cases, since there's no institution behind it. My tentative principle is that looping needs the labelled people to somehow take up the label and change because of it. But then I don't know what to do with a case my researcher told me about: a term that appears only in charts and billing codes, never said to patients, where prevalence still climbs because the code unlocks a treatment that changes how people then describe themselves. That's uptake one step removed, and my principle says it isn't looping while my gut says it is. Can you state a principle that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, tell me honestly which ones it forces me to drop, and say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — say, a condition first picked up by a blood test whose patient community then develops its own distinctive way of being ill, or people arriving at a diagnosis through a recommendation algorithm rather than a doctor.
