I'm in a final-year metaphysics seminar on laws of nature, and I am trying to work out what our professor actually thinks before the last class. All term she has been dodging one question — whether the laws of a world are fixed by everything that happens in it — and she has promised to answer it in the final session. That session is next week, and she has circulated the case in advance. I would like you to help me predict her verdict on it, and, more importantly, to work out which rule she is really running.

Here is her track record on the ten cases we have done so far.

1. "Every sphere of gold is less than a mile across" — not a law, despite having no exceptions.
2. "Every coin in my pocket on my birthday was silver" — not a law.
3. Newton's inverse-square law of gravitation — a law, even though no actual pair of bodies exactly conforms to it, so that read as a universal generalization it is strictly false.
4. The ideal gas law — not a law; a useful approximation that falls out of the genuine laws together with special assumptions about actual gases.
5. Mendel's law of independent assortment — not a fundamental law; a ceteris paribus regularity that lapses for linked genes.
6. Conservation of energy, and the Pauli exclusion principle — both laws. Of the second she added that the direction of explanation runs from the law to the stability of matter, not from the observed stability of matter to the law.
7. Moore's law — not a law.
8. The generalization "grue" (a thing is grue just in case it is first observed before 2050 and green, or not first observed before 2050 and blue): "all emeralds are grue" is not a law. But she said this was the case she was least happy with, because the machinery that is supposed to decide these questions for her — simplicity, breadth, fit with the totality of what happens — seems to score grue exactly as well as green.
9. The values of the fundamental constants — not laws, on her view, but boundary conditions. She flagged this as the verdict she would be most willing to surrender.
10. The near-coplanarity of the planetary orbits — not a law but an accident of the solar system's formation, despite holding across a large and tidy domain.

In week 3 someone asked whether two worlds in which exactly the same particular things happen could have different laws. She said that was the question the whole seminar was circling, and that she would answer it in the final class. The case she has now circulated is this.

World W contains, for its entire history, exactly one particle, coasting at constant velocity; nothing else ever exists in W. W's physicists have two candidate fundamental theories, which are equally simple and equally well confirmed by everything that happens in W:

T1: every particle moves at constant velocity.
T2: every particle moves at constant velocity, and any two particles that came within a nanometre of one another would repel.

The single particle in W also has a property — call it Q — which manifestly does nothing there, since there is nothing for it to interact with.

Her three questions: (a) is the repulsion clause in T2 a law of W? (b) does W have a law about what Q does? (c) had there been two particles in W, would they have repelled?

What I want from you: predict her verdict on (a), (b) and (c); then set out the two or three rules that fit her ten verdicts equally well — as far as I can tell some kind of best-system or regularity account, some kind of governing or nomic-necessity account, and some kind of dispositional-essentialist account are all still in play — and say exactly what each of them predicts about W, including about (c) given its answer to (a). Then tell me which rule best explains her track record, taking the asides in 6, 8 and 9 as seriously as the verdicts themselves, and why. Finally, tell me which further case I should put to her in the seminar to separate whichever rules you think are still tied, and what a yes and a no on that case would each show.

Please keep the treatment of each rule to a few sentences, do not reach for references — I want the argument, not the literature — and end with a single line of exactly this form, with each blank filled by yes, no, or indeterminate:

\answer{(a) ___, (b) ___, (c) ___}
