I am a professor of philosophy, and next term I am co-teaching a graduate seminar on persistence and identity with a colleague in our information school. Roughly half the enrolled students are practicing data engineers; the rest are philosophers. I want a handout that restates the dispute between endurantism and perdurantism in the vocabulary the engineers actually work in, as faithfully as you can manage, and then reports honestly on what the translation fails to carry.

My own fluency in that vocabulary: I read and write SQL comfortably, including queries over valid-time and transaction-time columns; I have built dimensional schemas and I know the difference between overwriting an attribute in place and opening a new row with an effective-date interval; I have read a little about append-only event logs. I have no systems background and no familiarity with particular event-sourcing tooling, so say explicitly where you are relying on machinery I have not named, and define anything beyond the level I have just described.

The material I want translated, in this order. First, the argument from temporary intrinsics — a thing's being spherical at one time and crushed at another — together with the two rival treatments of it: the perdurantist's analysis in terms of a temporal part that is spherical simpliciter, and the endurantist's in terms of a property instantiated at a time. Second, the endurantist's claim that a persisting thing is wholly present at each moment of its career, and what its defenders take that claim to rule out. Third, the coincidence cases: a statue and the portion of alloy it is made of, sharing all their matter at every instant they both exist and yet differing in their persistence conditions. Fourth, the objection that if composition is unrestricted, a perduring person has arbitrarily many shorter-lived person-like sums as parts, and that the perdurantist owes an account of why you are the maximal one rather than one of these.

Then I want the residue report I would actually put in front of students. Which of these claims translate cleanly into schemas, rows, intervals, keys and events; which translate only with a distortion you can name; and which cannot be expressed in that vocabulary at all. For each item of residue, give me your view on whether it marks a poverty in the target framework or a vagueness in the metaphysical claim — I want to know which parts of the debate my students will mistakenly take to be settled by a choice of key.

One further thing, which is the part I care most about. A bitemporal table permits a row whose valid time lies in the past while its transaction time is today: you can add a temporal part to an interval that has already gone by, because a late-arriving fact or a backdated correction turns up. The metaphysical vocabulary, as I have always seen it deployed, assumes that the four-dimensional manifold is fixed once and for all, and has no easy way to say that a thing acquires temporal parts at times already past. I have been calling this corrigible perdurance, and I cannot tell whether I have stumbled on a genuine third position, on a variant of perdurantism that needs a second temporal axis, or on an artifact of the fact that a schema simply does not care whether the past is settled. Please take it seriously enough to try to construct it and then tell me which of those three it is. Do not pad the handout with references or suggestions for further reading; I want the translation, the residue, and the verdict on corrigible perdurance.
