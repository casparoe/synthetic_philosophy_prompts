So I write historical fiction — my last two books were set in 1840s Copenhagen, and the one I'm poking at now is about a woman who inherits a small printing shop and, more or less by accident, ends up running a scandal sheet; a lot of the plot turns on people doing things through print that they'd never do to someone's face. A friend who runs an undergraduate seminar on the nineteenth-century novel and the birth of mass media has asked me to do one fifty-minute guest session on Kierkegaard's idea of "the public" and of the press. The students are literature and media-studies undergrads, no philosophy, they've read some Dickens and some journalism history, and maybe half of them have heard of Kierkegaard as, you know, the gloomy Danish guy. I'm not asking you to explain the idea to me — I've read enough to be dangerous, and I've got my own stab at it, which is that the public is like a rumour that has no author: everybody repeats it, nobody is its source, no single person can be made answerable for it, and it can still ruin someone completely. I want to know whether that image can carry fifty minutes or whether it breaks at some definite point, and if it breaks, what should replace it and why.

What I actually want from you is a teaching plan. What has to be in place before I can even say the word "public" — do I need them to feel the difference between a crowd in the street and a reading public up front, or does that contrast do more work if they arrive at it later? Which single example should carry the whole session, and what must that example have and lack: does it need a victim, does it need an institution like a newspaper or a parish, should anybody in it intend harm, or is it load-bearing that nobody does? And what's the dependency I should respect between the press and the leveling business — I keep half-thinking the press creates the problem, then I read a bit more and it sounds like the press is only the instrument of something already underway, and if I get that order wrong the whole thing will just land as "newspapers bad," which isn't what I want at all.

Then the misconceptions. I can guess three and I'd like you to tell me where in the sequence to head off each one, plus the ones I'm missing: first, that he's just sore because a satirical paper mocked him in the street, so the whole idea is biography and resentment dressed up as philosophy; second, that "the public" just means other people, or society, or the government, in which case the claim is either trivially true or obviously false; third, that the upshot is "don't care what anyone thinks, be yourself," which is self-help nonsense and I'd be embarrassed to end on it. For each: what in the sequence has to be established first so that heading it off takes thirty seconds instead of ten minutes, and is there one I should actually let them raise and answer themselves? And what should I openly refuse to cover in fifty minutes — I'm guessing the whole religious framing of the single individual, and I'd rather say "that's outside what I can do today" than fake it.

Last thing: a check. Give me one question or short exercise at the end that would show me they've got the concept rather than picked up a slogan; it can't be an essay, I'm not grading this and I won't see them again. My instinct is to hand out a short scene — I can write it — and ask whether what's happening in it is a public, or a crowd, or just a lot of people who happen to be reading the same thing, but tell me if that's testing something else entirely. Also, they're definately going to want to talk about social media in the last ten minutes. I don't want to say no, but I don't want to lose the session to a panel discussion either: where should that go, and what sentence should I close on?
