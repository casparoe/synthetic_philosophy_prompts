I sit on the interpretive review committee of a small regional museum that is reinstalling its prehistoric gallery around a single fen-edge assemblage. Let me call it the Alderwood Fen material — the findspot is being withheld under our heritage-protection agreement, so please don't ask me for excavation reports or comparanda; treat the description below as the entire body of evidence available to us. Recovered from a waterlogged shaft spanning roughly two centuries of use: fourteen fired-clay anthropomorphic figurines of strikingly similar size and manufacture, each with the head snapped off at the neck and the head itself absent from the deposit; fragmentary animal bone dominated by pig and cattle jaw elements; three broken querns; and a scatter of worn domestic pottery. There is no settlement layer within four hundred metres, no architecture that reads as a shrine or precinct, no iconographic or textual corroboration of any kind, no grave goods nearby, and no skeletal material at all.

Our curator of archaeology wants a case panel that says, in effect: these were votive offerings to a mother-goddess, deposited by a priestly corporation attached to a shrine we have not yet located, and the heads were removed as part of the offering. Our education officer wants the opposite — a panel saying that we simply do not know what any of this meant. I think both are too crude, and I have been trying to get the committee to reason using two devices of my own. The first I call the two-gap problem: there is a formation gap between what people did and what ended up in the shaft (loss, discard, curation, taphonomy, the assumption that what we lifted is a frozen behavioural moment rather than a palimpsest), and a separate interpretation gap between the deposit as recovered and any claim about belief or office; an argument can be strong across one gap and worthless across the other, and I want the memo to score them separately rather than averaging them into a single confidence level. The second is what I call hostage predictions: for any interpretation we print, we should be able to name at least one material consequence it is genuinely committed to — something that ought to show up in the record and that would be surprising or unexplained if the interpretation were false. On my count, the goddess-and-priesthood story is hostage to restricted access or provisioning (stored surplus, a bounded precinct, markedly unequal distribution of cult paraphernalia across households) and to recurrence of a specific deposition signature; we have evidence for none of these. Worse, the pattern we do have — small, repeated, low-cost deposits in a place with no architecture — is produced just as readily by household-level practice, by the deliberate disabling or killing of objects at the end of their use-lives, by the disposal of things that had become dangerous, polluting, or embarrassing to keep, and by any number of possibilities we would never think of. My underlying worry is that *ritual* and *cult* are functioning as what I call a ritual sink: categories whose extension silently grows to absorb every deposit our economic and functional vocabularies cannot explain, so that the explanation becomes unfalsifiable and the apparent interpretive gain is illusory.

What I need is the memo I will circulate before Tuesday's vote. Assume I already know the standard toolkit — middle-range theory, equifinality, the palimpsest point, the general worry that all interpretation is theory-laden — so don't spend the memo re-explaining those; deploy them. The memo should (i) recommend one concrete labelling policy for the case — the confident cult panel, the agnostic panel, or some layered alternative you specify yourself — and indicate what wording or panel structure you would actually accept; (ii) say what, if anything, we should spend our small research budget on before committing to any stronger claim, and why those particular tests would discriminate between the competing readings rather than merely add descriptive detail; and (iii) give an honest statement of the strongest thing that can be said for the position you are rejecting. Two pressures should be engaged rather than waved aside: a donor has offered a six-figure sum conditional on a goddess-themed gallery, and a local group describing itself as descended from the people who made these objects has asked that the figurines either be displayed as ancestors or not displayed at all. Write it as a document a committee can vote on — recommendation up front, reasons underneath, roughly nine hundred words — and please don't settle the question by observing that the sacred/secular distinction is itself a modern projection; I have made that point in committee twice and it has not moved anyone.
