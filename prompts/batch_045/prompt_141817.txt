I write for the science desk of a national newspaper, and next month I am giving the twenty-minute opening explainer at a festival panel on consciousness. The audience will be paying general readers: educated, attentive, with at most a half-remembered introductory course, and most of them will have come because they want to know whether neuroscience has found the place in the brain where experience happens. My plan is to spend the time on the case against the Cartesian theater and on the multiple-drafts picture Dennett puts in its place, and I want the audience to walk out holding the argument rather than a slogan.

What I need from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself.

First: the order the components have to arrive in, what depends on what, and roughly how much of the twenty minutes each beat gets. My instinct is that the intuitive picture has to be made genuinely attractive before it is attacked, since it does real work — it explains why the visual world looks unified rather than like a bundle of separately timed signals, why some of what the brain handles is available to me and some is not, why there seems to be a moment at which something crosses over. Until the audience has felt that pull, I suspect the denial will sound like pedantry. Test that instinct and tell me what actually has to precede what.

Second: the one example that carries the whole thing, with what it needs to have and what it needs to lack. My candidate is a masked-presentation case of the kind Dennett uses to raise the Orwellian/Stalinesque question — a briefly presented stimulus the subject may or may not report, where the irresistible question is whether the content was briefly present in experience and then revised out of the record, or whether it was never present as such and the record was as it now stands all along. Say whether that is the right vehicle, what it requires in order to work (a crisp temporal structure, a report the audience will trust, and no easy way to settle the question, I assume), and spend a sentence on why the familiar crowd-pleasers — change blindness, the cutaneous rabbit, the color-phi, phantom sensation — would each carry less.

Third: the misconceptions this particular audience will bring or form, and the exact point in the sequence where I should defuse each one. The ones I expect are professional-grade rather than naive, and I would like your judgment on which are live and which I am inventing: that the upshot is that consciousness is an illusion, or that Dennett is denying that anyone is conscious at all; that the multiple-drafts picture is merely the negative claim that there is no single place where it happens, so that any talk of distributed processing counts as agreeing with it; that a global-workspace or broadcast architecture answers the objection rather than restating the very picture in new vocabulary; and that if there is no theater, then there is no fact of the matter about a subject's report, so that accuracy and confabulation collapse into one another. The last two are the ones I care about most, since I expect a well-read listener to raise them in questions.

Fourth: a closing check that would show me the idea has landed rather than been memorized. Mine is this: I describe a lab announcement claiming that, with finer-grained timing and a masking design, researchers have finally determined whether the revision in a case like my example is Orwellian or Stalinesque, and I ask the audience what they make of it. Tell me what a listener who has actually got the point should be able to say about that announcement, and what response would tell me they had only absorbed the conclusion.

Fifth: what I should openly decline to explain in twenty minutes, and say out loud that I am declining. I would rather name a question and hand it back than mangle it: I take it that the dispute over whether there is something over and above the drafts, and the metaphysics of the self that listeners will want to bolt on, both belong in this category, but tell me what else does, and tell me what the audience will suspect I am hiding.

Two constraints on the plan. I will not lean on the slogans — I am not going to tell a festival audience that consciousness is an illusion, and I would rather nothing in the sequence rested on the book's well-known lines. And the argument has to leave intact two things the audience is right to insist on: that there is a real difference between a subject's seeing something and their confabulating it, and a real difference between being conscious and being unconscious. So show me where in the sequence each of those gets secured, and what goes wrong if I secure them too late.
