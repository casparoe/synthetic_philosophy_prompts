I chair the renewal panel for a national funder's programme-grant scheme. Twice a year, seven of us decide whether to continue roughly fifteen multi-group research programmes for another five-year term, and we must attach to each decision a one-page rationale a lay board can sign off on. The panelists are working scientists with no philosophy training, and we have about forty-five minutes per programme using only documents the teams already submit: the founding proposal, the previous renewal report, and the current progress report with its publication list. No new evidence, no interviews.

Our arguments keep reinventing, badly, the vocabulary of Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes. Someone says a group has stopped predicting anything and is only patching its model; someone replies that every worthwhile programme looks like that for a while; we end up voting by temperament. I want a decision procedure instead. It must operationalize the distinctions that do the work — the protected core versus the belt of auxiliary hypotheses, a progressive versus a degenerating problem shift, an auxiliary that increases what the programme claims and predicts versus one adopted purely to absorb a result that had already come in, and a novel fact anticipated in advance versus one merely accommodated afterward — using only the paperwork we have, in the time we have, in language a physicist or a geneticist will apply without being taught the theory. Give it as ordered questions, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, say where it terminates in a wind-down and where it hands the question to the board because the issue is our appetite for risk rather than a fact about the science, and mark plainly the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and who owns it.

Two things will make or break it. First, several of our applicants now file what I call a deferral register: at the outset they list the anomalies they intend to set aside and those they claim they will solve. Does an anomaly's appearing on that list exempt it from counting against the programme, or does declaring it merely make a later degenerating verdict cleaner? And where does repeated narrowing of the class of systems the core is claimed to cover fall — content-decreasing, or genuine discovery about scope — when each narrowing comes with a well-argued paper explaining why the broader claim was overstated? Second, I take Lakatos to hold that whether a programme is degenerating is visible only with hindsight, and that young programmes deserve a long leash; we have an afternoon and a fixed budget. Tell me what my procedure is doing that his framework forbids, and what dated proxy it substitutes for a historical verdict.

Then run it on three cases. (1) A programme that stated three quantitative results in advance and had all three confirmed, but whose model now needs five auxiliary parameters fitted after the fact, two of them introduced after a measurement conflicted. (2) A programme that registered two anomalies for a ten-year deferral, deferred them, and in the meantime published heavily on questions internal to its own framework, work everyone agrees is excellent and that settles nothing anyone outside cares about. (3) Our flagship, which three of us including me were trained in and which the board cites as a success, whose core claim has been narrowed four times over three terms. If the procedure says to wind down (3), say so plainly and tell me whether to believe it. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do — in particular whether it can distinguish a degenerating programme from a field whose instruments are temporarily inadequate, since that distinction decides most of our hard cases.
