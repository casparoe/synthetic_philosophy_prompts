Analyze the response to Hurricane Katrina as a case for luck egalitarianism. I don't need the storm narrated back to me, and I don't need the standard machinery defined — brute versus option luck, the choice/circumstance cut, expensive tastes, the complaint that the view either abandons the imprudent or helps them only on demeaning terms. I have that; use it.

Treat the following as stipulated, since I don't want the analysis to turn on a disputed number: on 29 August 2005 the federally built levee system around New Orleans failed and most of the city flooded; a mandatory evacuation had been ordered a day or two earlier; a large and heterogeneous population remained — people with no car, no cash and nowhere to go; people who stayed to care for relatives; people who had sat through earlier evacuation orders that came to nothing or produced gridlock; people who judged the designated shelter of last resort less safe than their own roof; and people who stayed out of stubbornness, fatalism or bravado. Roughly 1,800 people died. Rescue and relief failed for days. The evacuation plan had presupposed private vehicles. In the aftermath came the familiar fights: who gets rescued first, who gets emergency help, who gets compensated, and whether public money should subsidize rebuilding in a floodplain.

What I want is a disciplined mapping that runs both directions.

First, the mapping. What does luck egalitarianism take the relevant outcome here to be, over what interval, and for whom — and which distinctions among the people who stayed can it represent and which are invisible to it? Take the subgroups one at a time rather than averaging over them. Say what it implies about rescue priority, emergency relief, compensation, and rebuilding subsidies separately, since I suspect they come apart. And say plainly where it implies nothing: it is normally a comparative doctrine about how much people should have, not a doctrine about urgency or about anyone's duty to do anything, and it may have no place at all for institutional agents, even though the city and the federal government are the obvious other candidates for responsibility in this story.

Second, two structural problems I think the case exposes. One is the choice of temporal unit. Assessed at the level of the episode, a strict line seems to say the imprudent are owed less or nothing; assessed over whole lives, a catastrophe of this size plausibly swamps whatever imprudent choices preceded it, and the same doctrine may now demand generosity. Does luck egalitarianism specify which unit of assessment is correct, or is that an unmarked parameter it needs and never supplies — and if so, does anything internal to the view settle it? The other is entanglement. For most of those who stayed, the decision to stay was itself shaped by things the view classifies as brute: poverty, dependence, prior experience of official failure, the degraded character of the options actually offered. Can the choice/circumstance distinction be unpicked when circumstances shape a choice rather than merely constrain it, and what is left of option luck when the institution now allocating aid is among the authors of the option set?

Then the verdict on the doctrine itself: is Katrina a confirmation of luck egalitarianism, an anomaly for it, or a case it was never built to anticipate? Does an emergency setting sharpen the objection that it abandons or demeans the imprudent, or disarm it? And if you had to patch the view minimally so that it can speak to a case like this, what is the smallest addition that does the work, and what does that addition cost it elsewhere?
