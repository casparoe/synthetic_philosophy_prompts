I'm giving the best man toast at my brother's wedding this Saturday and I need help with it, because there's something structurally weird about the occasion that I don't want to paper over with a nice line.

The weirdness: my brother and Ana have been married for eighteen months already. They went to a courthouse on a random Tuesday in the middle of a visa mess, told exactly one person, and have been legally married ever since. Saturday is a ceremony in a barn with no legal or religious effect whatsoever — the registrar signed the paperwork a year and a half ago, so nothing changes when they say the vows. Ana is a philosopher of action, which is how they met (long story, she was at a thing my sister dragged me to). I have no philosophy training — I'm a wildland firefighter — but over about four years of Sunday dinners I've picked up enough to know the shape of Kavka's toxin puzzle: the one where the billionaire hands you a pile of money right now for forming, tonight, the intention to drink something foul tomorrow, and you know perfectly well that once the money is in hand you'll have no reason at all to drink it, and the question is whether you can really intend it.

Ive been chewing on why that keeps coming back to me at 2am. Saturday looks like the toxin case run backwards: the payoff is already in the bank and the act comes afterwards. So what is everyone actually watching? Is there anything left for the vows to do? My own half-baked thought, which Ana says is probably wrong, is that the courthouse only handed over some of the goods — the legal ones — and that the recognition of her mother, my parents, the aunts, is still genuinely contingent on them showing up and saying it out loud in front of a hundred people, which would make Saturday not pure theater after all. And if that's right then the puzzle's setup, with the payoff as a single lump delivered up front, is hiding something, and the real question is about staggered payoffs rather than about intentions. But I might just be rationalising because I want the day to mean something.

The constraints, which are most of the work:

- About twenty people from Ana's department will be there and will recognise the puzzle the second you go near it. The other hundred are my brothers' family from a town of nine hundred people who will hear the word toxin and start worrying. It has to land for both without becoming a lecture or a joke that eight people get.
- You can't say Saturday is the real wedding. Ana asked me not to and she'd hear it anyway.
- You also can't say it's just a party. Her mother cried when she found out about the courthouse and has told everyone for a year that this is the wedding, and she's in the front row.
- My brother would rather be told the true uncomfortable thing than a comfortable false one, so don't resolve the weirdness with something you think is false just because it's warm.
- Four or five minutes, in my voice, not a textbook's. I'll cut it up myself afterwards.

Then write me a short note, a page at most: where the toast leans on a contested reading of the puzzle or on a solution that people actually argue about, where it fudges to keep the room comfortable, and what would make you rewrite it. I mean that last one seriously — give me the single consideration that, if you took it seriously, would flip the toast into saying something I'd hate to hear. Don't bother with citations, I'll chase down whatever reading I want later on my own.
