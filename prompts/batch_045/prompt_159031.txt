I have been asked to advise the board of the public school district that hosts my university's teacher-training program, and I want to be sure I audit the argument the superintendent is actually making rather than the one it resembles. The board is deadlocked over whether promotion from eighth to ninth grade should turn on a single standardized exam. Rather than settle that, the superintendent proposes a four-year "parallel pilot": half the district's middle schools keep the exam gate, half replace it with a portfolio review conducted by teachers; every school reports annually to a common dashboard (ninth-grade course completion, subsequent test scores, attendance, dropout); and the board then adopts whichever version scores better, revisiting the question every four years thereafter. She presents this as taking Dewey seriously: inquiry begins in an indeterminate situation and resolves it by acting on the world and attending to what follows, so we do not need a prior account of what promotion is for — the experiment will tell us. Please audit the analogy, not the policy.

Give me, in this order and at roughly these lengths: "The mapping" — a table of what is supposed to play the role of hypothesis, treatment group, control group, random assignment, replication, outcome measure, experimenter's standpoint, subjects, and closure, plus about 120 words on where the mapping is doing real work; "Where it holds" (about 120 words); "Disanalogies that matter for the use being made" (about 300 words: for each, say which step of her argument it undercuts — note in particular that the schools are not exchangeable units, that the units are cohorts rather than repeatable trials, that the dashboard's measures are not independent of the treatment and appear to presuppose one contested answer about what promotion is for, that the costs of a failed "trial" fall on children who cannot be re-run, and that there is no standpoint outside the dispute from which the results simply speak); "Disanalogies I should ignore" (about 100 words); "The strongest reply from inside the view" (about 150 words — I have in mind the claim that in social inquiry the fact that those affected are also the inquirers is a virtue, not a defect, since the public constitutes itself through inquiry; tell me what granting that costs the epistemic work the analogy was doing); "Does the conclusion survive?" (about 200 words, separating the weak claim that deliberate iteration beats deadlock from the strong claim that iteration can replace settling the end in advance, and specifying what would have to be supplied for the strong claim to be licensed — including whether pre-registering the dashboard before the pilot begins helps, and why or why not); and finally "A better analogy, if there is one" (about 150 words — consider whether the familiar federalist image of jurisdictions as laboratories, or something closer to common-law reasoning from precedent, carries more of the argument than the laboratory does).
