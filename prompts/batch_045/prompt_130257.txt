I am trying to work out what I actually believe about reparations for historical injustice, and my verdicts on particular cases do not seem to line up with one another. I would be grateful for help extracting a principle from them, or, if no single principle will serve, for a small set of candidate principles together with an honest account of what each one forces me to give up.

The cases below are invented composites rather than real claims. In every one of them, assume that the wrong is real and well documented, that it was a serious wrong by any standard, and that it would ground a claim for compensation if the original parties were still alive and the clock had been stopped.

1. Traceable gain. In 1860 a colonial government seizes a fertile valley from a farming community and transfers title to a chartered company. The company's successor still holds the land. The community's descendants are identifiable, poorer than the national average, and include people who were displaced as children. Reparation is owed, to the descendants, from the current holder. This is my easy case.

2. Dissipated gain. The same seizure, except that the valley turns out to be worthless, the company abandons it and collapses in 1890, and nothing of value passes to anyone. The descendants are still disadvantaged relative to the national average. Something is owed, and I am not confident that it should be less than in case 1, even though no one is now enriched. I do not know what the payment would be for.

3. Legality at the time. The seizure was authorised by statute and approved by most of the enfranchised population, and everyone involved knew it would never be reversed. Owed, and in no smaller measure than in case 1. I reject this defence outright.

4. Living victim. A state imprisons an innocent person for twelve years, knowing the evidence was fabricated. Owed. I take this to be the paradigm that the other cases must be shown to resemble, and I distrust any principle that handles it by a separate mechanism.

5. Unwanted benefits. The perpetrating state later built roads, schools and hospitals in the victims' region, with the result that the descendants' living standards are higher than they would have been had the wrong never occurred. I do not think this reduces what is owed at all. The roads-and-hospitals offset strikes me as an evasion, though I cannot say precisely why it differs from an offset I do accept.

6. Accepted part-payment. In 1900 the same state pays a compensation package, accepted in good faith by the victims' leaders, worth roughly a third of what we would now judge adequate. I think this genuinely reduces the debt and may discharge it.

7. Overlapping descent. The wrongdoing state's population has turned over: nine in ten of its current citizens descend from people who arrived after the wrong, as refugees the state later admitted, and roughly half of the claimants also descend from the perpetrators. I do not think the newcomers owe anything personally. But I also think the state owes reparation out of general taxation, which is to say partly out of their pockets. I cannot make these two verdicts consistent.

8. Existential precondition. Had the wrong never occurred, almost none of the people now claiming would have been born, since it changed who met, married and migrated. This is not a bar to a claim, but I want to know why it is not a bar, given that it seems to show that no claimant was made worse off than they would otherwise have been.

9. Dead culture, no demand. The wrong destroyed a language and a set of religious practices. The descendants are now glad that their children speak the majority language and do not want the practices revived. Something is owed, but restoration is neither available nor wanted, and I do not know what shape the payment should take.

10. The case I go back and forth on. A wrong committed two centuries ago by a private corporation whose shareholders cannot be traced. The wrong itself is over, but its effects are locked into a still-operative constitutional clause that continues to advantage one group. Sometimes I think the state must act, because it is the state that now enforces the clause; sometimes I think the chain has gone cold and nothing is owed.

My tentative principle, which I suspect is inadequate: a historical wrong generates a debt held by the transgenerational political community that committed it and owed to the transgenerational community that suffered it; the debt does not lapse with time; its magnitude is the loss that the wrong explains, net of benefits the victims received because of the wrong; and it is discharged by payment to the victim community's legitimate representatives. As far as I can tell, this gets case 5 wrong and leaves cases 2, 7 and 9 unexplained.

What I would like from you:

One or two candidate principles, stated precisely enough that they generate verdicts rather than gestures. If the correct answer is a two-level account, with a ground of duty separate from a rule of quantification, say so and state both parts.

For each clause of each principle, the case that motivates it, and what breaks if the clause is removed.

Verdicts on at least three cases I have not listed. Suggestions, though you need not use them: wrongs committed by a church or a corporation rather than a state; claimant groups that were themselves perpetrators of a comparable wrong against a third group; wrongs that are still ongoing rather than historical; wrongs whose perpetrator state was later conquered and replaced by a government drawn largely from the victims' descendants; wrongs whose modern descendants publicly endorse what was done.

An explicit statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, whether a more complicated principle could accommodate them, and whether you think the added complexity is worth paying for.

A verdict on case 10, with the reasoning that produces it.
