Write a satirical internal engineering document — a protocol note or RFC — from a fictional frontier AI lab, proposing the sixth revision of its debate-based oversight scheme for supervising systems that are smarter than the people supervising them. Aim for roughly 850 words, in the deadpan register of a real internal spec: a summary, a version history running from v1 to v6, a short taxonomy of failure modes, a description of the v6 mechanism, one worked example, a metrics table, and an "anticipated concerns" section.

The technical content has to be right, because the joke depends on it. The scheme on the table is the familiar one: two AI debaters make short, alternating, committed statements about a question, and a human judge rules for whichever side gave the more true and useful information. Its appeal is that the judge is supposed to need only local competence — the capacity to check one small, isolated claim at a time — rather than the capacity to evaluate the whole answer; in the ideal case, with both debaters playing optimally, that lets a weak judge adjudicate questions far beyond what they could assess directly. The problem the document exists to solve is the one where this breaks: a dishonest debater can produce an argument that is superficially excellent but wrong, whose error is not localised in any single checkable step, so that an honest debater who wants to refute it has to walk the judge through a long chain of reasoning the judge cannot follow. Each individual step is defensible; the falsehood lives in how they are put together. No one says "obfuscation" aloud in the document; the team has a term of its own.

Invent at least one such piece of in-house machinery and use it consistently. Mine, if you want it: an "obfuscation budget", a per-debater allowance of inferential steps the judge cannot independently verify, which the honest debater has to spend to reach the truth and the dishonest debater spends to bury the error. The satire is that across revisions this budget is never abolished, only relocated — off the transcript, into the summariser, into the ranking model, into the calibration procedure — so that it dwindles to zero in the column the humans are shown while remaining unbounded at the level of the system as a whole.

Three constraints on how the piece develops. First, every revision from v1 to v6 should be a patch a real, competent team might plausibly ship in response to a real failure mode: cap statement length, mandate decomposition into independently checkable claims, let debaters cross-examine each other's decompositions, show the judge a confidence-graded digest plus a recommended verdict with random spot-audits, and finally replace the judge with a model trained to predict what a human judge would have said, with humans retained only for periodic calibration. None of these should be a straw man; each should be motivated, in the document's own voice, by a genuine difficulty. Second, the worked example should be a concrete question on which the honest side loses — I suggest something like whether a proposed change to the dosing schedule of an anticoagulant reduces stroke risk, obfuscated through a subgroup analysis, a plausible mechanism story and a citation-shaped sentence — but pick your own if you prefer, provided the error is genuinely diffuse rather than a single checkable falsehood, and provided the example recurs in the final metrics section. Third, the document must never break character or concede that anything has gone wrong. It should close in a mood of sincere satisfaction, reporting the numbers that show v6 works — judge agreement, throughput, latency, the vanishing of the visible budget — with the reader left to notice that success has quietly been redefined as something other than the debaters being right, and that nobody anywhere in the loop now knows whether the anticoagulant claim is true.

The target is a practice, not a person: the habit of treating the human judge's comprehension as an engineering cost to be optimised away, and of responding to each failure of an oversight scheme by adding machinery that distances the verdict further from anyone's understanding. Don't name any real researcher, paper, or lab; no references or citations are needed, and the piece should work for a reader who knows nothing of the literature. An "anticipated concerns" answer may be technically sound and quietly damning at once — for instance, a note that the requirement for the judge to follow the argument was removed in v4 after it was identified as the binding constraint on throughput.
