I cover cognitive science for a national newspaper, and there is a line I keep hearing at consciousness conferences that functions less as a claim than as a conversation-stopper: if you couldn't have reported it, you weren't conscious of it. I heard it most recently in a Q&A, when a global-workspace theorist was asked whether the extra detail people seem to enjoy in Sperling-style partial-report cases — the sense of having seen the whole array when only a few items can be named — is really conscious experience that overflows what we can access. He answered with that sentence and moved on, and the room treated the question as closed. I don't think it is, and I want to give my readers a sharper map than the one I currently have.

The complication is that the sentence seems to slide between a methodological caution, an empirical hypothesis, and a claim about what consciousness is. To make it worse, I was recently shown unpublished data from a lab I visited that muddies the categories I'd been using. Subjects viewed briefly masked scenes and reported seeing nothing, and could not discriminate the scenes above chance on a forced-choice task immediately afterwards. Yet during the masked presentation there was a late, sustained long-range synchronization between prefrontal and parietal sites, the specific scene category was decodable from delay-period activity, and — this is the part I keep turning over — the unseen scenes later intruded into a surprise recognition test and shifted subjects' aesthetic preferences in a way that a matched unseen-but-not-decodable control did not. Nobody in the lab wanted to say what this shows.

It also seems to me that "available for report" bundles together at least three different things, and I'd like the analysis to work with my rough three-way split: availability to verbal report and deliberate action; availability to consumer systems that have no route to speech or to any current task (memory consolidation, affective weighting, response priming); and availability to the wide set of consumer systems that global workspace theorists seem to mean by broadcast, where the signature is something like ignition plus recruitment of many downstream systems. Whether the lab finding counts as access at all depends on which of these is meant, and I suspect the slogan's rhetorical force comes from trading on that ambiguity.

So here is what I want, as something I can adapt into a piece rather than a verdict I can quote without understanding. First, lay out the distinct precise theses that the line could be expressing, from the near-trivial to the radical, with a verdict on each: which are methodological cautions about evidence, which are substantive empirical claims about the neural correlates of availability, which are constitutive or metaphysical claims about what phenomenal consciousness consists in, and which are false or empty. Second, say for each reading what it implies about my three-way distinction and about the masked-scene finding, and separately about the clinical literature on patients diagnosed as unresponsive who show command-following in their neural responses — a case where everyone is happy to attribute consciousness without any report at all, and which I think the slogan handles badly. Third, and most important for my purposes: tell me which of those readings would actually entitle the speaker to dismiss the overflow question as he did, and whether that reading is one he could plausibly defend or one he would retreat from once stated precisely. Fourth, tell me how much of this dispute is verbal — a fight over what the word "conscious" is being used to pick out, a theoretical kind versus a pretheoretical one — and how much is a genuine substantive disagreement. Finally, given your diagnosis, say what would count as evidence that could actually settle something here, and be honest if the answer is that nothing could, and why.
