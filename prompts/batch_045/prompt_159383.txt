I have been moving towards a position about public reason and religious arguments in politics, and I want the strongest case against it set out before I commit myself to defending it.

Nearly every view in this neighbourhood seems to me to locate the requirement in the wrong place. The restraint view puts it in what the citizen may vote on. The translation view puts it in what the citizen must be able to produce when challenged. The convergence view puts it in whether the considerations the citizen acts on happen to be ones their fellow citizens also accept. All three, as I read them, make public reason a property of someone's actual psychology at the moment of acting, and all three then inherit the familiar difficulty that motives are mixed, that deliberation is expensive, and that most coercion is produced by officials rather than by citizens.

The position I am attracted to makes public reason a property of the record rather than of any mind. Call it the deposit view: a piece of coercion is publicly justified just in case there exists, somewhere in the accessible public record — select committee reports, civil service memoranda, explanatory notes laid alongside the statute, judicial reasoning, official guidance — a rationale for it that does not presuppose the truth of any contested comprehensive doctrine. On this view the deposited rationale need not be the reason anyone in fact acted on, need not be known to more than a handful of officials, and need not be persuasive to anyone who reads it. It must simply be there, in principle retrievable by a citizen who went looking, and not fabricated after the fact in a form that no one at the time of enactment could have accepted. I find it attractive because it appears to answer the objection that restraint silences religious citizens, it explains why we do not in practice require voters to have read the committee reports, and it fits the messy fact that most legislation is drafted by officials whose actual reasons are doctrinal, electoral and administrative all at once.

What I want from you is a map of what this view risks.

Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and in each case say whether a defender of the deposit view should concede it or should dismiss it as changing the subject. I am especially interested in three lines of attack. First, whether the view collapses into one of its rivals: if every stable regime generates some deposited rationale as a by-product of ordinary administration, then convergence reasoning will get there anyway and the view does no work; conversely, if any competent government lawyer can produce one on request, the view looks like little more than a licence for what one might call reason-laundering. Second, the retrospective case: a legislature bans a practice for straightforwardly doctrinal reasons, and a genuinely non-religious rationale comes to exist only a decade later because it depends on empirical findings made after the ban took effect. Does the deposit view legitimate the ban retroactively, and if so is that a reductio or a consequence I should learn to live with? Third, the asymmetry objection: the view looks trivially satisfiable in a large religious democracy with a thick civil service and impossibly demanding for a small jurisdiction with no research staff, which suggests it is measuring bureaucratic capacity rather than legitimacy.

I also want you to say what the view predicts that its rivals do not — for instance that legitimacy can improve through officials' action with no change in anyone's motives, that a regime can be fully legitimate while nearly everyone in it is acting on religious reasons, and that destroying or withholding the file would itself undermine legitimacy — and whether any of those predictions are ones I should find unacceptable. Finally, assess honestly whether the clauses "in principle retrievable" and "not fabricated after the fact" have insulated the view into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a defect in a view of this kind in the way it would be in an empirical theory.

For what it is worth, the two things I would personally find most moving are the reason-laundering worry and the case of a deposited rationale nobody at the time could have accepted — say one resting on statistical claims since discredited. Tell me if you think I am wrong to weight them so heavily.
