I spent forty years working in stratigraphy and palaeomagnetism, and since retiring I have been attending a philosophy reading group at our local library — about fifteen of us, mostly retired engineers, two physicists, a former classics teacher, and nobody with any formal training in the subject. I have rather rashly agreed to lead one session, forty minutes with a whiteboard and no handout, on whether the past and the future are real in the way the present is. I am not asking you to explain presentism and eternalism to me; I have read enough to know the crude versions of each, and I find myself convinced by one on Mondays and the other on Thursdays. What I want is a plan for how I should explain what the disagreement actually is, to that room, in that time.

The bit I most need help with is the order, because I have a private gloss on the dispute that I suspect distorts it and I would rather find that out now than at the whiteboard. I have been telling myself that everything turns on whether there is a standpoint-independent fact about what time it is — a detached "now", if you like — so that presentism is the claim that there is exactly one privileged time, the block view the claim that there is none, and the moving-spotlight picture the claim that there is one but that which one it is keeps shifting. Tell me whether to build the session on that spine or, if it misleads, where exactly it goes wrong; and tell me what has to be securely in place before any of it can be introduced, and what depends on what afterwards. Relatedly, I had thought to carry the whole thing on one example, and my candidate is the end-Permian mass extinction: everyone in the room will grant that it happened, nobody has feelings about it, and in my old field we discuss events 250 million years back as comfortably as we discuss the weather. Is that the right case, and what would a carrying example have to have — and, just as importantly, lack — to do this work? I worry that deep time makes the block view look trivially correct, that a bereavement would make presentism look morally compulsory, and that any example involving clocks or signals will drag us into simultaneity within two minutes. Say which to use and why not the others.

On misconceptions, I can predict most of them and I would like you to place them in the sequence rather than merely list them. The physicists will announce that special relativity settled the matter — no absolute simultaneity, therefore the block universe — and someone will treat that as a conversation-stopper. Someone else will hear the block view as saying the future is already written and deliberation therefore a sham. A third will object that the present is a durationless instant, so on presentism nothing ever changes. Our brightest member will very likely conclude that the whole thing is a verbal quibble about the word "exists" and that both sides should just go home. And I expect at least one person to run together "the future is unreal" with "the future is open and undetermined", and to take eternalism to be committed to denying that anything we do makes a difference. Tell me the point at which each must be headed off, in what order, and whether my instinct to defuse the relativity point early, before it hardens into a verdict, is right or whether it should be held back. Finally, give me one question to put to the group in the last five minutes whose answers would show me whether they have grasped what is at issue or have merely acquired a label — including what a confident wrong answer would sound like — and tell me what I should openly decline to settle and say I am declining: whether relativity really forces the block view, whether the question is substantive at all, and whatever else you think belongs outside forty minutes. I do not want to convert anyone; I would like them to leave disagreeing with one another about the right thing.

Set the plan out as a numbered sequence with rough minute marks, in British English, and keep the whole thing under 600 words.
