The set-up, briefly: you know the standard mugging case — someone says give me five dollars or I'll kill some astronomically large number of people, and expected-utility maximization with unbounded values says pay. The move everyone reaches for first is evidential: my credence in any such claim should be tiny, the bigger the number the mugger names the more competing claims of that size there are for my credence to spread over, so the credence collapses faster than the payoff grows and the product stays small. My worry is that this only works against a mugger who has to pick their number before finding out how suspicious I am. Call it an **adaptive mugger**: one who gets to name a number as a function of my credence (I'll kill K divided by however confident you are in me), or who can just crank the number up and repeat the threat with a fresh one every time I damp, or who can observe my dampening rule and name the number that maximises the product under it.

What I want you to argue: that no reply which works purely by shrinking credence — including the many-possible-muggers point, the symmetry between muggers and anti-muggers, and the self-undermining-because-the-claim-is-so-specific point — can be robust against an adaptive mugger. Any credence-shrinking rule that defeats *every* number the mugger might name is either (a) assigning effectively zero credence to something I have some positive evidence for, or (b) secretly a structural fix: equivalent to putting a ceiling on how much value a payoff can have, or to adopting a decision rule that doesn't let arbitrarily small probabilities win by brute force. So the real content of the mugging isn't be careful of weird skeptical scenarios, it's that expected-utility maximization plus unbounded value plus any nonzero probability at all, however tiny, is what's doing the work — you can give up any one of those, but you can't get out of it by getting cleverer about the evidence.
