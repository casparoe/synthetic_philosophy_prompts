I am a senior in a philosophy elective, and for the last few weeks my teacher has been working through a set of cases she calls the non-identity problem. I have made up a case of my own and I want to know where she will land on it. More than the verdict, though, I want the rule: what principle she is actually running, which other principles fit everything she has said just as well, and what further case would tell them apart.

Here are her verdicts so far. She presents these orally and we are not reading the original texts, so please work from my summaries, and please keep harmed and wronged separate, because she insists on that distinction.

One. A fourteen-year-old becomes pregnant. Had she waited until she was twenty she would have had a different child, conceived from different gametes, whose life would have gone much better: better health, a steadier home, better prospects. The child she actually has gets a hard start, but her life is well worth living and turns out decent. Verdict: what the girl did was wrong and she should have waited, but she neither harmed nor wronged the child she has, and the wrongness is not a matter of what the act did to that child.

Two. A country chooses between two energy policies. The first makes the current generation better off and later generations worse off, but the policies also change which people are ever conceived, and the people in the worse-off future still have lives that are good and clearly worth living. Verdict: the first policy is the worse choice and should not be picked, but it harms no one; the case for the second cannot be that it benefits the people who would have existed under the first.

Three. A health authority can fund a fertility treatment for women with a certain condition, or fund prevention of that condition. Under the treatment, children who have the condition are born; under prevention, different and healthy children are born later. Verdict: fund prevention, and again not because that benefits the children who would have existed under the treatment programme.

Four. A woman smokes heavily in the third month of an already existing pregnancy, and the child is born with a health problem she would otherwise not have had. Verdict: here the mother does harm her child and does wrong her; the non-identity problem does not even arise, because the same child would have existed either way.

Three further things she has said that I think carry weight. First, she is entirely comfortable saying that an act can be seriously wrong even when it wrongs nobody, and she says the wrongness in cases one through three is of that impersonal kind. Second, she runs harming and wronging together in one direction only: no harming, then no wronging, whatever else may be wrong with the act. Third, and this is what I cannot resolve, she has offered two different lines for when a person who owes her existence to the act counts as harmed by it. Early on she said, roughly, that a child is wronged by being brought into existence only if the life is not worth living. Later she said she was not sure that is the right line, and that it might instead be a decent minimum, so that a child created into a life below that minimum is harmed even if she is glad to be alive and would rather exist than not. When a classmate pointed out that these are different lines, she said she had never had to choose between them, because every case we had done involved someone whose life sat clearly above both.

So my case is built to fall between them. A woman in her late thirties is prescribed a course of medication that, for roughly eight weeks while it is in her system, causes severe damage to any embryo conceived during that window. Her doctor knows this and negligently fails to mention it; he is careless, not hostile. She conceives during the window. The child, Lena, is born with a severe condition: major physical disability, chronic pain, dependence on others for most of her care, a much shortened life expectancy. There is no question that Lena's life is worth living or that she thinks it is: she is in school, has close friends, plays wheelchair basketball, and has said clearly and more than once that she is glad she exists and would not trade her life for nothing. Everyone also agrees that her life is far harder than any decent minimum would allow. Had the doctor warned her mother, she would have waited eight weeks and conceived a different child, who would have been healthy and whose life would have gone much better. Her mother says both of the things parents in this position say: that she cannot regret having Lena specifically, and that she wishes she had been told in time to wait.

What I want from you: two or three rules that fit her verdicts in cases one through four, not just the textbook one; what each of them predicts about whether the doctor harmed Lena, whether he wronged her, and whether what he did was wrong; your best guess about what she will say, with the reason you prefer it and how confident you are; and one further case I could bring to office hours that would separate whichever rules survive my case. Two complications I would like you to address directly rather than dodge. Does it change anything that the doctor's carelessness also wronged the mother, by depriving her of the chance to wait, so that the act is wrong for person-affecting reasons anyway? And does Lena's own retrospective attitude, that she is glad to exist and would not give her life up, do any work here, or should it be set aside as irrelevant to the comparison that matters? Finally, if my case has some structural feature the earlier ones lacked that makes it a bad test of the two lines she named, say so plainly and tell me what to change.
