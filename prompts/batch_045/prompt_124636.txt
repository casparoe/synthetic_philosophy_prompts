A few months ago I was appointed to my university's academic-misconduct panel, and since then I have read around sixty mitigation and appeal letters. Almost none of them dispute what happened. Most argue that what happened does not settle anything, and they do it in one of two ways. The first says, roughly, that the student could not have done otherwise. The second says that it made no difference anyway: the plagiarism detector would have flagged the submission whether or not she had thought about it, and the new submission filter would have blocked it if the matter had been serious. My colleagues treat the first as the philosophically serious excuse and the second as confused. I have come to think the ordering is backwards, and I want a semester to find out whether that is right.

The background is the one you would expect: the standard argument against the principle that moral responsibility requires alternative possibilities is the Frankfurt-style case, in which an agent acts on her own reasons while a counterfactual intervener, who never actually does anything, would have brought about the same action had she shown any sign of holding back. The usual verdict is that she is responsible and could not have done otherwise, and the live work has since gone into whether she retains some flicker of an alternative, whether such a case can be built without a reliable prior sign, and whether responsibility is better located in the actual sequence, via reasons-responsiveness or quality of will. What I have not seen is a clean separation between the feature these cases are built to remove, alternative possibilities, and a distinct feature they also remove: any difference the agent's agency makes to what happens. These come apart. There are cases with full alternative possibilities but no difference-making, as when the agent acts and a safeguard would have reversed the outcome whatever she had done, or when someone else was going to do it anyway. And there are cases where difference-making survives in one dimension but not another, as when the agent cannot affect whether the outcome occurs but does affect how, or for what reasons, it occurs. My hypothesis is that the intuitions Frankfurt-style cases are used to elicit track difference-making rather than the absence of alternatives, and that the principle of alternative possibilities has been a crude proxy for a graded difference-making condition that nobody has stated precisely, partly because nobody has said which dimension of difference is supposed to matter.

I need a proposal for a one-semester fellowship to write a single paper of about eight thousand words testing that hypothesis. Please give me no literature review and no citations; I will fill those in myself and can place the argument against actual-sequence and reasons-responsiveness views on my own. What I want argued is a one-sentence question with a determinate answer; why existing positions leave it open; why this reframing is a better bet than the obvious alternatives, which are constructing yet another variant Frankfurt case, returning to the flicker-of-freedom dispute, or running a vignette study, which I am not trained to do; what the two or three possible conclusions are and what would decide between them; what would count as the project collapsing rather than succeeding, for instance if every difference-making case turns out to remove an alternative possibility as well; a realistic division of the semester; and what the paper could offer, at the end, to a body like my panel that has to decide whether installing a safeguard changes which blaming practices are warranted. Structure it under those six headings and keep the whole thing under six hundred words.
