I'm a second-year PhD student writing up a one-page "where I stand" on speaker meaning for my department's proseminar — the point of the exercise is that the others get to attack it, so I'd like a consistency check before I post it. I came to pragmatics sideways: I was trained mostly in formal semantics and have drifted toward a broadly Gricean picture without ever giving up the reflexes I picked up there, and I have a nagging feeling that my list is a coalition that can't actually govern.

Here is where I've landed, as six claims plus two case verdicts.

(a) Speaker meaning is the theoretically basic notion. The standing meaning of an expression-type in a language is to be explained as a pattern in what speakers have meant by it, not the other way round.
(b) What is said is fixed by conventional meaning plus disambiguation and reference assignment and by nothing else — no unarticulated constituents, no free enrichment, no modulation. Whatever goes beyond that belongs on the pragmatic side.
(c) Implicature is a species of speaker meaning. To implicate something is, in the relevant broad sense, to mean it, so the analysis of speaker meaning applies to it, including the requirement that the speaker intend the hearer's uptake to come about by way of the hearer's recognizing that very intention.
(d) Generalized conversational implicatures are default inferences keyed to lexical alternatives: they go through whether or not the speaker had any corresponding intention, and they survive the speaker's sincere and public disavowal of them.
(e) Metaphor, irony, and hyperbole are cases of making as if to say: what is said is retained, often something patently false, and the real content is implicated.
(f) The diagnostic for implicature is calculability — it should be reconstructible from what is said together with the assumption that the speaker is observing the cooperative principle and the maxims, or flouting them in the service of something further. I also lean hard on cancellability without contradiction as my working test for distinguishing implicature from entailment.

Two verdicts I am attached to, which I take to fall out of (d). First, the familiar reference-letter case: a professor writes of a candidate that he has beautiful handwriting and is unfailingly punctual. I want to say the letter implicates that the candidate is no good at the job, and that it still does so if the professor, testifying under oath and entirely sincerely, says he intended to convey only what he literally said and had no critical intent at all. Second, and this is the one I invented and care about most, the automated-message case: an airline's system pushes out notifications generated by a template no individual reviews, and one reads, "Some flights out of Newark are delayed this morning." Passengers take themselves to learn that not all of them are. I want to say this is a genuine conversational implicature of the airline's utterance, not merely a habit of interpretation on the passengers' side, even though there is no person who formed any intention about those passengers and the airline, if asked, would deny meaning to rule out a complete shutdown.

What I want is an audit, not an overview. I have four worries of my own and I suspect at least one is a non-issue, but I can't tell which. (i) That (a) and (b) are each perfectly standard but are motivated by pictures of the semantics/pragmatics interface that cannot both be right — (b) wants what is said to be a stable, intention-free input, while (a) says nothing in linguistic meaning is intention-free — and that if this is correct then the calculability story in (f) is being asked to start from an input it does not have. (ii) That (c) on the one hand and (d) together with my two verdicts on the other simply cannot co-exist, in which case something has to go and I don't know which loss is cheaper. (iii) That (b) plus the cancellability test in (f) leaves a class of contents homeless: ordinary enrichments that are not cancellable and that, on (b)'s austere story, are not part of what is said either. (iv) A friend in psycholinguistics insists that (d)'s defaultness is incompatible with (f)'s calculability, on the grounds that anything automatic isn't an inference; I think she is wrong, but I'd like that settled. One pairing I am *not* worried about is (a) with (e) — it has never bothered me that irony requires positing a what-is-said the speaker manifestly does not mean — and I'd like to know whether that complacency is justified.

So: tell me which worries are real and which dissolve on inspection, rank the real ones by how badly they hurt rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each one say precisely which claim would have to be revised and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the list. If one of my claims is merely underspecified, so that the tension depends on how I precisify it, say so and give me the precisification that preserves the most. Finally, name the single revision that would clean up the most at the least cost, and tell me whether what remains is still recognizably a Gricean position or whether I have quietly become something else.
