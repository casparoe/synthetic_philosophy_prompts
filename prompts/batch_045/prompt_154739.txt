Im in a phil of physics seminar right now and we just spent two weeks on substantivalism vs relationism — newtons bucket, whether a lone planet in an otherwise empty universe would still bulge at the equator, Mach, the hole argument, all of it. Everyone argues about the metaphysics but I got stuck on something much more boring: what do the people who actually have to say whether something is rotating do?

Because as far as I can tell the working practice does two things that pull in opposite directions. (1) The official standard for not rotating in astronomy — the International Celestial Reference System and its realization, the ICRF — fixes its axes by requiring that they show no net rotation relative to a set of very distant extragalactic objects, mostly quasars, tracked by radio interferometry across the earth; the optical frame from the Gaia satellite is then tied to that, and Earth orientation parameters (where the pole is, how far the planet has turned today) plus deep-space navigation are all measured against it. So the standard of non-rotation is defined by the distant matter in the universe. (2) At the same time the field also trusts sealed-box instruments: ring laser gyroscopes and other Sagnac devices, including big ones bolted to bedrock in Germany and Italy that are used to measure the earth's rotation directly, which never look at the sky at all — they just report an angular velocity for the lab, full stop.

My prof says (1) shows the practice is basically Machian, my textbook says (2) shows general relativity keeps rotation absolute because inertial structure is a real field, and I cant tell who is reading too much into plumbing. So: what does the practice as a whole actually presuppose about what rotation is? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and be sharp about what it strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits fine with. Is (1) and (2) an inconsistency, or are they just measuring different things — is the quasar frame only a convenient, accessible marker for a frame that's there anyway?

Two more wrinkles I want in. First, frame dragging: spinning mass does shift local inertial frames a bit. Does that mean the practice is secretly committed to a partly relational picture, or is it one effect among many? Second, a made-up case: suppose over a couple of decades VLBI and the big ring lasers slowly drift apart by a tiny amount, and the drift tracks how the mass in our cosmic neighbourhood is distributed and moving — imagine a huge rotating shell of matter around the observatory. Which instrument then gets to say what is really not rotating, and what would geodesists have to do about Earth orientation parameters and the frame definition, and on what grounds? Does it matter if the drift is local (only near the mass) rather than global?

And what would concretely have to change in the day-to-day work if you swapped in full relationism, or full substantivalism, or the view that spacetime is just a field and the question is ill-posed — or is the practice underdetermined, so that several theories license exactly the same measurements and both my prof and the textbook are over-reading? Also worth saying whether GR even gives one global answer about who is rotating or only local ones, because if it's only local then the practice might presuppose less than the whole metaphysics fight assumes.
