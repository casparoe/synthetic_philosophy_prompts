I'm in a philosophy of language seminar and we finished the Kripke unit last week; the assigned question for the study group was whether the existence of necessary a posteriori truths settles anything about the semantics of names. Two people in the group argued about it for most of an hour and then asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Nadia and Dev are both the kind of person who has done the reading twice, so I don't think either of them is confused in a shallow way, but I also can't tell which of their moves are load-bearing. Here is how the exchange went, as close to verbatim as I can manage.

(1) Nadia opened by saying that the modal argument only refutes naïve descriptivism. If the description associated with a name is rigidified — the name is held to express something on the order of 'the actual F', a description that latches onto whatever is F in the actual world and then keeps hold of it across other possible worlds — then an identity like 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' comes out necessary, and it is still an a posteriori matter which object the actual F is. So, she said, a descriptivist predicts the necessary a posteriori too, and the modal argument by itself does not discriminate between direct reference and a sophisticated descriptivism; whatever work Kripke does against descriptivism is done by the epistemic and semantic arguments, not by the modal one.

(2) Dev replied that the rigidification move buys necessity at a price the descriptivist cannot pay. First, rigidifying a description does nothing about reference failure: in the style of case where a name is introduced via a description that turns out to have no unique satisfier, the rigidified description fails to denote just as the unrigidified one does, so no identity involving it is true, let alone necessary, whereas a causal chain secures a referent whether or not any description is satisfied. Second, if a name really meant 'the actual F', then a fully competent speaker who reflected on the meaning of the name would be in a position to know a priori which object it picks out; but no one knows a priori which heavenly body 'Hesperus' picks out, so the name cannot mean that.

(3) Nadia granted that the epistemic argument has real force against naïve descriptivism, but said Dev's second point begs the question by assuming that whatever a term means must be a priori accessible to a competent speaker — that is just the assumption a Fregean or a two-dimensionalist denies, since on those views a term presents its referent under a mode of presentation whose referent is discovered rather than read off. And she said reference failure is everyone's problem, not just the descriptivist's, since Kripke's own picture has to allow names like 'Vulcan' to come out empty, and in any case there are hybrid views that fix reference causally while retaining descriptive content. She then introduced a stipulation of her own — she was explicit that the term is hers and not from the reading. Call a singular term a 'tracking' term if the convention governing it is not to hold the referent fixed across possible worlds but to denote, at each world, whatever satisfies the associated description in that world. For tracking terms the identity comes out contingent. Her challenge: whether English names are rigid or tracking looks like a contingent empirical question about the conventions of our linguistic community, not something settleable from the armchair. But then the necessity of 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' rests on a contingent metasemantic fact about us, and in some possible community that used the same words with a tracking convention the same sentence would express a contingent proposition — so Kripke's conclusion is itself only knowable a posteriori, and the argument has no a priori purchase on it.

(4) Dev answered that we do have evidence about which convention is in force, namely our own counterfactual judgments under qualitatively specified suppositions: describe a situation with two distinct bodies in the morning and evening positions and ask whether the identity is true there, and speakers who have the terms judge that it is, and re-describe the case as one in which Hesperus turns out not to have been visible in the evening rather than one in which the identity fails. And he added that Nadia's tracking terms are just definite descriptions read with narrow scope, so her hypothesis is the hypothesis that names are scopally descriptions — which is the thesis under dispute, not a new option — and framing it as an empirical question about conventions misdescribes the dialectic, since the relevant evidence is the pattern of modal judgments and not a survey of what speakers say they mean.

(5) Nadia made three points in reply. (a) She said she has read that speakers' judgments about just these cases vary across populations and with how the case is worded, which she took to undercut treating the intuitions as evidence for a semantic theory rather than as data to be explained. (b) She said rigidity may be doing no work at all: the necessity of identity follows from the logic of identity together with Leibniz's law, so any true identity between co-referring singular terms is necessary, and the appearance of contingency when definite descriptions are involved is an artifact of scope, since the de dicto reading is available and is the one hearers default to. On this reading the necessary a posteriori is not evidence about names in particular; it falls out of the logic of identity plus the truth of the identity. (c) She pressed a symmetry worry: if fixing reference by a contingent property and then designating rigidly yields necessary a posteriori identities, why does the analogous case with the standard metre yield merely a contingent a priori truth rather than a necessary a posteriori one? Either the cases are disanalogous and the direct-referentist owes an explanation, or the apparatus of rigid designation plus reference-fixing is not doing the explanatory work it is credited with.

(6) Dev said that (b) concedes his point in different vocabulary: what distinguishes names from descriptions is precisely that names are not scope-sensitive, and 'not scope-sensitive in modal contexts' is another way of saying rigid, so rigidity is exactly what is carrying the necessity, and Nadia has at most redescribed it. He also said that the whole discussion may have been too narrow: whatever we say about names, a theoretical identification like 'water is H2O' is not an identity between objects, and kinds do not have their identity conditions settled in the same way particulars do, so the necessary a posteriori may turn out to be a family of phenomena with different explanations rather than one phenomenon with one.
