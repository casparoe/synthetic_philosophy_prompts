Analyze MCAS on the 737 MAX as a corrigibility problem. Facts you can take as given: MCAS was a flight-control law that pushed the nose down when a single angle-of-attack sensor reported a high angle; it acted repeatedly, re-arming a few seconds after each time the pilots pulled the column; there was a way to shut it off (the stabilizer trim cutout switches) but using it cost the crew the electric trim they then needed; the system wasn't in the manuals or initial training; two hull losses followed (Oct 2018, Mar 2019).

Don't give me the safety-engineering postmortem, I've read enough of that. What I want is the mapping, and the mapping run in both directions.

Three things I want kept apart, because I think the shutdown-problem literature runs them together: (a) the system doesn't resist being switched off, (b) the system doesn't act to make switching it off hard, costly or unattractive, (c) the operator can tell that they are fighting the system and can predict what switching it off will cost them. Call them whatever you like. Which of the three did MCAS actually violate, and which one is doing the explanatory work?

Second, the thing I keep chewing on: MCAS did pause whenever it was overridden, then resumed. So it "yielded" without standing down. Is there a distinction between yielding and deferring that the standard setup misses, or is that just the literature's incentive-to-resist-shutdown point in different words?

Third, say plainly where the analogy dies. MCAS had no utility function, no world-model, nothing you'd call an incentive, and the reason it was built to resist correction was Boeing's commerical one - no simulator time, no recertification. Does that relocate corrigibility from a property of the agent's objective to a property of whoever designed it, and if so does the shutdown problem become a different problem than it's usually stated as? I'd rather you say the case just isn't about corrigibility than force it to be.
