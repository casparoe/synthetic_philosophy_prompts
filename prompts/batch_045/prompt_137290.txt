I had a long argument last night with a friend about AI safety via debate, and I would rather settle it with a room full of students than with more talking. Her position: give a judge who cannot check the facts directly two adversarial debaters and the truth wins, because a false position is harder to defend — every further fabrication has to stay consistent with the earlier ones, so error compounds and gets exposed. My position: that is an empirical claim about the shape of justifications, and it fails whenever the true answer's support is long and conjunctive while a false answer's support is short and sounds conclusive. In that case the liar is playing a cheaper game, and a judge with a small question budget will reliably reward them. I want my students to hit this themselves rather than hear me assert it.

Design a runnable in-class game for that purpose. Setting: about 18 mixed CS and philosophy graduate students, one 75-minute seminar slot in a course on machine learning safety, one room, whatever I can photocopy, no money (points toward a homework token, or just tally marks on the board). Participants should come away having felt that debate's verdict is a function of which position is cheaper to defend, not only of which is true, and having noticed themselves slipping from arguing for the truth into arguing to win.

What I need: a real question with a determinate correct answer that these students cannot verify from background knowledge, where the true answer requires the judge to follow a chain of several checks and the false answer rests on a single item that looks decisive; private dossiers for the two debaters; debaters assigned a side and scored on winning rather than on being right; and the asymmetry built into the rules and incentives rather than announced — a small fixed budget of judge questions, some bonus for brevity or confident delivery, a scoring rule keyed to the judge's stated confidence rather than to accuracy, a penalty for hedging. Run it in two rounds so the same rules are seen working in one case and failing in the other, and give me the dossier template so I can build it from ordinary non-specialist material. No lecture up front; do not name the failure mode until the debrief.

The debrief should map particular moments onto the pieces of the disagreement: what debate is supposed to buy a weak judge, the difference between conjunctive and disjunctive justification, the way scoring on winning turns a debater into a lawyer, and whether the remedy is better judging or a different incentive. State plainly which of these the game genuinely demonstrates and which it only gestures at. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guesses are that someone who knows the answer leaks it, or that the debaters lie so ineptly that the collapse never happens — and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce about the real proposal: many rounds, a judge with far more knowledge than my students, debaters far more competent than mine, and no pre-labeled correct answer to check against.

Lead with a single sentence naming your core design move and the reaction it is built to provoke, then give materials, rules, timeline, debrief, failure modes, and limits. No preamble and no restating my question.
