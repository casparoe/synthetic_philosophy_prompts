Epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness holds that phenomenal states — the states a subject is in when there is something it is like for her — are causally inert: they are brought about by physical processes, but they in turn produce nothing physical, and so play no causal role in what a subject believes or says. The paradox of phenomenal judgment presses on this. A subject's judgment that she is now in phenomenal state S seems to be true because of S, and to count as knowledge at least partly in virtue of that; yet if S does no causal work in producing the judgment, it is unclear why the judgment should be anything better than a coincidence, and equally unclear how the epiphenomenalist's own introspectively grounded conviction that she has phenomenal states could survive her view.

Suppose an epiphenomenalist tries to blunt the paradox by substituting a non-causal tracking relation for causation. Say that a subject's judgment is S-sensitive when this counterfactual holds: had she not been in S at t, she would not have judged, at or around t, that she was in S. There are two very different routes to S-sensitivity, and they are easy to run together. On the first, phenomenal states supervene on the physical, so any situation in which S is absent is one in which the subject's brain is different, and if the introspective mechanism registers that difference, the judgment differs as well. On the second, we hold the subject's entire physical history fixed and simply suppose S away; since S was doing no causal work anyway, the judgment is exactly what it actually is. Call these, if you like, supervenience-based sensitivity and deletion-based sensitivity.

Give an example of a conceptual issue that arises here: a reasonably precise question about the paradox — not the paradox itself restated, and not the bare question of which of the two readings of the counterfactual is correct — whose content, force, or significance depends on whether the epiphenomenalist is appealing to supervenience-based or to deletion-based sensitivity. It should be a question that reasonable epiphenomenalists could still disagree about even after agreeing which reading is the correct one. State the issue in a sentence or two, identify which reading generates it, and explain in a few sentences why it is a substantive question for the epiphenomenalist rather than a merely verbal dispute about the semantics of counterfactuals. Examples that only ask whether epiphenomenalism is self-undermining, whether phenomenal zombies are conceivable, or whether a physical duplicate of a subject would make the same judgments are too coarse-grained to count.
