hi — I'm a medical humanities PhD student (anthropology-ish by training, but I sit in on the clinical ethics consultations at the hospital where I do fieldwork) and there's a pattern I keep bumping into that I want to get properly clear about rather than just gesturing at. Here's the pattern as I see it:

Someone at the end of life whose suffering can't be controlled any other way can be sedated to unconsciousness and kept there until they die of the underlying disease, or of not being hydrated — continuous deep sedation. Everyone treats this as good, humane medicine. The same person, in the same bed, asking for the same relief, cannot be given something that would end things quickly, even though I think everyone also agrees the quick version involves less of the grim stuff: breakthrough agitation, families sitting around for days, the patient possibly having been suffering for longer before anyone okays it. And the sedation route is basically only on offer for people who are already dying. Someone with comparable suffering who is physically healthy gets told no. There's also the thing where patients can refuse food and fluids and receive palliative care while they starve, which as far as I can tell is another slow method that's permitted.

I've started calling this the wait-it-out clause: relief is legally available only in a form that works by waiting rather than by acting.

What I'd like from you is the implicit theory underneath the whole package, not the summary of the doctrine I can get anywhere. Specifically:

1. I can see the intending-vs-foreseeing story and the doing-vs-allowing story, but neither seems to do the work on its own — deep sedation to death and stopping eating and drinking both look pretty goal-directed to me. So what does the practice actually presuppose? Is it something like: death is not a benefit anyone may be given, but it is an outcome you may be allowed to arrive at? Or does it presuppose something stronger about the patient's remaining life being a positive good that clinicians have no standing to write off?

2. Which part is really carrying the justification — the severity of the suffering, or the shortness of the remaining life? If it's severity, then the terminal-illness requirement looks arbitrary and the practice is underdetermined in a way nobody admits. If it's the shortness of remaining life, then the practice is committed to some quantity-of-future-welfare view, with terminal illness as a rough proxy for a negative balance, and it's quietly refusing to ever check the quantity itself. Which reading fits the actual practice better, and are there corners where it presupposes inconsistent things?

3. The bit I care most about, since I've been reading the suffering-focused ethics stuff: the whole area seems to rest on a premise that view denies — that ongoing existence is good by default, so extreme suffering has to be managed by switching off the consciousness rather than by ending the person. If you actually took the asymmetry seriously, what would end-of-life practice look like? Would continuous sedation become available to anyone whose prospects are bad enough? Would the wait-it-out clause obviously collapse, or is there a version of it a suffering-focused view might still want, for its own reasons? And what would concretely have to change in the practice if the sanctity-flavoured premise were dropped but the doing/allowing distinction were kept, or the other way round?

Please tell me which bits are strictly presupposed by the practice and which merely fit comfortably with it — I need to know where I'd be overclaiming if I argued the practice commits clinicians to a full-blown view of welfare. I don't need citations or a reading list, I can find those myself; I want the reconstruction and the argument.
