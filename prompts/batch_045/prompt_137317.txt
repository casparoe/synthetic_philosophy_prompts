I'm a journalist working on a feature about collective intelligence — the "superorganism" literature on insect societies, swarm robotics, the idea that groups can be cognizers. The piece keeps circling back to a fictional case I first met in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach: Aunt Hillary, the ant colony who is a witty conversationalist, who has views about the weather and the aphid crop, and none of whose individual ants understands any of it. (The dialogue contrasts her with us: neurons are to a person, roughly, as ants are to the colony. Achilles is happy to grant the point for brains and chokes on it for anthills.) I keep meeting readers who think the whole thing is obviously just a metaphor, and one of them — a philosopher friend — handed me an argument that I now can't find the exit from. It seems to establish something much stronger than she wanted, and I'd like to know what I'd have to give up to avoid it.

Here is the argument, as close to her wording as I can manage. (1) A system has genuine mental states only if those states do real causal-explanatory work in producing what the system does; a state that makes no difference to behaviour is not a real belief, only a convenient as-if description — which is, I take it, roughly what people mean when they talk about the intentional stance. (2) Everything Aunt Hillary does — rerouting foragers to the new aphid cluster, sealing the nest before the rain — is fully caused by the movements of individual ants tracking local chemical cues; nothing over and above those movements and the pheromone gradients does any causal work in the colony. (3) The colony-level states I want to call her belief that the aphids are on the south branch are realized in those very movements and gradients, and the movements and gradients are not themselves mental. (4) If the causal work attributed to a putatively mental state is entirely discharged by goings-on that are not mental, then the mental state itself does no causal work: it is preempted rather than over-determining, since the non-mental goings-on already suffice on their own. (5) So Aunt Hillary's colony-level states do no causal work, and by (1) they are not real mental states; she does not literally believe anything, and there is no fact of the matter about what she believes, if anything. (6) But nothing in (2) through (4) depends on anything special about ants: my neurons are to me what her ants are to her, their activity is non-mental, and it is causally sufficient for everything I do. (7) Therefore no human being has beliefs either — which is absurd.

Two things I should say about where I stand. I'd like to keep (1) and (2): the demand that beliefs earn their keep causally, and the claim that the ant-level story is causally complete, both feel non-negotiable to me, and I don't want a diagnosis that rescues Aunt Hillary by making her spooky or by leaving gaps in the ant-level explanation. And I'd like the diagnosis to survive a wrinkle I invented after talking to a myrmecologist. Suppose we run two experiments. In the first, we swap Aunt Hillary's workers out a few at a time for workers from a neighbouring colony, holding the colony's signal network — trail gradients, alarm cascades, caste ratios — fixed as we go, until none of the original ants remain; call the result Leased Hillary. In the second, we fuse two neighbouring colonies' foraging networks into a single signal pattern, so that one pattern of trail and alarm activity is now realized jointly by ants from two nests; call the result the Siamese Colony. My instinct is that a theory which says the mind follows the signal pattern rather than the ants gets Leased Hillary right and says something interesting about the Siamese Colony, but I can't tell whether that instinct is independent support for the theory or just the same confusion dressed up in a new costume.
