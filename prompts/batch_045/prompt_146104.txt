I came to philosophy late, after a career in experimental physical chemistry, and I now attend a fortnightly reading group at our local adult-education college. Last term we spent an evening on the surprise examination, and I have not been able to let it go.

The version we were given was the usual one. A teacher announces on the Friday beforehand that there will be exactly one examination during the following week, Monday to Friday, and that the pupils will not know which morning it is to be until they are told on that morning. A pupil then argues by backward induction that it cannot be Friday, hence cannot be Thursday, and so on, and concludes that it cannot happen at all; the paper is duly set on the Wednesday and, apparently, comes as a surprise.

It seemed to me that the argument turns on the all-or-nothing notion of knowing or deducing in advance, so I suggested — half in jest — that the puzzle might behave differently if surprise were given a probabilistic gloss. Let us say that the examination is a surprise just in case, on the morning it is set and before the papers are handed out, the pupils' subjective probability that it is that morning is below one half. On this reading the teacher's announcement becomes a precise constraint: whichever day she chooses, the pupils' credence on that morning must be less than a half. Assume, as the original story does, that the pupils trust her completely and that there is exactly one examination.

I rather expected this to dissolve the difficulty. Two other members of the group promptly took opposite views about whether it does. I have set down their exchange as faithfully as I can, without editorialising; I have no settled view of my own.

Gerald:

(1) The probabilistic version does dissolve it. Surprise-as-low-credence is a graded notion, whereas the classical argument needs an all-or-nothing one, and at a threshold of one half the announcement is perfectly satisfiable. If the pupils spread their credence evenly over the five days and simply conditionalise as the week goes on, then their credence that the examination is that morning is a fifth on Monday, a quarter on Tuesday and a third on Wednesday — all below the threshold — and only Thursday, at exactly a half, and Friday, at one, fail. So the announcement is true on three days out of five and false on two. That is a restriction on when the paper may be set, not a contradiction.

(2) In any case, the objection Nadra makes asks too much of the pupils. It requires them to be perfect Bayesians sharing a common prior who can also compute a fixed point of their own credal state. Real pupils cannot do that, and surprise requires only that they have not in fact worked it out. On a bounded reading the announcement is satisfiable, and what looked like a paradox turns out to be a fact about the limits of human reasoning.

(3) That is not a cost of my view but a prediction, and it is the empirically adequate one. On my reading, making the pupils better at reasoning makes the announcement harder to satisfy. On hers, the announcement is unsatisfiable for every threshold short of certainty, which implies that no pupil has ever been surprised by an examination set under such an announcement. That is plainly false.

Nadia:

(4) The restriction in (1) is not a footnote; it is the paradox in new clothes. The pupils hear the announcement and are entitled to rely on it, so their credences cannot be the naïve uniform ones: those credences must themselves incorporate the constraint that the examination falls on a day on which the constraint holds. Once you require the credence function to be a fixed point of that constraint, no such function exists — and not merely at a threshold of one half, but at any threshold below one — because the last day is always eliminated, since on the Friday morning the credence would be one whatever the threshold, and the elimination then propagates backwards through Thursday, Wednesday and the rest. So the announcement is unsatisfiable, exactly as the classical argument claims, and nothing has been gained by going probabilistic.

(5) Boundedness, as invoked in (2), changes the subject. If the announcement is satisfiable only because the pupils are too slow to complete a derivation, then it is in effect a claim about their computational limits rather than about the examination: of a kind with saying that you will not manage to factor this number within the hour. That may be a coherent thing for a teacher to say, but it is not the claim that generated the puzzle, and it leaves her announcement true only for as long as her pupils remain slow enough.

(6) The objection in (3) misfires. What follows from unsatisfiability is not that surprise is impossible but that the announcement cannot be true and rationally believed by the very people it addresses. It has the structure of a self-referential statement, and the inconsistency surfaces only when the pupils accept it and use it. Papers do surprise people because people do not in fact treat such announcements as constraints on their own credences. That is a substantive discovery about a whole family of announcements of the form "this will happen at a time when you will not expect it", not a remark about the limits of pupils' reasoning.

At that point we adjourned.

I should be grateful for your adjudication, in three parts.

First, please go through the six moves and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification, and say what each gets right even where you think it fails. I am interested in who is right about which specific point, not only in who wins.

Second, Nadia sketched her argument in a hurry and I could not follow the details, though I have tried to write out the algebra myself. Please reconstruct it rigorously: does it go through at a threshold of one half; does it genuinely generalise to every threshold below one, and to any number of days; and does anything turn on the pupils being required to update by conditionalisation from a single prior, or on their fully believing the announcement? If there is a gap in it, I want to know exactly where it lies.

Third, who is more right overall, and what — if anything — are both of them missing? In particular, I would welcome your view on whether there is any reading of the announcement on which it is at once true and rationally believable by the pupils it addresses, or whether the honest conclusion is that the two of them are answering different questions.
