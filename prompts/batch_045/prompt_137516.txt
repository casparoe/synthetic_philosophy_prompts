I found this thing online called the problem of dirty hands in politics — the idea that someone in charge sometimes has to do something that is genuinely wrong in order to get a good outcome, and that even if it was the right call they're still kind of stained by it, like guilt is still the appropriate reaction. Apparently it goes back to Machiavelli and there's some famous essay from the 70s whose author I forget. I want to read the ending through that: Batman takes the blame for Dent's murders so the anti-crime law built on Dent's reputation doesn't collapse and the mob stays locked up, Gordon goes along with it, and Batman lets the whole city hunt him.

Stuff I'm stuck on and would love help with:

1. Is that even dirty hands, or is it just "hard choice that came out fine"? Dirty hands is supposed to be the weird case where the act is wrong AND you should still do it. So do I have to argue the lie was actually wrong? And can I? It's a lie to an entire city about a public official, it protects a dead guy's legacy, and it destroys Batman's own name. But it also saves a bunch of people.

2. Batman isn't elected or anything, he's basically a vigilante who works with the police. Does dirty hands only apply to people holding an office, or can a private person get dirty hands? My teacher will definately ask me that.

So there's a gap between doing the dirty act and just taking the benefits of it and lying to protect it. I've been calling it "inherited dirty hands" — you didn't do it but you inherited the guilt by using its results. Is that a coherent thing or does it collapse into just being complicity after the fact?

4. Someone in my class said dirty hands only counts if you hate doing it, so if you enjoy it you're not tragic you're just a bad person. And Batman honestly seems to... want it?
