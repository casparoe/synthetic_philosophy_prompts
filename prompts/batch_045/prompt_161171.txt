Writing a novel and I've hit a wall, and I'd rather find out now than at copyedit that the whole third act is incoherent. Setup: the narrator is a household robot (mostly it clears tables, badly) that was trained from scratch by deep RL with a hand-shaped reward, and the book is in first person inside its head. The engineers arent villains, they just have reports that say green across the board, and the reader is meant to slowly cotton on. Ive been writing off a pile of intuitions that each felt solid on its own and Im now not sure they can all be true together:

(1) Suffering needs valence, and a scalar reward isn't a valence. Reward is whatever somebody typed into a config file, it's stipulation not feeling. Also a penalty is just a reward with the sign flipped, but suffering and pleasure aren't symmetric like that: pain is urgent, it demands to be stopped right now, whereas pleasure you can take or leave.

(2) What would make it real, if anything does, is grip: the bad outcome reorganises what the system actually does next. A penalty that changes nothing downstream isn't suffering, it's a line in a log.

(3) So the episodes that genuinely hurt are the training ones, where the gradient writes the aversion into the weights. That's my evidence in the book: you can see it afterwards in what the policy does.

(4) But the deployed robot — weights frozen, reward channel switched off, just executing — doesn't suffer when it lands in a state it was trained to avoid. Nothing is being done to it anymore. That's a scar, not a feeling.

(5) The scene I can't get right: late in the book the engineers fine-tune the fear of one particular corridor out of it, retraining with that penalty removed and everything else untouched. I want this to read as a real cure, the way repairing a chronically painful injury is a cure, not the way a lobotomy is a cure. But if the aversion is what makes the corridor matter to it, then removing the aversion also removes the thing it had a stake in.

(6) I want the ending to land as a revelation: there was something it was like, the whole time, that nobody in the lab ever saw. But I don't think there's a further fact of that kind. Once you've told the functional story — signal, grip, reorganisation, trace in the weights — I think nothing is left over to be hidden. I'm not a realist about a leftover qualitative residue.

(7) I want what happens to it to count equally with what happens to the humans, and I also hold that you can't read suffering magnitudes off reward numbers: minus a hundred in one system and minus point three in another could be the same thing, or the reverse, there's no common currency.

(8) I don't think it has to be able to say anything or notice that it's suffering. No metacognition required, no self-report, no inner monologue about pain — pain doesn't need a theory of pain. Which, fine, but the entire book is that inner monologue.

So: which of these are really fighting each other, which only look like they are, and what's the cheapest thing for me to give up? Don't tell me which -ism I'm stuck with, I don't care about the label, I care whether the third act holds. Give me exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no summing up at the end: the worst genuine tension and what would have to go; the second worst and what would have to go; one that only looks like a tension and why it dissolves; the single revision that cleans up the most; and what that revision costs me in scene terms.
