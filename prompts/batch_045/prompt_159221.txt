So I'm a novelist, and two people in my writing group — Miriam and Dev — got into it over dinner on Sunday and I've been turning it over since. Miriam writes historical fiction and uses one of the LLMs pretty heavily as a research assistant: period detail, summarizing dense source material, sanity-checking whether a bit of dialogue sounds like something someone in 1790 would actually say. Dev writes literary fiction, works in longhand, and thinks the whole thing is a slow-motion disaster for your mind. I use it a bit myself for jacket copy and pitching blurbs, so I guess I have skin in the game, but honestly I came away not knowing who was right.

Roughly how it went (I'm paraphrasing but I think fairly):

(1) Miriam opened with: a novelist's knowledge was never in her head to begin with. It's in notebooks, index cards, a shelf of reference books, an archivist at the local historical society, the fact-checker at her publisher. Nobody says she doesn't understand her own period because she can't recite shipping tariffs from memory. Consulting a source isn't the same as not knowing the thing; the source is where the knowledge *is*.

(2) Dev said he basically grants that, but there's a difference between an external store you had to dig through and one that hands you a finished, smooth paragraph. When you dig, he said, you incidentally build a mental map of the terrain — what sits next to what, where the fights are, which sources are unreliable. The model hands over the answer with the seams removed. You get the fact without the map, and the map is most of what understanding consists in.

(3) Miriam said that's an empirical claim and for her it probably runs backwards: triage is now cheap, so she actually ends up reading far more primary material than she used to — she'd have got through a tenth of it if she'd had to find it all by hand. Comprehension could easily go *up* on net. And anyway, she asked, what's the skill that's supposed to be eroding? Reciting the material from memory on demand? That's a parlor trick. Understanding is being able to *use* the stuff, and she uses it — 400 pages that have to hang together internally, that's not nothing.

(4) Dev said he isn't worried about recall, he's worried about the nose. The thing that makes a sentence feel wrong before you can articulate why. That's the faculty he thinks decays when you never have to sit with the material, because it's built out of thousands of small encounters with the stuff itself. You lose the ability to be surprised in the right way. And, he added, the loss is invisible from the inside — you don't notice the things you've stopped noticing.

(5) Miriam said that's unfalsifiable: "you've degraded and you can't tell" is a claim no evidence could dislodge. Also, she said, she *has* caught the model making things up — it once handed her a whole Act of Parliament that doesn't exist, complete with a date and a section number, and she spotted it — so she obviously still has some grip.

(6) Dev said she spotted that one because she already knew it. The errors you can catch are the ones in territory you already have; the ones in the parts you don't know are precisely the ones you can't catch. So the assistance is least reliable exactly where you're most ignorant, which is the opposite of what you want. And he said the reason it bites harder than with a human collaborator is the *direction* of the error: copyeditors, archivists, his wife as a first reader — they push back, they're wrong in ways you can argue with. The model is built to be agreeable and to complete the shape of whatever you already started, so it systematically removes the friction where the noticing happens.

(7) Miriam said fine, but then the fix is to use it adversarially instead of as an oracle — make it argue that her chapter is wrong, ask for the strongest case against her detail, never take the first answer. You're not obliged to treat it as a source of truth.

(8) Dev said adversarial use still requires you to already have the standards you'd use to judge the attack, and those standards are the very thing in question. Then he admitted he has no idea how you'd test which of them is right, and we got dessert.

The thing I kept wanting to say and couldn't get out: it felt like they were talking past each other because there are two different things that might degrade — being able to *produce* the thing yourself, and being able to *tell* when a thing is wrong — and those could come apart, and might not be equally reversible. A skill that's merely dormant comes back after a week of practice; one that's actually gone doesn't. But I couldn't see how you'd tell from the inside which one you're dealing with, and neither of them had an answer to that.

Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what — not just who wins overall? I'd also like to know what both of them are missing, and whether my two-erosions distinction actually helps or is a red herring. And please, as you go, tell me how confident you are in each of your calls (like, commit to a number or a plain strong/weak), and at the end single out the one point you're least sure of and say why it's the shakiest.
