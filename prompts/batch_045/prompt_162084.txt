I'm a 4th-year PhD student in comparative neuroanatomy -- mostly allometric regressions, region volumes, neuron counts, and arguing with people about whether any of it means anything. I keep getting pulled into animal-ethics arguments (a philosopher friend, a reading group, the usual internet) where brain size comes up, and I want to finally get one position straight in my own terms instead of nodding along.

So here's the thing I want translated. The philosophers' move, as I hear it, has three parts: (1) brain size as such is never morally relevant; it's at best a proxy for the psychological capacities that actually do the moral work (consciousness, valenced experience, whatever the theory says); (2) it's a pretty bad proxy anyway, since whales and elephants have brains several times the mass of ours and nobody thinks they outrank us, while insects have essentially none and some people still want to count them; (3) so the honest version of the question isn't about size at all, it's about the underlying capacities, and once you can measure those directly the size variable drops out entirely. Sometimes there's a fourth move where someone swaps mass for neuron number and thinks they've fixed it.

My vocabulary is: body-size scaling and allometric exponents, residuals off a regression ( encephalization-style corrections, though I know the specifics are contested), absolute versus relative size, neuron number and neuron density, mosaic versus concerted evolution, mosaic versus concerted evolution, convergent versus homologous enlargement, metabolic cost, developmental and ontogenetic scaling, phylogenetic correction because species aren't independent data points, within-species versus between-species variance, and the general proxy-versus-mechanism / latent-variable-versus-measurement distinction. I'm fluent in all of that; I'm not fluent in 'morally relevant', 'moral status', or 'mattering for its own sake', and I don't need them explained to me as concepts -- I want them rebuilt out of things I already use, or shown to resist being rebuilt.

So: do the translation as faithfully as you can, one move at a time, and then give me the residue report. Specifically:

- Which parts map cleanly? My guess is the proxy point translates almost perfectly -- a proxy is only valid relative to a target and a population, and its validity can come apart from the thing it tracks -- but tell me if I'm flattering myself.
- Which parts only translate with distortion? I suspect 'morally relevant' does something my 'predictive of X' can't do, and that the philosophers want a difference in kind to ride on what are, in my data, purely graded quantities. Also: my variables are basically species- or clade-level, and the arguments that actually bite (human infants, dementia, the chimp-versus-disabled-human comparison everyone drags out) are about individuals. Say where the unit-of-analysis mismatch is doing work.
- What has no expression at all in my vocabulary? Is it just the normativity -- the fact that nothing in comparative neuroanatomy can tell you that a capacity is the kind of thing that makes a being count -- or is there something more specific, like the idea of a threshold, or of a being mattering independently of any comparison class?
- And the verdict I actually care about: is that residue a hole in my framework (it's built to do descriptive and causal work, so of course it can't say what matters), or is it evidence that the philosophers' three-part position is less determinate than it sounds -- e.g. are they quietly running together 'evidence for', 'constitutive of', and 'crosses a threshold', and would separating those change what they should say about brain size?

One more thing, and it's the part I'd push back on if I were being annoying: they say brain size is a bad proxy. Bad for what? In my field a measure is only good or bad relative to a specified target and a specified comparison set, and mosaic evolution means there isn't really one variable called brain size to begin with. So tell me whether the philosophers are implicitly committed to some empirical claim my literature could in principle adjudicate --and whether, if I handed them a genuinely better neural construct for whatever they actually care about, it would sharpen their debate or dissolve it. Don't give me references; I want the argument, not reading.
