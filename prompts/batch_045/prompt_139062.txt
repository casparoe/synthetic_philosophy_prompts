Write a short story of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, in American English, that dramatizes a conflict about the authority an earlier self has over a later one.

The setting is invented for this story and refers to no real statute, case, or author. Suppose a jurisdiction has adopted what its drafters call the Two-Lives Rule for advance medical directives. Under the rule, a directive stays binding when the patient's later condition counts as a decline: a thinning, erosion, or confusion of the concerns that organized the earlier life. The directive lapses, however, when a review panel finds that the patient has entered a second life — a new and self-consistent set of concerns and attachments that the earlier self could not reasonably have foreseen, and around which a recognizably whole life has been rebuilt. The rule reads cleanly. It is very hard to apply.

Build the story around the following situation. Maren Kask, seventy-one, formerly a composer of orchestral music, signed a directive twelve years ago refusing life-prolonging treatment in the event of a dementia diagnosis. She added a clause of her own devising: if she later becomes contented, that contentment is not to be taken as evidence that she wishes to go on living, because she regards such contentment as the pleasure of a person who has forgotten what she was for. Now she has early-stage Alzheimer's, lives in a memory-care residence, sings in a residents' choir, and has begun making short, simple songs — technically unlike anything she wrote before, but built on a four-note figure that recurs in a piece she composed at forty and that she no longer remembers having written. She has a treatable infection. If the directive binds, it is not treated.

The protagonist is Ada Sorensen, fifty-four, who works as a continuity reviewer: she interviews patients, reads the documents they left behind, and writes a finding that the panel rarely overrules. Twenty-two years ago, during a year of severe depression she now thinks of as a foreign country, Ada signed a directive of her own and has never revised it. Give her Maren's case.

Requirements:

1. Give both the earlier self's claim and the later self's claim their full strength, but embody them in objects, actions, habits, and memories rather than in speeches. No character should explain the philosophy; if anyone argues a position in words, let the argument be partial, interested, or awkward, and let the story show what each position costs.
2. Include at least one concrete artifact of the earlier self (Maren's clause, a letter, a score, a voicemail) and one of the later self (a song, a recording, a note).
3. Write in a literary but plain register. No melodrama, no sudden lucid interlude, no reassurance that the old Maren is "still in there," and no tidy verdict. Portray Maren's daughter and the panel's procedures fairly; no one is a villain.
4. End in the act of deciding rather than after it. The reader should be left genuinely unsure whether the earlier self has been obeyed or betrayed.
5. Use no citations, footnotes, or real authors' names, and include nothing that depends on looking anything up.
