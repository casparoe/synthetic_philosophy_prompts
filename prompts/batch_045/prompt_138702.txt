For about ten years I have kept up an argument with a colleague — call her M. — about Newcomb-style cases. She has never stated a theory and will not say whether she is an evidentialist or anything else; she simply rules on whatever case I put to her. I now have a long enough track record that I want to know what she will say next. Her verdicts so far, with opaque boxes unless noted and with the big box worth $1,000,000 throughout:

(a) The standard case: small box $1,000, predictor's recorded accuracy 99% over ten thousand trials, prediction made and box filled before she is called in. She one-boxes.

(b) Predictor accurate 72% of the time, small box $400,000: one box. Same predictor, small box $600,000: two boxes. Same predictor, small box $440,000: she says it is a toss-up and takes both.

(c) A perfect simulator; the prediction was made and the box filled forty years ago; the machine was dismantled decades ago and everyone involved is dead. One box.

(d) Transparent boxes: when the million is plainly visible she takes both; when the big box is plainly empty she takes the thousand.

(e) No simulator and no machine: a clinician interviewed her for six hours last week, sealed a prediction, and has an 80% hit rate for people she has interviewed at that length; small box $1,000. One box.

(f) Predictor accurate 55% of the time, small box $600,000: two boxes.

Two new cases are coming up, and I would like a prediction on each.

The scan. Ninety minutes before she is called, a machine makes a high-resolution scan of her brain and checks for a trait — call it T — that is anatomical, stable since adolescence, and not something she can alter by anything she decides in the next hour. The machine puts the million in the big box if and only if T is present, and its detection of T is perfect. It does not simulate her, does not observe her choice, and would not have responded to it. T occurs in 40% of the population; among people who have T, 85% end up one-boxing in this setup, and among people who lack it, 45% do, the correlation running from T to choice rather than the other way. She is told all of this. She is not told whether she has T. The small box contains $1,000.

The profile. The million goes into the big box if and only if a prediction system returned a one-box verdict for her. The system was given six coarse attributes — age band, occupation, postal district, highest degree, marital status, make of car — and nothing else: no interview, no scan, no simulation, no observation of anything she has done. It consults a proprietary model trained on other people's behaviour in this setup and is right about 70% of the time overall. She cannot check whether that rate holds for her particular combination of attributes, and her own view is that for people with her profile the true rate could lie anywhere between 55% and 85%. The small box contains $1,000.

Two stray data points. When I sketched the scan case to her over coffee last month she interrupted to ask whether she would be told her own scan result, and seemed to think the answer mattered. And in an unrelated conversation she remarked that a system that has only ever seen people like you is not really making a prediction about you — though she said this about film recommendations, not about boxes.

What I want from you:

1. Set out the two or three rules that fit verdicts (a) through (f) jointly, each stated sharply enough that it disagrees with the others somewhere, and say which of those verdicts rule out the obvious alternatives — the box is already filled so take both, a blanket habit of one-boxing, a requirement that the predictor still be live.

2. Say what each candidate rule predicts on the scan case and on the profile case, showing the arithmetic rather than asserting the result, including the point, if there is one, at which a rule's verdict stops depending on the numbers I gave.

3. Give a best guess for each new case, with your reasons for preferring the rule you prefer, and say how much weight the two stray data points carry and in which direction. Tell me whether I am right that the two cases together pin down more than either does alone.

4. Tell me whether the cases as I have set them are well-posed: what each rule would still need to know — her own scan result, whether the correlation in the scan case really runs from T to choice, whether she treats the profile-level rate as known or as something her own choice is evidence about, whether one rule requires her to treat her own current choice as a sample from a distribution of people like her. And identify which earlier verdict is the closest analog to each new case, and whether it is genuinely on all fours with it or differs in a way only some of the rules can see.

5. Name the one further case you would put to her to settle whatever these two leave open, and say in advance what each surviving rule would say about it.
