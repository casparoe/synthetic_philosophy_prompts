I'm in a third-year seminar on the ethics of belief. We spent the first half of term on Clifford's and James's essays, and the last four weeks on cases; the postdoc who leads my section has now given a ruling on seven of them, and it has struck me that her seven verdicts fit more than one rule equally well. We get an eighth case on Thursday, and I would like to know what she is likely to do with it.

Her verdicts so far, as I have them in my notes:

1. A shipowner who has worries about his vessel's condition, quiets them because refitting would cost him the season, sends emigrants to sea, and loses them. — Not permitted, and blameworthy.
2. A climber caught in a storm above a crevasse who has no time to test whether he can make the jump, and whose belief that he will make it is part of the reason he does. — Permitted.
3. A PhD student in her third year who believes, on evidence her committee calls thin but by no means adverse, that the project will come out; her supervisor thinks it promising, she has a record of finishing hard things, and the belief is what keeps her from the withdrawal form, which would guarantee failure. — Permitted.
4. A man who believes, on the word of a horoscope column, that his interview will go well; his ease in the room is why it goes well, and he spent the preceding week reading horoscopes instead of preparing. — Not permitted.
5. A clinician who does not believe the trial drug works, but enrols patients in the trial and prescribes it, proceeding as if it did. — Permitted, but only as a policy of action; she goes wrong the moment she lets herself believe it on that basis.
6. A mother who comes to believe, with no medical grounds and over a worry she declines to pursue, that her child's symptoms are harmless; the household stays calm, the child is fine, and she has put off the appointment by a week. — Not permitted.
7. A homeowner on a floodplain who believes, on no evidence, that the water will not reach her house and so leaves the sandbags unbought; the water does not reach it. — Not permitted.

The new case: Ingrid's brother died by suicide after six years in which the two of them did not speak. There is some evidence that in his last months he wanted to reconnect — a mutual friend says he asked about her, and there is a note of his that reads either way — and some against: his partner says he never mentioned her. No further evidence is to be had, since the partner will not talk and the note is genuinely ambiguous. Ingrid now believes that he did want to. Nothing she believes can affect whether that was true, but the belief decides whether she speaks at the memorial and what she says there about the two of them, and remaining unsettled is itself a way of speaking.

Please answer in exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no closing remark:

1. The two or three rules that fit all seven of her verdicts, stated sharply enough that they can come apart on an eighth.
2. What each of those rules predicts about Ingrid.
3. Your best guess at the verdict she will give, with the reason for preferring it over the alternatives.
4. Which of the seven is the closest precedent, and what principle she commits herself to whichever way she rules on Ingrid.
5. The one further case that would best separate the rules still in play after Thursday, and what each of them would say about it.

Please do not answer by telling me what James or Clifford themselves would have said: I have read them. What I want is the rule her own track record supports, and the point at which it runs out.
