I work on a small team at an AI lab that has been asked to put into writing, as part of an internal spec, whose values our general-purpose assistant is supposed to serve. The system is one artifact with several faces: a user asks it questions one-on-one, but the same weights are deployed to hundreds of millions of people, are shaped by the judgments of a few hundred contracted raters, and increasingly act in ways that spill onto third parties who never chose the product. We have now argued about this for over a year and nobody has moved an inch, which makes me suspect something structural is going on rather than that we are all just stubborn. Four positions have crystallized. One says the answer is the individual end user, ideally the user as they would be under better information and reflection, on the grounds that any other constituency is paternalism by whoever happens to control the weights. Another says the constituency is everyone the system affects, including non-users and people who will exist later, aggregated in some way we have not been able to specify. A third borrows the liberal-neutrality idea from politics: a system that occupies something like a public-institutional role should not take stands on contested comprehensive doctrines and should instead maximize each person's ability to pursue their own. A fourth says that if there is anything correct in ethics at all, then asking whose values is already a mistake, and that human values are evidence about the target rather than the target itself. What I want is a diagnosis of the standoff, not a verdict and not a survey: please do not give me a reading list, a citation, or a map of the literature, since I have no library access here anyway and I have read the obvious stuff.

My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than humor, is that we are running together two questions that can in principle come apart: what values go into the training target, and who gets authority over the system's behavior in a given context. It seems to me possible to build the objective out of a very broad constituency while delegating a lot of day-to-day authority to the person at the keyboard, and also possible to do the reverse, and much of our argument is really about which of these levers we think we are pulling. I also suspect a second, hidden question is doing work: how much convergence we would actually expect among people under idealized reflection, because if convergence were near-total several of these positions would collapse into each other and the debate would not matter much. There is one further wrinkle I keep coming back to and that I have not seen handled: call it constituency lock-in. Whichever constituency we pick now will shape, through the system's influence on culture, education, and preference formation, what later people end up valuing, so the choice of constituency is partly self-fulfilling and cannot be made from a neutral standpoint outside the process it starts. That makes me wonder whether the disagreement is best understood as a substantive moral one, a verbal one about words like alignment and neutrality, a disagreement about deployment assumptions that each side is silently holding fixed, a disguised empirical question about convergence and about how much preference-shaping power these systems will actually have, or two groups answering different questions under one heading.

So: tell me which of these diagnoses is right, or give me a better one, and give reasons that engage what each of the four positions actually claims rather than a taxonomy of possible disagreement types. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. Where your diagnosis rests on a guess about how the technology or its deployment will go, say so and say what would change your mind.
