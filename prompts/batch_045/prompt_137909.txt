Two friends of mine spent most of a barbecue on Saturday arguing about something I'd never really thought about, and I've been turning it over since, so I want a second opinion.

Background: one of them, Nadia, spent last year working in a mid-sized city abroad (I'm probably mangling the name, so I'll just call it V.). She described three local customs I hadn't heard of anywhere else:

- No tipping in restaurants, and not in the mild "it isn't expected" way: leaving money on the table is read as a comment on the server, a leftover from when the people serving were household servants, and older staff take it as a deliberate insult. But at the late-night soup stalls there is a jar, and the custom is that you drop in one small coin, you don't look at the jar, nobody acknowledges it, and you never put in more than one coin — putting in a lot is showing off.
- No lines. At the bakery or the tram stop people stand in a loose cluster and the order sorts itself out: whoever is least in a hurry goes last, and you're supposed to say so out loud (something like "I'm fine, go ahead"). And whoever has clearly been waiting longest is still expected to offer their place once, to an older person, before taking it.
- Crossing mid-block is completely normal, but at crossings with a signal, pedestrians don't step off when the little green walking figure lights up, because that's exactly when the turning traffic gets its arrow. Locals watch the traffic and ignore the figure. Nadia said a visiting cousin of hers waited politely for the figure, stepped out, and a stranger grabbed him by the collar and pulled him back in front of a turning bus.

She also told a story about a tourist at a soup stall who put a large note in the jar and said something like "keep the change," after which the queue went quiet and the women behind the counter slid the note back across without a word and went on serving.

Then the argument. Nadia's side: a visitor morally ought to follow local conventions like these. Her reasons went roughly like this:

(1) You're a guest. Knowingly causing friction, awkwardness or offense in a place that's hosting you is a genuine wrong, not just bad manners, and it isn't cancelled out by the fact that the rule looks arbitrary from the outside.

(2) A lot of these aren't arbitrary anyway. The crossing one is a safety adaptation to a badly designed signal; the bakery one is a rule about who bears the cost of waiting, and who waits is not a matter of indifference. Even the soup-stall coin has content: the point is not to treat the person serving you as someone you throw money at.

(3) The general duties you'd try to replace them with are empty until the local code fills them in. "Don't be contemptuous" tells you nothing until you know what counts as contempt there, and if you skip the coin because the general principle permits it, you still end up doing the contemptuous thing by local standards.

(4) There's also a downstream-harm story: visitors overtipping at the stalls is part of why prices there have gone up, and if you insist on strict first-come-first-served at the bakery, the extra waiting and the awkwardness land on the locals standing there, not on you.

Wes, who has barely travelled, took the other side:

(5) The whole point of a convention is that either way would be fine — think of driving on the left versus the right. That's a coordination problem, and the only reason to comply is that everyone else is complying. That makes compliance prudential, not moral. Calling it a moral obligation is just dressing up etiquette in moral language because it flatters the person doing it.

(6) If some of these really do have moral content, then they aren't conventions at all — they're ordinary moral duties with local input: don't be careless with people's safety, don't treat workers as inferiors. The conventional residue (which coin, which side, which order) is the arbitrary part. So Nadia's principle collapses into "don't be careless or contemptuous," which everyone already accepts and which doesn't obviously tell you to follow the custom: it might just as well tell you to skip the jar.

(7) The downstream-harm argument proves too much. Whether frustrated expectations count depends on whether the expectation was reasonable, and if it wasn't, you don't fix anything by going along with it. And the same argument would tell a woman in a place with a sexist custom to comply, since non-compliance causes friction and awkwardness for everyone around her. If your principle can't tell the difference between the soup-stall coin and that, it isn't a principle about conventions — it's a principle about not making waves.

(8) Then the bit I keep comming back to. Nadia mentioned, almost as an aside, that in V. it's also slightly off-putting when a newcomer performs the local stuff perfectly within a week — the bakery routine, the coin, the whole thing — because it reads as mimicry, like they're taking the piss. Wes jumped on this: if the code itself says you're allowed to be a beginner, and that doing it too well is worse than doing it badly, then "follow the local convention" can't be a determinate duty at all, because the code tells you your compliance is optional and your fluency is suspect. So the most that survives, he said, is: pay attention, defer, apologise easily, don't be the person who makes a scene — and none of that is "follow the convention."

I couldn't referee it, and I'd rather not say which of them I lean toward because I keep flip-flopping and don't want to bias you. What I want is for you to go through it move by move, (1) through (8), and tell me what's actually right and what's actually wrong in each — specific verdicts on each move, including on the ones where I suspect both of them are half right, rather than a survey of what could be said on either side. Then give me a definite overall verdict: who is more right and why, and whether there's something the argument as a whole is missing. If you think one of the moves quietly depends on a distinction that's doing all the real work, say which move and spell the distinction out.
