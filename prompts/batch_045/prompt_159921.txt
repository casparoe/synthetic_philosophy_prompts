I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the mid-2000s and I've accumulated a cluster of views about population ethics and about the structure of the betterness relation that I have never actually stress-tested as a set. I want an audit, not a tour of the literature. Here is the profile as I would write it up:

(1) Transitivity. All-things-considered "better than," over outcomes, is transitive. My reason is practical as much as anything: I want it to be a relation an agent can aim by. A cyclic ranking seems to leave "which outcome should I try to bring about" without an answer, and it invites the money-pump style argument that gets deployed around here against anyone who admits intransitive preferences.

(2) Localism. Whether a given change is an improvement depends only on what happens in that change — who gains how much, who loses how much, from what baseline — and arbitrarily small changes along a continuous dimension produce arbitrarily small changes in value. I arrived at this by way of spectrum arguments in general: I don't think there are sharp moral cutoffs sitting at unknown locations on a smooth continuum, and I'm not an epistemicist who thinks the cutoff is real but forever hidden from us.

(3) Same-people priority. Holding fixed who exists: benefiting a badly-off person at small cost to a very well-off person is an improvement, and I'm happy to weight gains to the worse off somewhat more heavily. Any Pareto improvement is an improvement. This is meant as a local principle about individual transfers, not as a global ranking of populations.

(4) Adding people. Adding a life that is worth living but only barely — welfare just above neutral — changes nothing either way. Adding a life below neutral makes things worse. I hold the usual asymmetry about creating people, and I feel no pull toward the claim that we ought to be adding happy lives.

(5) The endpoint verdict. I reject the repugnant conclusion. Let A be a modest number of people at a very high welfare level, and let Z be a vastly larger population in which everyone sits just barely above neutral. I want to say A is better than Z, and to say it on roughly person-affecting grounds: the people in A end up far worse off in Z, and the extra people in Z sit at a level where their existence isn't a benefit to them.

(6) Incommensurability. Some of the things in play here — length of a thriving life, avoidance of a particular kind of suffering, number of people who get to exist — have no single exchange rate. There is no fact of the matter how many mild headaches equal a year of flourishing. I still want to go on doing expected-value style reasoning over interventions, and I've never been clear whether (6) collides with (1) or with something else in my toolkit.

(7) A ladder I built and can't find the flaw in. Start at A. Step type one: add one person at welfare just above neutral — by (4), indifference. Step type two: move a little welfare from one of the very well-off people to one of the barely-above-neutral people, in a way that (3) says is an improvement. Repeat: add, level down a notch, add, level down a notch, choosing each transfer so that (3) endorses it locally. Keep going until the population is enormous and everyone sits at a common level just above neutral, i.e. until you have arrived at Z. Every step is indifference or local improvement, so by (1) the endpoint is better than the start: Z is better than A. But (5) says A is better than Z, and as far as I can tell (4) does nothing to block this, since the type-one steps claim only indifference.

I don't know which commitment I would drop and I'd rather you told me than asked me.
