hi — I need to get a one-year dissertation fellowship application out in about a week and the two-page proposal is the part I keep rewriting. Area: Humeanism vs anti-Humeanism about laws of nature. I have a half-formed idea and I need you to beat it into something fundable.

Here's the thought. The way this debate actually gets conducted, both sides have quietly agreed on a scoreboard: a candidate account of lawhood is tested by whether it gets the counterfactuals right. The Humean says the best system over the whole mosaic delivers the right counterfactuals cheaply, with no extra metaphysical furniture; the anti-Humean says sure, but your laws don't *make* those counterfactuals true, they don't govern, they just ride along. And then everyone argues about grounding forever. My suspicion is that this is an artifact of the dialectic — counterfactuals are the only thing both camps can agree to score, so the fight drifted there — and that if you change the scoreboard the stalemate looks different.

So: split the job a law is supposed to do into three. (1) Underwriting counterfactuals. (2) Licensing inductive policy for agents who are *inside* the world and don't get to see the mosaic from outside. (3) Explaining why the mosaic is like this rather than otherwise. Then ask, for each family of account, what happens when these three come apart — and my claim is that they come apart a lot more than anyone discusses.

To make it tractable I want a test case, something I'm calling a policy-feedback world: a mosaic in which some strong regularity holds exactly as long as no inhabitant predicts it, and degrades once the inhabitants start relying on it. (Don't worry about whether that's physically possible, treat it as a model.) My hunch, which I want you to pressure-test: the Humean can describe this world fine — the best system of the total mosaic is still well defined — but the resulting laws are useless *to* the inhabitants in a way that ordinary Humeanism has no vocabulary for, while the anti-Humean has nothing to say either, because there's no stable thing for the powers or necessities to govern. If that's right, the real disagreement may not be about grounding at all but about whose standpoint laws are laws *for*, i.e. whether "law" is a notion with an essential audience.

Second thread, more formal, which I'm not sure I can pull off: define something like counterfactual slack for a candidate law — the class of mosaics that differ from the actual one but under which (a) the law still comes out a law and (b) an optimal predictor embedded in the world would behave identically. Guess: for Humean accounts the slack is enormous and the standard response is that this is fine, the mosaic is what it is; for anti-Humean accounts the slack is supposed to be zero, but only because the account helps itself to an answer to why *these* necessities rather than others. If that's right, the standard pump cases in the literature — super-simple worlds, ties between equally simple systems, worlds with exactly one exception — are all downstream of slack, and there's a cleaner way to state what they're supposed to show.
