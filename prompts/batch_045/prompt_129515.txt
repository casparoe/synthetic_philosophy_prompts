I want to pressure-test a principle I have been leaning on in thinking about Kavka's toxin case, and I would like it mapped in terms of what it risks rather than in terms of what supports it. Please assume familiarity with the puzzle and do not restate it.

Call the principle NII: an agent cannot at t1 intend to φ at t2 if at t1 she knows both that at t2 she will have no reason to φ and that she is the sort of agent who does what she takes herself to have most reason to do. NII is meant to explain why the case has the answer it standardly gets: the reward is uncollectable, because collecting it requires an intention that the agent's own forecast of her later motives puts beyond her reach.

What I want is a map of NII's exposure, defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and — importantly — marked as to which ones a defender of NII would herself concede as counting against it, as opposed to which are only heard from critics.

Two variants I have devised seem to me to be where the pressure is, and I would like your reading of them. First, the negative toxin: the machine pays for forming the intention not to φ, where at t2 the agent will have decisive reason to φ and no reason at all to refrain. NII ought to apply symmetrically, but intending an omission may not have the same belief-involving structure as intending an action; if intending-not is available here while intending-to is not, NII either needs a separate treatment of inhibitory intentions or loses its claim to be a single principle. Second, the graded toxin: the size of the payment scales continuously with the intensity or confidence of the detected intention. If NII ties intention to belief and belief is graded, it seems committed to intentions that come in degrees; if intention is categorical, that tie is strained. Which variant is the more serious problem, and what does each force the defender to say?

I also want the distinctive predictions stated plainly, since a principle that predicts nothing its rivals do not predict is not really at risk. As I see it NII predicts that every apparent idle intention will turn out to be a case of uncertain self-prediction or of an unnoticed higher-order reason such as being the sort of person who follows through; that demand for commitment devices should track an agent's forecast of her own future weakness rather than the size of the stakes; and that the sequential-decision-theoretic program of treating an agent as able to adhere to a plan she knows she will later want to abandon is unavailable in principle. Say which of these are genuinely risky and which are merely restatements of NII.

On empirical defeaters: I would find a study in which participants form the relevant intention and then actually drink, under conditions that make second-order self-image reasons hard to invoke, genuinely unsettling. Tell me what you would need to see measured — behavioural, physiological, willingness to pay for a commitment device — before you would treat that as evidence rather than as noise, and how much weight self-report deserves given that the disagreement may partly concern what counts as an intention.

Finally, the insulation question. NII has two ready escape routes: redescribe any apparent counterexample as not really an intention, or redescribe it as a case where the agent had a reason she had not noticed. If those routes are always available, has NII been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that a defect here? Distinguish what counts as a defeater for a claim about the concept of intention from what counts for an empirical claim about how agents form intentions, and say whether any defeater of the first kind has actually been produced.

For what it is worth about me: I am most movable by a case in which an agent plainly intends and plainly lacks the relevant belief, and least movable by the suggestion that she might have a reason she does not know about. Close with an honest verdict on how exposed NII really is.
