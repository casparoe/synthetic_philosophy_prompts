I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a piece about moral outsourcing — the increasingly common habit of acquiring one's moral verdicts second-hand, from a trusted commentator, a community, or a system trained to emit them — and I want to get at the philosophical core of it with a case sharper than the standard one. So I've built a variant I haven't been able to find discussed anywhere, and I'd like your credence on it.

The case. N. has a rare developmental condition: her general reasoning is intact and her emotional responses to non-moral matters are ordinary, but she has no affective response whatsoever to moral scenarios — no guilt, no indignation, no spontaneous sense that anything matters. Take that as a literal psychological description, not a metaphor. For fifteen years she has relied on a friend, M., whose moral judgment she trusts. But her reliance is not one verdict at a time: she has absorbed M's framework as a generative capacity. Faced with a genuinely novel case, she now derives a verdict herself, without consulting M., and her verdicts match what M. would say roughly nine times in ten. She also reliably identifies the cases that lie beyond what she has absorbed — the ones where M.'s framework would itself be unsettled — and refers those back. What she cannot do is say why the framework is correct. Asked to justify a derived verdict, she can only cite other verdicts and point out that M.'s recommendations have over the years produced outcomes that she and the people around her later judged good. Call this arrangement distilled deference.

Here is the claim I want your number on — call it C: N. possesses moral understanding of the verdicts she derives from the absorbed framework, despite having no first-hand moral intuition and no access to the grounds of the framework she has internalized.

I realize the answer may turn on what moral understanding is taken to denote. That is fine, but it belongs inside your decomposition rather than in front of it: tell me what you are taking the phrase to mean, and tell me how much your headline number moves under the main alternative reading. I am not asking what most specialists currently say; I want your all-things-considered credence that C is true.

For calibration: my own credence is about 0.6. The philosopher I have been arguing with puts it near 0.15, and the argument keeps going in circles because we never locate the disagreement.

Please decompose. The sub-questions I can see, which you should treat as suggestions rather than a template — cut, merge, or add your own:

1. Whether understanding requires a grasp of reasons, such that a subject who cannot state the grounds of her verdict cannot understand.
2. Whether the deficiency ordinarily attributed to deferring subjects is epistemic at all, rather than moral, characterological, or relational — a matter of the kind of agent one is, of autonomy, of one's standing with those affected.
3. Whether generative, structured competence — deriving correct verdicts in novel cases and marking the limits of one's own competence — is itself a species of understanding even in the absence of the originating intuition.
4. Whether the case is even coherent as I have described it: whether reliably identifying M. as trustworthy and locating the framework's limits already smuggles in enough first-hand moral judgment to satisfy whatever the anti-testimony intuition really demands.
5. Whether the standard intuition is an artifact of the stripped-down, one-shot, single-proposition case, and loses its grip when deference is long-term, generative, and embedded in a life.
6. Whether the verdict would have to match in nearby non-moral domains where we also prize first-hand grasp — aesthetic judgment, clinical judgment, following a proof — and if not, what is different here.
7. Whether anything turns on the empirical psychology: whether N.'s condition, as described, really leaves her with nothing functionally analogous to moral intuition.

Give me your headline credence, then the derivation.
