For most of my working life I was a structural chemist — protein crystallography, mostly — and since retiring I have been attending a small fortnightly reading group at the local library, which our convener, a retired historian, runs rather more as a lecture than a discussion. For next week he circulated a two-page handout summarising Nietzsche's 1874 essay on history (the second of the *Untimely Meditations*), on the grounds that we would not manage the original. I have copied its summary out below more or less as written:

"Nietzsche's target is the academic historiography of his own century, and his point is that history is not a neutral science but something a living culture uses — and that too much of it, of the wrong kind, makes the culture sick and unable to act. He distinguishes three ways of doing history. Monumental history looks for great moments and great individuals in the past and offers them to the present as examples to imitate; it belongs to the man of action, and it degenerates into myth-making, in which a later age concludes that greatness is no longer possible. Antiquarian history belongs to the reverent conservative and the collector: it preserves the past out of piety, because a people needs to know where it comes from; but it cannot tell the significant from the trivial, reveres everything indiscriminately, and so knows how to preserve life and never how to engender it. Critical history — the mode Nietzsche himself recommends — hauls the past before the tribunal of reason and convicts it, using the standards of modern enquiry to expose the errors and crimes of earlier ages; its danger is that we are ourselves products of what we condemn, and so inherit the vices we judge. Alongside all three, a healthy culture needs what he calls plastic power: the strength to shut out whatever in the past has nothing to say to it, and to keep its own horizon closed. It also needs the unhistorical — the capacity to forget, or simply not to know, those periods which research has not yet reached. Finally, Nietzsche calls suprahistorical the demand that history should become a dispassionate science, a standpoint that no human being could actually occupy. The moral is one of proportion: every culture needs all three kinds of history, and an excess of any one of them incapacitates it."

Something in it strikes me as too tidy — in my own field a threefold scheme that neat was almost always imposed by the lecturer rather than found in the data — and two claims in particular made me suspicious as I read: the identification of the "suprahistorical" with the demand for dispassionate science, which is the opposite of what I had gathered Nietzsche thought of that demand, and the description of critical history as the mode he himself recommends, which sounds rather more like our convener commending his own professional standards than like anything Nietzsche would endorse. But I have read only the opening sections of the essay and cannot locate the faults myself, and I would rather not make a fool of myself in front of the group by asserting a correction I cannot defend.

I would be grateful, then, for an accuracy audit of the handout, taken claim by claim in the order given: for each, tell me whether Nietzsche would accept it, whether it is a fair simplification of something he does say, or whether it goes wrong — and where it goes wrong, say briefly what he actually distinguishes or claims in its place. I am especially interested in whether "plastic power", "the unhistorical", and "the suprahistorical" are being used in anything like his sense, and in whether the closing recommendation — that a healthy culture needs all three kinds of history in the right proportion — is something the essay supports or something the handout imports into it.
