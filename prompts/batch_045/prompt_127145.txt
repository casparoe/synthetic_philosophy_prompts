I'm a staff engineer at a mid-sized software company. I have no philosophy background, but I'm comfortable with algebra, and I'd rather have the real argument than a simplified version of it. I'd be grateful for your help with a disagreement at work, and for a recommendation about what I should actually do.

The situation. My team and a colleague's team are being merged into a single platform team. Leadership has given the merged team twenty engineer-months of capacity next quarter and told us to agree between ourselves how to divide it between the two product areas we currently serve. If we can't agree, nothing changes: we stay separate, and in that case we're worth roughly the same to the company — about 10 units of impact each, by the metric our organisation uses.

What we disagree about is leverage. My product has about five times her user base, so an engineer-month spent on my area produces about five times the measured impact of one spent on hers. That makes my ceiling — all twenty months to me — about 100 impact units, and hers about 20; our fallbacks, though, are identical.

I proposed that each of us should get the same fraction of our own best case. Working it through, that gives me about 7.1 months and her about 12.9: my impact 35.7, hers 12.9, and each of us receiving about 29% of the gain we'd have received in our best case. She counters that we should instead divide the capacity so as to make the product of our gains over the fallback as large as possible; that gives me 6 months and her 14, i.e. 30 and 14.

My rule happens to favour me, which is exactly why I distrust it, and I can't locate the problem with it. It sounds even-handed — the same proportion of your own best case — yet it hands me nearly nine times her gain, and it does so because of a number describing a state of affairs neither of us expects ever to obtain. It also produces more total impact, about 48.6 against 44, which is a point in its favour, though I'm not sure total impact is what fairness asks about.

Two further things nag at me. First, this isn't a one-off: we re-organise every six months, other teams watch what we settle on, and whatever rule we pick will in effect become the house rule. I worry about a ratchet that keeps funnelling capacity to whoever already has the largest user base, and about the incentive it creates for everyone to overstate their own ceiling. Second, my ceiling of 100 ignores an informal remark from leadership that whoever runs the merged platform might be put up for a director-level role; that would be worth far more than 100 to me, but the odds are maybe one in ten, and it feels wrong that a long shot like that should move the split at all.

I'd rather be fair than win. Please tell me which rule, if either, we should adopt, and what the argument for it really is — in plain terms, but not watered down. I'm not after citations or further reading, only the reasoning. Tell me how confident you are, and tell me honestly what would change your mind or where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong. Please answer in British English.
