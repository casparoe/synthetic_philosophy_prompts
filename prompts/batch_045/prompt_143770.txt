At my local EA-adjacent Sunday meetup — same six people, same argument about wild animal suffering every single time — one guy keeps defending strong longtermism with an analogy I can't shake. Humanity right now, he says, is like a toddler: it has nearly all of its life ahead of it, so small interventions early on (what it eats, whether anyone reads to it) compound over eighty years, while the same money spent on its retired grandmother's comfort does less good, because she has less future left for it to shape. The mapping he gives is: we're the toddler, the trillion-year future is the eighty years, work on AI alignment and pandemic preparedness and whatever else is the education, and bednets or cash transfers are the grandmothers armchair. He's using it to license the strong version of the view, not just "the future matters somewhat" but "near-termist causes are a rounding error by comparison, and it's fine to make real tradeoffs against people alive right now in order to nudge the trajectory."

I think the analogy is doing a lot of quiet work but I can't tell wich parts are load bearing. Can you audit it? Give me the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the toddler, the parent, the education, the eighty years — and above all who the parent is supposed to be, since on his own telling we seem to be simultaneously the toddler and the person deciding what the toddler eats, which feels like it's hiding something. Then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually matter for his conclusion and the ones that are just noise: a toddler has a fairly determinate adult endpoint and a caretaker with some idea of what growing up well looks like, whereas the whole longtermist case rests on our not knowing whether the grown-up future is worth having (the "maybe it goes badly / maybe we lock in something awful" worry); a toddler's death is tragic partly because of attachment and a life cut short, which doesn't obviously transfer to a population; education has an evidence base and roughly predictable returns, while "trajectory change" mostly doesn't; and the picture seems to smuggle in a whiggish story where we mature out of our present moral confusion, which is precisely what his critics deny.

I've read the sequences, so please don't just tell me that expected value blows up under tiny probabilities or that Pascal's mugging is a problem — I want to know whether the toddler picture is what's quietly supplying the confidence that the EV calculation is allowed to be this aggressive in the first place. Also: he's started using a variant, that we're not a toddler but a toddler alone in a room with a loaded gun, so the real analogue of education is 'get the gun away first' — does that variant fix the problems, or does it just rebuild risk-minimization from scratch under a new label and quietly drop the "the future is vast and valuable" part that was doing the work? If there's a better analogy for the strong claim, say what it is. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — does the analogy carry the strong conclusion, or only the weak one? — then give me the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question.
