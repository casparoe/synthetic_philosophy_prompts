Chapter three of my dissertation turns on a distinction I am no longer sure I can defend, so I want a number from outside my own head. Consider two hypothetical clinical profiles. In the first — call it *conative asymbolia* — a patient retains the full sensory-discriminative profile of pain and retains its unpleasantness: they locate it, grade its intensity, and report that while it lasts it is awful and they dislike it. What is gone is the motivational response: no urge to escape or end it, no avoidance learning, no anticipatory dread, no objection to having it again tomorrow. In the second — call it *affective asymbolia* — the motivational profile is fully intact while the affect is not: the subject insists the pain is bad and demands that it stop, learns to avoid it, resents it in advance, but reports no unpleasantness whatever.

The claim I need to assess is that what makes a pain bad for its subject is the aversive motivation — the being-moved-to-get-rid-of-it — rather than how the experience feels. On that account the conative-asymbolic patient is not badly off at all, and the affective-asymbolic one is.

My credence is about 0.5; my supervisor's is about 0.15. Give me yours, decomposed. The terms I would include: whether the two components really are dissociable even in principle; whether the badness of pain is one thing or a cluster — sensory, affective, conative, reason-giving — that can come apart; whether the conative view can accommodate pain that is bad although the subject welcomes it, wants it to continue, or has no conception of its ceasing; whether "bad for the subject" should be analyzed in terms of desire, well-being, or reasons; whether the actual clinical asymbolia cases are best read as genuine dissociation or as a change in reporting and response; and how much probability to reserve for a third option, on which badness is intrinsic to the sensation itself or to what the experience represents. Assign a number and one line of justification to each, say how they combine, and put a rough distribution over the competing accounts. Then tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to, what finding would move it most, and which of the two of us at 0.5 and 0.15 is more likely to be the one who has that term wrong.

Lead with your credence in the first sentence, then the decomposition; no preamble and no restatement of the question.
