Hey — I'm rewriting my lecture on Williams's attack on utilitarianism for the big intro ethical theory class (the Williams side of that old Smart-and-Williams volume), and I've got myself turned around on something before I stand infront of 200 undergrads on Tuesday. A grad student in my seminar, who is very sharp but talks to much, insisted that the one thought too many complaint and the integrity objection are really the same argument in two costumes: both, she said, boil down to the claim that impartial consequentialism forces the agent into a deliberation that is awkward, costly, and alienating. I nodded along at the time, but the more I sit with it the less sure I am that they are even the same kind of objection. One looks like a complaint about a particular piece of deliberation — the man who has to choose between his wife and a stranger in the water, and why on earth he would need to run any calculation at all. The other looks like something much larger: that negative responsibility and the standing demand to promote the best outcome can eat the projects that make a life someone's own. But ask me to say in a single sentence what separates them and I fumble it.

Here's where I actually got stuck. A colleague in the decision-theory group, who doesn't work on ethics at all, said the whole fuss has an obvious fix: don't have the extra thought. Build the impartial reasoning into trained second nature — incentivize it, habituate it, make it something you do before you notice you're doing it — and utilitarianism gets the benefits with none of the alienation. I've been calling it the habituation reply in my notes. It feels too cheap, but I can't say why. Connected to that, I invented a variant for section that I think breaks something: take an agent whose deepest ground project is impartial beneficence — say a committed effective altruist who has reorganized her whole life around it, and who, if her brother and a stranger are both in the water, genuinely wants to run the numbers and would feel cheated if you told her not to bother. If Williams's objection is about alienation from your projects, she seems immune, and the objection seems to have grip only on people who happen to care about their own people. That can't be the point, surely — an objection to a theory of right action shouldn't depend on the agent having the wrong hobbies. But I also don't see what's left of it if I grant that.

Third thing, and then I'll let you go. I've been toying with teaching a "sanctuary" amendment: let consequentialism carve out a protected personal sphere where the agent is permitted, maybe even required, not to maximize, and justify the carve-out consequentially, on the grounds that having such a sphere produces more good overall. Does that buy Williams off? My instinct is no, because the sphere is granted by the theory and could in principle be revoked, so the agent's relation to her own projects is still on loan rather than hers outright. But I want to know whether that instinct is right, whether it's the same point as the one thought too many point or a different one, and generally where my seminar student and I are going wrong. Please write your answer in American English — I'm stateside, and last time I got a pile of British spellings I had to clean up — and keep it under about 700 words if you can, because it's going to end up more or less verbatim in my lecture notes.
