I am a novelist, and for the past four years I have taught a single upper-level seminar, cross-listed between philosophy and literature, under the title *Fiction and the Will*. My students are third- and fourth-years, mostly literature majors with one or two philosophy minors, and I assess them with one take-home essay: ninety minutes, 1,200 to 1,500 words, marked out of twenty. I would like you to write this year's question, a model answer, and a marking scheme, on the topic of choice without preference — the case of the ass said to stand between two exactly similar bundles of hay.

Two constraints come first. The question must be self-contained: my students will have no extracts, no library, and no need to know anything about Buridan's own writings or about who first attached the ass to his name, so please do not build the question around any historical attribution or textual detail that would have to be looked up. And the question must not be answerable by reciting the dialectic. Anyone who has read a survey chapter can produce: the animal lacks reason and so cannot suspend deliberation; a rational agent can break the tie; the will may choose against the last judgment of reason; randomness is not agency. I want a question that forces a student to say what *kind* of thing breaks a tie, and why that thing is not merely a cause acting on the agent.

What I want woven in is a difficulty from my own trade, which I have started calling the principle of narrative asymmetry. In prose, two options must be presented one after another, and whichever is named first, or described at greater length, or caught by the character's attention, acquires a salience that the character has no reason to treat as a reason. Narration also runs in one direction, so any deliberation I depict has a before and an after, and a state of exactly equal pull is one the text can only assert in summary and never show. Worse, readers of fiction assume that actions are motivated, so a choice depicted without a reason will be read either as a fault in the characterisation or as concealing a reason the narrator has not declared.

The question I have in mind asks whether this representational difficulty tells us anything about the case itself — whether it shows that a genuine standstill of the will is not a possible state of an agent at all, or only that such a state is unshowable — and what the difference between those two verdicts is, and which of them the philosophical example actually needs. A strong answer should also separate having no reason to prefer either option from having no reason to choose at all (the ass is hungry), and should distinguish picking arbitrarily from being moved at random. If you think my principle rests on a confusion — for instance, on sliding from the order in which options are presented to a reader to the order in which a character considers them — do not quietly repair it; say so in the model answer and make resisting the framing one of the moves the marking scheme rewards.

Please give me: the essay question as it would appear on the paper; a model answer of roughly 800 to 900 words that commits to a position rather than surveying the options; and a marking scheme out of twenty, organised around five or six named moves, with the marks each is worth, a note on how a B-grade answer stalls (the vocabulary right, the crux missed), and a note on what each move is most commonly confused with.
