Hey. I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I've somehow let myself be talked into running a 75-minute session at a small ideas festival next month. Setting: about 18 adults, general public, almost nobody with a philosophy background, a couple of them who've read some pop-philosophy and will be smug about it, and at least two who think philosophy is just opinion with better vocab. Room with tables, a projector I'd rather not use, no budget worth speaking of. I can manage tiny real stakes — a box of good chocolate, a bottle of something, a few pounds each.

The theme I got assigned is the "separateness of persons" complaint against utilitarianism. You know the one: that summing welfare across people treats a collection of distinct lives as if they were one big experiencing subject, and that this is meant to be a deep mistake rather than just a gripe about unfair shares. I want the room to *feel* that. Not hear me explain it. I'm not lecturing, and I definately don't want to say the words utilitarianism, or Rawls, or separateness of persons until the last five minutes — ideally someone in the room half-says it first and I just put a name on what they already said.

So design me the thing. Rules, materials, minute-by-minute timeline, and what the facilitator says versus keeps their mouth shut about.

The bit I actually need to happen is narrow, and this is where I expect designs to fail. I do *not* want an exercise that lands as "be fairer about who pays" or "spread the burden around" or "rotate the cost" — that's the shallow reading and I can feel a general audience sliding there immediately. What I need them to hit is the gap between "the total went up, so it was worth doing" and the standpoint of the single person who has to bear the cost, and to notice that the first claim isn't an answer to the second. If the mechanic only produces distributive-fairness intuitions, it's a dud. Tell me how your rules make that specific collision likely rather than hoping for it.

Some things I want you to deal with:

- I'd like real but genuinely trivial and reversible stakes — holding ice, a bitter cracker, a silly forfeit, whatever — with the group's payoff depending on someone taking it. But give me your honest read on whether that mechanic is a trap. What if several people volunteer at once and it turns into a warm story about self-sacrifice? What if nobody does? Both are live possibilities, I want a plan for each, and I want to know which outcome is actually the better one for my purposes and why.
- A fallback paper-and-pen version, for when the festival organisers tell me I can't make anyone eat anything or hold ice.
- The debrief: map particular moments in what happened onto particular parts of the objection, and be explicit about which moment *has* to occur for the session to have worked, plus what I do in the room if it doesn't occur.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how I handle it without breaking character and turning into a lecturer.
- Where the exercise is structurally incapable of going. My sense is it can make the complaint felt but can't show it's *sound* — that a utilitarian has moves here, something like treating rights as useful fictions, leaning on diminishing marginal returns, or saying the complaint is just a psychological fact about creatures like us. Which features of the objection can the activity not reproduce, and which therefore have to come from me in the last five minutes?

Please: you're a moral philosopher who also designs this kind of session for real rooms full of real skeptical adults. Brainstorm two or three mechanics first, show me the ones you rejected and why, and then commit to one. No menu of options with "depending on your group" hedges — pick one game, one fallback, tell me your reasons, and give me your confidence that it will actually produce the collision I described above. If you think my framing of the complaint is off in a way that matters for the design, say so up front rather than designing around a bad brief. I'm writing the session up for a magazine afterwards, so I need to know where the design is doing the work and where I'm just asserting something.
