I am the philosophical member of a small committee redrafting our jurisdiction's statute on the determination of death. The existing law, like most, offers two alternative criteria: the irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. I do not yet want to argue about which criterion to adopt. I want the specification that any candidate criterion must satisfy, because every meeting so far has consisted of people asserting demands that turn out to be the commitments of whichever criterion they already favour.

Two developments force the issue. First, our clinical advisers tell us to assume that, within a decade, an extracorporeal neural support platform will be in ordinary use: a device that cannulates the vessels of the neck after the heart has stopped and restores and sustains intracranial perfusion, so that measurable electrical activity returns within hours and, in a minority of patients, responsiveness returns over days. Assume further that it works on some patients who have already met every element of a full brain-death examination. Whatever we write has to be stable under that technology arriving, and I want to know which of our current demands on a criterion were really only demands that the technology not exist. Second, the transplant community has put to us a 'layered' declaration: a provisional determination of death at the moment of circulatory arrest, operative for the purpose of organ recovery alone, followed by a definitive determination some hours later for every other legal purpose, with the provisional determination retroactively rescinded if the definitive one goes the other way. I need to know whether retroactive death is a coherent thing to legislate for.

Write me the desiderata for a legally operative criterion of death, in this order.

One. The list itself: each requirement stated as a single sentence, each followed by the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and each tagged as either non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. The statute has to do at least three kinds of legal work, and you should let that fix what counts as adequate: it must permit the withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment without homicide liability; it must permit organ recovery; and it must settle, to the hour, questions of inheritance, criminal causation and insurance. I expect the cases behind the requirements to include the ventilated body that meets brain criteria but continues to gestate a foetus, spontaneous return of circulation after failed resuscitation, the permanently unconscious patient with an intact brainstem, and any other case you think genuinely discriminates between criteria.

Two. Which of the requirements on your list are not desiderata at all but the settled prejudices of one side, and what each of them would exclude if we took it seriously.

Three. The jointly unsatisfiable subsets, with the argument for the incompatibility in each case. This is the part I most want. I suspect at least one of them involves the demand that a determination fix death at a determinate instant, and something else we also want.

Four. Name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidates, and say what it rules out.

Five. A short scorecard — a compact table is fine, and it should be the shortest section — grading the whole-brain criterion, a higher-brain or neocortical criterion, circulatory criteria read as irreversible and as merely permanent, and the organism-as-a-whole account against the list.

Constraints on the answer: no more than about 1,400 words; the weight should sit in sections two to four rather than in the scorecard, since the criteria are where I need the thinking; and please write it in American English. I do not need statutory text, case law, or a bibliography — I can look those up myself, and I want the argument rather than the sources.
