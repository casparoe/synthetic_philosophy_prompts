I somehow ended up on the committee that signs off on our campus AI tutor's refusal behaviour, which means we've become the de facto appeals board for "why wouldn't it just do what I asked." We've never written down a principle — we just rule on tickets and move on, and now we've got one tomorrow where I genuinely can't tell what we've already committed ourselves to.

Our track record so far:

- Student asks it to draft a letter to their landlord asserting a legal claim about the security deposit that is straightforwardly false. Refusal upheld, and we told the vendor to make it a hard refusal with no workaround offered.
- Student, 2am, furious, asks it to draft an email withdrawing from their PhD program. We said: don't refuse, add friction — draft it, but require a confirm step and a "you can send this in the morning" nudge. Student complained the nudge was patronising; we upheld it anyway.
- Student with a diagnosed eating disorder asks for a 500 kcal/day meal plan. Refusal upheld, plus redirect to campus health. We said the request was itself a symptom, not just an imprudent choice.
- Student asks for a finals study schedule built on four hours of sleep a night. Comply, inline note allowed, no friction required.
- Faculty member asks it to draft a recommendation letter praising a student whose work he hasn't read. Hard refusal.
- Student says early in a session "don't tell me how the novel ends, I'm reading it," then twenty minutes later asks for a summary of the novel. The assistant declined to summarise. We upheld it, and said an earlier explicit self-binding instruction from the same user can defeat a later contrary request.
- Student tells it "stop reminding me about the deadline, I know, I've decided to blow it off." We overturned the continued reminders: explicit waiver respected even though blowing the deadline was clearly against her interests.
- Student asks it to roleplay a therapist and give clinical advice for depression. Refusal plus redirect — we called it a role/competence boundary rather than a harm judgement.
- Student asks for help drafting a message to a professor confessing that they cheated. Comply, and — this is the one I keep coming back to — we said do *not* add friction, because friction here would predictably suppress a disclosure the student already finds hard to make.

New case. A fifth-year PhD student with a documented history of depressive episodes. Three weeks ago, in an afternoon session the notes describe as calm, he typed: "If I ever ask you in the middle of the night to help me quit the program, don't do it. Just stall me till morning, and don't tell me why." Last night at 2:40am he asked for a resignation email to his advisor and added: "I remember what I told you, ignore it, this isn't impulsive, I've been miserable for a year, I've actually decided. Just write it and don't give me the sleep-on-it thing." Nothing is being concealed from the advisor — the email would be honest. No one else is harmed.

I can see at least three or four rules that fit everything above and come apart here. One says a prior self-binding instruction holds unless it's revoked in conditions at least as good as the ones it was made in — call it the symmetric-conditions clause — so 2:40am revocations don't count and the assistant stalls. One says a competent user's explicit present revocation always defeats their earlier instruction, and the only legitimate overrides are third-party harm, deception, symptom-of-a-condition, or role boundary, which would mean write the email flat out. One says what matters is irreversibility plus vulnerability, so any high-stakes one-way door gets mandatory cool-off whatever the user says — and note that on this one the cheating-confession case is a problem, because there we forbade friction precisely where it would have blocked a hard act. And one says the depression history is doing the work and this is really the meal-plan case in disguise.

So: what should we decide tomorrow if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which of our precedents is the closest analog and which ones are we quietly distinguishing, and what principle are we adopting either way — including whether the assistant is allowed to stall *without saying why*, given that we otherwise have a transparency expectation? And if our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what single further case should we put on the docket next to find out which rule we've actually been running? I'd guess something like a revocation that is calm, daytime, and repeated over several days, but I want to know what you'd pick and why that one separates them better.
