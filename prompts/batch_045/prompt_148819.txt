we're wrapping up a joint reading group this thursday — half philosophy grad students, half psychiatry residents — and the final session is on Hacking's looping effects and psychiatric classification. I run the thing and I'd rather close with a poem than with a summary of the discussion, so: I want a pantoum, 5 or 6 quatrains, ~20-24 lines, that dramatises the loop instead of describing it.

The mechanism, in case the form isn't one you know cold: the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third lines of the next one, and in the last stanza the poem's opening lines come back, so it ends on the line it started with. That's the whole reason I want this form — lines that migrate should change meaning as they move. A line we first hear as a clinician's criterion should come back later as a patient's own self-description, or as a support-group bit of shorthand, or as a box on an insurance form. The reader should be able to feel the kind and the people chasing each other without anyone ever saying 'looping effect' out loud.

Quick reminder of the idea, since I want this to work even cold: the claim is that when you classify people, the classification gets taken up by the people classified — they experience themselves in that vocabulary, they have new things to do and new ways to be stuck, some of them present differently next time — so the category itself has to be revised, and the revision feeds back again. Hacking contrasts interactive kinds (which answer back) with indifferent ones (which don't care how we sort them, like a mineral or a synapse). I'd like one of the recurring lines to be an indifferent one — something that means exactly the same thing every single time it returns — sitting right there in the middle of all the lines that don't, as a kind of control.

For content, I've been using a made-up example in the group and I'd like you to use it: a fictional, largely forgotten diagnosis, first written up in the early 90s at a memory clinic, whose main complaint was that rooms you've known for years feel very slightly rearranged — patients kept saying the furniture had moved an inch. Give it about a decade: the write-up, the shared vocabulary the patients build for it, the documentary that makes the presentations cleaner and then weirder, the retired category and whatever replaced it. Please don't resolve it into 'diagnoses are just social constructs' (nobody in the room believes that and Hacking doesn't either) or into 'it was a real illness all along' — land it somewhere like 'the name was a place to be, and then it wasn't'; the residents should not feel caricatured and the philosophers should not wince, and somebody who knows the literature should be able to point at the exact lines where the loop turns.
