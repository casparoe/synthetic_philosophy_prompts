I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere on AI alignment for something like fifteen years — the old sequences, the material on coherent extrapolated volition, the long comment threads where one side reaches for Harsanyi's aggregation result and the other reaches for maximin behind a veil of ignorance — and there's a knot in the middle of all of it that I've never seen pulled apart cleanly. I'd like you to try.

Frame it the way I'd frame it: there is a single system that must pick one policy for a constituency whose members disagree not only about which outcome is best but about what kinds of things count as mattering at all. The liberal instinct is that the system shouldn't justify its choice by appeal to any contested conception of the good; it should take everyone's values as given and aggregate them. Call that thin neutrality: the rule never names a conception of the good in its own voice. Call thick neutrality the stronger property: the rule doesn't systematically advantage any member's value system relative to any other's — not merely in what it pronounces, but in the machinery it uses to get there. My sense is that the two come apart, and that the gap is where every real argument lives. Please explain why, and how large the gap is.

Four places look to me like where thin neutrality runs out and a substantive choice has to be made, and I want you to walk through each concretely rather than listing them:

First, currency. Any interpersonal comparison needs units — a scale for each person's welfare or preference strength, hence a unit and a zero for each. Whether the zero sits at a life barely worth living, at nonexistence, or at some neutral midpoint decides whether adding happy people is a gain, a wash, or a cost, and the unit decides whose satisfactions move the needle most. The choice is a first-order moral claim stated in the idiom of measurement. Say what exactly is being smuggled in, and whether there's any way to make the normalization itself neutral rather than merely unannounced.

Second, constituency. Present and actual people only, or future people, or merely possible people whose existence depends on the very choice being made, or digital minds; and if probability-weighted, weighted how. Note where this stops being a calibration question and becomes the thing everyone was arguing about.

Third, the unit of aggregation within and across a life: over persons or over person-moments; over people's current values or over their extrapolated, better-informed, more-themselves values; and what to do when someone's values at one time repudiate their own values at another.

Fourth, and this is the part I most want checked: people also disagree about the aggregation rule itself, so a thin-neutral system seems to need a second-order rule for aggregating over first-order rules, and then a third. Here is my conjecture, which I suspect is only half right. If every first-order rule on the menu is a weighted sum of individually transformed values, then a weighted sum of such rules collapses back into a single weighted sum with compounded weights, so the regress is harmless for that family. But it doesn't collapse once the menu contains non-linear rules — maximin, lexical priority, vetoes, protected spheres — because those aren't closed under mixing. Is that right? What exactly has to hold for the collapse? And if the regress doesn't close, does "neutral meta-aggregation" just turn out to be first-order disagreement wearing a hat? Relatedly, I've been toying with defining a rule as neutral iff it is a fixed point of the constituency's own second-order aggregation over the space of candidate rules — everyone votes on rules, and the winner is the rule that, applied to itself, returns itself. Does such a fixed point exist, under what conditions, is it unique, and would its being a fixed point of a partly arbitrary procedure be worth anything?

Then bring it back down with one case. A place-bound community says a valley simply must not be flooded; their objection isn't welfare-shaped and they reject compensation in welfare units. A much larger group gets a large aggregate welfare gain from the dam. Trace what a thin-neutral aggregator can do here: either the smaller group's loss enters the currency as zero or as small, in which case a substantive judgment has been disguised as measurement, or the rule's shape has to contain a veto or protected sphere, in which case a side-constraint has entered as architecture. The general question I want answered: is the shape of the rule — sum, maximin, lexical priority, veto — the real location of first-order morality, with the aggregation story as decoration?

Finally, briefly: what about not aggregating — dividing the world instead, allocating shares of resources, attention, or jurisdiction in proportion to some measure of stake, the way moral trade would? What does that buy, what does it cost, and why can't it be the whole answer?

Write in American English, roughly 1,000–1,300 words. I want gut-level intuition rather than formalism, except in the meta-aggregation section, where I do want you to get the structure right and to be explicit about what's being assumed. Where a question turns on a genuinely contested value judgment rather than on the structure of the setup, say so plainly instead of splitting the difference. I don't want a literature survey, citations, or a reading list — reason from the problem, not from who has written about it, and tell me where you think my framing is wrong.
