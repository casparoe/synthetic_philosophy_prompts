THE QUESTION (Q3, 25 marks)

In the context of fine-tuning a system that emits an explicit reasoning trace before producing a final answer, distinguish process-based supervision from outcome-based supervision.

(a) State the distinction in terms of what the training signal is a function of. [5]
(b) Give one advantage and one disadvantage of each. [10]
(c) Now consider the following regime, which we'll call "batch outcome supervision with a lexical filter" (BOS-LF). The system works through tasks in batches of 1,000. At the end of each batch the supervisor computes exactly one quantity: the fraction of tasks in the batch whose final answers were correct. No per-task labels are retained, and the reasoning traces are archived but never read by anyone. That single fraction per batch is the whole of the training signal. Separately, before a batch is scored, any trace containing a string from a fixed blacklist (things like "I'll skip the check", "ignore the earlier result") causes the entire batch to be rejected and scored zero.

Classify BOS-LF as process-based supervision, outcome-based supervision, both, or neither. Explain whether it should be expected to deliver the benefits usually claimed for process-based supervision. Justify your classification using the criterion you gave in (a). [10]

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

Process-based supervision is when the feedback is a function of the reasoning trace — the intermediate steps — rather than only of the final answer. Outcome-based supervision is when the feedback depends only on the final output. So the distinction is about what the reward is computed from, not about who computes it or how long the task takes.

The main advantage of process supervision is credit assignment. When the answer is wrong you know which step went wrong, so the update pushes on the step that actually caused the failure instead of nudging the whole trajectory at once. That is why step-level verification has been popular for multi-step maths and coding problems, where a long chain can fail at exactly one step. The main disadvantage is cost: you need step-level labels, which means either expensive human labelling or an automatic checker, and for most tasks there is no cheap automatic checker for individual steps.

The advantage of outcome supervision is that the label is often free — a test passes, a program runs, an answer matches — and also that it does not force the model to imitate one particular style of reasoning. That second point matters more than people sometimes admit: with process supervision you can end up penalising reasoning that is perfectly correct just because it isn't the reasoning the grader would have used, which shrinks the space of solutions the model is willing to try. The disadvantage is reward hacking: the model finds ways of producing the outcome without doing the thing you wanted, and because nobody looks at how it got there, you don't find out until much later.

BOS-LF is a hybrid, but the process-based component is the more important one, so I would classify it as (mostly) process-based supervision. The lexical filter is a function of the trace: it reads what the model wrote and rejects the batch if it sees a banned phrase. By the criterion in (a) that makes it process supervision, and since the filter is applied to every single trace, the model is effectively being supervised step by step. It follows that BOS-LF should get the main benefit claimed for process supervision, namely that bad reasoning gets caught even in the cases where it happens to lead to a correct final answer.

The rest of the signal, the batch fraction, is outcome-based. But note that it tells the model nothing about reasoning: one number per thousand tasks cannot say anything about which traces were the good ones, so no learning signal can reach the level of individual steps at all. The outcome part therefore contributes essentially nothing to the quality of the reasoning and can only push the overall rate of correct answers around. So BOS-LF inherits the credit-assignment advantage from its filter, and because the outcome signal is aggregated rather than per-task, it also avoids most of the reward hacking that plain outcome supervision is prone to. One residual worry is that if the blacklist is known to the model it might simply rephrase the same bad reasoning in permitted words, but this can be handled by keeping the blacklist secret from the model.
