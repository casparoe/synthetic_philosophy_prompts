I'm putting together a prospectus for a one-semester master's thesis in formal epistemology (about 8,000 words, roughly four months of actual writing, with an advisor who works mainly on credence and updating), and I want the proposal organized around a question I keep circling without being able to state cleanly. I came at it from a reading group where we spent a whole session on why Dutch book arguments are supposed to be normative arguments at all rather than just theorems about betting, and I came away convinced that the answer depends on an assumption nobody states.

Here is the half-formed idea. Dutch book arguments are advertised as vindicating probabilism for agents like us, but every version I know assumes that the bets the bookie offers are settleable: that for each state, the payoff is determinate and the loss can actually be collected. That assumption looks harmless only because the propositions in the examples are trivial. Replace them with propositions that are perfectly decidable but expensive to decide — whether a large graph has a Hamiltonian path, whether a given Diophantine equation has a solution below some explicit bound, whether a sentence has a proof shorter than some length — and it can happen that neither the bookie nor the agent can determine what, if anything, is owed. The "sure loss" is then a sure loss only for someone with unbounded computational resources, and it isn't clear why a bounded agent should be moved by that, or why exploitation by a bookie with more compute is a criticism of the agent's coherence rather than a straightforward resource asymmetry. My suspicion is that this does more damage to the pragmatic vindication than the usual logical-omniscience caveat does, because here the problem isn't that the agent has false beliefs about logic but that the settlement condition on the bets fails.

What I want from you is a proposal, not an essay on the topic. State one precise question with a determinate answer — I'm currently tempted by something like: does the Dutch book defense of probabilism survive when agent and bookie are placed under the same uniform computational bound, and if it does not, is the correct replacement norm still recognizably probabilistic, or does it collapse into something merely permissive? Say why the obvious replies don't already settle it: idealizing the agent, restricting the bet space to a decidable-in-practice fragment, switching to accuracy or scoring-rule vindications instead of pragmatic ones, or treating the whole argument as a result about an idealized surrogate. I want the proposal to be clear about which of these it is arguing against and why the question isn't just a restatement of the omniscience problem everyone grants. Give the method in concrete terms: what the formalism would have to look like, what the toy model of a bounded agent and a bounded bookie is, which cases get worked through in which order, and a month-by-month schedule over four months. Then say what a positive result, a negative result, and a genuinely failed project would each look like — I would count it as failure if the project collapsed into the observation that bounded agents cannot be fully coherent, since that is where it started. Finally, be honest about scope: I'm inclined to include a diachromic part on whether conditionalization has a bounded analogue, but I suspect that should be cut, and I'd rather the proposal argue for cutting it than hedge.

Two more constraints. If the distinction between books that guarantee a sure loss and books that merely guarantee an arbitrarily large loss, or between books that exist and books the bookie can construct, does real work in your framing, use it; if not, don't mention it — I don't want the proposal gesturing at refinances it doesn't need. And don't give me citations, a bibliography, footnotes, or a "literature" section: I'll fill those in myself, and I'd rather not be pointed at sources I'd then have to track down.

Format: exactly five numbered sections, no introduction and no conclusion, each section at most about 80 words.

1. The question, stated so that it has a determinate answer.
2. The specific gap in the existing positions — the one the project exploits, not a survey.
3. The method: formalism, toy model, the ordered cases, and the monthly schedule.
4. The possible outcomes, including the one that would make the project a failure.
5. Scope: what is in, what is cut, and what would have to be true for me not to cut it.
