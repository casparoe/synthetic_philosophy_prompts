I'm a PhD student in computer science working on reward modelling for RLHF, and Goodhart's law has started doing an enormous amount of unexamined work in my surroundings. Two episodes prompted this. In a lab meeting last month, a senior student killed a proposal to train a learned reward model as a proxy for helpfulness by saying, flatly, that the moment you optimize against a proxy it stops tracking the thing you care about, so the only honest options are direct human oversight or nothing. A week later, in a reading group on accountability in public administration that I sit in with the policy school, a visitor used what sounded like the same argument against hospital waiting-time targets and against test-based accountability in schools: set a number, and the number is destroyed. I notice that the wording in circulation is usually credited to Marilyn Strathern, quoting Keith Hoskin, as: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"; whereas what I'm told is Goodhart's own 1975 statement is: "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." Those strike me as claims of quite different shapes — one about a measure's fidelity, one about a regularity's stability under deliberate control — and I don't want to spend the answer on the genealogy (I know there are neighbouring formulations in the social sciences and in economics and I don't need them rehearsed or cited). What I want is the semantics.

So: give me a menu of the distinct precise theses that "Goodhart's law" could be asserting, ordered from the near-trivial to the radical, and for each one specify (a) the mechanism it posits, (b) its quantifier strength — does it say this of every measure, every proxy, every measure under sufficient optimization pressure, or only some — (c) whether the degradation claimed is total collapse, decay past some threshold, or mere shrinkage, and (d) whether the claim is meant as an empirical generalization, something like a consequence of selection theory or decision theory or game theory, or a conceptual truth about what a measure is. Then give each reading a verdict: platitude, substantive but contingent, substantive and well supported, false, or not clearly coherent. I want the menu to be discriminated against cases rather than asserted, and here are the cases I care about, because they seem to pull apart readings that people conflate. First, specification gaming in RL: a policy drives its reward-model score up while human preference ratings fall, and there is no human agent gaming anything — just optimization pressure from a gradient. Whatever explains that cannot essentially involve strategizing, and I'd like to know which readings predict it and which don't. Second, the hospital case, where reported waits fall and no one can agree whether care improved: is the mechanism misreporting, reallocation of effort away from unmeasured dimensions, or something else, and does the reading on offer distinguish them. Third, a case that I think breaks the slogan: a well-aligned high-stakes exam, where intensive coaching to the test raises scores and also raises performance on independent measures of the underlying skill — here the measure becomes a target and apparently remains a good measure, and I want to know what that shows about scope rather than being told it isn't a real counterexample. Fourth, pure selection: a proxy correlated with the target in the general population is used to pick the top fraction of a percent, and its correlation at the selected extreme decays even though nobody has adapted to anything. I suspect this is sometimes counted as Goodhart and is really just a fact about truncated distributions and noisy proxies, and I want you to say whether that's right and what follows for whether the law has any content beyond elementary statistics. Fifth, and most interesting to me: cases where targeting the measure doesn't so much fail to track the target as reconstitute it — where the practice reshapes what people mean by research quality or by literacy, so that the target has migrated rather than the measure having failed. Please keep a clean distinction throughout between the measure losing its grip on a fixed target and the target itself moving under measurement, and tell me which readings even have the resources to mark it.

Then the part I actually need for the arguments I keep losing: for each of the uses the slogan was put to in my two settings, say which reading would be strong enough to support it, and whether that reading is one you've endorsed. Specifically: does any defensible reading support never optimizing against a learned proxy at all; does any support the milder engineering responses my lab could actually adopt — hold the metric out, rotate or ensemble several proxies, keep the specification hidden, randomize audits, cap optimization pressure — and are those responses supported by the same mechanism or by different ones; and does any support the sweeping claim that every writable objective will be gamed including the true one, which is where the lab conversation ended up and which smells like a different thesis wearing the same name. If the readings turn out to be logically independent claims sharing a label rather than variants of one claim, say so explicitly, and say which reading people most likely mean when they deploy the line to close off a discussion. I'd also like your view on one reflexive worry of my own: if resistance to gaming is itself something we can measure and select for, does optimizing that property degrade it in the way the law describes, or does the law fail to apply to its own avoidance in a way that reveals its scope conditions? A numbered list of readings with the verdicts attached, plus a short table mapping readings onto my two arguments and onto the five cases, would be perfect; no citations or bibliography, and please don't start with the history.
