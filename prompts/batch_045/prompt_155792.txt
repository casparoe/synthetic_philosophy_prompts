Hi — I'm running a 90-minute seminar next week for about 14 third-year philosophy students on consequentialism and its limits, and I'd like to build the whole session around one activity rather than around a discussion of a paper. I've tried this before by simply asking them how much they think they owe to charity, and it went exactly as you'd expect: everyone agrees giving is a good thing, nobody actually feels squeezed, and we end up rehashing talking points instead of the real objection. A friend who runs a similar session says hers collapsed in the same way. So I want a game or structured simulation where they run into the problem themselves, and where the force comes out of the rules and the incentives rather than out of me explaining anything. Ideally they construct the escalation themselves — each round pushes them a bit further — and only notice at the end where they've arrived. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the name of the objection until afterwards.

The version of the worry I actually care about isn't the headline one about how much consequentialism asks you to give up — the shallow-pond case, give until it hurts, that sort of thing, which they've allready seen a dozen times. It's the structural complaint: that the view seems to leave you with no stopping point. Whatever threshold you settle on — only opportunities above a certain size, only sacrifices below some fraction of your income, only the ten best causes, only what you can do without wrecking your own projects — the view tells you to cross it, because crossing it is just one more optimisation. And there's a wrinkle I'd really like built into the mechanics: the individual demands are meant to be cheap but collectively untrackable. Participants should have to spend a scarce resource (attention tokens, or "hours spent working it out") just to find out whether an opportunity is worth taking, so that deliberation itself becomes one of the competing demands, and how much to deliberate is itself something they'd have to deliberate about. If the activity can also stage the moment where someone says they're going to spend their life writing their novel instead, and then has to answer why that isn't just another line item to be maximised away, that's the moment I'm after.

Concretely, could you give me: the rules and materials (I've got index cards, a whiteboard and about £15 of chocolate — no real money and no laptops), how the rounds and payoffs work, a rough timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what will have actually happened onto the parts of the objection. I'd also like your honest predictions about what will go wrong the first time I run it — my guess is that one student will try to be a saint on purpose for effect, and another will simply refuse to play — plus how to handle each. And please be frank about which feature of the objection the activity can't reproduce, so I know what I have to supply in the debrief myself. Please write the whole thing in British English, and thanks!
