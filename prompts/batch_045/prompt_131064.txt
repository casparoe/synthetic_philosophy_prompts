I sit on the seven-member Compensation Design Panel for our city's voluntary buyout program in the Riverbend neighborhood, a low-lying district that has flooded twice in six years and is expected to flood more often as rainfall patterns shift. Of the 118 occupied households, 74 are owner-occupants and 44 are renters, many of them long-tenured and several including a resident with a disability for whom the home has been physically adapted. The city has received a state resilience grant large enough to make offers to every household that wants one, but the grant requires the council to approve a single written valuation formula before any offers go out, and our panel's recommendation is due in three weeks.

The panel has divided over what the formula should measure, and the disagreement is not really about money; it is about what a "value" is.

One bloc — including our staff economist — defends a market-anchored formula: set each offer at the price the property would actually fetch, accepting whatever discount buyers apply to floodplain location, and add nothing for the losses that never appear in any market transaction. Their argument is that observed behavior is the only non-arbitrary evidence we have: households chose to live in Riverbend, they chose whether to buy flood insurance, and they chose whether to move after the last flood, so their choices reveal how much the location and the risk were worth to them. Any other number, they say, is a guess dressed up as a measurement, and a survey would simply invite people to name the largest figure they think they can get away with.

A second bloc defends a replacement-and-disturbance formula: pay the cost of genuinely comparable housing outside the floodplain, plus a fixed relocation payment and a fixed payment for non-traded losses. They argue that Riverbend's prices are low precisely because its residents have few options — constrained credit, a tight regional housing market, a history of disinvestment — so the observed discount records the shape of their opportunity set rather than what the location is worth to them; that renters leave almost no market trace at all and would get nearly nothing under the first formula; and that what people actually lose (proximity to kin and to a congregation, a child's school route, a home modified for a wheelchair) is not something they ever chose among.

Three further complications are on the record and should be dealt with rather than noted and dropped. First, our pilot mailing found that take-up shifted measurably with how an offer was framed — lump sum versus monthly payments, "market value" versus "pre-flood value" — even where the amounts were equivalent, which raises the question of whether we are uncovering one settled preference or eliciting one. Second, stated minimum-acceptance figures collected informally by a community group run far above every market-based estimate; the gap is large, stable, and not explained by income. Third, the market-anchored bloc itself splits between those who think the revealed numbers are literally what these households value and those who hold the weaker view that the numbers are merely a useful predictive device and should not be read as a measure of anyone's welfare at all.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the panel and then to the council. It should:

- Open with a bottom line: which formula to adopt, stated concretely enough to be written into a council resolution, including what renters receive, what happens to adapted or non-conforming structures, and how a household whose stated minimum acceptance exceeds the formula is to be handled.
- State plainly which reading of "utility," "value," or "welfare" the recommended formula presupposes, and defend the choice of that reading for a public compensation program specifically — not as a general theory of rational choice.
- Give the strongest version of the argument for the approach you reject, in a form its defenders would recognize, and explain what it gets right even though it loses.
- Include an implementation timeline with the next three concrete steps and who is responsible for each.
- Name what would count as evidence for revisiting the formula in eighteen months, including any post-hoc behavioral check you think is worth running.

Do not cite, quote, or summarize published literature; reason from the case as I have described it, and treat the numbers I have given as the whole of the evidence available. Please write in American English, in the register of a professional policy memo, at roughly 900 to 1,100 words.
