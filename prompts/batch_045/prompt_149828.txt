I'm on my town's capital planning committee — volunteer thing, I write backend code for a living, I got on it by showing up to one meeting two years ago and now I'm the guy who reads the spreadsheets. We vote on projects with cost-benefit spreads, and the manual says use a 5% real discount rate. I get the mechanics fine, it's a decay factor on future dollars. But I fell into a rabbit hole reading about whether it's even ok to discount the future, and now I definately can't tell what rule we've been running. Our record:

- New water main, cost now, benefits over ~30 years to people alive today: approved, nobody argued.
- Treatment plant sized for demand 60 years out: staff had it negative at 5%, we approved anyway, mostly on "we'll need it eventually and building it in stages costs more."
- Geothermal loop for a new subdivision, payoff 60-100 years out, staff positive at 2% and negative at 5%: rejected. The argument that carried was that the payoff depends on future councils maintaining the loop, and nobody trusts that.
- Fire station on a 30-year bond, so the costs land on people 20-30 years from now: approved, "the people paying are the people using it."
- Staff once proposed a higher rate for anything past 50 years and we voted that down; the chair said we're not going to say a flooded basement in 2125 counts less just because it's later. Same meeting, we killed a project whose benefit was 90% dependent on some technology still being in use.

New one Thursday and I need to know where I land. Quarry Ridge: a quarry wants to expand into the recharge zone for our only aquifer. If it does, the wells go bad in roughly 60-70 years and stay bad for centuries; the fallback is a treatment plant on the river, call it $60M in today's dollars plus permanent operating costs. We can buy the land plus a perpetual conservation easement held by a land trust for $4.1M, one time, no maintenance, and it doesn't depend on any future council doing anything at all. Staff has it at about −$1.7M at 5%, around +$18M at 2%, and silly-money positive at 0%. The people who benefit are almost all people who don't exist yet. Maybe a 10-15% chance nobody's using those wells by then for some unrelated reason.

Our finance person says the real test isn't the spreadsheet, it's this: $4.1M compounding at 5% real for a couple centuries is tens of billions, we could build that plant a hundred times over, so protecting the aquifer is the sucker's move even though the aquifer is irreversable. She admits that below roughly 4% real, on her own test, protecting wins. What I notice is that she's comparing dollars to dollars, the aquifer can't be bought back with money at any point, and nobody has written down what that hypothetical endowment would be obligated to spend on.

So: what two or three rules fit all five of our votes but come apart here? What does each one say about Thursday, and which is my best guess? Which of those five votes is the closest analog, really? What exactly are we committing to with a yes — that we never discount, or only that irreplaceable stuff doesn't get discounted, or something about who's alive when the bill and the benefit land? And what's the single case we'd have to vote on that would actually expose which rule we've been running all along? I was thinking a version where the thing at stake far out is just money rather than an asset you can't replace, but tell me if there's a better probe than that.
