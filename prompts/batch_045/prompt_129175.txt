I'm a high school senior, and I'm the student member of my district's curriculum committee. We are approving the standards for a new semester-long elective, "Atrocity and Memory," which will teach four or five twentieth-century cases of mass violence in a single course. I'm also using roughly this question for my Theory of Knowledge essay, so I care both about what our document ought to say and about what it already commits us to whether or not anyone notices.

The draft contains four provisions that I cannot get to line up, although the committee treats them as obviously consistent:

1. **Equal time.** Each case study receives the same number of class periods regardless of how many people died. The teacher's guide explains that instructional time is "not a measure of magnitude."
2. **No ranking.** "No assessment item may ask students to order the cases by gravity, or to state which was worse." When I asked why, a teacher said that comparing turns victims into amounts.
3. **Shared pattern.** For each case, students must fill in the same chart of mechanisms — singling out a group, stripping it of property and legal standing, concentrating it, dehumanizing language, killing — and mark the point at which outside intervention might have worked. The chair says the point of the course is to produce "transferable warning signs."
4. **Comparative final assessment.** Given what was known at the time, in which case did outside actors have the clearest opportunity to prevent the killing, and why did they not take it?

Two further things sit oddly against this. The same teachers who say that ranking is obscene have us learn approximate death tolls. And the legal category of genocide, as I understand it, turns on intent to destroy a group as such, so that mass killing without that intent falls outside the category — which looks like the law itself sorting atrocities into one specially grave kind and everything else, even when the everything else involves more deaths. (I have also read that in several European countries denying one particular atrocity is a crime while denying others is not, though I can't check the details and they aren't what I'm asking about; it just struck me as the same shape of asymmetry.)

So my question is: what does this package of practices actually presuppose about what it is to compare two atrocities?

Specifically:

- Is the ban on ranking in clause 2 committed to the strong claim that atrocities have no common measure of badness at all, or only to something weaker — that ranking is a form of disrespect to the dead, or that it corrupts what a community remembers? When my teacher says comparison turns victims into amounts, is that a claim about measurement failing, or a claim about what we owe people?
- Does clause 3 contradict clause 2? It seems to require the cases to be comparable in their causes while denying that they are comparable in their gravity. Can causal comparability come apart from comparability of badness, or is the course quietly leaning on a distinction it cannot state?
- Where does clause 4 belong? Is preventability a way of smuggling severity back in under a different name, or a genuinely independent axis — a matter of what we could have done rather than of how bad it was? And is clause 1's equal time an expression of respect, or is it itself a ranking, a claim that each case deserves equal attention — and if so, what does that claim rest on?
- Which rival views does the document rule out? It seems to exclude the view that we owe the dead an honest ranking. Does it also exclude the view that the cases are incomparable in *every* respect?
- Clause 2 is the one I'm supposed to defend, and I've drafted an alternative: replace the ban with a rule that students may identify "what each case uniquely destroyed" but may never place two cases on a single scale — a separate-ledgers approach. Does that escape the problem clause 2 was written to solve, or does it just rename it?

What I want is the implicit theory reconstructed: which parts of the document are strictly *presupposed* by clauses 1–4, which merely sit comfortably with them, and where the course is underdetermined so that two quite different theories would license the same language. And if we replaced the implicit view with its opposite — a single scale on which atrocities really are worse and better — which of the four clauses would have to change, and which would survive untouched? I'd rather you tell me whether the package hangs together than tell me which side to be on, though I'd like to know if you think it's defensible. The cases themselves are the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge, Rwanda, and Srebrenica; I'm interested in the structure, not in relitigating the histories.
