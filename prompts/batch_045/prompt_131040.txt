I build inference infrastructure for large language models. I have no formal training in philosophy, so please define any term you use that isn't standard engineering vocabulary.

I recently read von Neumann's *The Computer and the Brain* — the unfinished manuscript of his Silliman lectures, published by Yale in 1958 — and I keep running into places where the thing I do all day looks as though it were predicted by it. My rough understanding of his position, which please correct if I have it wrong: the nervous system is a computing machine of a completely different profile from the ones we build — something like ten billion neurons, an elementary act lasting on the order of hundredths of a second, and arithmetic carried at only about two or three significant decimal digits. From this he concludes that the brain has enormous parallelism but very little serial depth available inside a single act, and that it must therefore be doing something more like statistical computation than like the long chains of exact deduction a digital machine performs. He also, as I understand it, doubted that the brain's primary "language" was mathematics or formal logic at all, and in his automata work he was preoccupied both with getting reliable organisms out of unreliable components and with thresholds of complication past which a machine can produce things more complicated than itself.

What I want is a disciplined guess at what he would have said about what we actually have now, and I want it anchored to five specific things:

1. **Low precision works.** Production models are routinely quantized to eight or four bits per weight and lose a couple of percent of benchmark accuracy, which is far less damage than our intuitions predicted. Is this his point about two-decimal-digit arithmetic being enough? Or does it vindicate him for the wrong reason — because we are overparameterized and can absorb the noise, not because the computation is intrinsically statistical?

2. **Serial depth is bought with tokens.** A modern transformer is on the order of a hundred layers deep: shallow in serial terms relative to its size. The way we get more serial computation out of it is to let it emit more tokens before answering — chain-of-thought, test-time compute scaling, deliberation written out as text. Within his budget of only a few hundred sequential steps per act, how should this be read? Is converting serial depth into "more tokens" the same kind of trick he thought the brain was playing, or is it cheating, by outsourcing to a scratchpad outside the system that does the thinking?

3. **Almost everything is learned rather than specified.** The human genome is about three billion base pairs, a few billion bits, which is far too little to specify the wiring of the brain, so most of the connectivity has to arrive through a generative rule plus experience. And our largest models have hundreds of billions of parameters, essentially none hand-specified, all fitted to data. He thought about machines that are designed and about organisms that are evolved. (The bare numerical similarity between parameter counts and neuron counts is not what I am asking about.) Would the thought that brains and networks both acquire their structure by being fit rather than written down have struck him as the answer to a puzzle he had posed, or as a dodge?

4. **Reliability without reliable parts.** He was interested in synthesizing reliable organisms from unreliable components. Modern training does something adjacent but not obviously identical: we inject noise on purpose — dropout, stochastic depth, low-precision accumulation, minibatch sampling — and reliability emerges from vast numbers of individually sloppy units. Same insight, or a different one wearing the same clothes?

5. **Language as the medium.** He apparently doubted that the brain's native code was logic or mathematics. Our models are trained to predict natural language and then appear to reason by continuing it. Would he have taken that as evidence for his picture of the brain, with the symbolic AI that dominated the decades after his death being the wrong turn? Or would he say that a system with no sensorimotor loop is not doing statistics on anything, and that the appearance of reasoning is an artifact of text being a record left by creatures who did have one?

For each of the five: say whether it vindicates his view, embarrasses it, or falls outside anything his framework can express. I would much rather you wrote "his apparatus has nothing to say here" than stretched it to cover the case. Then tell me which single development since 1957 would have forced the largest revision in his picture of the brain, and why.

I have read the book and do not need pointers to further reading. I want the argument.
