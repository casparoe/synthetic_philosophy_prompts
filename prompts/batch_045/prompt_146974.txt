I'm in a small reading group of safety people at my lab and we've been going back and forth on worst-case vs average-case guarantees for about a year now, and it's completely stalled. The worst-case side says that for a system doing something as open-ended as "be a useful agent", failures are exactly the kind of thing that come rare, correlated and adversarially selected, so "our evals have looked fine so far" tells you almost nothing about the tail. The average-case side says we're never getting a proof for anything real, worst-case framing throws away nearly all the evidence we actually have (deployment behaviour, fine-tuning dynamics, how failures show up in practice), and it quietly assumes an adversary who is maximally clever and maximally unlucky-for-us at once, which is its own empirical claim nobody has defended.

My hypothesis is that we think we're arguing about how much tail risk to accept, but we're really arguing about whether the failure distribution has warning shots: whether a system that would catastrophically fail tends to produce smaller visible non-catastrophic failures first, so that iterative deployment buys you information faster than it buys the system capabilities. That would be a real empirical question nobody can settle yet, which would explain the stall. But maybe not — maybe it's a disagreement about what counts as evidence (is a mechanistic story evidence? is absence of failure?), or about whether "guarantee" means proof or just very high confidence. Diagnose it: what kind of disagreement is this actually, and given your diagnosis, what would move it — a theorem, some specific experiment, a distinction, or nothing at all?
