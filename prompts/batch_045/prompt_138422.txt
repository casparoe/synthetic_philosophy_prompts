Here's the half-formed thing I keep circling. We normally talk as if there are two statuses an act can have: required, and optional-but-praiseworthy. But there's a big middle class of things that we don't treat as required and don't treat as merely optional either. Texting a friend back about something small within a day or two. Turning up to a colleague's talk after you said you might. Splitting a bill in a way that's merely fair, rather than in the way that's to the penny in your favour. Answering a student's email over the weekend. If you do these, you get essentially no credit — not admiration, not gratitude, often not even noticed. If you don't do them, you aren't blamed exactly, but something happens that isn't nothing: people are disappointed, they update on you, they may say something next time. And there seem to be speech acts available here that aren't available on either side of the line: you can be reminded, prompted, gently asked, chased up. Whereas with a genuinely optional act you can only ask in a way that's basically begging, and with a required one you can just demand it.

So the question: is expected-but-not-required a real third normative status, or is it either (a) just the optional tier plus a statistical fact about what most people in fact do, or (b) a way-station en route to required — a temporary phase, because once near-enough everyone complies, the demandingness/capacity style of argument converts it into an obligation, so the middle tier is unstable by its nature? I want the proposal to commit to which of those three is right, or to argue that the question has no determinate answer, and to say what would settle it.

A worry I want you to actually grapple with: every test I can come up with for whether the middle tier is a real status ends up measuring how widespread compliance is, which is just what option (a) already says it is. So propose a test that doesn't collapse into that — or argue that collapse is informative rather than a bug.
