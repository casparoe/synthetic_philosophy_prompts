I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in anything like this — I left school at eighteen and have spent the last couple of years reading about it on my own, mostly by chasing up things I disagreed with. I've reached a point where I can't tell whether I'm stuck on a real difficulty or just on the way the popular books put it, and I think the clearest way to find out is to hear two people who know what they're about argue it properly.

The question is whether the shift from foraging to farming was a mistake. I first met the case for saying yes in Marshall Sahlins's 1966 argument about hunter-gatherers as the original affluent society — limited wants, short working hours, no stored surplus — and in a magazine essay from the late 1980s that called agriculture the worst mistake in the history of the human race. Since then I've read the pushback: that the change was slow, patchy and often reversed rather than a single event; that early farming settlements and the first states built on them were fragile and that people kept walking away from them; that skeletons from early farming communities are on average shorter and carry more signs of anaemia, infection and joint wear than those of the foragers who came before; and that a foraging planet could not have supported more than a small fraction of the people alive today. I don't trust my own ability to weigh one of those against the other.

What I'd like is a dialogue of roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words between two old friends in their fifties who meet once a year for a long walk and always end up on this subject. One of them has spent the past five years trying to live off a couple of acres and takes the mistake side; the other takes the opposite view. Please give them names and let them annoy each other a bit, but make them equally well-informed and equally intelligent: I don't want either of them winning on rhetoric or on the other one being thick. Each should reply to what the other has actually said, not to a rehearsed version of the position.

Three particular knots, which I've been turning over and can't undo, should be the spine of the argument.

First, what I've started calling the severance test. For something to be a mistake in the ordinary sense, there has to be someone who could have foreseen the outcome, chosen otherwise, and then borne the cost. But here the extra work, the worse teeth, the crowding and the epidemics arrived within a few generations, while the payoffs — writing, surgery, the whole apparatus of modern medicine — took thousands of years and went to people who would not otherwise have existed at all. So it looks as if it could be a ruinous bargain and still nobody's mistake. Is a mistake with no victim and no decision-maker a coherent thing, or does the absence of a decision show we're using the wrong word and that trap, spiral or arms race is the honest one?

Second, what I think of as the counterfactual ledger. If you add up well-being across everyone who ever lives, farming wins by arithmetic, because there are vastly more people. But those extra people only exist because of farming. I want the friend defending farming to press the point that you cannot be harmed by being brought into existence, and I want the other one to have a real answer to it rather than a shrug — including the question of whether the people who were never born should count as nothing at all in the summing up.

Third, the exit test. The defender of farming should be free to point out that the person making the mistake case is herself a farmer — a smallholding is agriculture, and so are her tools, her fencing and her apple trees — and that foragers demonstrably kept leaving early states to go back to an older way of life, which seems to cut both ways: it shows the door was open, but also that most people didn't walk through it. And I'd like them to face whether the same style of argument would condemn everything that came after, from writing to antibiotics.

Some constraints, if you don't mind. Please don't add footnotes, citations, quotations or suggestions for further reading — I've no way to check any of them, and I don't want words put into the mouths of writers that they didn't say. Don't invent statistics or attribute findings to named studies; if evidence matters, describe it by kind and as roughly as you like. Explain any term a general reader wouldn't know within the dialogue itself, so that someone like me could follow it without a reference work to hand. It shouldn't end with either of them clearly winning: end on whichever of the three knots you think is genuinely hardest. Please write in British English throughout.

Finally, after the dialogue, add a short note of about 150 to 200 words saying how confident you are in each position as you've represented it — which of the claims involved are reasonably settled, which are contested among people who study this, and which are your own extrapolation — and naming the single inference in the whole exchange that you're least sure is sound. I'd rather know where the argument is thin than be given something that reads smoothly.
