The passage (my paraphrase of a couple of pages, but I've tried to keep its claims intact):

There are two kinds of conditional, and they are governed by completely different theories. Take indicatives first: If the train was on time, she is home by now. An indicative conditional has no truth conditions at all. What Adams showed — and what Lewis's triviality result later established beyond dispute — is that the only thing to say about an indicative conditional is how confident you should be in the consequent given the antecedent, that is, the conditional probability P(B|A). It follows that indicatives are not the sort of thing that can be true or false, and that is why they cannot be embedded inside other operators without changing meaning: you cannot prefix one with probably, negate one, or put one inside a quantifier and expect the result to mean what it looks like it means. Possible-worlds semantics simply does not apply to them. Counterfactuals are different in kind: If the train had been on time, she would have been home by now. Here the antecedent is by definition false, so there is no probability of the antecedent to conditionalize on and the whole probabilistic apparatus drops out. Instead, counterfactuals are evaluated by similarity between possible worlds: a counterfactual is true just in case the consequent holds in all the worlds closest to the actual world at which the antecedent holds. Lewis's account is the standard one. Because the similarity ordering is fixed by the facts about which worlds resemble which, adding further detail to the antecedent can never overturn a true counterfactual — the closest antecedent-worlds are the closest antecedent-worlds, and if the consequent holds throughout them, it holds. The context-sensitivity that people notice in counterfactuals is just context settling which respects of similarity we are scoring. So the difference between the two kinds reduces to this: the antecedent of an indicative is epistemically open for the speaker, while the antecedent of a counterfactual is settled false. Same connective, different information. Finally, the well-known poker-game standoff cases, in which a speaker can be in a position to assert both if A, B and if A, not-B, refute outright the idea that indicatives have truth conditions; there is nothing for a possible-worlds or truth-conditional theory to say here.
