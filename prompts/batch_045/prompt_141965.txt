You are a philosopher of action with a research-level command of the literature on intention, practical rationality, and the toxin puzzle that Gregory Kavka published in 1983. I want you to think carefully before you answer: start by sketching, briefly and for yourself, several candidate diagnoses of the dispute I describe below — including the ones you are inclined to reject — and only then commit to one. Do not give me a literature survey, a reading list, or citations. Argue from the positions as I characterize them.

Here is the dispute. Kavka's case, as I'll reconstruct it: an eccentric billionaire puts a vial of toxin in front of you. Drinking it tomorrow afternoon would make you painfully ill for a day, with no lasting effects. He will pay you a large sum if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon. You need not drink it: the money is in your account hours before the time for drinking arrives, and you are free to change your mind. All you must do is be in the state of intending, at midnight, to drink. There is a strong case that it would be rational to be in that state at midnight (there is a lot of money in it, and nothing else is at stake), and an equally strong case that once the money is in hand it would not be rational to drink (nothing further is gained, and you'd be causing yourself a bad day). So: can you do it?

The dialectic, as I've watched it go round, has four moves in it, and nobody seems to leave their move.

1. The impossibility move. You cannot intend what you know full well you will not do. Intention is partly constituted by a commitment to treating the intended action as settling further deliberation, and it is subject to norms of means–end coherence and to a no-regret style condition; an agent who is certain she will abandon the plan at plan's end cannot be in that state. Best case, at midnight you can predict that you will drink, or want to be the sort of person who would, but you cannot intend it.

2. The rationalist or commitment move. The intention, once sincerely formed, is itself normative: it constrains what counts as the rational thing for you to do tomorrow. The rational action tomorrow is the best one compatible with the commitment you made, and drinking is that action. So intending is not a prediction of what you will do, and the puzzle shows a gap between intention and belief about your own future action.

3. The indirect-route move. You may not be able to intend at will, but there are things you can do at will that would cause you to intend, and causing yourself to intend is not the same as intending — or maybe it is, once you have done enough of it. Take a drug that makes drinking attractive, make a side bet with a friend, enlist someone to shame you, exploit your own akrasia, form a second-order intention and let it do the work. The fight inside this move is about whether these produce an intention or merely manufacture one, and whether manufactured intentions are the thing the billionaire is buying.

4. The defective-case move. The set-up is not coherent, or not coherent in the way it needs to be. Perhaps it conflates intending with predicting, or with desiring, or with trying; perhaps "intend at midnight tonight" does not pick out a determinate act-type you could perform to order; perhaps the payoff is ill-defined because the billionaire's detection of your state is doing causal work that has been smuggled in under the word "detect." On this move the puzzle is an artifact and the right response is to dissolve it.

Forty-plus years on, all four moves are still on the table, and — this is what I find most telling — competent people report flatly opposite verdicts about what is "obvious" at midnight. That pattern is what I want diagnosed, not adjudicated.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: this is not one dispute. At least four questions are being run under one label. (i) A psychological question about our actual capacity: can a creature with our architecture get itself into that state on demand, and is the answer different for an agent who has had practice with commitment devices? (ii) A normative question: what is it rational to do or to intend, given that you can affect your own future reasons? (iii) A conceptual or metaphysical question: what must intention be for this to be possible or impossible? (iv) A question about the folk concept, which is at least partly empirical: would ordinary speakers say of someone in that position that she intended to drink, and does the answer track anything the theorists care about? My suspicion is that the intuitions treated as data in this literature are answers to different members of this list, which is why they do not converge, and why the debate has the peculiar texture it has: everybody is right about something and nobody is talking to anybody.

To help test that, here are four variations I've invented, which as far as I know are not in the literature, so treat them as instruments rather than as received cases:

(a) The fractional toxin. The vial also relieves a mild headache you expect to have tomorrow, worth a very small amount to you — far less than a day of illness. Now you have a genuine but tiny independent reason to drink. Now vary that residual reason continuously upward until it is decisive. Does the possibility of intending switch on at some point, and if so, is the transition smooth or does it snap? What does the shape of the transition tell you about what the original intuition is tracking?

(b) The noisy monitor. The billionaire cannot read your midnight state with certainty; his device is, say, 80% reliable, and pays out on its reading. Forming the intention is now evidentially relevant to your getting the money without being sufficient for it. Does the puzzle get easier, and does the answer differ depending on whether the unreliability is in the detection or in the payout?

(c) The adjustable deadline. You may choose when midnight is: a year before the drinking, or one minute before. Run your four moves at each end. If the intuition about whether you can intend changes with the deadline, is the real variable the expected stability of your reasons over the interval rather than anything about intention as such?

(d) The surrogate agent. You must arrange for something you have built — a software agent whose internal states you can inspect and set directly — to be in the relevant state at midnight, on pain of no payout. You can write whatever policy you like into it. Does the puzzle survive contact with a case where the "intending" is fully transparent to you, and if it does not, is that because artifacts cannot intend, or because the original intuition was never about intention?

Deliverable: a diagnosis of what kind of standoff this is, with reasons, engaging all four moves and using my variations wherever they discriminate between diagnoses. Specifically: how much of the disagreement is verbal (sides meaning different things by intention), how much is a difference about which cases and which intuitions count as data, how much is a bedrock divergence in starting commitments that neither side has an argument for, and how much is a hidden empirical or formal question in disguise. Say whether the question is ill-posed, or merely hard. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, an empirical study, a redescribed target — or argue honestly that no progress is to be expected and why. One further thing I'd like addressed: the puzzle came out of work on deterrence and the credibility of threats, and commitment under an incentive to renege has been formalized in game theory. Is that a sign that the philosophical standoff is a proxy for a question that has been settled elsewhere, or is the formal apparatus silent precisely on the part the philosophers are fighting about?

Finally, and this matters as much as the diagnosis: separate, explicitly, what is settled and uncontroversial among people who know this material from what is genuinely contested, and from what is your own view. Flag each place where you are attributing a position you are less than confident about, and say how confident you are. Tell me where a specialist would most likely push back on your diagnosis, and where you yourself are least sure. Do not present a contested reading as the consensus, and do not let your own preferred theory of intention do quiet work in the guise of a neutral description of the data.
