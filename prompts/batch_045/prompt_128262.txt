I serve on a small committee that advises our college's development office on how to segment roughly forty thousand alumni for a capital campaign. The consultants they retained propose assigning every alum to one of four cohorts — Silent, Boomer, Gen X, Millennial — and tailoring both the size of the ask and the tone of the messaging to the assigned label. The deck consistently writes as though these groupings had been found rather than drawn: it says, for instance, that Millennials give later and give differently because they entered the labour market during a particular recession.

Three features of the setup trouble me, and I cannot tell whether they are one worry in three guises. First, the boundaries move: two charts in the same deck place the Millennial/Gen Z edge two years apart, and the discrepancy seems to matter to no one but me. Second, the reported differences are small mean differences over heavily overlapping distributions; the same birth years in South Korea or Poland did not, so far as I can tell, share the formative events the story appeals to; and when I asked whether the pattern might really be an effect of age at the time of the ask, or of the year in which the ask was made, I was told the model cannot separate these three, since for any given alum they are locked together. Third — the one I find hardest to place — the labels appear to act back on their instances. Once people are addressed in the solicitations themselves as Millennials and spoken to as such, some of them seem to become more Millennial; and the vocabulary has since migrated into our alumni magazine and our reunion programming in ways that were not in the original data.

My colleagues pull in opposite directions. The sociologist on the committee says the matter is entirely empirical and that philosophy has nothing to add. A colleague in gender studies says the categories are real but only because we make them real, and that this makes them no less binding — which I take to be true, and do not know how to convert into a recommendation. The development office, for its part, would like a yes-or-no answer: are these real groups doing genuine causal work, or convenient filing labels we could replace with anything?

I would be grateful for help with three things. (i) Is there a defensible position between "these are real kinds whose boundaries are out there to be discovered" and "these are arbitrary conventions, so nothing about the world could make one segmentation better than another"? State it precisely enough that I can see what would show it to be wrong. A historian friend adds that a generation, strictly speaking, exists only once people begin describing themselves in its terms — which is why, she says, there was a generation of 1968 but not yet a "Gen Z" in 2019. Is that a substantive thesis or a stipulation about a word? (ii) On whatever position you defend, what, if anything, licenses using these categories as the consultants propose — for prediction and for deciding how to address people? Would we do better to keep the four labels, to use year of birth as a continuous variable, or to replace the labels with something directly about people's circumstances, such as whether they finished school into a weak labour market? What does each option buy, and what does it cost? (iii) Finally: does using the categories change the answer to the first two questions? If addressing people as members of a cohort tends to make them into more reliable instances of it, then a segmentation that is merely convenient now may predict well in ten years for reasons that have nothing to do with its having been correct. I cannot tell whether that is a reason to adopt it, a reason to refuse it, or simply a feature of the situation that I should stop treating as an objection.
