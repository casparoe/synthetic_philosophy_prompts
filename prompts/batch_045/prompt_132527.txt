Hey — I'm noodling on a short paper about advance directives and I want to see whether the view I've drifted into can actually be defended. Please argue for the following position.

The position: the earlier self's authority over the later self isn't grounded in personal identity, or in autonomy understood as self-rule across time, or in anything like a promise the earlier self made to herself. It's *delegated* authority. The later self can't decide well, so the decision gets delegated to whoever is best placed to make it for her, and the earlier self is usually the best available candidate. And delegated authority is the kind of thing that lapses when the delegate is badly mistaken about the very facts she was supposed to be reasoning from. So a directive should carry weight roughly in proportion to how well placed its author was to make the judgement it encodes — which means, among other things, that a directive written by someone who has actually lived in something like the condition it describes (written two years into a dementia diagnosis, say, or by someone with a long-standing disability) should count for *more*, not less, than one written from full health, even though the full-health case is the one everyone treats as paradigmatically authoritative.

What I want from the argument:

- Take on the standard hard case instead of dodging it. A woman who, while healthy and fully informed, wrote a directive refusing life-prolonging treatment under severe dementia; who now, severely demented, seems content, responds to music, and shows no sign of wanting to die. Say what the view implies there, and make the implication sound like a feature rather than a cost — or explain why it's a cost the view can absorb.

- The obvious objection is that this collapses into a best-interests standard with extra steps: if the earlier self's authority is hostage to how good her predictions turned out to be, then really we're just asking what's good for the later self, and the earlier self drops out of the picture entirely. That's the objection I most want answered, and I definately don't want it answered by saying something like 'well, both identity and well-being matter a bit' — I want a reason the epistemic framing doesn't eat itself.

- A second objection worries me more. If, on the kind of psychological-continuity view that's popular here, the earlier self is barely the same person as the later self, why should her being *well informed* matter at all? Being well informed isn't normally a source of authority over somebody else's body.

- Then handle the case I think might actually kill the view: directives that don't seem to rest on any prediction. Someone refuses a treatment not because she thinks being demented would be awful, but because she thinks being kept alive in that state is demeaning, or violates something she regards as sacred, or because she doesn't want that to be her grandchildren's last memory of her — and she says, plausibly, that she'd want this even if she turned out to be perfectly content. Is that directive 'mistaken about the facts' in the relevant sense, or does the view have to to admit a non-epistemic core and then explain why the epistemic condition still governs everything else?

One more wrinkle if you can fit it in: people write directives in front of other people, and sometimes the content looks shaped by what will make the writer look good, or by what will spare her family guilt, rather than by what she wants. Does the delegated-authority picture have the resources to discount for that, or does it have to treat the directive as a species of testimony?

Give me one sustained argument rather than a survey of positions — somewhere in the 700–900 word range — and invent a bit of terminology for the condition you're defending if that helps you keep it precise. Don't cite anything; I care whether the argument works, not who has said what. At the end, name the single premise you'd least like to be forced to give up.
