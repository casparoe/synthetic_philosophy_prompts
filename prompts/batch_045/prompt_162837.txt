So I'm a software engineer with no philosophy or econ background, and I help run the grants committee for a mid-size open source foundation — we hand out a couple million a year across ~40 grants, and right now the decisions are made by five people arguing over a spreadsheet. A friend who works at a prediction market startup keeps telling us we should just run futarchy on it: the committee votes on what we're supposed to maximize (say, downstream adoption or contributor retention measured 18 months out), then we open conditional markets that trade claims like total adoption conditional on us funding project A versus conditional on us not funding it, and we fund whichever side prices higher. I think I get the basic mechanic, and I get that the standard objections are about manipulation, thin markets, and how you even pick the metric. What I need is an agenda, not an explainer.

Concretely: we have maybe six months of two people's part-time time and around $50k we could put into market subsidies, and the decision at the end of that is whether to run one live pilot on a handful of grants or drop the whole idea. So which open questions here actually matter for a decision like that, in what order should we take them on, and why that order? I care much more about the reasoning than the list — which questions are upstream of the others (so that getting them wrong quietly poisons everything downstream), which are tractable for two engineers with no econ PhD versus which genuinely need mechanism-design expertise, which are already well trodden versus actually neglected, and for each one, what we would concretely do differently depending on how it came out. Also please say which of the famous philosophical objections basically don't bite at our scale and that we should stop spending time on.

A few things we've been kicking around that I want you to put into the ranking rather than politely ignore: (1) shadow mode, where we run the markets non-binding alongside our normal process for a year and only escalate to binding if the market's implied forecasts beat the committee's on grants we didn't route through it; (2) locking the metric before any trading starts, plus letting anyone buy a capped dispute bond that forces a pause and a public audit of whether the metric is being gamed; (3) instead of one welfare metric, requiring a proposal to clear a small bundle of them (adoption, contributor retention, maintainer bus factor) with an explicit rule for what counts as clearing; (4) trading on what a future, better-informed version of our own electorate would retrospectively endorse, rather than on an objective index; (5) a liquidity subsidy that decays as volume arrives, and the separate question of who eats the losses when the market turns out to be wrong. Please defintely rank all five against whatever you think matters more, and don't tell me they're all good ideas.
