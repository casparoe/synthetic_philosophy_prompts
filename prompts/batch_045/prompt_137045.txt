Hey — so I'm in my fourth year of a philosophy PhD (mind/metaphysics mostly, and I TA the consciousness seminar, which is rotting my brain), and my department has this ongoing bit where we compete to write the most drearily bureaucratic document we can about whatever we happen to be reading. Panpsychism is up next and I want you to take a swing at it. Write me the approved minutes of the third meeting of the Microphenomenal Welfare Subcommittee, a standing subcommittee of the ethics board at a big national physics funding agency, set maybe fifteen or twenty years from now, in a world where Russellian monist panpsychism quietly became the default somewhere along the way: everybody now grants that the categorical grounds of mass, charge and spin have intrinsic natures, that those natures are experiential, that micro-experiences have valence, and that realizing this created an entire compliance industry. The item on the agenda is a routine renewal of beam time for a high-luminosity collider run, and the committee has to sign off on the applicant's fork-risk assessment.

Here's the setup to build on. The subcommittee's governing white paper — written by a now-deceased and extremely serious European philosopher of your invention — adopted what everyone calls the fork criterion: a system's experience has negative valence, i.e. counts as suffering, only if the system's dynamics genuinely admits more than one incompatible immediate continuation, only if it's actually poised at a branch point rather than sliding along a single deterministic trajectory. Pure-state evolution doesn't fork; real chance does. I want the comedy to come from the committee being forced to live with what that criterion actually implies: if branching is what carries all the moral weight, then whether turning on the beam is a atrocity or a nonevent depends entirely on which interpretation of quantum mechanics is true, the most morally urgent research program on earth turns out to be settling the interpretation question, and Bohmian mechanics is the great humanitarian hope of the age. Let somebody say that in deadpan earnest and let the rest of the room try to squirm out from under it.

Aim for about 700–800 words, formatted as numbered agenda items with recorded discussion and at least one formal dissent appended. Along the way I want: the aggregation problem, with someone running the numbers on the ~10^80-ish particles in the observable universe and concluding that if per-subject valence is even microscopically nonzero then the expected disvalue of the run swamps every other line in the federal budget, and someone else replying that the total isn't well defined because there's no fact of the matter about how many subjects there are; the combination problem, arriving as a welfare consultant asking whether the sufferer here is each electron, or some larger subject the electrons compose, or nobody at all, given that composition is precisely the thing the view can't deliver; the conservation point that you can't reduce the number of fundamental subjects, so the only available mitigation is keeping the equipment cold, dark, and unmeasured; and finally the moment where the committee notices that on its own criterion the worst thing human beings do isn't factory farming or war but measurement, and quietly drafts some baseline exemption for naturally occurring forks so it can approve the run and get to lunch. Give me five or so recurring members with distinct briefs — I'm picturing an aggregationist, someone who thinks the framework is fine but that valence needs a functional organization no fundamental particle has, a department physicist who only cares whether the paperwork clears, and a chair trying to get everyone out of the room by five. It should be fair satire: someone who actually works on Russellian monism should read it and wince in recognition rather than roll their eyes at a strawman. Don't break frame to explain the jokes.
