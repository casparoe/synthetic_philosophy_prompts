Last night my friend and I argued for hours about whether the astronomical number of future people is what makes human extinction catastrophic. When I said that this way of valuing future people looks like it commits her to something in the neighbourhood of the repugnant conclusion — a huge population of lives barely worth living beating a much smaller population of excellent ones — she answered 'make people happy, not make happy people' and treated that as settling it. The trouble is that she had spent the previous hour insisting that extinction would be a vast loss precisely because of all the people who would then never exist, so she cannot have meant the strongest thing the slogan seems to say.

Please set out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that one-liner could be expressing. I can already see three rough candidates — that a benefit needs a beneficiary, that the value of a life worth living does not aggregate additively across numbers of people, and that no value comparison is even defined between populations containing different people — but I want the fuller list, ordered from trivial to radical, with a verdict on each: platitude, substantive and defensible, or false. Where a reading is substantive, say what else it commits you to, including whether it generates an unattractive conclusion of its own in some other case.

Test each reading against three cases: (1) ten billion people with lives barely worth living versus five billion with wonderful ones; (2) a mere addition, where an extra group with lives worth living is added without affecting anyone else; (3) a population on a distant planet whose existence no one else ever learns of, containing lives barely worth living. The part I care most about is which readings, if any, actually block the step from 'this extra life would be worth living' to 'the larger population is better' — and whether blocking that step costs her the longtermist claim she opened with. Also tell me which reading someone who deploys the slogan in an argument like ours probably has in mind, and where the residual disagreement with a total view is genuine rather than merely verbal. Assume I know what the repugnant conclusion and the mere addition paradox are, and don't give me citations or a reading list.
