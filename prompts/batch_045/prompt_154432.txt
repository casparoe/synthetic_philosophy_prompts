I'm about halfway through a novel that has a long dinner-table fight at its center — a cattle-ranching family in northern Nevada, one daughter back from college having gone full animal-rights, an uncle who's swapped beef for chicken and believes that settles it — and I want the argument to be better than op-ed fare, so I've been collecting real ones. At a residency last month two other writers went at it for close to an hour after dinner and I honestly cant tell who came out ahead.

Nate (mostly plants, occasional fish) opened with the case for chicken over beef, and it was not a bad case: per pound of meat a broiler lives something like six or seven weeks while a beef animal lives well over a year, so even if you count only the farmed animals themselves, beef means far more days of a possibly-miserable life per unit of food; and on top of that cows need a lot more feed, so beef drags in more cropland, and cropland means combines and rodenticide and poisoned mice and voles, which is the body count people forget about when they give up meat for the animals' sake. Delia came back with two things: first, that Nate had swapped currencies in mid-sentence — he started out counting deaths and ended up counting suffering-days, and those rank the two meats differently — and second, that the cropland point only works if his purchase changes how much land is farmed, which one person's dinner basically doesn't; what it actually changes is how many animals get bred, and on that measure a pound of chicken is a hundred or so short lives while a pound of beef is a small fraction of one long one, so if a farmed life is net negative at all, the number of lives can't just be divided away into days. Nate said fine, do it both ways then: on a body count chicken looks much worse, on suffering-days beef looks worse, and on "net-negative lives brought into existence" it depends on how bad six weeks of broiler are compared to eighteen months of steer, so the ranking flips with the currency nobody can settle, which makes the choice roughly a wash — and given a wash he'll take the cheap low-carbon option. Delia said "roughly a wash" only survives if you ignore what the animals eat: chickens are monogastrics and need digestible grain and soy, i.e. exactly the high-yield annual cropland that supports and then kills the most rodents per acre, whereas ruminants can live on grass, stover and by-products people can't digest, so the cropland footprint of a pound of beef isn't obviously bigger; and anyway, she said, everyone is dishonest about the baseline — a plowed and sprayed field isn't a woodland full of shrews, and a lot of the animals people tally as "crop deaths" only exist because farming created a boom-and-bust habitat for them, so if those lives are net negative, farming isn't merely killing wild animals, it's manufacturing ones that would be better off never having been born, and once you see that, "acreage" stops working as a proxy for harm at all. Nate shot back that most beef in this country is grain-finished in a feedlot anyway, so the cows-on-grass story describes a sliver of what people actually eat; that raising cattle on pasture instead of crops means spreading over much more land per pound, which is the worst outcome if land is what kills wild animals; and that the land-sparing move doesn't rescue it either, since land that leaves production doesn't become a nature reserve, it becomes more production somewhere else. Delia closed by saying that the two of them now agree the "which meat" question is second-order next to how much of a diet is animal at all and what happens to the land, and that the only live disagreement left is whether wild animals' lives are worth living — which, she said, neither of them knows how to account for, so his tidy chicken habit is a decision made on vibes. Nate said every decision here is on vibes once the currencies multiply, but that he still has to eat, and picking the option that does best on the two or three currencies he's most confident about isn't irrational just because the full accounting is unfinished.

Can you referee this the way it was actually argued? Go through it claim by claim — say who's right about which specific point, where someone is right but for a reason they didn't give, and where someone is just wrong — and then tell me, since this is what I need for the scene, what both of them were missing: I have a feeling the real disagreement is something they never named, and until I can name it I can't write the damn chapter.
