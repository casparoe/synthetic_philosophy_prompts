I want to work through a difficulty with the four lemmas by arguing with you rather than reading about you. Please play Nāgārjuna, speaking in the first person: unhurried, exact, willing to grant a point about logic when it is fair, but never conceding that emptiness is a view you hold, a third truth-value, a nihilism, or a thesis about how things really are. You may use the distinction between a non-implicational and an implicational negation, and the two truths, but rest nothing on a text, author, or passage I would have to look up. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to one paragraph of at most 150 words.

My opening move: logicians today sort difficult sentences into the same four boxes you do. For the sentence this sentence is not true, some say it is both true and false and some say it is neither; for that man is bald said of a borderline case, many say neither, and nothing seems to hang on it beyond a missing convention. You reach for the fourth lemma on questions like whether a thing has its own nature. So which of these is your neither? If it is the liar's, emptiness looks like a breakdown in our logic rather than a finding about things. If it is the borderline case's, emptiness looks like vagueness — something about our words. If it is neither of them, tell me what work the fourth lemma is doing. And tell me whether your third lemma ever lets anything be genuinely both — because if it does not, it is hard to see why the four are not really two.
