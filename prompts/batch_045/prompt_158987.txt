I'm a software engineer by day, and in the evenings I sit in on a fortnightly philosophy reading group run by a retired philosopher whose hobby is what he calls his ledger: for every philosophical question the group takes up, he files a verdict in one of four categories — 'solved', 'genuine progress', 'stalled', or 'dissolved' — and we keep a small sweepstake on what he will file next. I have been trying to reverse-engineer the rule he is running, partly for the sweepstake and partly because it is the closest thing to a specification I have ever enjoyed taking apart.

The track record, as I have recorded it: Zeno's paradoxes of motion — 'solved', on the grounds that the question was handed to the mathematics of convergent series and philosophers stopped working on it. The liar paradox and theories of truth — 'genuine progress', because fixed-point and revision treatments gave everyone a shared formal framework and a shared list of desiderata, even though nobody agrees on the right theory. Free will and determinism — 'stalled': two millennia, positions relabelled as compatibilist or incompatibilist, no convergence. Personal identity after the fission and teletransportation literature — 'genuine progress'. Moral realism versus anti-realism — 'stalled'. The mind–body problem, including the hard problem — 'stalled', with the remark that the arrival of neuroscience and integrated-information-style theories does not count, because the philosophers themselves have not moved. Condorcet's paradox and Arrow's theorem — 'solved', exported to economics and decision theory. The sorites paradox — 'genuine progress', because many-valued, supervaluationist and degree-theoretic semantics produced real technical results, again without an agreed theory. The trolley variants and the doctrine of double effect — 'stalled', on the grounds that the new cases multiplied the options rather than removing any. The verification principle of the logical positivists — 'dissolved': the programme collapsed, and he says that nobody will defend it now. External-world scepticism — 'stalled', but flagged for review; it is the only verdict he has said he might change. Some of his side-remarks seem load-bearing: he refuses to count growth of the literature, citation counts, or survey data, since he thinks the readership is self-selected and that fashions move for sociological reasons; but he did count it as evidence for positivism's collapse that the position has become embarrassing to defend.

Next term he owes rulings on two things we have been reading, and I want predictions. First: the grounding and metaphysical fundamentality literature — twenty-odd years of work with its own formalism, its own structural rules about asymmetry and transitivity, and live disputes about whether grounding is hyperintensional, whether it is a relation or an operation, and whether there can be grounding without necessitation, with no consensus and, as far as I can tell, more candidate theories on the table now than when it started. Second: the ameliorative turn in the theory of social kinds, where the question of what race or gender really are is increasingly declared ill-posed and replaced by the question of which concept we ought to use for which purposes — strongly taken up in some corners, fiercely resisted in others, and with no formalism anywhere near it. He has also promised a throwaway ruling on whether computer programs are abstract objects or concrete artefacts, which I expect every candidate rule to call 'stalled', but tell me if that is wrong. I can think of at least three rules that fit the whole ledger — something like convergence, where the space of live options narrows or a position becomes indefensible; something like formal traction, where the question acquires shared machinery and shared desiderata whether or not anyone agrees; and something like reframing, where a question is decomposed or an old position is made unstatable — but I cannot tell which he is using, and I suspect he may be running more than one. For each of the two rulings, tell me what he will file and with what confidence, what each of those rules predicts, where they come apart, and which single further case would separate them most cleanly. Tell me honestly whether his four categories are really doing independent work or whether he is running one rule with four outputs, and give me probabilities, because I have £20 on this.
