You are a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of war, and I want you to actually think this through rather than summarize positions — start by brainstorming what could be going on underneath the disagreement, then commit to a diagnosis.

Here's the thing. The fight over the moral equality of combatants looks to me like it's completely stuck. Very roughly: on one side there's the traditional picture, where a soldier on the unjust side and a soldier on the just side are in the same moral position as far as the fighting itself goes — if both stay within the rules of war, both may kill enemy combatants, both are liable to be killed, and that symmetry is supposed to explain (or at least line up with) why the law of war refuses to distinguish them. On the other side there's the revisionist picture: if one side's war is unjust, then its soldiers are, basically, implementers of a wrongful threat, whatever they subjectively believe; their liability is a matter of what they're actually responsible for, and the other side's soldiers are permissible defenders — so there's no deep moral equality, even between two equally scrupulous soldiers. As far as I can tell every move on both sides has been made, the replies to those moves have been made, and nobody has changed anyone's mind. I've been going back and forth on this for about a year now (me, my supervisor, and a friend who's an armour officer), and I can't tell anymore whether we're disagreeing about anything real.

So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. My own guess, offered as a guess, is that it's mostly a confusion of questions: I suspect the traditionalists are really defending an *institutional* claim — that a workable rule for an activity where participants are systematically uncertain about whether their own side is in the right has to be side-neutral, and that soldiers can't reasonably be asked to adjudicate their own war before fighting — while the revisionists are making a claim about *individual moral liability* in a case that has been stripped of all that uncertainty, and both claims can be true at once. But there are other candidates and I want you to weigh them: maybe the two camps are secretly arguing about different cases (the well-informed volunteer vs. the nineteen-year-old conscript who has no access to intelligence and whose marginal causal contribution to the fighting is tiny); maybe they disagree about whether moral responsibility can even attach to someone by way of their participation in a collective action, which is a prior question neither side argues for; maybe there's a hidden empirical question doing the work — how much ordinary soldiers actually know, how much they could know, and how much difference a side-neutral rule would make to the outcomes of wars; or maybe the whole thing is stalled because there's an unformalized structural question inside it. Here's the version of that last idea I keep playing with: imagine liability isn't a threshold that a soldier either crosses or doesn't, but a scalar quantity that tracks expected contribution to an unjust threat, and then ask where the revisionist's verdict flips as you dial a soldier's contribution continuously upward from rear-echelon cook to sniper. If it flips at a point, the debate should be about where the point is; if it doesn't flip, both sides might be using incompatible shapes for the same function, which would explain why they talk past each other.
