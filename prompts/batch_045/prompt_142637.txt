I'm trying to determine how much of expected utility theory can be extracted from the idea of coherence alone, and I would like a number with the derivation exposed rather than a tour of the options.

Here is the proposition I want evaluated. Call it **P**: *There exists a sound argument, from purely structural premises, that establishes that an ideally reflective agent whose preferences over lotteries violate the independence axiom is ipso facto rationally defective — incoherent, or exploitable in a way that constitutes a rational defect, or otherwise rationally required to revise.* Two clarifications. "Structural" excludes any premise about what is worth caring about: no premise about permissible attitudes to risk, about whether regret, disappointment, suspense, or the having-had-a-guarantee of something has value, and no premise that is itself as contested as independence or extensionally equivalent to it. "Ideally reflective" means the agent has no computational limits, has no false factual beliefs, has settled and stable preferences, and has fully understood every argument available to them.

The test case is the usual Allais pattern: an agent who prefers $1M with certainty to a lottery yielding $5M with probability 0.10, $1M with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01, while simultaneously preferring a 0.10 chance at $5M (nothing otherwise) to an 0.11 chance at $1M (nothing otherwise).

Give me your credence that P is true, decomposed. I care about the sub-questions more than the headline number, so lay out at least the following, each with a number and a one-line justification, and tell me how you are combining them and whether the terms are independent:

(a) **Reduction.** Whether the argument can even be stated without assuming that preferences over acts depend only on the induced distribution over outcomes — that is, without assuming something like reduction of compound lotteries or consequentialism about decision trees. Note that this assumption is what licenses treating the two Allais pairs as involving one and the same residual comparison against a common branch, so an Allais agent who rejects reduction rejects the description under which their preferences conflict. Is that rejection itself something a structural argument can rule out?

(b) **Chance versus ambiguity.** Whether a structural argument must presuppose that the agent faces known objective chances, and how much of the intuitive pull of independence-violation is really a response to ambiguity that a coherence argument should not reach.

(c) **Menu-dependence and the failure of the naive money pump.** Whether an Allais agent's preferences can be stable across choice menus, so that no sequence of trades each endorsed at the time of making it leads them to an outcome they rank worse ex ante. If they can be stable, the classic sequential route is blocked, and I want your probability that it is blocked for agents who are neither myopic nor dynamically inconsistent.

(d) **Whether the sure-thing principle is structural.** Your credence that the sure-thing principle — and with it independence, given the remaining structural assumptions — can be derived from premises an ideal reflector must accept, rather than being itself a substantive separability claim about how value composes across mutually exclusive states.

(e) **The implementation constraint, which is the sub-question I am least sure about.** Consider that agents rarely execute their own ex ante choices directly: they act through later selves, delegates, institutions, or algorithms that know more than the ex ante chooser did — say, which cell of a partition of the relevant states obtains. Suppose such an executor shares the agent's own conditional preferences given the cell and will not carry out an instruction it regards as conditionally suboptimal. Then an agent who violates the sure-thing principle cannot implement their ex ante optimal act through any partially informed executor without either paying for compliance, precommitting, or accepting sabotage. Does this add argumentative force that the standard sequential arguments lack, or does it merely redescribe them with extra machinery? Does it covertly assume what it needs to prove, for instance by helping itself to the claim that the executor's conditional preferences are the agent's own?

(f) **Exploitable but also helpable, and the authority of coherence.** If the agent's nonconformity permits a money pump, it also permits a reverse sequence in which a benefactor leads them through trades they endorse at each step to an outcome they prefer ex ante. Does that symmetry defuse the pragmatic argument? More generally: is demonstrated exploitability or incoherence a reason for the agent to revise their preferences at all, as opposed to a cost to be budgeted, insured against, or precommitted around?

(g) **The direction of repair.** Conditional on a coherence defect being successfully demonstrated, your credence that the rationally required repair is convergence on expected utility maximization, as opposed to abandoning reduction, adopting a dynamically consistent non-expected-utility framework with resolute choice, abandoning the modeling of agency in terms of synchronic preference orderings in favor of plans or policies, or something else.

Then: state what you would count as settling P in either direction — for example, what a valid derivation from suitably uncontested premises would have to look like, or what a characterization of an independence-violating agent immune to every structural objection would have to establish. Tell me which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and what one argument or result would move you furthest.
