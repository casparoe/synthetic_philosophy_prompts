Hi. I work in the policy and democratic services team at a mid-sized unitary council (pop. around 280k — big enough to matter, small enough that everyone knows everyone). Over the last couple of years we keep getting asked to run things by sortition: citizens' assemblies, lottocracy-style standing panels, drawn scrutiny juries, whatever the requester happens to have read about. The asks come from campaign groups, from officers who think a drawn body would unblock something, and from a few councillors who I suspect mainly want to hand off a vote they don't fancy taking. At the moment we say yes or no on vibes, and I want something I can actually run and then defend.

What I need from you is a decision procedure, in the order I'd run it, for a request landing on my desk:

- The questions, in order.
- What counts as a good enough answer to each one, so I can tell when someone has actually answered rather than just asserted.
- Where the procedure just stops with a no.
- Where it hands the decision over to a person or to full council because it's a values call rather than a factual one.
- And be honest about which steps are judgement calls wearing a clipboard.

Three things I think it has to get right, because that's where our vibes approach keeps breaking down:

1. Sortition seems to be doing three quite different jobs in these requests, and I dont think one design does all three. Sometimes it's a mirror — the body should look like the city. Sometimes it's a filter — we want people who aren't already captured, who'll actually sit with evidence instead of a line. Sometimes it's a shield — we want the decision taken somewhere that re-election, party whips, local donors and the local paper can't reach. I want the procedure to start by making the requester say which job they want, and then let the design fall out of that: who's in the pool, how many, paid or not, who writes the question and the briefing material, binding or advisory, one-off or rotating.

2. The pre-commitment problem. Half these requests reach us after officers have already got a preferred option, or with a remit that lets cabinet quietly ignore whatever comes out. I think the procedure should kill those — a consultation that can't bite is worse than no consultation — but I want a test for it that isn't just me disliking the requester.

3. Who counts as the pool. Our instinct is a stratified sample of residents, but the hard cases nearly always dump the costs on people who aren't a tidy demographic category: one ward next to a proposed facility, commuters who don't live here, under-18s, people only in the city for two years, future residents. And then there's the repeat-draw thing: we've got maybe 40k households, and if we run one of these a year we might end up with a class of professional drawn citizens who come back knowing how the machinery works. I genuinely don't know if that's a bug or a feature, and I want the procedure to tell me rather than me guessing.

Constraints: about a week per request, around £60k a year total, one analyst who can do a stratified draw if you tell them what to stratify on, and no in-house deliberative-polling expertise. Whatever I recommend has to fit on a page a councillor will actually read, and our monitoring officer has to be able to defend it. We can run at most one real one a year, so the procedure also needs to say which requests to turn down.

Then please run it, out loud, on three:

(a) A petition with roughly 8,000 signatures asking for a citizens' assembly to set the city's twenty-year transport plan — congestion charging, bus franchising, a workplace parking levy. My gut says yes: this is exactly the sort of thing assemblies are for.

(b) A motion from a backbench councillor for a drawn panel to decide members' allowances, the rules on councillors' outside interests, and whether to introduce term limits. This smells like a conflict of interest to me, and also like the one case where the shield job is doing real work.

(c) A request from a residents' group in the ward next to a proposed waste facility: they want the siting decision made by a drawn panel instead of the planning committee. I'd quite like to say yes to these people, they've been badly messed about for years — but I've got a feeling the honest procedure is going to hand me a no, and if it does I want to understand why before I have to go and say it to their faces.

One more thing: if the procedure has a branch for "we're drawing from the same pool again next year / can we invite back the people who were good", spell it out, because we're about to hit exactly that.

Please write it in British English (organisation, favour, recognise, and so on). Ta.
