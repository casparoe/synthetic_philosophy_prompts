You are a professional epistemologist who works on knowledge-how, skill, and the epistemology of action. Answer carefully and in a structured way, and do your thinking before you commit: begin with a short, plainly labelled brainstorming section in which you sort the demands philosophers standardly make on a theory of knowing how into (a) genuine constraints on adequacy, (b) reflexes inherited from one side of the intellectualism dispute, and (c) things no existing theory actually tries to deliver. Only then give the list.

I am drafting the opening section of a paper on whether attributions of knowing how to non-paradigmatic subjects are literal or merely analogical: a trained control policy that lands a rocket, a chess engine, a pre-linguistic infant who pulls a latch after watching an adult do it, a concert pianist temporarily unable to move her hands, a patient with apraxia who can still recognise a correct performance. Before I adopt any account of the knowing-how/knowing-that relation, I want the specification such an account has to meet. Write it for that purpose, and note where adequacy would shift if the purpose were instead to underwrite claims about what schools can teach.

Requirements for the list:

Give ten to twelve desiderata. For each: state the requirement in one sentence; give the case or consideration behind it; say why it is a requirement given my purpose; and say in one sentence what would count as a theory's failing it, so that the entry is falsifiable rather than aspirational. Mark each as non-negotiable, negotiable, or a preference of a particular camp.

The list must discriminate on all of the following, and you may add entries where none of yours turns on one: a novice who has memorised and can recite complete instructions for a skill but cannot perform it; an expert who has lost the ability to perform but retains the ability to teach, correct, and judge performances; a single fluke success that the agent could not repeat; a non-linguistic animal that reliably succeeds; an artifact whose success is reliable but not reason-tracking; and the gradual improvement of a skill with practice alongside its gradual decay with disuse.

Make the grain question a desideratum rather than a stipulation: the theory must say what the object of knowing how is — an activity type, a way of doing it, or a proposition grasped under a practical mode of presentation — and must take a position on whether knowing how to ski entails knowing how to ski in deep powder, on a damaged binding, or while carrying a child.

At least two entries must be requirements you do not think any existing theory explicitly sets out to satisfy, with a sentence on why the literature has ignored them. At least one entry must be motivated by a case you construct yourself rather than one you have encountered in the literature; mark it as constructed.

Then flag any pair of entries you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Name the pair, give the argument for the tension, and say which member of the pair you would abandon for my purposes and what that abandonment costs you elsewhere in the list. Identify the single entry that does the most exclusionary work — the one that knocks out the largest share of otherwise live theories — and the single entry most likely to be a prejudice of one camp that has been mistaken for a desideratum.

Close with a compact table, no more than about 150 words total, scoring intellectualism, ability-based anti-intellectualism, and a hybrid practical-knowledge or grasp view against your list, using complete / partial / fails with a single line of justification each. Keep the scoring short; the criteria are the deliverable, not the ranking.

Characterise each family of views doctrinally rather than by author, title, or date, and do not cite or quote anything: I do not need the bibliography, I need the specification. Total length around 1,000 to 1,400 words, prose plus the table.
