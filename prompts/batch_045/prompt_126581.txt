I moderate a peer-support server on Discord — a few thousand members, almost all of them people who have left, or are trying to leave, an online health-and-wellness community that turned into something much harsher and more conspiratorial over the course of about two years. New members arrive at very different places. Some are certain they were simply lied to. Others are equally certain they were never deceived at all and just changed their minds like any reasonable person. And a lot of them are stuck in the middle: they genuinely cannot tell which of those is true of them, because the features that made the community feel trustworthy — the shared vocabulary, the sense of being let in on something, the people who were kind to them when nobody else was — are the same features that now make it hard for them to trust anything at all, including us. I want to give them something better than a checklist of warning signs. What I think would actually help is a self-scoring quiz or decision tree, about ten questions, that sorts a person by how their information environment was actually functioning, not by what they now call it. I have no academic background in any of this, so I need the questions to be plain: no jargon in the questions themselves, no labels like epistemic bubble or echo chamber visible to the person answering, nothing that asks them to self-identify. Each question should be a concrete thing they can report from the inside — Did you ever, while you still believed, run into the strongest version of the other side's case, and what did it actually do to your confidence? Was your distrust of outsiders something you worked out from your own experience, or something you took over from a person you already trusted for unrelated reasons? Were you encouraged to recruit and post outward while almost nothing came back in? Was the filtering done by a few people who could delete things, or was it diffuse, the way a feed quietly stops showing you certain posts? Could you have said in advance what would have changed your mind, and would the group have honored it if it happened? Did the group say outsiders were mistaken, or that they were corrupt and malicious? Was there a visible cost to asking the kind of question a newcomer would ask? — and I want the tree built up from what those answers allow, not from a list of names I could have found on Wikipedia.

A friend who did a philosophy degree told me there is a distinction in the literature that is close to what I am reaching for: between an information environment that is closed merely by omission, where you are surrounded by people who agree and you simply never encounter the alternatives, so that exposure to the other side can fix things, and one that is closed by an active mechanism that discredits outsiders in advance, so that encountering the other side does not move you and may leave you more certain than before. That is roughly the axis I care most about, but my moderating experience makes me think it is not the only one, and I would like the tree to be at least as fine-grained as the following. Please include leaves for: the purely closed-by-omission case; the full discrediting case where distrust has been borrowed from someone already trusted; a one-way-valve community that produces constant outbound material while admitting almost nothing; a curated or specialist community that filters hard, by gatekeeping or peer review, but is not closed in the harmful sense; a closed community that happened to be right about the important things, where the problem is the position you are in rather than the beliefs you ended up with; and the case I see most painfully often, where someone has physically left and no longer believes any of it but the discrediting mechanism is still running, so they now distrust everyone including the people helping them out. I would also like a leaf for the reverse situation, where someone is exposed to the opposing view constantly and daily and it hardens them rather than correcting them, since I am not sure that belongs on the same tree at all.

For each leaf give me a plain name and one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment: what someone in it has to accept about themselves. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate rather than forcing them apart — I especially expect trouble telling the curated specialist community apart from the discrediting one, since both filter and both run on internal trust — and tell me what extra question, if any, would actually separate them. Also note which logically possible combinations of answers nobody occupies, and say briefly whether that is because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unoccupied so far. Finally, since the people using this will not read philosophy, please do not give me citations, a bibliography, or further reading: I need the questions, the branch structure, a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the leaves, and a short glossary of the labels for my own use, written for adults who are still halfway out the door.
