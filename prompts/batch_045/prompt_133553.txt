hey — I'm writing a position paper for a seminar on "collapse" as a concept (Bronze Age, Maya, Rapa Nui, the usual set of cases) and I want a consistency check on where I've landed before I present it. Roughly:

(1) Collapse is a real, measurable phenomenon, not just a label we slap on change we disapprove of — there are cases with a fast, large drop in sociopolitical complexity, and in principle you can measure it.

(2) Collapse is level-relative: you have to say which system you mean. The palace economy can collapse while the households that fed it go on pretty much as before, and both descriptions can be true at the same time.

(3) The best general mechanism is Tainter-style diminishing returns: complexity is a problem-solving strategy you pay for, and past some point an extra unit of it buys less than it costs, so shedding it is what a system does when the strategy stops paying.

(4) The "questioning collapse" crowd (McAnany, Yoffee and co.) are right that the people, languages and practices mostly continue — but that's a claim about continuity, not evidence against (1).

(5) The Rapa Nui story as Diamond tells it (self-inflicted ecocide, population crash before contact) is basically a bad parable: the deforestation happened, but the morality tale built on top of it doesn't survive scrutiny.

(6) Collapse is genuinely bad for the people living through it, and that's why it's worth studying and why it's something to worry about rather than an interesting case of reorganization.

(7) Something collapse-shaped is a live risk for industrial society, and the past cases give us at least some grip on the mechanisms, so there are lessons to draw.

(8) Methodological scruple: collapse is multicausal, and I don't want to moralize it or turn it into a story about decadence and bad choices.

Two places I suspect I'm in trouble but can't tell: (3) and (6) seem to pull apart — if shedding complexity is what a system does when the strategy stops paying, why should that be bad for the people inside it? And (5) together with (7): if the one case everyone uses as the overshoot parable is junk, is (7) left with nothing to stand on?

I also have this half-baked notion I want to use in the paper — call it a "complexity ratchet": past some threshold, the know-how needed to rebuild complexity is itself gone (writing drops out, the specialists are dead, the supply chains that fed them don't exist), so recovery isn't available on the timescale of a single lifetime. My idea is that the ratchet, not the drop in complexity as such, is what makes collapse bad. Can it do the work I need in (6) without sneaking back in the moralizing I banned in (8)?

Go through the eight and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and how badly, which only look like they are, and for the real ones what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me. Rank the tensions by severity, tell me which single revision cleans up the most, and if the ratchet is carrying more weight than it can bear, say so. Also flag if I have any of my two suspicions backwards — I'd rather find out now than in front of the seminar.
