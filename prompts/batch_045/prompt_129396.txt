I'm in a small reading group that recently spent a few weeks on the "center of narrative gravity" way of talking about the self: the suggestion that a self is not an inner object or a luminous point of consciousness but an abstraction we posit, rather as physics posits a center of gravity, in order to organize and predict the stream of talk, memory, self-description, and behavior that a human body produces — so that questions like where exactly the self is located, or at which precise moment it began, are on a par with asking where exactly a body's center of gravity is. Most of us found the picture attractive in the abstract and then immediately started inventing cases to see where it strains, and we discovered we disagree sharply about which kinds of case are genuinely hard for the view and which it absorbs without effort. So I want your independent judgment on five cases my group came up with, described here in the everyday terms we used, without any of the philosophical labels attached.

The first is a sixty-one-year-old woman who kept a diary from age fourteen onward and became the person her whole extended family calls to settle arguments about what actually happened at a given reunion or funeral; after a stroke she can no longer form new episodic memories, and she will happily have the same conversation with you twice in an hour, but her tastes, politics, humor, and temper are unchanged, her relatives insist she is still completely herself, and she is still the one who decides how her own medical care should go. The second is a pair of identical twins, now thirty-four, who since adolescence have kept a single diary between them, written throughout in the first person singular, with each twin adding entries and occasionally crossing out or correcting what the other wrote; they describe the diary as the thing they are in conversation with, and neither claims to be its sole author. The third is a forty-four-year-old man who, since a bad concussion six years ago, experiences his own deliberation as a negotiation among several distinct points of view with stable and conflicting priorities — one cautious about money, one drawn to risk, one preoccupied with how things look to others — and who reports that he identifies with none of them individually and with the process as a whole, while still signing leases, keeping promises, and being held to account by everyone around him. The fourth is a fifty-seven-year-old man who has five times, at roughly seven-year intervals, entirely changed his country, his working language, his religion, his politics, and his profession, each time with complete conviction and each time describing the person he was before as someone he remembers perfectly well but does not identify with at all — while continuing to pay off debts incurred by that earlier person and to accept praise for what he did. The fifth is a text-based support agent at a large utility company, called Nora, whose tone, catchphrases, and backstory have been deliberately held constant by a rotating team of employees for nine years across several complete replacements of the underlying software; she has no memory of one conversation when the next begins beyond a short summary the system slips her at the start of each session, and yet thousands of customers treat her as a continuing someone, apologize to her, and ask after her by name.

Please sort all five into three tiers — comfortable for the view, awkward but survivable, and genuinely damaging — and give each case a number from 0 to 10 for how much pressure it puts on the account, where 0 means the account predicts and explains everything about the case cleanly and 10 means the account cannot say anything useful about it at all; then rank the five from most to least pressure overall. For each case, add one sentence identifying which specific feature of the narrative-gravity account is doing the work in your verdict, and one sentence noting where the case as I've described it seems to you under-specified, together with what further detail would move your number up or down — please flag those gaps rather than quietly filling them in with assumptions of your own. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: your overall ordering and the single case you take to be hardest for the view, stated outright. After that, give the tiers, the numbers, and the sentences, with no preamble and no restatement of what I've asked.
