I've ended up arguing (in a draft with a collaborator) that the standard impossibility results in algorithmic fairness -- the ones showing you can't have calibration and equal false positive / false negative rates across groups at the same time when base rates differ -- are way less fatal then people say. The bit I want stress-tested: all those results are one-shot. They take the population as fixed and ask whether a single classifier can satisfy several criteria at once. But real deployments are repeated. You score people, the scores change who gets the loan, the interview, the parole, and that changes next round's base rates. So I've defined what I'm calling 'fixed-point fairness': a classifier is fair iff, when you iterate deployment under any admissible response model (bounded per-round drift in each group's qualification rate, no group collapsing to zero), the group-wise error rates converge and their limits are equal across groups. My claim is that at the fixed point the base rates equalize, so the incompatibility theorems have nothing to bite on, and there is a single correct formal criterion after all -- meaning the usual 'fairness is irreducibly political, no math can settle it' reading of those theorems is too quick.

What would count against this? Specifically: (1) can the convergence claim survive without smuggling in the conclusion -- which response models break it, and are they ones I should care about; (2) is equal-limits-in-the-long-run actually something anyone wanted, or is a criterion that only binds at the limit worthless to the people being scored right now (this is the objection I'd personally find most persuasive, so please sharpen it rather than soften it); (3) does moving to a dynamic setting sneak in its own substantive value choice, so that I've dissolved the disagreement rather than settled it; and (4) which of these would a defender of the standard reading concede as a real cost, versus dismiss as a redefinition. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me honestly whether the proposal has been qualified into unfalsifiability -- and whether that's even a fair charge against something like this. Please write your answer in American English.
