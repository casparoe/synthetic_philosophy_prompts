I co-teach an undergraduate seminar on virtue ethics, and this term we spent two sessions on the situationist critique — the family of arguments, built on the obedience experiments, the bystander studies, the seminary-student-in-a-hurry study, the dime-left-in-a-phone-booth study and their successors, which conclude that ordinary people lack the robust, cross-situationally stable character traits that virtue ethics needs. The students left with the impression that this is one blanket result that hits every virtue equally hard, and I think that is wrong, but I have not found a way to make the difference precise. So I want numbers, not discussion.

Below is a list of traits. For each, give me a score from 0 to 100 — call it a robustness score — meaning: taking the critique's empirical claims entirely at face value, and bracketing both the reply that appeals to practical wisdom and the reply that simply redefines the trait as a narrow, local one, how much confidence should a virtue ethicist retain that a typical adult in a contemporary society possesses this trait as a genuinely cross-situationally stable disposition? Then sort the traits into three or four tiers, and give one sentence per trait naming what drives its placement. I have two candidate explanations for why the scores should differ, and I would like you to use them as the spine of the ranking unless you think one of them is backwards, in which case say so and rank on better grounds: (1) opportunity density — a trait should be more stable across a life the more often, and the more variously, ordinary circumstances present occasions to exercise it, since frequent and varied practice is what consolidates anything into a disposition; (2) cue-concordance — a trait should be more stable when the situational cue that prompts it is reliably present on exactly the occasions when the trait is called for, as opposed to traits whose eliciting cue and whose occasion of application come apart.

The traits: truthfulness in speech; compassionate helping of a stranger in visible distress; physical courage under threat of harm; fidelity to costly promises; gratitude; humility about one's own accomplishments; temperance with everyday pleasures; fairness in dividing resources with strangers; loyalty to a friend when the association is costly; playful ease in conversation (the trait Aristotle discusses under the heading of wittiness); and punctuality, which is deliberately not on the classical list of virtues. Two further constraints. First, if you are tempted to give some trait a high score for a reason that should embarrass a virtue ethicist — because it is a social script or an unthinking habit rather than a disposition the agent has made her own — flag that explicitly instead of quietly awarding the points. Second, tell me honestly whether the finished ranking is measuring which traits are robust or merely which traits psychologists happened to run experiments on, and if there is a way to tell those two hypotheses apart, say what it is. I will put the tier list on the handout, so the one-sentence justifications matter as much as the numbers.
