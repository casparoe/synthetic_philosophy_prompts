I've been running a reading group on Pascal on justice and force for about two years and we are completely stuck, so I want a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right about Pascal.

Rough setup. In the loose papers published after his death as the Pensées (1670), he says things like: justice without force is helpless and force without justice is tyrannical, so you have to make the just strong or the strong just; that a river or a mountain range settles what counts as justice, truth on one side of the Pyrenees and error on the other; that people obey the law only because they take it to be just and eternal rather than merely established, which is why anyone founding a law does well to hide the fact that it was founded; that custom, not reason, is what makes a claim to authority stick, and that unsettling established customs is the reliable way to topple a state.

Half of us reads all that as straight skepticism about justice: there is no natural justice we can get at, what passes for right is local custom plus whatever force backs it, so the only sane posture is to obey what's established, maybe with an ironist's shrug. The other half reads him as an Augustinian: justice is real, sin has wrecked our capacity to recognise it, and the wild variation in human law is evidence of corruption rather than evidence that there's nothing there — the bleak inventory is meant to send you looking for justice somewhere other than in politics.

My hypothesis, which I'd rather you tested than humoured: the two camps are both half right because they're each answering one of three different questions and helping themselves to an answer on the other two. One: does justice exist as anything over and above human arrangement? Two: can we know it well enough to use it to criticise existing law? Three: what does the belief that the law is just actually do, and is anyone better off if that belief is taken away? I suspect Pascal goes yes, no, and it holds the state up so don't take it away — which makes the skeptic right about two, the Augustinian right about one, and both of them silent about three. There's even a stronger version of that third point I half believe: the justice material isn't a theory at all but a move, i.e. he's not telling us whether natural justice exists, he's trying to make the reader feel how unstable any merely human arrangement is so that the religious part lands.

So: is that right, and if it's wrong, what's the real disagreement underneath — about what 'justice' even means for him, about whether enough custom can turn an arbitrary arrangement into a just one, or about whether we should read a pile of unpublished and chopped-up fragments as a position in political philosophy at all rather than as material inside an argument for religion? And whatever you conclude, I want the bit about progress: is there a distinction, or a fact about how the fragments are ordered and classified in the different editions (the numbering is a nightmare and changes depending on which one you use), or something about what he could have read, that would actually move this? Or is it structured so that no amount of arguing will shift anyone?

Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no summing up: what kind of disagreement this is, the strongest evidence drawn from what each camp actually says, your verdict on my three-questions hypothesis including the speech-act version, anything genuinely textual or historical that could settle it, and what I should tell the group to try next.
