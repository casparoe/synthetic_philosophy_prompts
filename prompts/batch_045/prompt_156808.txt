I have been reading the vagueness literature for a few years now, and the standoff between epistemicism and supervaluationism has started to feel like the sorites itself: every step is small, each step looks compulsory, and yet you end up somewhere nobody meant to go. I do not want another survey of the two theories, and I do not want a verdict on which one is true. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse, argued rather than asserted.

Let me fix the exchange as I see it, so that the diagnosis has something to bite on.

Epistemicism: every vague predicate effects a perfectly sharp partition of its domain; borderline cases are cases where we do not, and in principle could not, know where the boundary lies; classical bivalence and classical logic survive intact; and the ignorance is meant to be explained rather than merely asserted — any candidate cut-off would be so close to neighbouring cases that a belief locating the boundary there could not be safe, hence could not be knowledge.

Supervaluationism: semantics proceeds via a space of admissible precisifications of the language; a sentence is true simpliciter just in case it is true on all of them; every classical tautology is thereby supertrue while bivalence fails; validity splits into local and global notions; and the determinacy operator is what lets the theory say that a sentence holds on every admissible sharpening.

The objections I keep seeing traded look to me entirely mutual. Supervaluationists charge that epistemicism buys classical logic at the cost of an unexplained primitive — a boundary fixed by nothing we can point to, unknowable in principle, doing no predictive work. Epistemicists reply that admissibility is no less a primitive, and that theirs is at least a primitive epistemologists already use. Epistemicists then charge that supervaluationism fails its own motivating problem, since the determinacy operator regenerates higher-order vagueness at every level. Supervaluationists reply that the regress is benign, or can be closed with a fixed-point construction, and that the epistemicist has no better account of why our concepts feel open-ended. Circling outside: penumbral connections, the report that a borderline case seems unfixed rather than merely unknown, and the patterns of hesitation and retraction in step-by-step sorites questioning — each camp claims these as data, and each camp has a retrofit ready.

My own hypothesis, which I want tested rather than flattered, has two halves. First: the dispute is not substantive at the level of predictions, because the theories are hermeneutically equivalent — for any supervaluationist model there is an epistemicist model reproducing every verdict about which sentences a competent speaker will accept, assert, or withhold, and conversely, with the admissibility relation over precisifications doing formally the same work as the safety-or-margin structure over candidate cut-offs. If that holds, what is left is a choice of ideology — which primitives are admitted into the semantics and which are exiled to the epistemology — settled by theoretical virtues rather than by cases. Second: the residual appearance of substance comes from the two camps answering different questions under one label, one asking what the truth conditions of vague discourse must be like for it to compose systematically, the other asking what our actual epistemic situation with respect to vague predicates is.

Three wrinkles I want handled rather than gestured at. (a) The bilingual case: a speaker fully competent in two languages whose corresponding vague predicates admit different sharpenings, so that the same object is determinate for her under one description and borderline under the other. What does each theory say, and is the case diagnostic at all, or does each theory contain a free parameter that absorbs it? (b) The step-by-step sorites with a required answer at each step, where speakers' switch points are stable within a session and unstable across sessions. Is this genuine evidence either theory is answerable to, or is it downstream of performance factors — anchoring, memory, conversational pressure — that neither theory constrains? (c) Whether either theory owes us an account of what speakers are doing when they say of a borderline case that there is no fact of the matter, and whether that report is data for the theory or a prediction of it.
