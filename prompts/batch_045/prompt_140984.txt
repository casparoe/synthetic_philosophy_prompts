My friend M from grad school has an opinion about every anthropic thought experiment anyone throws at her, and I've been collecting her verdicts for years because I want to guess what she'll say about a case I made up last week. Here's the track record:

- Sleeping Beauty, standard setup with the memory wipe: 1/2 for heads when she wakes up, on the grounds that she already knew she'd be awake at least once, so waking up isn't news.
- The incubator version, where a coin decides whether the machine creates one observer or two and you then find yourself in a room with no way to tell which happened: also 1/2 for heads, same reasoning.
- Doomsday argument: she buys it. Being somewhere around the sixtieth billionth human is real evidence that the total number of humans is nearer the low end of what we'd have thought otherwise, though she always adds that people oversell it because they forget the prior is doing half the work.
- The presumptuous philosopher setup, two cosmological theories that fit every telescope datum equally well but where one says the universe contains something like a trillion trillion times more observers than the other: no. She denies that our existing at all is a reason to prefer the crowded theory. Existing, she says, isn't the kind of thing that can be evidence.
- Boltzmann brains: she shrugs. If a cosmology implies that nearly every observer having exactly her experiences is a brief thermal fluctuation, she treats that as a reductio of the cosmology, not as a reason to think she's a fluctuation.
- And when I've asked her whether the doomsday calculation should be run over humans or over everything intelligent everywhere, she's never given me a straight answer, just "ask me when we meet some aliens."

The new case: suppose the observatories settle everything they can and two pictures are left, equally liked by the data and by our priors. Picture A: technological life is almost unimaginably rare, we are the only civilization there has ever been in our observable universe, and the total number of humans who will ever live is about a hundred billion. Picture B: the universe ends up hosting roughly a thousand trillion separate civilizations, each with about a hundred billion members, all permanently out of sight and out of contact with each other, so nothing we can observe tells A from B. The question is whether the bare fact that we find ourselves in *this* civilization, rather than one of the thousand trillion that B says exist, is itself evidence for A, something on the order of a thousand trillion to one.

What I want: your best guess at M's verdict, but mostly the two or three different rules that fit those six verdicts about equally well, and what each of them predicts here. As I see it, she's caught between sampling from everything that has her evidence, which I think does hand her the enormous update toward A but makes her anti-presumptuous-philosopher stance look strange, since she'd be letting observer counts count in one direction and not the other, and sampling only from a group she already knows herself to be in, humans, which gives no update at all and leaves it to the astronomers, but then her doomsday argument only works because she quietly fixed the reference class that way. There may be a third rule I'm missing where you're allowed to learn from your rank inside a population and never from the size of anything, and if so I want to know what it says here, because I genuinely can't tell whether "which civilization am I in" is a rank fact or a size fact. There's also the uncharitable possibility that she has no rule at all and just accepts the anthropic arguments whose conclusions she already liked.

Tell me which you'd bet on and why, and then name the single further case that would separate them. I've been toying with two: rerun B but make the aliens subjectively identical to us, same memories and experiences, so that from the inside you couldn't tell whether you were one of them, or else have us actually detect one alien civilization and learn it has 10^20 members, and then ask whether that dilutes her doomsday number for humans. I can't work out which is the sharper test, or whether there's a better one. And if none of it would change a single decision, say which of these rules would actually move something, like whether to spend money looking for aliens or on making sure we get through this century.
