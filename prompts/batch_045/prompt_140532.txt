I'm a high school senior, and I run my school's philosophy club. Our next session is fifty minutes, twelve people, and I've promised them something on panpsychism: the idea that experience, in some minimal or prototypical form, belongs to the basic constituents of things, and the notorious puzzle of how very many microscopic experiences are supposed to amount to the single unified stream of experience I take myself to have. I know that puzzle has a name, and I know the replies have names: constitutive micropsychism, cosmopsychism, panprotopsychism, brute emergence, illusionism, and so on. What I do not want is a handout that is a glossary of those names. I want a flowchart that sorts a person by what they are willing to say about cases, so that where they end up is a discovery rather than a choice of team.

Could you build me that flowchart? Specifics:

1. One entry node, phrased as the thing that gets you into the neighborhood at all, and at least one early exit leaf for someone who rejects it, since about half my club will. No node may simply ask whether consciousness is fundamental; every node should be a question about a case or about a specific claim.

2. Eight to ten internal branch nodes, two or three options each, phrased so that a person who has never heard any of the labels can answer. Ten to fourteen leaves. Each leaf should carry the usual label, or else a name you coin plus a note that you do not think anyone actually defends it; one sentence on the view; one sentence on its most awkward commitment; and one sentence answering whether a smartphone has any experience and why, because that is the question the room will actually fight about.

3. Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of the list of names. Pick the three questions that do the most sorting work, lay out the grid of answer combinations, mark which cells are occupied by something people really hold, and for each empty cell say whether it is empty because that combination is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to work it out.

4. Flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot distinguish, and name the further question that would separate them.

Here are the cases I want wired in. The last two are ones I invented, precisely because I could not find anything like them:

- The Fade: a living brain whose long-range connectivity is degraded by about one percent per day.
- The Substitute: the same brain with each neuron replaced, one at a time, by a functionally identical inorganic part.
- The Merge: two people whose cortices are surgically cross-wired, a little more each week, over roughly six months.
- The Three-Second Duplicate: a molecule-for-molecule copy of a person, run for three seconds, then switched off.
- The Chorus: a million minimal experiential systems wired in a chain, each receiving only the output of its predecessor, with nothing anywhere that integrates the whole.
- The Bonding Lens: a device I am simply stipulating into existence. Point it at any two subjects and it fuses their experiences into one, changing nothing physical at all. I want this case to force the question of whether combination is fixed by physical structure alone or is an extra relation laid on top of it.

The distinction I care most about, and the reason I am asking for a tree rather than an essay, is this: which of these views can actually explain why there is one subject here rather than a swarm, and which quietly give up on that and start doing something else instead? Please also tell me which single node does the most work, meaning which answer constrains a person's destination the most. And please do not point me to papers, articles, or reading lists; I am after the structure of the debate, not the sources.
