I'm giving a twenty-minute paper with ten minutes of questions at an interdisciplinary consciousness-science workshop next month, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view. My thesis is a revision of heterophenomenology rather than a rejection of it. The standard protocol advertises itself as ontologically neutral: collect subjects' reports, treat them as texts, and read them as telling you how things seem to the subject while withholding judgment about whether they are really that way — the reported world is all the data a science of consciousness needs, and the ontology can be settled later or not at all. I argue that this neutrality is unstable in exactly the cases that matter. Where a report is contested — people who say they have no mental imagery at all, patients who deny seeing in the blindsight paradigm while discriminating well above chance, subjects in the richness-of-perception disputes who insist they saw the whole scene in detail that no measure of their access supports — the heterophenomenologist must decide whether to treat the report as about experience, about a judgment, or about a disposition to speak, and that decision is made with whatever theory of report-production happens to be in the room. My fix, which I'll call second-order heterophenomenology, is to reconceive the heterophenomenological world as the content of the subject's own self-attribution system rather than as a rival ontology. On that reading, the gap between what a subject reports and what a first-order measure finds is not noise to be bracketed but the primary datum: it is information about metacognitive access, which is itself something a science of consciousness needs to explain. The upside I'll stress is that this version makes peace with disciplined first-person methods — descriptive experience sampling, micro-phenomenological elicitation interviews — instead of disqualifying them in advance, and it gives no-report and decoding work a principled account of what the report-based data were ever evidence of. The cost, which I mean to concede in the talk itself, is that the method is no longer ontologically neutral and buys its tractability by giving up any purchase on why there is experience at all.

The room will not be a philosophy seminar, and that matters for what I get asked. Roughly: two vision scientists who run no-report and multivariate decoding paradigms and think of reports as a nuisance variable; three experimental psychologists who live on confidence ratings, metacognitive sensitivity measures, and the literature on introspective confabulation; one neuroscientist with integrated-information-theory sympathies who holds that any method defined over reports has already abandoned the intrinsic and will hear my self-model talk as functionalist evasion; a neurophenomenologist and a Husserlian who will say that I have changed the subject and that training in bracketing is exactly what makes a report evidentially valuable in the first place; and, chairing the session, a card-carrying heterophenomenologist who thinks the instability charge is answered by the bracketing move itself and that my revision solves a problem the method never had. Give me the eight or so objections I should actually expect, ordered by likelihood times damage rather than by abstract importance, each phrased the way that person would phrase it in their own vocabulary, each with a ninety-second reply and an honest note on where the reply goes soft. Tell me which two objections are strong enough that I should concede them in the talk instead of being dragged to them in question time, and which of my replies you expect to genuinely land versus which are placeholders that would buy me thirty seconds and then collapse. I'd also like the question I'm hoping nobody asks; my own candidate is how my method could distinguish a subject with no imagery from a subject with imagery but no access to it, but if you think a different question is worse for me, say so instead.

Two more things. First, mark how confident you are in each predicted objection — I would rather have three you are sure of and five you are guessing at, labeled as such, than eight delivered in the same even tone. Second, push back where the framing deserves it: if my characterization of the standard protocol is a straw man, if defenders have a ready answer to the instability charge that I have not engaged with, or if the workshop audience I described would not in fact divide along these lines, say that plainly rather than producing a polite prep sheet around my assumptions. And if the honest verdict is that my revision collapses back into plain heterophenomenology with extra steps, or into straightforward eliminativism, tell me which one it collapses into and where in the argument the collapse happens.
