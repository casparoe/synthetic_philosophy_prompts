I cover science and ideas for a newspaper, and I've been unable to stop thinking about an argument I overheard at a dinner after a conference on medical prediction. Names and details below are changed, and the company is one I'll call Corvid Health, since the specifics aren't what I want adjudicated.

Corvid markets a prognostic engine for cardiology. Its selling point concerns what philosophers call the reference class problem: the risk figure for a particular patient depends on which set of past patients you compare her to, and the data don't select a set for you. Corvid's answer is what it calls "interval honesty." For any patient the engine scans every reference class that (a) contains the patient, (b) is defined using at most four of the available clinical variables, and (c) contains at least 250 historical patients, and it reports the lowest and highest risk across those classes. For one patient at the centre of the dinner conversation — a woman in her early sixties with a moderately uncommon genotype — it returned 8 per cent to 41 per cent five-year risk. Her insurer's rule is to fund the procedure above 30 per cent, and it denied coverage on the strength of the top of the interval. Her cardiologist objected, and the two people at my end of the table took it from there.

(1) He said the interval is not candour but laundering. Reference classes are partially ordered, not ranked along one dimension, so "every admissible class" is not a determinate set: whether age is recorded in years or months, how finely diagnoses are coded, which labs happen to be stored, all change the endpoints. And because classes get thinner as you add variables, loosening the four-variable cap inflates the interval toward zero and one. The 250-patient floor and the cap are administrative conventions dressed up as epistemology.

(2) She replied that he was litigating metaphysics when the live question is decision. For a threshold decision what you need is a score that is calibrated — that when it says 20 per cent, about a fifth of such patients have the event — and calibration is checkable, and there are now training procedures that aim for calibration simultaneously within every subgroup an auditor can define, so overlapping classes are no longer in tension. The demand that there be one true probability for this individual, she said, is an artifact of a frequentist picture in which probabilities just are frequencies in a class.

(3) He answered that calibration is itself relative to a chosen family of classes, so the problem relocates rather than dissolves. A predictor that assigns every patient the base rate for her group is perfectly calibrated within that group and tells you nothing about her. Worse, two predictors can both pass every calibration check an auditor happens to run and still assign this patient different numbers; nothing in the calibration criterion picks between them.

(4) She conceded that calibration is necessary and not sufficient, but said that is exactly why it is paired with requirements that the score extract all the available signal, and — more to the point — that the insurer's misuse is not a failure of probability theory at all. The abuse is that the payer was allowed to choose the convenient endpoint after seeing it. A decision rule has to be fixed in advance, and a wide interval should trigger collecting more information about this patient, not authorise whichever bound is cheaper.

(5) He then widened the frame: there is a version of the problem where narrowing cannot save you, because the individual is yourself and the candidate classes are things like all humans, all humans alive this century, or all agents in technologically developed civilisations. There you cannot inspect the class from outside, and the choice of class changes the answer qualitatively rather than trading bias against variance. He said this is the same disease.

(6) She said it is a different disease: there the difficulty is indexical — which observers get to count as evidence — and importing it into an actuarial tool is a category error. Even so, its remedy has the same shape as the clinical one: state the class explicitly and test how much the conclusion depends on it. He shot back that "be explicit and run a sensitivity analysis" is laboratory hygiene, not a solution, and that Corvid's product is deceptive in both directions: a single number hides the choice, while the interval hides it behind spurious precision about its own endpoints.

I said nothing at the time because I couldn't sort it out. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six numbered moves, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or wrong, and why, flagging any move that rests on a claim that is simply false. Then say who is more right overall, and what both of them are missing. In particular I'd like your view on whether calibration-based criteria really do dissolve the reference class problem or merely relocate it, and on whether there is a principled answer — as opposed to a regulatory one — to what a clinician ought to be told about a patient when the admissible reference classes disagree.
