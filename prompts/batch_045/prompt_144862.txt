For my graduate seminar on rational choice I need one document, and I need it to be a document that two particular people could actually put their names to: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a causal decision theorist — someone who scores acts by their expected utility computed from the counterfactual probabilities of causally independent states, and who therefore refuses to conditionalize on her own act — and an evidential decision theorist — someone who scores acts by expected utility computed from probabilities of states conditional on the act. Both have argued about the "Death in Damascus" case for a decade and have, I think, started talking past each other. I have enriched the case so that they cannot both retreat to the observation that the acts come out on a par.

The case. Death has gone to one of two cities, Damascus or Aleppo, and you die at dawn if and only if you are in the same city as he is. You are in Damascus now. Death's destination was fixed yesterday by a dossier method that predicts your unaided choice with 80 percent accuracy, and its accuracy does not vary with which choice you make: whether you stay or go, the chance that the dossier names the city you end up in is 0.8. Conditional on your unaided act, that accuracy is the whole story — no further sign about you carries any additional information about what the dossier says.

You do, however, have one such sign before you act: a familiar presentiment, the "itch," which you know from long experience to be a 90 percent reliable indicator of what you will end up doing when you make up your own mind. Right now the itch says: flee. The numbers are meant to be consistent, and they fix your current credence that the dossier names Aleppo at 0.74, hence your credence that it names Damascus at 0.26.

There are four options: (i) stay in Damascus; (ii) go to Aleppo; (iii) delegate — let a fair coin, which the dossier could not have anticipated, choose your city, where delegating leaves your credence that the dossier names Aleppo unchanged; (iv) take a pill first, at negligible cost, that erases the presentiment, and then make up your own mind, in which case your chance of fleeing is one-half.

Both signatories accept the probability model above. Neither has reneged on her theory. What I want from you is the document they should have produced, in four parts.

One. A numbered section of agreed claims — claims both can sign and that are not vacuous. I expect it to cover at least: whether Death's location is causally independent of what you now do; why this case is not Newcomb's problem, and specifically why nothing you might have arranged in advance — no pre-commitment, no self-training, no trait of character — buys you anything here, whereas there the entire dispute was about whether it does; the act-conditional symmetry, on which staying and fleeing each carry a 0.2 chance of survival while your unconditional credence that Death is in Aleppo is 0.74; what the long-run survival frequency would be for agents who have the itch and who follow each of the four options; whether the coin and the pill are genuinely available acts; and whether there is any standpoint from which some available act does better than a 0.5 chance of survival.

Two. A disagreements table. Rows are the claims on which they still differ; columns are the causalist's own formulation of her position, the evidentialist's own formulation of hers, and your verdict on whether that row is a substantive disagreement or a disagreement about words. Each side's formulation must be one that side would accept as fair to it — no straw formulations, and no formulations so thin that they say nothing.

Three. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of the two. For each crux, give a concrete case, statistic, or argument that both would accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction about that case in advance. I have in mind at least these four: (a) whether the itch is admissible as evidence about a fixed state when part of its evidential force comes from being evidence about your own act — that is, whether an agent may deliberate with, and then deliberately act against, a sign whose content is that she will do the other thing; (b) whether the quantity to be maximized is an act's expected utility by the agent's lights at the moment of action, or the long-run frequency of survival for agents who follow the policy the act instantiates, given that here those two quantities come apart; (c) what the pill option shows — whether the causalist's 0.74 is a reason for staying or an artifact of evidence she could have destroyed at no cost; (d) whether delegation to a coin is the sort of thing a decision theory may recommend, and what it would take for the dossier to have anticipated it.

Four. A short closing section in your own voice: what, in your judgment, is genuinely at stake between them, and which part of the dispute you take to be verbal, and why.

Two constraints. First, derive each side's verdict from the numbers rather than assuming you know in advance which theory says what, and do not let the document end on the note that nothing the agent does matters — if that is where one side ends up, say what follows from it. Second, no citations, no reading lists, no appeal to what is in the literature: the arguments should stand entirely on the case as specified above. Aim for 1,200 to 1,600 words, prose and table together.
