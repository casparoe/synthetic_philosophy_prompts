I've been helping out with a citizen-science SETI project for a couple of years — the kind where a survey hands you a pile of candidate signals and you flag the ones that look like they might be artificial. I've gotten stuck on something and I can't tell whether it's a real problem or just me missing a piece.

Here's the thing. Everyone in and around the field talks as if the silence is this enormous puzzle — where is everybody, the galaxy should be crawling with them, and so on. But the actual search procedure looks built in a way that would miss almost any civilization that isn't both a lot like us and a lot louder than us:

- the basic artifact test is something like "no known natural process makes a signal this narrow in frequency or this regular in time," so a technology gets inferred from the shape of the signal, without anybody decoding any content;
- a candidate doesn't count until it's been re-observed, ideally by a different telescope, which means it only counts if the source is still transmitting in our direction years later. The famous one-off signal from the 70s apparently never repeated and so never counted as a detection, even though nobody ever produced a natural or human explanation for it either;
- whenever someone points out that we've found nothing, the standard reply is that we've barely looked — something about having searched a glass of water's worth of the whole ocean — which sounds like it's saying the null result tells us nothing. But then why was the silence supposed to be surprising in the first place?
- and the targets get picked because they're like us: Sun-ish stars, frequencies near the hydrogen line, that kind of thing.

So: what does this package of practices actually presuppose? I don't mean the boring answer of "it assumes aliens use radio," though maybe that's part of it. I mean: what's the implicit theory of what makes something evidence of a mind, and what's the implicit theory of what a null result means? Does the re-observation rule secretly presuppose that civilizations are long-lived and persistent — which is roughly the thing whose absence is supposed to be the puzzle — or can it be defended as just being careful about false alarms from satellites and radar, without any view about aliens at all? Is it even coherent to keep saying the silence is baffling while running a search that's only sensitive to one narrow kind of civilization? And be honest about which parts of the protocol are doing real philosophical work and which are just engineering convenience.

The second half is where I actually want you to go. Our group has started trialing what we call a one-shot rule: if a single unrepeated burst has enough internal structure that you'd never expect it from anything in the catalogue of known natural sources — a pulse train whose gaps encode the first hundred primes, say, or a signal who's frequency drifts exactly the way a transmitter sitting on a rotating planet would — we log it as a technosignature candidate even if we never see it again, on the grounds that the odds of a natural source producing that pattern are tiny. Half of us think this is obviously right and half think it's a god-of-the-gaps machine, because the first pulsars would have passed a weaker version of the same test and they turned out to be rotating neutron stars.

What does adopting the one-shot rule commit you to that the re-observation rule didn't? Is it just a different empirical bet about aliens, or a different view of what evidence is — trading "we can go check again" for "we can't think of anything else that does this"? If we take it seriously, do we also owe it to ourselves to treat non-radio anomalies (a star that dims in a way nobody can explain, that sort of thing) as technosignature candidates, or is there a principled place to draw the line? And where is the whole practice genuinely underdetermined — where would two quite different theories of evidence, or of intelligence, license exactly the same search protocol, so that nothing we do settles which one is right?

One last asymmetry I can't place: nobody minds listening, but people get genuinely angry about transmitting, and treat it as something that should be decided collectively rather than by whoever owns the dish — while a detection on its own is treated as settling the question even though, at these distances, we'd never get to say anything back. Whatever that asymmetry presupposes about who the agent is supposed to be, I'd like it included in the audit.
