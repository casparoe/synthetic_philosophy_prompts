Some context. I'm a third-year PhD student finishing a dissertation chapter on what the Knobe asymmetry — the chairman who starts a new program knowing it will help or harm the environment and says he doesn't care about the environment, only about profits — actually shows about the concept of intentional action. A referee on an earlier paper said my overall package is internally unstable without saying where, and I can't tell which bit is doing the damage. Alongside the published findings I've run three small pilots of my own on Prolific (n ≈ 120 per cell, US-only, one attention check, pre-registered direction but not the analysis), and I've been arguing from them in reading groups, which is how I noticed I don't have a clean story. Here, as best I can state it, is what I believe:

1. The asymmetry is a real, competence-level effect on judgments about intentional action, not an artifact: not an artifact of between-subjects design, and not an artifact of what it is conversationally natural to say in answer to a question. I take the fact that people with philosophical training show it too as confirmation that it isn't a shallow confusion.

2. The mechanism is broadly normative rather than specifically moral or affective. What moves the verdict, I claim, is that the agent's indifference to a foreseen harm is itself a failure to act on a norm that applied to him, whereas his indifference to a foreseen benefit is not. So the asymmetry is about norms and reasons, not about dislike, blame, or emotional blame-validation. I've argued this in print and it is load-bearing for the chapter.

3. For the help case in particular I lean on something like a skill-and-awareness story: participants deny that he intentionally helped the environment because producing that benefit required no skill or effort beyond what starting the program already involved, so there is nothing in his performance for the intentionality judgment to latch onto — unlike the harm case, where he brings the harm about by exercising skill and awareness in guiding the program.

4. The upshot I draw is debunking: once you see that verdicts track normative appraisal rather than descriptive features of the action, action theorists should stop using intuitions about what was done intentionally as evidence on either side of their disputes.

5. But I am not a revisionist about the target. I think the theory of action is in the business of giving an account of our notion of intentional action — the one at work in ordinary explanation, in moral appraisal, and in criminal law — and I don't want to end up stipulating a technical notion and saying "never mind the ordinary one."

6. Pilot A ("hard helping"): the agent spends months carefully calibrating the program so that it produces the environmental benefit, foresees it, and brings it about by exercising real skill — but does it purely for the tax credits and is indifferent to the environment as an end. Participants still deny that he intentionally helped the environment, at roughly the original rate.

7. Pilot B ("conventional norm"): the foreseen side effect violates a purely conventional rule with no moral content — a local ordinance nobody thinks it is wrong to break, they just note that it exists. The asymmetry shows up at roughly the size it does in the environmental case.

8. Pilot C ("within subjects"): when participants see both versions, the asymmetry shrinks by about half but does not disappear. A colleague says this shows the whole thing was conversational after all. I think that is too strong, but I can't say exactly why.

9. I am skeptical that the effect generalizes to epistemic predicates: I have never been convinced by reports that people withhold "knew" in the help version of the CEO case, and if it does replicate I assume it has a different explanation, something about evidence or stakes. This matters to me because I want my claim to be about the concept of intentional action, not about a global moral contamination of folk psychology.
