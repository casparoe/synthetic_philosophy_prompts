I'm writing a short piece for a general-readership philosophy magazine on whether individual agents can have duties in collective-harm cases where their own act makes no discernible difference — flying, buying meat, voting in a safe seat. After a long series of arguments with a friend who is a fairly hard-nosed act-consequentialist, I've ended up defending a package I'll call the interchangeability view, and before I commit it to print I want to know how exposed it actually is. The view has three moving parts. First, the membership claim: an act can be pro tanto wrong not because of what it causes but because it is an interchangeable instance of the kind of act whose collective performance constitutes the harm — my litre of effluent is substitutable for yours, the flood is constituted by all of them together, and the fact that no single litre is pivotal is therefore beside the point. Second, a no-free-riding condition: I cannot excuse my instance by observing that enough others will perform theirs anyway, since that excuse is available to everyone, and a consideration that cannot be universally accepted without destroying the practice it is meant to excuse is not the right kind of consideration. Third, a difference-making backstop for anyone who refuses the membership claim: expected difference is rarely zero, because in real supply chains and electorates I never know whether I am sitting just below a threshold — call this the grain problem — and because my acting is evidence about what others relevantly like me will do, which multiplies the expected difference by something like the size of the cluster I am correlated with. I also add three limiting conditions to stop the view running away: the practice must be one I could exit at modest cost, the harm must be one the practice constitutes rather than merely risks, and acting on the reason must not crowd out more effective collective action.

What I want from you is a map of the view's exposure, not a survey of the debate. Lay out what would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a defender of the interchangeability view would themselves concede it as counting against them or would dismiss it as resting on a rival assumption — I care much more about the concessions than about what a critic would say from outside. Please deal with at least the following, and add anything I've missed. (i) The disjunction worry: my two positive grounds look like they cover disjoint territory — membership does the work in the cases where expected difference is provably zero (a reservoir whose outflow is quantised, so that most individual litres make no difference at all and can't be said to raise a probability), while the backstop does the work in the ordinary cases — so the package may just be two weaker views bolted together. Is that ad hoc, or is it what honest moral pluralism looks like, and what breaks if you delete either half? (ii) Overgeneration: give me the best case you can in which membership and no-free-riding are both satisfied and yet blame seems intuitively absent. I have two candidates I can't get a grip on: the waste-not case, where the meat is already bought and will be binned uneaten if I abstain, so the harm looks fully constituted by someone else's act and my eating is a free instance; and the legitimation case, where the practice is one we have all agreed to be bound by, as in voting. (iii) The empirical question: suppose we learned that individual consumer choices really do have no effect at any margin — no batching in restocking, no recorded demand signals, no thresholds anywhere in the chain. Would that be decisive against the backstop and merely awkward for the membership claim, or the other way round? And, symmetrically, would evidence that such choices do ripple count as support for the view, or is the view's claim meant to be independent of how the causal facts turn out? (iv) Insulation: I'm worried my three limiting conditions have made the view unfalsifiable, since any counterexample can be excluded by saying the exit costs weren't modest enough or that the practice merely risked the harm. Tell me whether that is a real problem for a normative view in a way it wouldn't be for an empirical one, and what I would have to see in order to think the insulation was principled rather than evasive. (v) The crowding-out evidence: does it count against the view's wrongness claim, against its advice about what to do, or only against its implications for what we should urge on others? I'd rate as most persuasive either a case in which all the conditions are met and blame is still intuitively absent, or a demonstration that my two positive grounds pull against each other rather than reinforcing.

Please write in British English, keep it to roughly nine hundred to a thousand words, and reason from the structure of the cases and from the informal decision theory rather than from published studies or citations, which I know you can't look up — I'd rather have your own reconstruction of what is at stake than a report on what has been argued.
