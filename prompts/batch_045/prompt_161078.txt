Hey — pretend you're a political philosopher who also does game-theoretic work on social norms: precise about mechanisms, not precious about jargon. Please think it through properly and brainstorm three or four candidate principles before you settle on one; I want to see the ones you reject and why.

My partner and I keep arguing about how much rule-following a society should demand — the whole tight vs. loose cultures thing — and going case by case I've definately got strong gut verdicts that I can't get into one shape.

Jaywalking at 2am on a dead-empty street: tolerate it, don't even tut at it. Queue-jumping at a packed bus stop: sanction it, hard. Not tipping: shouldn't be expected at all, and I resent that it is. Loud music after 11pm in a block of flats: enforce. Restaurant kitchen staff skipping handwashing: enforce to the hilt, even though literally no customer sees it. Indoor masking during a respiratory surge: enforce, but exempt small kids. Dress code for back-end engineers who never meet a client: don't enforce; for staff on the shop floor: enforce mildly. Doing 10 over on an empty motorway: tolerate. Missing or sloppy citation in a student essay, where nobody is harmed and nothing is really stolen: still punish, and I feel weirdly strongly about this. Dog off-leash in a crowded park: enforce. Bins out on the wrong day, lawn too long: don't enforce, and I actively dislike the kind of person who does.

The pair I can't reconcile is the speeding one and the citation one — both are harmless-in-the-instance, visible, low-stakes violations, and I want them treated completely differently — plus I want the invisible handwashing rule enforced most strictly of all, which seems to wreck any "enforce what you can see" idea.

My tentative principle: a norm should be tight just in case violations impose costs on people who didn't choose the interaction and can't cheaply avoid it, and the costs are big enough to matter; otherwise loose. That gets part of the list and dies on the rest.

What I want back: (a) that principle tested case by case and then repaired or replaced — state the version you'd actually defend precisely, and say which of my cases motivates each clause; (b) its verdicts on three cases I haven't listed: mandatory cameras-on for internal remote meetings, households required to clear snow off the pavement in front of their house, and using your phone in a hospital waiting room; (c) an honest list of which of my verdicts your principle can't save, and which one you'd overturn.

Two half-baked ideas of mine I want you to either build in or kill: (1) "bystander legibility" — a rule can be tight only if the people expected to do the enforcing can tell violation from compliance without an investigation or a hunch, because otherwise enforcement turns into suspicion and harassment (but then the handwashing case needs handling: maybe legibility constrains informal neighbour-to-neighbour enforcement and not institutional enforcement?); (2) "sorting drift" — the longer a rule survives past the conditions that made it useful, the more its enforcement selects for loyalty and display rather than compliance, so old rules should default to loose unless someone re-justifies them.

Be concrete, skip the literature and any citations, and don't just lay out both sides — give me the principle you'd defend.
