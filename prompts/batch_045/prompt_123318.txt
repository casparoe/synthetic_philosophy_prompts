I write about cognitive science for a newspaper, and I'm working on a piece about linguistic relativity. Rather than rehearse the debate, I want to know which version of the claim my own reactions commit me to. So here are my verdicts on six vignettes; the fieldwork in each is stipulated, and I'd like it taken as given.

(i) A community whose language has three basic colour terms — light, dark, and a warm term covering reds, oranges and yellows — discriminates and recognises chip boundaries around our blue/green divide about as finely as English speakers do. My verdict: no effect worth the name. Whatever language does, it does not repaint the visual field.

(ii) A community with no exact number words beyond one, two and many. Speakers match seven objects to seven objects when both arrays sit still in front of them, but fail when one array is hidden, rearranged, or presented sequentially. My verdict: a genuine cognitive limitation, and language is part of the explanation.

(iii) A community with no left/right vocabulary, which specifies all positions in absolute terms and can do so only because speakers track orientation constantly. They point accurately and navigate unfamiliar terrain where English speakers are lost. My verdict: real, but I read it as a capacity kept in training rather than one language installed — which sits awkwardly with (ii), where the absence of words clearly cost something. Poverty of vocabulary seems to hurt; richness doesn't seem to add.

(iv) A language with obligatory grammatical gender: speakers describe a noun the way its gender would suggest and misremember which adjective was paired with it. My verdict: a real effect but a parlour trick — a response bias in a verbal task, not a difference in thought.

(v) A language with obligatory evidential marking, where every statement must be coded as seen, heard, or inferred. Speakers are markedly better at recalling the source of a belief and at not relaying hearsay as fact. My verdict: this is the real thing, language shaping thought — even though structurally it is the same situation as (iv), an obligatory distinction English lacks.

(vi) The one I go back and forth on: a language with no spatial vocabulary for time — no long or short meetings, no looking forward or back, no front/behind axis for past and future, only order and duration of events. Speakers plan, estimate durations, and gesture about time much as English speakers do. My verdict: no effect. But if metaphor is supposed to scaffold abstract thought, I can't say why (ii) shows a deficit and this doesn't.

Is there a principle that returns all six verdicts — one that says when language is constitutive of a representation and when it merely biases a verbal response, and why the absence of a resource can impoverish while its presence only ever trains? I'd like it stated precisely, clause by clause with the vignette motivating each clause; I'd like to know which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would have me overturn; and I'd like it applied to at least one language type I haven't listed, so I can tell whether the piece I'm writing is really about thought or only about attention and practice.
