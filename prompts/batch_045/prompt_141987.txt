I direct a small technical unit in a national geological survey. Several times a year we publish single-number probability statements that are then used downstream by regional planners doing expected-cost arithmetic, and increasingly for one-off go/no-go decisions on single projects that will never be repeated: a levee to be raised or not, a town to be relocated or not. Examples: "0.22 probability that the Kestrel fault segment produces a magnitude 7 or greater rupture in the next thirty years"; "0.04 probability per year that the Alder Valley levee system fails."

Our numbers come from three quite different places. Some come from long-run relative frequencies in a reference class — paleoseismic records of past ruptures, or component-failure databases for levee systems. Some come from physical models that assign a single-case tendency to a particular set-up: a stress-transfer model of this specific fault, a hydrological model of this specific valley. Some come from structured expert judgment, elicited and then revised as new data arrive. We have also found that some of our frequency-based statements move by a factor of two depending on how narrowly the reference class is drawn — all faults in the basin versus faults of comparable slip rate and geometry.

A new oversight board has now ruled that every number we publish must be traceable: we must be able to say what kind of support it has, and we may not silently average or mix supports of different kinds, because the planners downstream treat our number as something to bet with, which is not the same thing as a frequency and not the same thing as a physical tendency.

Please write a procedure my reviewers can run on a candidate statement before we sign off on it. It should consist of: an ordered set of questions; for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer drawn from our own files and modelling reports; the points at which the procedure returns "do not publish a number" or "publish only as an interval or qualitative band"; and the points at which it must hand the decision back to a person because the issue is a judgment call rather than a checkable one. Ground it in what the three interpretations actually say — the frequentist reading of probability as long-run relative frequency in a fixed reference class, the subjectivist or Bayesian reading as degree of belief constrained by coherence and revised by conditioning, and the propensity reading as the objective single-case tendency of a particular set-up — and in the fact that these are not three rival estimates of one quantity so much as three different things a probability claim can be.

Constraints: my reviewers have roughly forty minutes per statement and no formal training in the philosophy of probability, so each step must say what to look for in material we actually have; the procedure must be strict enough that two reviewers reach the same verdict on the same file.

Then apply it to three cases. (1) The Kestrel fault statement above, where we have six hundred years of paleoseismic records but the segment is now thought to be in a stress state not represented anywhere in that record, and where the reference class choice gives 0.22 on one drawing and 0.11 on another. (2) A proposed statement about a first-of-a-kind carbon-capture containment vault: no failure history at all, a physics model giving a 0.02 failure probability over fifty years, and an expert panel giving 0.05 to 0.15. (3) A statement about whether a named glacier will "collapse" within the decade, where there is no agreed threshold for collapse and different teams' figures range from 0.1 to 0.6 accordingly. At least one of these should come out as an answer the board will not like.

Close with a short section, headed "What this procedure cannot do," that says plainly how the procedure should treat the gap between a number supported as a frequency or a physical propensity and the confidence a planner is entitled to place in a single non-repeated project, and that states which of the reference-class and threshold difficulties are managed rather than solved.

Format the answer as numbered procedural steps, with the three cases in clearly labelled subsections and the closing section under the heading above. Hard limit: 1,100 words, counting the cases and the closing section. Do not cite, quote, or attribute any philosophers or published works; the reasoning should stand on its own.
