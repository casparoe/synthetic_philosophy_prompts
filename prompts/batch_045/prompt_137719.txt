In W. E. B. Du Bois’s 1926 essay “Criteria of Negro Art,” he writes, “I do not care a damn for any art that is not used for propaganda.” The line is usually heard as a blunt repudiation of the slogan “art for art’s sake,” and I recently heard it invoked at a symposium on race and aesthetics in a way I could not reconstruct clearly enough to evaluate.

The dispute was about a commissioning policy for a new Black arts center. The center’s draft mission said it would support work that takes a side in the struggle for Black freedom. An artist proposed a room-scale installation consisting entirely of ambient sound—processed subway noise, breath, fragments of gospel radio—and she explicitly said the work was not about race and was not trying to say anything. A board member argued that Du Bois’s line requires the center to reject the piece: if it is not being used for propaganda, then on Du Bois’s view it is not the kind of art the center should support, or perhaps not art that matters at all. Another speaker replied that the board member had it backwards: read properly, Du Bois’s line means that under conditions of racial domination no artwork is neutral, so the installation is already taking a side simply by shaping how a public hears and feels, regardless of the artist’s stated intentions. At that point the Du Bois line was quoted as if it settled the matter.

I would like a menu of the precise claims this one-liner could be making, not a single decision in favor of one reading. Please separate out claims about:

1. An artwork’s directive content—the propositional or prescriptive political message it encodes, if any.
2. An artwork’s ambient uptake—the effects it has on the affective, perceptual, or social orientation of a public, independent of any intended message.
3. An artist’s duty to make sure that ambient uptake serves the cause of Black freedom.
4. The relation between propagandistic efficacy and artistic or aesthetic value.
5. Whether the claim is really that all art is in fact propaganda, or instead that all art ought to be used as propaganda.

For each reading, say whether it is close to a platitude, whether it is a substantive and defensible thesis, and whether it is false or confused. Then say which of these readings would actually support the board member’s attempt to exclude the installation, and which would support the other speaker’s claim that the installation is already propaganda. Finally, indicate which reading is most plausibly the one Du Bois himself was expressing in the essay, and be explicit about how much of that attribution is settled among people who know Du Bois and African American political thought versus how much remains contested. Do not present a contested reading as the consensus, and do not give me citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I only want the conceptual map and the verdicts.
