I am marking a problem set for an upper-level course on rational choice and its limits, and I would like an independent read before I finalise the marks. Grade only against the question as set below; no outside sources are needed.

**The question** (20 marks: part (i) 6, part (ii) 8, part (iii) 6)

> **The dollar auction.** An auctioneer offers a single dollar bill on the following terms. Bidding is open and ascending in five-cent increments; the highest bidder receives the dollar and pays their final bid; the runner-up pays their final bid as well and receives nothing. There are two bidders. Both are risk-neutral expected-payoff maximisers, the rules and the bidders' rationality are common knowledge, and neither bidder faces any budget constraint.
>
> (i) Explain how play can escalate past a dollar bid for a one-dollar prize even though both bidders are fully rational and fully forward-looking. *(6)*
>
> (ii) Now consider a sealed, one-shot variant: the same two bidders simultaneously submit a single bid each, of any amount in dollars; the higher bid wins the dollar; and both bidders pay their own bids whatever the outcome. Characterise a symmetric mixed-strategy equilibrium of this variant, compute the auctioneer's expected revenue, and explain how the outcome differs from that of the open, ascending version. *(8)*
>
> (iii) The organisational literature on escalation of commitment reports that decision-makers who have themselves authorised an initial investment in a failing course of action subsequently commit more resources to it than decision-makers who inherited the same failing project. Is the dollar auction simply a laboratory model of that phenomenon? Discuss. *(6)*

**The student's answer**

> **(i)** Suppose I have bid 95c and my rival has just bid $1.00. If I stop now I pay my 95c and receive nothing. If I bid $1.05 I pay $1.05 and receive the dollar, a net loss of 5c — provided she then stops. So one more bid converts a certain 95c loss into a 5c loss, and it is worth making whenever she will quit after it. Exactly the same calculation confronts her one move later, and again after that. The general point is that continuing rather than stopping is worth exactly one dollar minus the size of the increment I must add to my own last bid, which is always positive; so each increment improves my position on the assumption that this bid ends the contest. Crucially, the calculation is forward-looking in the strict sense: what I have already bid is not merely spent, it is forfeited if I stop, so it is genuinely at stake in my decision. This is why no irrationality has to be postulated anywhere along the path — the escalation is just the accumulation of locally rational steps. If there is a mistake anywhere in the open game it must lie in the decision to enter at all, not in any subsequent bid; and since an opening bid of 5c nets 95c if the other player declines to bid, entering looks defensible as well.
>
> **(ii)** In the sealed variant, bidding more than a dollar is strictly dominated: the most a bid can win is one dollar, so any bid above it yields a negative payoff for certain. There is a symmetric mixed-strategy equilibrium in which each bidder bids uniformly on the interval from zero to one dollar. If I bid *b*, I win whenever my rival bids less than *b*, which happens with probability *b*, so my expected payoff is 1·*b* − *b* = 0 for every *b* in the support; that flatness is exactly what indifference in a mixed equilibrium requires, and bids above a dollar do worse than zero. Expected revenue is the sum of the two bids, 50c + 50c = $1, so the auctioneer takes in the full value of the prize. Each bidder's expected profit is therefore zero. This is the same moral as in the open version: competition between the two bidders dissipates the entire surplus, and the auctioneer ends up with the full value of the prize either way. The paradox thus does not really depend on the sequential structure of the open auction; the sealed variant reproduces it with the timing removed.
>
> **(iii)** Yes — the dollar auction is essentially the escalation-of-commitment phenomenon in miniature. In the organisational experiments, administrators who had personally approved the initial funding kept pouring money into a failing project because they were unwilling to concede that the earlier money had been wasted; in the dollar auction, bidders keep raising because they are unwilling to concede that their earlier bids were wasted. In both cases the driver is reluctance to write off an outlay one has already committed, and in both cases it should be resisted in the same way: ignore what has already been spent and decide purely on the forward-looking comparison of the costs still to be incurred against the benefits still to be gained.
