I teach an upper-level phil language seminar, 14 students, 75 minutes, normal seminar room, no lab. I want an in-class game that makes semantic externalism land from the inside instead of everyone reciting the Twin Earth story back at me. Nobody should hear 'Twin Earth', 'externalism', 'Putnam', or 'natural kind term' until the debrief, and I don't want any lecture up front.

The bit I'm stuck on: I give everyone a sealed vial of something that looks and tastes like water, and all of them actually are water — the difference between batches is purely stipulated by me. Halfway through I announce that half the rooms vials contain a different compound, its undetectable by anything available in the room, so nothing in anyone's experience changes. I like this, but I need a real game around it, not just a reveal.

So: rules and materials for a game with tiny stakes (points or tokens, no money) where what the shared word picks out matters for whether you win. The word should get introduced by ostension — 'blyme' is the stuff in your vial — and then there's a trading or betting market where people have to commit to trades before they know which batch they had. I also want a third phase where a sample gets passed down a chain of eight students and gets swapped once, at an unknown point, with nobody told: then they bet on whether the last person's use of 'blyme' picked out the first sample, the last sample, or nothing.

The debrief should map what actually happened onto three things: that two speakers can be in the same internal state and mean different things; whether reference follows the speakers' deferral or the object at the far end of the chain; and what, if anything, changes when they find out. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the externalist claim the game can't dramatise and I'll just have to say out loud afterwards.
