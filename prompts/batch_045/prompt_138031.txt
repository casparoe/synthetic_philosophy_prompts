Not a philosophy guy, I write code, and I keep shipping commitment features and then arguing with myself about wether they're a tool or a cage. Here's where my gut lands, no theory attached:

- Handing my car keys to a friend at the start of a party so I can't drive home drunk: fine, and he's right to say no at 1am when I ask for them back.
- A five-year casino self-exclusion I signed sober, with no way to revoke: fine, and wanting out later is not a reason to let me out.
- The thing I just built: you set a rule in the app, and any request to unlock it only takes effect after a 7-day delay: fine, my favorite feature.
- The same thing with a 90-day delay: not fine, a cage with extra steps. I can't tell you the number where it flips.
- Unlocking instead requires turning up in person at an office twice, 30 days apart, no fee: fine, and I like it more than my timer, which is strange since its strictly more annoying.
- A constitution amendable only by a two-thirds vote in two consecutive parliaments with an election in between: fine, that's just good engineering.
- An eternity clause that no future majority can ever amend: not fine, even when I like what it protects.
- A city council that votes to require a two-thirds supermajority to approve any new housing: not fine, and it drives me crazy that this is structurally the same as the amendment rule I just called good design.
- A court ordering me to actually go back and work for someone I signed with, instead of just paying damages: not fine, even though I signed it.

My guess at the rule is: you may bind a future self only if that self can still get out, and the cost of getting out has to be real but small. That gets most of them but it's mush (what counts as small) and it says nothing about the council case. Two I keep flip-flopping on: a psychiatric advance directive saying that if I'm manic I should be hospitalized and medicated against my will, which I want and which feels fine even though every rule I write seems to say I'm overriding the self that actually exists then; and a rule that any company has to let me cancel in one click, which is obviously right, and yet my 7-day delay is a company definately not letting me cancel in one click.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no conclusion: (1) the tightest version of the rule you'd actually defend, (2) the extra clause you had to bolt on for the case that breaks my version, (3) the verdict above you'd make me give up, (4) what the rule says when the delay isn't mine — my employer switches on a 7-day delay on my own account, (5) what it says about the psychiatric directive.
