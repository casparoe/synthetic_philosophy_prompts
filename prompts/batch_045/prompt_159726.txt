So I run a fortnightly novel-writing workshop out of the back room of a secondhand bookshop, about 12 people, mostly literary fiction plus one very opinionated sci-fi guy, and we get two hours including a tea break. I'm planning a session for next month and I want something that isn't just more craft talk.

Here's what I keep circling around. I've been reading about Jain anekāntavāda — the many-sidedness / non-one-sidedness thing — and the sevenfold conditional predication that goes with it. I'm shaky on which Sanskrit word maps onto which bit (saptabhaṅgī? syādvāda? are the standpoints a separate list from the seven modes or the same list? please quietly fix me if I have it tangled). What I want people to walk out feeling is not "everyone has their own truth, how lovely" — I'm fairly sure that's the misreading — but something harder: that several incompatible-sounding accounts of one event can each be genuinely, non-ironically true; that you can't recover the whole by averaging them or by finding the most sincere one; and that none of this leaves you paralysed when you have to act, because acting still means committing to one standpoint for the purpose at hand.

What I'd like from you is a runnable two-hour session: the shared incident we write from, the roles, the rules, the handouts, a timeline, and a debrief. Some constraints, because I've tried things like this and they've flopped: no lecture up front before anyone writes, and the participants should run into the problem themselves rather than have it named for them. Definitely not a read-the-blind-men-and-elephant-parable opener — apart from being a cliché, I gather it gets told in half a dozen traditions and probably isnt even Jain in origin, so I dont want to hang the evening on it. Ideally nobody hears the Sanskrit words until the debrief. Two things I'd like built in specifically. First, a forced-choice moment where a writer has to commit to one version and do something irreversible with it, so they discover on their own that many-sidedness isn't the same as can't-decide. Second, a tagging or scoring device so statements get sorted rather than just discussed — I was imagining each line getting marked with which of the modes its claiming, and a ledger separating contradictions that stay live under challenge from ones that dissolve once you ask "true relative to what, stated by whom, about which aspect". Steal that or improve on it. And my group will reach for "unreliable narrator" within about four minutes; I think that frame will actively block the point, since it smuggles in that there's one real version the narrator is distorting, so tell me how to work with it when it comes up rather than how to head it off.

The debrief should map specific moments onto the actual structure of the view — the standpoints versus the predication versus the doctrine itself, if its three different things. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the view a writing workshop simply cannot stage and I'd have to just tell them flat out. One more ask: when you explain the doctrine, separate what's settled among people who actually know this material from what scholars genuinely argue about — I'd guess that whether the sevenfold scheme is a real many-valued logic or a dialectical teaching tool is fought over, and how you translate the avaktavya/ineffable mode is too, but honestly I don't know. Don't smooth a live dispute into a consensus, and flag where your own reconstruction is a stretch or where you're trading orthodoxy for something that works in a room of twelve tired writers on a Tuesday.
