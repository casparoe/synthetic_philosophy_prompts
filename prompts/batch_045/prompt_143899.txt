I'm defending a chapter of my dissertation in a fortnight and I need Q&A prep. The thesis is basically this: higher-order evidence comes in two kinds, and the debate has been running them together. There's *diagnostic* higher-order evidence, which pins a specific fault in the reasoning that produced the belief — I was shown only half the data, I did the sum while distracted, I mixed up the conditional and its converse. And there's *statistical* higher-order evidence, which only tells you that people in your situation, or you in cases of this shape, err at some rate, with no story about where the error lives: all four of my peers disagree with me, 30% of readers miss this tumour type. My claim (I call it the diagnostic constraint) is that only the first kind can rationally require you to move your first-order credence in p. Statistical evidence is a reason for hedging — widen your intervals, get a second read, change what you say and what you'd bet — but no reason at all to become less confident that p. So the familiar thought that rationality requires you to align your first-order attitude with your higher-order assessment, and that anything left over is epistemic akrasia, is a conflation. The argument is two steps: a discrimination step (a bare base rate doesn't discriminate the worlds where this judgment is the error case from the ones where it isn't, so it can't bear on p) and a no-double-counting step (the error rate already does its work in the hedging; putting it into the credence too counts it twice).

The room will be maybe fifteen people, and not one of them works on higher-order evidence specifically. About half are social epistemologists who work on peer disagreement and trust in experts — one of them has published on deference to experts and will take the view personally. Then three formal types, all Bayesian, one of whom does imprecise probabilities and will want to know what my update rule is.
