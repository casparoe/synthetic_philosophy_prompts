I'm a philosopher doing a clinical ethics fellowship at a teaching hospital, and on Thursday I'm presenting at the pain service's monthly teaching round — 40 minutes plus questions. My thesis, in one line: what makes pain bad isn't how it feels.

Quick sketch of the argument I'm going to give, so you have something to aim at:

1. Asymbolia: rare cases — a scattering of lesion reports and some old psychosurgery series — where the patient can tell you exactly where it hurts, how intense, and what quality it is (sharp, burning, crushing), but says it doesn't bother them, doesn't withdraw or protect the limb, sometimes laughs. So the sensory side of pain can come apart from the affective side.

2. The standard philosophical reading is that this shows pain's badness = its unpleasantness (or = the aversion or desire-not-to-be-in-it that pain carries). I say no. There's a third thing that comes apart from both of those: preemption. Pain is bad because of what it does to your practical landscape — it takes options off the table before they ever get to compete, rather than merely weighing against them and losing. In asymbolia the pain is still there, still felt, still located — but it stops foreclosing. It's a notification you can shrug off.

3. So the bad-making feature is structural and normative — a role in deliberation — not a phenomenal ingredient. Corollary I'm stuck with: the thing to ask about clinically isn't the 0–10 distress rating but whether the pain is still shrinking the patient's option space, and I want to float a one-item probe ("what did you not do today because of the pain, that youd have done otherwise?") instead of another rating scale.

4. The upshot I actually care about: the chronic pain patient in the acceptance-based program who still rates it 8/10 and still says it's awful but is back at work and hiking — on my account something real has improved, they haven't just learned to describe the same thing in a nicer way.

Who'll be in the room:

- Two interventional pain docs (anesthesiology). Roughly nociception-plus-suffering in outlook, don't read philosophy, want to know what changes in clinic on Monday. One of them is definitely going to say the asymbolia literature is a dozen case reports about people wth frontal or insular damage and tells her nothing about her patients.
- A clinical psychologist who runs the acceptance-based chronic pain program. Thinks suffering is dissociable from sensation and that the target is functioning and values, not distress scores. She'll think I'm either stealing her view or caricaturing it.
- A palliative care physician. Has patients who rate 10/10 and describe themselves as at peace, and patients who rate 4/10 and are wrecked. He'll worry that on my measure half his patients count as fine and he'll under-treat them.
- A visiting philosopher of mind, hard-line "pain is essentially unpleasant" type. She'll go straight for the metaphysics and she's fast.

What I want is the prep sheet. Six or seven objections, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do — not by how interesting they are — each phrased the way that particular person would actually say it out loud (the docs' version should sound like a doc, the philosopher's should sound like a philosopher), with a short reply for each, maybe two minutes of talking, and an honest note on where my reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes so I don't lose the room, and which single reply has to land or the whole talk doesn't survive.

And give me the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks. My guess is it's something like: can a creature that doesn't deliberate at all — an infant, a dog, someone with advanced dementia — have pain that's bad for them, on my account? But if you think there's a worse one than that, use it instead.
