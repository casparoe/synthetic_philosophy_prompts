I work on reliability for a large distributed system, and I keep watching the same argument in incident reviews without having a word for what goes wrong in it.

Two cases. (1) A service has a retry storm and, separately, a periodic latency spike. We fix the retries; the latency spike gets worse, and turns out to be a slow memory leak that the retry behaviour had been hiding all along. The postmortem then says these were always two mechanisms and one was merely masking the other. (2) Two unrelated services intermittently drop requests. A single config rollback makes both stop, and everyone says: same mechanism, one root cause, one bug. Six months later both symptoms return after unrelated deploys, and the new write-up says they were two mechanisms that happened to share a trigger.

What I keep noticing is that the answer to "is this one mechanism or two?" seems to be settled by what we are able to do rather than by what is there: a shared fix makes things one mechanism, separate fixes make them two, and a fix that unmasks something rewrites the earlier verdict retroactively. The count is also not stable over time — it moves with which interventions happen to be available and with how fine-grained our telemetry is — so we can end up saying A and B are the same mechanism, B and C are the same mechanism, and A and C are not.

Is there an established concept for this: for the identity and number of mechanisms being fixed by the available repertoire of interventions, and for the hindsight-dependence that masking introduces? It doesn't seem to be just the truism that correlation isn't causation, and it isn't quite a question of how finely one slices the system, since the slicing itself changes when a new fix becomes available. If there is a literature on it, I want the nearest concepts and how this differs from each; if there isn't, I want a name, a definition, and boundary cases saying when the verdict should and should not flip. I'd also want to know whether the honest conclusion is that "same mechanism" is only ever relative to an intervention repertoire, or that there is a fact of the matter here that our practice tracks badly. A bare label won't help me.
