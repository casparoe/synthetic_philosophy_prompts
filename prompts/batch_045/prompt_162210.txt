I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for years, and there is one piece of advice that shows up every single time someone worries out loud that their information diet is warped: steelman, don't strawman. It appeared again last week in a thread about political polarisation online, where a commenter described noticing that every source he trusted agreed with him and asked what he should do about it. The reply he got, and the reply that got upvoted, was essentially: go and steelman the other side. Nobody spelled out what that amounts to, and it struck me that the slogan was being asked to do a very specific piece of work there — to be a remedy for a defective epistemic environment — and that whether it can do that work depends entirely on which of several quite different things it is being used to say.

So I would like a disambiguation rather than an explanation. Please set out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that steelman, don't strawman could be expressing, and give a verdict on each. The ones I can already gesture at include: a principle of charity in interpretation, i.e. resolve ambiguities in your opponent's favour before attacking; a redirect of target, i.e. argue against the strongest member of the family of views rather than the particular formulation your interlocutor produced; a competence test, of the kind these circles sometimes call an ideological Turing test, where success is being able to produce the out-group's arguments well enough that its members can't tell you apart from one of their own; a trust instruction, i.e. treat some out-group sources as credible enough to learn from; a social move, i.e. a costly signal aimed at an audience rather than at your own beliefs; and the composite move of constructing the best case and then refuting it. There are no doubt readings I am missing, including ones that make the advice trivially true and ones that make it false.

For each reading I would like: whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible norm, or actually mistaken; whether it is a claim about belief, about argumentative conduct, or about signalling; and, most importantly, whether it could do the job the thread needed. In particular, I want the answer to engage with the difference between a merely insulated environment — one where contrary voices and evidence are simply absent, through filtering, homophily or nothing more sinister than convenience — and a genuinely engineered one, where those voices are present but have been made untrusted in advance, so that discredit survives any content they might later produce. It seems to me that these two cases should come apart sharply for this slogan. In the first, several readings look like real remedies. In the second, I suspect something worse: that a steelman must be built out of materials you already have, that those materials are exactly what the environment controls, that the resulting construction can look like a steelman from the inside while being unrecognisable to the out-group, and that you cannot detect the mismatch by trying harder, because detecting it requires the very testimony you have been taught to discount. I also suspect the ideological-Turing-test reading is perversely easy to satisfy inside such an environment, since an elaborate and accurate model of what the enemy says is compatible with, and may even reinforce, treating nothing they say as evidence. And I suspect the construct-then-refuse variant is not a misuse of the slogan but its most common actual use, and functions as inoculation.

Please say where my suspicions are right and where they are confused, tell me which reading the sort of person who deploys the line on these forums is most likely to mean, and finish with a plain verdict: if what I am worried about is the second kind of environment rather than the first, is steelman, don't strawman the right tool at all, and if not, what is the nearest true claim that it is being mistaken for? A table of readings with verdicts, plus a short paragraph of direct answer at the end, would suit me best.
