I have been turning over an argument about the possibility that the standard axioms of mathematics are inconsistent, and it has brought me to a conclusion I cannot accept while every individual step still looks like something I would defend in a seminar. I would like a diagnosis rather than a reading list, and I would like the answer in British English.

Take S to be ZFC, understood as the default background theory in which essentially all of modern mathematics is encoded.

(1) By Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, if S is consistent then S does not prove that S is consistent, and a consistency proof for S given in some other system T merely relocates the question to T. Nor is there any prospect of settling the matter from inside S: if S is consistent, then so is S together with the sentence asserting that S is inconsistent, and in such a theory the objects that S counts as proofs of a contradiction are ones that could never be exhibited. So a user of S can never reach a position in which the risk of inconsistency has been eliminated; the worry can be confirmed but never laid to rest.

(2) What remains is the record: a century of intensive use, no contradiction found, apparent interpretability in a structure we take ourselves to understand, and the dependence of successful science on mathematics. But this is weaker evidence than it appears. (a) The evidence bears on the consistency of S only via the assumption that S is reliable about Π₁ statements, which is strictly stronger than the consistency at issue; so leaning on it seems to presuppose what is in question. (b) The absence of a found contradiction is also what we should expect if there is a contradiction but a long and inaccessible one, since mathematical practice searches for theorems rather than for contradictions. (c) Foundational systems have collapsed before after being believed consistent on intuitive grounds — the unrestricted comprehension principle, Frege's Basic Law V, the early version of Martin-Löf's type theory that allowed a type of all types — and in each case the flaw lay in a principle that had seemed among the most evident. (d) Nor should we be reassured that those collapses were found quickly: they were found in systems with a handful of simple, surveyable principles, whereas a contradiction in S would most likely arise from a delicate interaction of Replacement, Choice and large-cardinal principles, in a region where independence is rampant and where intuitions are known to diverge.

(3) If S is inconsistent and its logic is classical, then by explosion S proves every sentence of its language. In that case no sentence is privileged by being provable in S, and the fact that S proves φ cannot be anyone's reason for believing φ. So if S is inconsistent, nothing proved in S is known or rationally believed: the corpus falls as a whole, not piecemeal.

(4) And the fall is not local. Because S is the background for nearly all of mathematics, and mathematics is the scaffolding of mathematical physics, a contradiction anywhere in S would strip the epistemic standing from countless theorems that make no use of whatever principle is responsible.

(5) The cost of hedging, by contrast, is bounded. One may work in the weakest system that suffices for the task — a subsystem of second-order arithmetic, a predicative theory, a constructive one — or in a non-explosive logic in which a contradiction somewhere need not spread everywhere. One surrenders theorems and convenience; one does not surrender everything.

(6) The natural reply fails. It is no good saying that if a contradiction appeared we should locate it, excise the offending principle and recover almost all of ordinary mathematics, as we did after the paradoxes. First, by (1) and (2) we have no assurance that we should ever find it: a search through the space of proofs is not a feasible procedure. Second, until it is found, every theorem we assert is asserted on the authority of a system that, for all we can show, proves everything, including the negations of those theorems. The repair story is a promissory note about some future theory, not an entitlement to assert the theorems of this one.

(7) Now run (1) to (6) on the hedge itself. Any weaker system S′ or weaker logic L is equally unable to establish its own consistency; indeed its reliability is if anything more in doubt, since weaker systems have been studied less and so enjoy less of whatever the record in (2) is worth. If S′ is classical and inconsistent, (3) and (4) apply to it directly. If L is non-explosive, (3) fails but (4) returns in another form: nothing guarantees that a contradiction stays out of the fragments we actually use, so weakening the logic buys less than (5) claims. Either way, (1) to (6) recommend retreating further, from S′ to S″ — and the recommendation is never discharged.

(8) Hence for every system there is a weaker one that it is more rational to use, and therefore no system, and no logic, that it is rational to use at all. But the reasoning in (1) to (7) is itself reasoning, and so condemns itself; and it would also seem to show that mathematicians working in the inconsistent naive theory had no reason to believe the results we still accept today. Both consequences are unacceptable.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? I should like, if possible, to keep (1) — Gödel's theorem is a theorem — and (3) — explosion is a theorem of classical logic, and I note that Gentzen's consistency proof for arithmetic by transfinite induction up to ε₀ does nothing for S itself. I also want to keep some version of the claim that a century of unbroken use, and the record of earlier shattered-and-repaired systems, is genuine evidence, which (2) and (6) appear to take away from me. Beyond that I have no settled view, and my sense of whether the trouble is epistemological, decision-theoretic or structural changes each time I read the argument again; please do not let me steer you.

What I want from you: name the step; say why it looked compelling; and price the alternatives — what each of the other candidate rejections would commit me to. Please also say what you would reply to someone who accepts (1) to (7) and embraces (8), whether in the form that no mathematical belief is knowledge or in the form that we ought to abandon classical logic forthwith. Set the probability that S is inconsistent wherever you like; I do not want the answer to turn on a number.
