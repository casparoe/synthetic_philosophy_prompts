hey — I do the back page of a foreign-policy magazine, the slot where we run one long piece of satire a month, and I want something for the issue we're putting together on "the lessons of history." Here's the brief.

The target isn't a person, it's a practice: the way policy arguments get settled by hanging a historical analogy on a current crisis. The Munich reflex (concede now and you're Chamberlain, act now and you've avoided him), the Vietnam reflex (go in and it's a quagmire, stay out and you've learned the lesson), and the Thucydides-trap framing where any rising power plus any established power equals structural inevitability and the war was always going to happen. All three are used as arguments, not illustrations, and all three seem to be available for pretty much any situation.

Form: a parody of a management-consulting white paper / service brochure from a made-up firm — you name it — offering to match a client's crisis to the right historical precedent. Numbered sections, a tiered pricing table, maybe a client testimonial and a couple of footnotes. Around 800–900 words, deadpan consultant voice (deliverables, right-sizing, optionality, scope of work). American English please — organize, defense, -ize endings.

The jokes have to come from getting the actual epistemology right. The things I want exaggerated rather than invented:

- The analogy gets picked after the conclusion does. Munich and Vietnam keep fitting the same fact pattern while pointing opposite ways, so the firm should sell them as packages or tiers and treat the contradiction as a selling point — optionality, a full range of lessons.
- Analogies get matched on surface features, not causal structure. So the firm's "fit rubric" should openly score the things that don't matter — mountains, a conference table, whether there's a photograph everyone remembers, how many countries are involved, whether the adversary looks like someone from a newsreel — while structural similarity is a premium module nobody ever buys.
- The Thucydides-trap material: a claim about one war gets repackaged as a general law, and because "rising" and "fear" are never fixed in advance the test fires on every pair of powers you feed it. Sell it as a Structural Determinism Module, maybe with a waiver.
- Invoking the analogy changes the thing being assessed: Munich takes concessions off the table, Vietnam takes action off it. The tool modifies the workpiece. There should be a clause about that, and it should be billable.
- The casebook only contains crises that got analogies. Put in a survivorship disclosure.
- Analogies wear out. Give me a depreciation or sunset schedule, with reissues.

Then the Swiftian turn: push the logic somewhere a reader flinches. If the map from situation to analogy is many-to-one and the map from analogy to prescription is one-to-one, the crisis itself is doing no work — so the top tier should deliver the recommendation up front, billed at crisis rates, at the moment a region is designated vital, with the after-action debrief confirming in retrospect that the analogy obviously applied. Nobody in the document may notice this is circular; the reader has to feel it.

Constraints: never step out of character to explain the joke, and never let the objections get stated — no character gets to say "analogies are post-hoc rationalizations" or "surface similarity isn't causal structure." They get enacted. Don't name any real living person, don't quote any real historical figure — paraphrase instead, including the famous line about the growth of Athenian power and the fear it caused in Sparta, which the firm can run as its "source text, lightly modernized for compliance." Invented numbers are fine but make them obviously fake. I think that's everything — let me know if anythings unclear, and feel free to add an FAQ appendix at the end if it earns its keep.
