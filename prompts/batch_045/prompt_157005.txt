Okay so I've been lurking on the rationalist blogs and forums since roughly the old Slate Star Codex era, and there is one argument I have literally never seen go anywhere: same-sex marriage. Every time it comes up both sides leave feeling steelmaned-but-unheard, and I think part of the problem is that nobody has ever written down the document the two sides could *both* sign. So: draft that document. An adversarial collaboration, joint statement, signed by both parties.

The two parties, named plainly:

- The conjugal view: marriage is something you recognize rather than invent. Its shape is given by the kind of bodies and dependencies humans actually have — it's the kind of union that characteristically unites a man and a woman and is oriented toward the children such unions produce — and the norms people associate with it (permanence, exclusivity, the expectation of kids) are internal to the thing, not policy levers the state gets to set.
- The revisionist view: marriage is a legal-social institution we made and keep remaking, and the right question about it isn't what its essence is but what work we need it to do — stable public recognition for the people who actually depend on each other, protection for caretakers and kids, a shared language for commitment. If the current definition does that work badly or unfairly, changing it is a repair, not a corruption.

Both of those are caricatures; un-caricaturing them is part of the job. Here's what I want in the deliverable:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. Not platitudes. I mean the substantive things both can sign: about what the institution historically did economically and legally before it was anything to do with romance; about how much of the legal package is separable from the part people are actually fighting over; about whether 'we've always done it this way' does any argumentative work at all; about what a society with no civil marriage whatsoever would look like.

2. A disagreements table. Columns: what's at stake / the wording the conjugal side would sign for its own view / the wording the revisionist side would sign for its own / your verdict on whether this row is a dispute about the world or about words. And where you think a row is verbal, say exactly which part and why. My own guess, which you should feel free to overrule: at least one big chunk is a fight about whether 'what is marriage for?' asks for a *purpose* or a *function*, and another is about whether social kinds have natures at all — and those two get run together constantly.

3. Cruxes, with teeth. For each crux: a concrete case, argument, or evidence-source that both sides agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction written down in advance. Here are four I want you to actually use (rename them if you like, and use your own too if you think I've picked badly):

- The double-entry problem: one word is currently doing two jobs. There's a thin legal bundle — tax, inheritance, hospital visitation, immigration, next-of-kin, and a long tail of statutory incidents — and a thick normative thing about permanence, exclusivity and children. The disaggregation proposal is that the state only issues registered caretaking relationships, and 'marriage' goes back to being whatever communities and traditions make of it. Who should be happy with that and who shouldn't? I genuinely can't tell, and I think that's diagnostic.
- The boundary cases. These are the ones the revisionist owes answers to and the conjugal view allegedly doesn't: two elderly siblings who have shared a household, finances and caretaking for thirty years; three adults raising a child together; a five-year renewable term marriage. Is there a principled stopping point? And note that 'you can't stop there' is an argument form that proves way too much — it also would have proved too much against no-fault divorce or against interracial marriage — so I want the *form* of that argument dealt with, not just the cases.
- The conscription worry. If marriage is an artifact whose content is set by what the state needs it to do, then a future state with different needs — a pronatalist one, say, handing out serious tax benefits to married-under-35 parents and nothing to anyone else — is doing structurally the same thing the revisionist did, just in a direction I'm guessing the revisionist hates. Is that a reductio? Or is there a principled constraint available — something like a public-reason constraint — that blocks it without quietly smuggling a nature back in?
- The closest thing we have to a natural experiment. In a handful of rich countries marriage has become optional and expressive: cohabitation is the default, a large share of children are born outside marriage, people marry later and more selectively. Both sides agree this is the nearest available evidence. So: what pattern in that world would make the conjugal side update? What pattern would make the revisionist side update? Write both down as predictions, with the rough time horizon and the size of effect each would take seriously, before either of them gets to interpret the data.

Two more constraints. Don't adjudicate — I don't want your verdict on who's right, I want the text both of them could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact. And I don't want citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I can't check them and nobody in my forum thread will read them, so argue everything from the cases themselves.

Last thing, which matters to me more than the rest of it: at the end, tell me how confident you are in the substantive claims you've made in this document, on whatever rough scale you like, and single out the one point you're least sure of — the thing you'd most expect a smart person on either side to say you got wrong. If you're willing, also put a number on how much of this dispute you think survives genuinely good-faith steelmanning from both sides, and say which part of the remainder is just talking past each other.
