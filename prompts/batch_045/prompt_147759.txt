so I've been going back and forth on the saving-the-many thing for weeks and I keep changing my mind, so I want to try doing it by cases instead of by argument. The Taurek-ish view that the numbers shouldn't count at all has always struck me as both compelling and completely unusable, and the straight total-up-the-stakes-and-maximize view feels fine right up until it starts telling me things I hate. So here's my gut on ten cases. Read them as one person's verdicts, not as arguments for anything.

1. One stranger drowning on the left, five on the right, same distance, everything else equal: save the five. Not close.
2. One stranger vs one stranger: coin flip is fine, or just whoever I can reach first.
3. My kid vs five strangers: my kid, and I don't feel guilty about it afterwards.
4. I'm a fire chief sending crews, my kid is in one house and five strangers in another: the five. But I'm a dad doesn't seem to be a reason in that role, and I don't know why the role changes the arithmetic.
5. One person dies vs a thousand people each get a bad migraine for a week: save the one.
6. One person dies vs a hundred people each lose a finger: still save the one, less confidently.
7. One person dies vs a hundred thousand people each lose a finger: ok, I think I switch here, and I hate that I switch, because there's no particular number where it obviously flips.
8. One person will die without the only dose of a drug, or five people who each need the whole dose and can't split it: my gut says give it to the five, but if someone flipped a coin I'd think that was odd yet not wrong, whereas if a hospital pharmacist flipped a coin I'd think that was dereliction.
9. Two 95-year-olds will die, or one 20-year-old will die: I think save the one, even though that's fewer people.
10. One person dying for sure vs a one-in-a-thousand shot at saving ten thousand people: take the shot. But one person dying for sure vs a one-in-a-thousand shot at saving five hundred: save the one. I can't tell you why 500 and 10000 come apart for me and I suspect that's just me being incoherent.

The principle I keep half-stating is something like: every person has an equal claim on me, so when I can't satisfy everyone each should get an equal chance, but how big a stake a person has sets how heavy their claim is, so someone facing death has a heavier claim than someone facing a migraine. On its own that obviously won't deliver 1 or 8, since equal chances with no adding up gets me coin flips everywhere, so maybe I need to add either that I should maximize total claim-weight or that chances get handed out in proportion to claim-weight. I can't tell which, and I can't see what either one does to 3 versus 4.

What I want: state a principle precisely enough that I could run it myself on a new case, or two principles if there really are two and the choice between them is mine, give it to me clause by clause with the case that motivates each clause, take it through all ten, and then be straight about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which one you'd throw out. Also run it on three cases I haven't given you: (a) one stranger dies vs ten strangers each lose a hand; (b) I can either save one person from death or save that same person from blindness, deafness and a leg; (c) two separate islands, a one-vs-five on each, decided by two different people who can't communicate — does anything change if they compare notes afterwards?

Sections in this order, please:

- "The principle" — about 150 words, stated precisely, clause by clause, with the case motivating each clause.
- "Case by case" — about 450 words, all ten of mine.
- "What it can't save" — about 150 words, which verdicts have to go and which you'd drop first.
- "Three predictions" — about 200 words, on a, b and c.
- "How sure I am" — about 100 words, which parts of your answer you actually believe, which are a coin flip, and where you're leaning on a contested reading or a contested assumption.

And don't be polite about it: if I've described these cases in a way that rigs the answer, or if there's something hidden in stake-sets-the-weight-of-the-claim that just assumes aggregation and begs the question against the view that numbers don't count, or if the whole search for one principle here is a fools errand, say that plainly instead of doing the task nicely.
