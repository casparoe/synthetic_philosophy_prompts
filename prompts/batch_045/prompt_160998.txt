I keep having the same argument at dinner with two friends and it has now happened maybe four times without anybody moving an inch, which is what makes me think we are not actually disagreeing about the thing we think we are disagreeing about. The setup: my friend who runs product at a small startup says honoring sunk costs is often rational and that people who are too willing to walk away from a big investment are worse off; my biologist friend says this is textbook Concorde-fallacy reasoning and that any agent who lets past investment drive current choice should get selected against; I keep trying to defend the econ-101 line that only forward-looking costs and benefits matter and past expenditure is by definition irrelevant. What makes it feel stalled rather than merely hard is the move that always ends it. Every single time someone produces a case where persistence looks obviously right — finishing things because a reputation for finishing is worth something to you later, continuing a failing project one more quarter because quitting now means you never learn whether the approach was wrong or the execution was, staying in because your team and your investors are watching and your exit changes what they'll do next — the econ line just reclassifies it: that is not a sunk cost doing work, that is a forward-looking consequence that happens to be caused by a past expenditure. Which is correct as far as I can tell, but it makes the principle unfalsifiable in a way that feels like cheating, and my product friend has never once managed to describe a case where the past cost matters and there is genuinely no forward-looking trace at all.

My own hypothesis, which I want you to either sharpen or kill: I think there are three different things tangled together. First, a verbal issue about what we screen off when we say "sunk" — I've started splitting costs into sealed costs (nothing downstream depends on them, no observer, no information, no change in the agent) and trailing costs (past expenditure that leaves reputational, informational, option-value, or identity-shaped traces behind), and if that distinction is doing the work then the econ claim is true but only about sealed costs, and the interesting question is how often real costs are actually sealed. Second, a level-of-analysis issue: my friend may be arguing about what policy or habit or kind of character to have, while I'm arguing about what to do at a single decision node, and "ignore sunk costs" is consistent with "adopt a rule of sticking with things" if following the rule is itself costly and the cost buys you something downstream. Third, a hidden empirical question about base rates that none of us can settle from the armchair and that we keep pretending isn't there. There's also the data problem: the classic illustrations people reach for are one-shot hypotheticals about theater tickets and half-eaten meals, and everyone in this argument is really thinking about multi-year projects with observers and with learning, which is a different environment entirely.

One more thing I want squeezed, because it's the argument I invented on the drive home and I can't tell if it's deep or empty: suppose my earlier self spent two years on something and my current self has forgotten most of why — the details have decayed, the tacit feel for the problem is gone, but the fact of the expenditure is still there. Then "I put two years into this" is evidence about what a better-informed past version of me believed, and deferring to that is not obviously irrational. Is that really just the informational channel again, so the econ line absorbs it, or is it a different kind of reason — a diachronic one about being the same agent over time rather than a sequence of decision nodes? So: diagnose this stalemate. Tell me which of these is really going on, using what each of the three of us actually says rather than a general essay on the fallacy, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a measurement, a change of question, a formal result — or make the case that no progress should be expected and say why. If you think the whole question is ill-posed as stated, say that plainly and show me where it breaks.
