So I'm giving one of the toasts at my sister's wedding in three weeks, and I've agreed to keep it to about four minutes. I've never tried to write anything where the philosophy actually constrains every sentence and I'm stuck, so I'd like you to just write it.

The context you need: my sister and her partner are both committed tranquilists — the view that the only thing that's basically good or bad for a person is the presence or absence of craving and suffering, and that getting what you want, feeling joy, having your deepest projects succeed, none of that adds anything on top of not-suffering. It isn't a phase. They met at a ten day silent retreat, and maybe sixty of the guests are from that world and will notice instantly if I smuggle in an ordinary well-wish and hope nobody hears it. The other half of the room is my family: warm, unphilosophical, Midwestern, and my dad runs a life-coaching business built entirely around identifying your core desires and going after them. He's giving the other toast and it will be exactly that, and I don't want mine to read as a rebuttal of his.

Here's the bit that's actually hard. Basically every move a wedding toast has available is unavailable to them. May you always be this happy — no, on their view that's not what makes a life go well. May all your dreams come true — those are cravings, and the picture where the dreams are what matter is precisely what they reject. You two complete each other — completeness that depends on being completed is a fragility, not a good. The best is yet to come — anticipation is a form of the thing they're trying to have less of. And I can't say anything that implies the marriage is good because it will make them happy; she'd hear that as a category mistake, she thinks it's good because of the kind of friction it removes.

Two refinements of theirs I want respected. First, they don't think tranquility is agent-relative in the ordinary way: it isn't specially better that she be the one who's tranquil, as opposed to anyone else, so while I can obviously say I wish her peace, I can't frame her peace as an achievement or as a goal she's pursuing, or as something that makes her life better than other people's. Second, they've thought hard about promising. They don't think vowing to love someone is bad, but they think a vow only makes sense if it isn't a standing stake that keeps generating new craving, and they rewrote their own vows around that idea. If you can use what they actually did with their vows as the structural spine of the toast, I think that's the move, but you do it if you see it.

What I don't want: a lecture, a three minute explanation of tranquilism for the relatives, jokes at my dad's expense, or the move where you wish them something that is transparently just happiness with the serial numbers filed off (rich with meaning, deeply alive, full of love, and so on). If there's a positive wish that genuinely survives the view, I want it. If there isn't one, I'd rather have a speech that's honest about what they're actually doing together — building a life with less friction in it, is roughly how I'd put it — than one that cheats. First-person stuff about how I feel is fine by the way, they won't object to me reporting my own state, but not to me treating my feeling as a reason the day is a good one.

Write me the actual toast, not an outline, and if it's useful add a few lines afterwards on the choices you made and what I should cut if the room goes quiet. And sorry if this is all a bit wierdly specific, I'd just rather it be defiantely true to them than smooth.
