I'm revising the spine of a paper on whether the diverse things we call pleasures have anything in common, and before I commit to the version I'll circulate at my reading group I'd like a hard reading of the argument itself rather than of its neighbours. I'm away from the university library for the next ten days, so please don't give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations: what I need is commentary on the reasoning, not pointers. The working title is *Pleasure as Sustained Deviation*, and here, compressed into five moves, is what I have.

First, the setup. I claim the heterogeneity objection is decisive only against accounts that look for a common ingredient shared by all pleasant experiences — paradigmatically the view that pleasure is a distinctive felt quality, a hedonic tone laid over whatever else is going on. List the cases (cool water on a hot day, a chord resolving, the last line of a proof landing, an itch scratched, a joke at a rival's expense) and no sensory or phenomenal ingredient survives. The attitudinal family of accounts — pleasure as a matter of the subject's taking some pro-attitude toward what is happening — survives that objection easily, and I say in the paper that it survives it for free, and that the price is deferred rather than paid: it either explains the attitude by the pleasantness and goes in a circle, or leaves the attitude brute and inherits every pathology of brute desire, including the familiar dissociation between how much a subject wants something and how much pleasure it actually gives. Second, the proposal. I treat pleasantness as a global regulatory mode of an episode rather than an ingredient in it: an experience is pleasant to the extent that the activity constituting it is being governed so as to approach or prolong its current trajectory, rather than being governed so as to escape or correct it. This is meant to be one property, multiply realisable, empirically scrutable in principle from the control signals rather than from the content, and capable of explaining why pleasure motivates without presupposing an antecedent desire. Third, the two sub-cases I need: prolonging pleasures (listening, warmth, being absorbed in work), where what is sustained is the trajectory itself, and relief or debt-discharge pleasures (thirst, itching, cool water), where the target lies ahead but the mode is the same, in that more of the present trajectory is immediately better. Fourth, three cases I concede are hard and intend to discuss rather than dissolve: completion pleasures, where the goodness seems to require the trajectory's ending (finishing a race, a proof closing, orgasm); mixed pleasures, where the same episode carries clear corrective signals alongside the pleasant ones (chilli, horror films, grief in music, hard exercise); and malicious pleasure, where what is sustained is another's discomfort. Fifth, the objection I am least sure about, which a friend in psychology put to me: is it not empty to say that the system is treating the current state as to-be-sustained, when the only evidence for that is that it feels good? My answer is that the mode has independent signatures — what the organism will work to maintain or re-enter, resistance to interruption, characteristic distortion of apparent duration — but I suspect the independence is thinner than I claim.

What I want from you is this. Tell me whether the setup in the first move is fair, by reconstructing the strongest version you can of each of the two families of accounts from memory and saying whether either escapes the pressure I put on it. Then tell me, bluntly, whether the positive proposal is a genuine third option or merely the attitudinal account translated into control-theory vocabulary, and if it is the latter, whether that is fatal or merely a change of idiom. Rank the five or six most damaging objections you can think of, including any I have not listed, as an ordered list or tier list with one sentence on each, and mark which one you think I cannot answer. Give the paper as it stands a score out of ten for whether you would want to read the rest of it, and say what that score is tracking. Finally, and this matters most to me: give me the single consideration which, if my revision handled it, would move your score the most, and say by roughly how much; and tell me where your own verdict is least reliable — where it rests on taste rather than argument, or on a half-remembered literature, or on a distinction you suspect you are drawing badly. Keep the whole reply under about 800 words.
