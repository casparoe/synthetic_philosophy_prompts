I had a stupidly long argument about this last night with my friend (she's half way through a philosophy PhD and will not drop anything) and I want to come out of it with something I can actually use at work.

I'm the continuity editor on a shared-universe sci-fi franchise with branching timelines — the main show plus two spin-offs, and we get about thirty pitches a season for "what if" episodes and alt-timeline spin-offs. I get roughly twenty minutes per pitch to give a yes/no before it goes up to the showrunner, who has final say and who cares mostly about marketing. The writers are good at plot and useless at metaphysics, and they will not read a page on modal logic, so whatever you give me has to be runnable by someone who has never heard the words "de re" and has never heard of Lewis. There's also a fan wiki that will absolutely scream if we contradict ourselves, so the rule has to be one we can state in public and apply the same way twice.

Our rule up to now has been: a character in a branch is the same character if and only if they share an origin scene — same parents, same birth, same inciting incident — and anything else has to be billed as a "variant," which marketing hates and which we use maybe three times a season. My friend says the whole rule is backwards: identity doesn't hold across worlds at all, and what we're actually doing every time is picking a counterpart relation that's context-relative, similarity-based, and not transitive, and that our origin test just smuggles in one answer to a question that has no fact of the matter and then breaks in predictable ways.

So: write me a decision procedure my team can run on a pitch. The questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where the procedure returns a hard no and stops, and where it has to stop and hand the call to a human because the question is really about what we want the story to mean rather than about what's true in the fiction. Make the counterpart-theory bit do real work — I specifically want the thing that falls out of taking counterparts seriously (that the relation is context-relative, that it isn't transitive, that a character can have two counterparts or none) to be built into the steps rather than being a paragraph of theory stapled to a checklist.

Cases the procedure has to survive: a branch of a branch, so that a counterpart of a counterpart has to be handled; a character who dies before the branch point and is alive in the branch; a "what if" that changes the character's origin itself (the pitch is: what if she'd been adopted); a version of a character who is a plausible counterpart of two different main characters at once; an evil-twin episode where the twin is more similar to the hero than the hero's own branch-self is; and a character who exists in only one timeline and therefore has no original to be a variant of.

Then run it on three pitches actually on the table right now: (1) a bottle episode in which the ship's doctor, who dies in episode four of the main show, is alive and practising in a branch, and which we want to advertise as "the doctor's story"; (2) a spin-off about the hero's son, conceived and born after the branch point in a timeline where the hero made a different choice, where marketing has already promised it's about "our hero's son"; (3) a crossover in which a minor character from show A appears in a branch of show B and, under any sane counterpart relation, reads as the counterpart of a major character from A — which would mean our already-announced tentpole crossover is technically a variant story.

I need at least one of those to come out as "no, that's not the same character and you can't bill it as such" — ideally the crossover, because I'm the one who has to walk into the room and tell the showrunner, and I'd like to know in advance exactly which step kills it. And finish by telling me plainly what the procedure can't do: where it can't settle whether an audience will just accept a counterpart relation without being told about it, and whether "is it the same character" is even the right question for us to be asking instead of "is this story licensed by the canon."
