I'm a TA for an undergrad seminar on philosophy of law — 18 students, one 90-minute slot, a whiteboard, index cards, and a projector I'd rather not use. Last week we did the rule of recognition and what happens when a legal order tries to regulate its own amendment, and it went about as well as you'd expect: they treated self-reference as a cute puzzle rather than as something that happens to you. So I want to run them through a short game instead, something in the neighbourhood of Nomic, that game from the early 80s where changing the rules is itself a legal move, but compressed and rigged so the interesting stuff shows up inside 90 minutes instead of after six weeks of mailing-list play. Assume they've read nothing and have never heard the word immutable applied to a rule.

Three things I want them to feel rather than be told. First, that a pile of amendable rules needs a fixed point somewhere, and that where that fixed point sits is itself something you'd have to legislate. Second, the difference between changing what a rule says and changing which tier of the hierarchy the rule occupies — in my notes I'm calling this a jurisdiction move: a proposal that never touches a rule's wording but reclassifies it, say, from mutable to entrenched or back. Third, that the paradoxes arrive as exploits somebody wants to pull off, not as exam questions. Two devices I've been playing with, which you should build in or replace if you think they're clumsy: a Janus clause, a form of proposal that specifies its own adoption conditions (this amendment passes if it gets a majority, or if it gets five votes, whichever is easier), so a proposal can fail under the standing procedure and pass under the procedure it would install; and provisional rules, which take effect the moment they're proposed and dissolve retroactively if they aren't ratified within a couple of rounds, so there's a window in which people act under a rule that may turn out never to have existed. For the rest, assume I'll hand out a short initial ruleset — maybe three tiers, mutable / entrenched / a single inviolate rule, plus a transmutation rule that is itself only entrenched, plus a rotating clerk whose default ruling a majority can override — and private mandate cards so everyone has something to protect and something to raid: you score for how well the final ruleset matches your mandate, not for winning votes, though you also get a small public token for each proposal of yours that passes (winner picks next week's reading, and there may be a coffe voucher in it). Nobody should hear phrases like inviolate level or self-amendment until the debrief.

What I want from you is a thing I can actually run: the exact initial ruleset, short enough for one printed page and worded so I can just hand it over; the mandate cards; turn order and a timeline, including how much of the 90 minutes goes to play versus debrief; the adjudication mechanics, including what happens when what's disputed is the clerk's own authority; and what I do when it derails. Then a debrief that maps specific moments I should be watching for onto the ideas — especially the moment somebody tries to lock in their own favourite rule at the inviolate level, and the moment when a proposal's legality depends on the outcome that proposal would bring about — plus an honest note about which feature of the problem the game can't produce and I'll have to say out loud at the end. Last year I handed them the standard published ruleset and one guy immediately proposed that he wins, and the session was over in twelve minutes, so also tell me what will predictably go wrong and how to steer it back without me turning into the lecturer.
