Hey — I'm putting together a zine for my old physics department's grad student union (long story, but the April issue has the theme "ethics across scales" and nobody else submitted anything), and I want one piece that takes the piss out of the panpsychism thing we spent three weeks arguing about in reading group. Could you write it?

Format: an in-universe document — a covering approval letter plus the "Form 7-B: Experiential Impact Statement" itself — from a university research ethics committee in a world where the Russellian move (physics only ever tells you about structure and relations, so nothing rules out faint experience all the way down) has gone mainstream and regulators now assume every fundamental system has some minimal experiential life. Keep it deadpan institutional, 600–800 words, in the voice of a committee that is trying extremely hard to be reasonable. The applicant is a condensed-matter group that just wants to run a dilution fridge down to about ten millikelvin, and they are very annoyed to be filling out any of this.

What I want the comedy to come from is the actual commitments, not from physicists being wacky or from anyone saying "can you believe this." Give me the committee having to take real positions on: whether the electron–positron pairs created in the readout chain count as new subjects given that lepton number is conserved and the net is zero (and whether an anti-subject cancels a subject, or has, like, negative experience); whether cooling the chip counts as a moral improvement because it eliminates a huge number of microwave photons that would otherwise have existed briefly; whether making a Bose–Einstein condensate is an unlicensed merging of subjects — the combination problem showing up as a compliance violation, with a separate form; whether cosmic-ray muons already passing through the roof are out of scope because nobody applied to create them; whether the Sun, which as far as I remember puts out something like 10^45 photons a second, is a mass atrocity or merely "noted, pending guidance from the national office." Let them have invented a unit — an experiential impact score, a simplicity threshold below which experiences are judged too simple to weigh — and let the applicants have obviously gamed it, say by splitting the run into shorter segments or by deliberately running warmer so the individual photons fall under the coherence threshold.

The thing I want to land without a single character ever saying it out loud: that you can produce a completely precise-looking moral ledger out of a metaphysics that tells you nothing about where the subjects actually are, and that the precautionary version of this ends up tightly regulating the one controlled piece of apparatus in a universe that is already doing all of it continuously, at vastly larger scale, in the sky. No editorializing, no winking, no character who says the quiet part. Sign it with someone with a plausible committee title.
