Hi — I'm an elected (unpaid) trustee of a small public library district, six of us on the board, no lawyer on staff though we can email the town attorney, and I've ended up on the committee that rules on meeting-room applications. It used to be yoga and the quilting guild; these days we get maybe three contentious requests a month, we have five business days to decide, and denials get appealed to the full board and twice now have ended up in the local paper. So I want something I can rerun each time rather than re-litigating it from scratch everytime.

Can you turn the "paradox of tolerance" idea into an actual decision procedure? I mean the thing where an unlimitedly tolerant institution gets destroyed by people who use its tolerance to dismantle it, so it supposedly has to reserve the right not to tolerate the intolerant. The pop versions of this are useless to us because they never say who counts as intolerant or what you're allowed to do once you've decided someone is.

What I'm flailing at informally, and want formalized:

- What the intolerance is *of*: a category of people, a set of views (a group that wants certain books silenced), or the procedure itself (a group that would close the room to others if it ever held the majority). I feel these deserve different treatment but can't say why.
- Our three tiers: permit (room, fee, no library name on it, we post a not-endorsement sign), host (staff set up, we publicize it on our channels), partner (co-branded, our logo). There's a lot of daylight between them and I don't know whether the tolerance obligation is the same at all three.
- The trigger: is it enough that a group's stated aims are intolerant, or do we need evidence of action or capacity? Our own policy says decisions must be viewpoint-neutral, so I can't just deny views I dislike — but if the paradox forces us to break viewpoint-neutrality somewhere, I'd rather be told where honestly.

Give me the questions in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure returns a deny on its own, and where it has to stop and hand things to a human because it's a values call. Also the failure modes: how a procedure like this gets abused by whoever already holds the gavel, and which cases it will misfire on.

Then run it on these three, one of which I'm dreading:

1. A local parents' group wanting the room to organize a campaign to get specific titles (mostly LGBTQ+ YA) removed from our shelves and to collect signatures for a petition to the board.
2. A small nationalist/identitarian group wanting a closed meeting, no press, to hear a speaker. They've done nothing violent locally; nationally their movement has.
3. A local antifascist collective wanting to run a workshop on deplatforming — tactics for getting venues, including ours, to cancel far-right events. These are people I mostly agree with, and I have a horrible feeling a consistent procedure has to treat them like case 2.

Say how confident you are in each step and single out the one you're least sure of.
