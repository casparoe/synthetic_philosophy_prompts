Ok so me and my flatmate got into this massive argument last night about what emotions actually are, we were up way to late and I've been turning it over all day and I still dont know who won.

His view — and he was annoyingly confident about it — was that an emotion just is a kind of evaluative judgement: to be afraid of the dog just is to judge, in some loose not-necessarily-verbal way, that the dog is dangerous and to be gotten away from; to be ashamed just is to judge that you've done something disgraceful; and so on. I kept saying that can't be right because of all the cases where the judgement and the emotion come apart. I know perfectly well flying is safe and I'm still terrified. I can be furious at someone while judging that they did nothing wrong. Grief keeps going long after you've fully accepted that the person is dead. His go-to reply was that I'm confusing the emotion with its causes or with how strongly it happens to feel, and that really I've got two conflicting judgements and one of them isn't fully believed. Something like that. But then he had a point I couldnt get around: emotions look like the kind of thing that can be fitting or unfitting, apt or inapt, and that can be what a reason calls for — you should be ashamed of yourself, there's every reason to be frightened. Bare feelings, he said, aren't like that.

Can you write me a dialogue between two people who actually know what they're talking about, one defending something like his judgement view and one defending the view that emotions are more like perceptions: experience-like states that present the world to you as having some evaluative property, roughly the way vision presents things as coloured. Both of them should be equally good at this, no strawmen, and I want real back and forth rather than two speeches that talk past each other. Some things I want them to actually get into:

- The cases where judgement and emotion come apart. Let the judgement theorist give her best real answer, and then push the perceptualist on why emotions, unlike what you literally see, so often dissolve once somebody talks you out of what you were reacting to. Sometimes that kind of re-evaluation really does work. Is that a difference in kind between seeing and feeling, or just a difference in how easy it is to get the input to change?
- Whether emotions are finer-grained than anything we can put into words. Sometimes I feel something at a funeral, or after a weird conversation, and there's no sentence I could produce that captures it, and there are emotion words in other languages with no real equivalent here. If an emotion is a judgement, what exactly is its content? If it's a perception, is it more like recognising a face you can't describe?
- Dogs and babies. Does the judgement theorist have to say they don't have emotions, or stretch the word judgement until it means nothing? Whoever has the more awkward answer here should just say so.
- Two made-up cases I thought up on the walk home, which I want both of them to answer consistently with each other. First, call them inverts: people whose fear feels the way relief feels to the rest of us — heart rate drops, muscles let go, everything calm and pleasant — but who still judge the situation dangerous, still avoid it, still attend to it, and would tell you they're frightened. Are they frightened? Second: a drug that knocks out the felt aspect of emotion completely while leaving the evaluation, the attention and the readiness to act totally intact. Does someone on it still have emotions? My hunch is that however you answer, one of the two theories is in real trouble, but I can't pin down which.
- Why it matters. I think this is the bit we were actually shouting about. Can an emotion itself be a reason, or a justification, for what you believe? He said that if emotions are judgements then they can only ever inherit whatever support your evidence already gives them, they can never be a source of it. Make the perceptualist say whether emotions are supposed to work like perceptions on exactly this point, and whether that's a strength or just a relabelling of the same problem. And have them get onto whether you can be criticised for having an emotion, rather than only for what you do about it.

At the end I'd like them to try to work out whether they're disagreeing about anything real, or partly about which state deserves to be called the emotion, or about whether emotion is even one kind of thing at all. Like, maybe fear and disgust are one sort of state and guilt, admiration and resentment are another, and both of them are right about different bits. If one of them wants to go for that, the other should get to say whether it's a genuine position or a way of dodging.

Please don't have them cite papers or say things like as so-and-so argues in such-and-such — I don't care who got there first and I've got no way to check it anyway, I just want the arguments. And let it read like an actual conversation rather than a debate transcript. Oh, and nobody gets to win a point by just announcing their intuition about a case; if they lean on what we'd say, make them say why that's worth anything.
