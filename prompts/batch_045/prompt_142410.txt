I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Concepts, Categories, and Analogies," cross-listed between philosophy and cognitive science: fourteen students, one ninety-minute session, whiteboard and paper only, no laptops. Last week we read about the Copycat project — the model of analogy-making built by Douglas Hofstadter, Melanie Mitchell, and colleagues at the Center for Research on Concepts and Cognition in the late 1980s, with its slipnet of concepts joined by weighted links whose effective weights shift as activation spreads, its population of codelets competing probabilistically to build and destroy structures in a workspace, its parallel terraced scan, and its temperature parameter governing how much run-to-run variation the system shows — all of it operating inside a deliberately tiny microdomain, answering problems of the form "abc is to abd as ijk is to what?". The students more or less followed the architecture, but they didn't feel anything. Several left saying, in effect, that the rule is obvious — advance the last letter one step along the alphabet — and that the machinery is overkill for it. A colleague who teaches the cognitive science side of the course thinks any exercise I build will look to them like a puzzle session with no answer key, and I half agree with her, which is part of why I'm asking.

What I want them to run into, rather than be told, is this: a case where "advance the last item one step" has no answer at all, where the only way forward is to let a concept slide into a neighbour — forward into backward, or "one step along the alphabet" into "one more of the same", or "the last item" into "the extreme item" or into "the string as a whole" — followed by the vertigo of there being several defensible slides and no fact of the matter about which is right, and then the discovery that which slide feels natural depends on what they saw five minutes earlier. By the end of the session I want three things to have been discovered rather than asserted: that which relation counts as "the same relation" is not read off the two strings but constructed; that a concept's boundary is set by what neighbours it has; and that a system which slips needs some principled and context-sensitive way of choosing among candidate slips, because "be creative" is not a rule.

My own sketch, which I'd like you to develop, correct, or replace: run the whole thing in a made-up microdomain so that their fluency with the real alphabet does no work — an alphabet of six symbols in a stated order, say B, F, K, M, Q, X with B first and X last, with items being short strings over it. A silent individual first round: a warm-up where the successor rule is uncontroversial, then an item where the last symbol is X and the rule dead-ends, then a nastier item where every symbol is X. A second, primed round: split the class, give half a handout whose example pair quietly establishes the backward slide and half a handout whose pair establishes the lengthening slide, then give both halves the same target item and compare the answer distributions. A wagering round in which teams spend points predicting the modal answer of the class as a whole, so that "there is no fact of the matter" and "some answers are nonetheless more probable" both become concrete at once. And possibly a temperature round: the same item answered twice, once under instructions to be as obvious as possible and once under instructions to be as inventive as you can while still being able to defend it, so the spread visibly widens.

What I need from you is a runnable plan. The microdomain itself: the alphabet, the worked example pairs, and the ordered item list, with a note on why each item sits where it does and where the collapse is supposed to land. The priming manipulation written up as two printable handouts, plus how to keep the split from leaking before the reveal and what to do if it leaks anyway. A payoff scheme for the wagering round that needs no money and cannot be gamed by a team that just copies whoever speaks loudest. A debrief of roughly twenty minutes that maps specific moments of the session onto specific pieces of the architecture — slippage, the slipnet's weighted links and time-varying activation, the probabilistic competition among codelets, temperature — and that names the tension between "no fact of the matter" and "some answers are better than others" explicitly instead of resolving it for them.

Finally, two honesty notes. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong: the student who announces one rule early and dominates the room, the student who refuses to answer on the grounds that the question is ill-posed, the risk that the endpoint cases collapse in the first round before the priming has any chance to work, and the timing. Second, be frank about which features of the architecture the exercise cannot reproduce, so I know what I will have to say myself: I assume the graded, massively parallel, sub-symbolic competition is one of them, but tell me the others, and tell me how much it matters that they are missing.
