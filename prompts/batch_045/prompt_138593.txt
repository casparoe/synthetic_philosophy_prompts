I'm an undergraduate in a decision-theory seminar, and I'm trying to work out how much of the optional primer our section leader handed out I should trust. It's attributed to a graduate student and has been passed down between cohorts, and it reads like this:

"Rationality is a matter of coherence, and the cleanest demonstration of that is the money pump. Suppose your preferences are cyclic: you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A. Then someone can offer you a sequence of swaps, each for a trivial fee — hand over A for B, then B for C, then C back for A. At every step you accept, because at every step you'd rather have what you're being offered than what you currently have. So you end up back where you started, poorer by the fees. And this is not a trick that depends on your being confused or shortsighted: even an agent who foresees the entire sequence from the start and plans it out in advance is stuck, because each individual trade is one he prefers, and declining the first trade would mean deliberately keeping an option he himself ranks below the one on offer.

The same argument, run with bets instead of certain trades, is the Dutch book. Violate any axiom of expected utility theory — the independence axiom, say — and a bookie can put together a set of bets on which you lose money for sure, however the world turns out. So the axioms aren't optional conventions; they are just the conditions under which you cannot be drained.

The diachronic version is the one that matters outside the seminar room. If your preferences change over time — if on Monday you would rather have $100 on Friday than $110 on Saturday, but come Friday you would rather wait for the $110 — you are the pump, and you can be drained repeatedly. Irrationality, in the end, just is being exploitable. Which is why any agent who discounts the future at all, who treats a pleasure next year as worth less than the same pleasure today, is making the same mistake and can be pumped in exactly the same way."
