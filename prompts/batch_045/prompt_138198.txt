I am seventeen, and on Saturday I am giving the eulogy at a small service in our backyard for my family's dog, June, who died last week at fourteen. She was ill, and my parents chose the day she died; I agreed with that decision, and I want to be able to say why it was right without implying that every death we arrange is the same kind of act. June was also not a good dog in the ways eulogies usually trade on. She bit my cousin, she killed two of the Hendersons' hens, and she was frightened of children, including my sister. We loved her anyway, and I do not want the speech to launder her into a saint.

The difficulty is that I cannot find a way of saying why she mattered that I actually believe. Two things I hold: first, that what makes a creature matter is, roughly, that there is something it is like to be her, a life that can go well or badly for her, and that this does not stop at the edge of our species, so the pigs Mr. Aksoy keeps are not morally cheaper than June; and second, that June mattered as this one, not as a unit of that kind of value, so grief for her is not the same as regret at a drop in the world's total well-being, and being told we can always get another dog is not consolation but a category mistake. I have been applying a test of my own to my drafts: every sentence has to survive replacing June with the name of one of Mr. Aksoy's pigs. It has killed almost everything I have written. She was family, she knew us, she trusted us, she had a name and a history: all of that is true of a pig too. I do not want to lean on a difference I have just said I do not believe in, and I do not want to imply that what made her matter was that we loved her.

Four or five minutes, spoken. My grandmother, who is convinced she will meet June again; the Hendersons and Mr. Aksoy, who are kind and who will be insulted if this turns into an argument about how they make their living; my sister, who is eight; and my school's animal-care club advisor, who has heard every version of this argument and will notice if I hedge. It has to be about June, and it has to sound like grief rather than a position paper: no statistics, no numbers, no slogans, nothing that asks the room to feel guilty, and no consolation I think is false.

Please begin with the speech itself: no title, no preface, no note about your approach. After its last line you may add a short note on which of my constraints were hardest to satisfy and what you did instead.
