Hi — I'm on the clinical ethics committee at our mental health trust. We've got a case for Friday morning and I've been asked to circulate a note beforehand, and I'd rather not write it from scratch.

The patient is 34, bipolar I, three admissions, its her third manic episode. Two years ago, between epsiodes, with an independent psychiatrist signing off on her capacity, she completed what we internally call a Ulysses arrangement: if she becomes manic and refuses medication, she is to be given a long-acting antipsychotic anyway, for up to twelve weeks, and she purports to waive her right to refuse during that window.

The bit that's dividing us is that the document anticipates her later objections. It says, in effect, that if she later says the last admission was traumatic, or that the drugs made her feel terrible, or that she'd rather be manic, that is the episode talking and should be discounted. And she is now nine days into an episode and saying almost exactly that. The difference is that the akathisia last time was severe and is documented in her notes by clinicians — she isn't deluded about it, and the version of her who wrote the directive had never experienced it. Her argument to us is that that earlier self was making policy about an experience she couldn't imagine, and that a clause telling your future self to disregard your future evidence is just self-binding with the exits welded shut.

Roughly half the committee wants to treat the directive as authorising and treat her now. Half says that once a conscious patient is saying no, the only honest route is the formal one — capacity assessment, second opinion, detention if necessary — which takes days. One or two want to offer an alternative agent with a much lower akathisia risk first and see whether she'll take it.

Can you draft the memo? What I need is one recommendation I can put to a vote, with a timeline — what happens in the next 48 hours, and at what point we revisit — not a survey of the options. If your bottom line is that we treat her against her current refusal, the memo has to say why her two arguments don't carry the day, in terms I could read out to her advocate without wincing.

Finish with a short section on what would change your recommendation: the fact or argument that would flip it, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to be wrong. British English, please, and keep it to about two pages.
