I teach a six-week undergraduate seminar on causation and counterfactuals in history, and one session is devoted to the "for want of a nail" trope: the thought that history turns on small, unmemorable events, and the chaos-theoretic image of sensitive dependence on initial conditions that is now routinely used to underwrite it. Rather than argue about the trope directly, I want to screen Tom Tykwer's Run Lola Run (1998) and treat the film as a thesis about historical contingency. I'd like a reading of it that takes both sides seriously: the film's structure — three replays of the same twenty minutes, each diverging on what look like trivial encounters, a dog on a staircase, a man with a bicycle, a stranger with a pram — and the actual dispute about whether small causes have large historical effects.

To stop the discussion collapsing into "small causes, big effects", I've drafted three distinctions for the class to work with, and I want them tested against the film as much as the film tested against them:

(i) Ampliative versus switching. Does the small event open a new trajectory with a new menu of outcomes, or does it merely select among outcomes that were already on the table — who wins the battle, not whether there is one?

(ii) Knife-edge versus buffered. A nail plausibly matters only where the system is near a bifurcation: where the downstream event is itself poised, or where nothing in the surrounding structure absorbs and repairs the loss. Elsewhere shocks decay, and the same large-scale outcome re-forms.

(iii) Token-level versus structural-level contingency. The nail may decide which individual lives and which dies, and that is everything to that individual, while changing nothing about the war, the succession, or the tax system.

Where does each distinction do real work in the film? Which two or three scenes would you put on the screen and pause on, and what would you say about them? I especially want your view on the flash-forward sequences the film attaches to the strangers Lola brushes past: they seem to me its strongest and least argued claim, namely that contingency is distributed across persons rather than concentrated in the nail.

I also want the resistance. Lola appears to carry memory and skill across the three runs, and the third run is not a butterfly effect at all but a search that converges. So is the film, read honestly, an anti-chaos film — a story about contingency being progressively defeated by an agent who gets more than one attempt? If so, is it still usable as a teaching text for the trope, or would I be misleading the students by presenting it as one? And tell me plainly if any of my three distinctions collapse into each other or are artifacts of a bad metaphor.

Finally: what does the film add to this debate that the arguments don't — what does showing a branched history let us see that asserting one does not?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning: no preamble, no restatement of the question, and describe scenes in your own words rather than quoting dialogue.
