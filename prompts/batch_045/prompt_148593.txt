My department keeps using this picture: a discovery is like fruit on a tree — the tree loads up on its own schedule, and whoever happens to be standing under it when the thing drops gets to name it after themselves. They use it to say priority is basically luck, so we should stop treating whoever published first as the author of the result (and, in the strong version, that even the credit we do give is mostly undeserved). I think the picture hides something but I cant say what cleanly.

Give me the correspondence table first — whats the fruit, the ripening, the tree, the ground, the other people under it — then the places it breaks. Two I especially want: (1) fruit ripens whether or not anyone is watching, but a field gets ripe because people work in it, and the people who made it ripe are often the ones who got scooped; (2) if I catch the apple you dont, so priority isnt luck shared out among pickers, its winner-take-all.

Then the bit I actually cant settle. My supervisor says multiple independent discovery is the evidence for inevitability — the more simultaneous, the more inevitable. Someone in my group chat says thats also exactly what youd get if one leaked preprint or one conference talk set six groups on the same problem, in which case the multiplicity is evidence of a shared cause instead. Those two stories fit the same record. Can you tell them apart from the shape of the record alone — timing, who cited whom, whether the methods converge or diverge — or do you need evidence from outside it? And if you cant, does the ripe-fruit picture lose the only support it ever had, or does the "credit is luck" conclusion survive anyway on other grounds? If theres a better analogy for what discoveries are like, give me that too.
