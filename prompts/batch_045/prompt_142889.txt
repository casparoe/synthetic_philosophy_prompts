I'm a senior applying for a six-week summer fellowship that lets high school students do an independent project with a philosophy mentor, and the application wants a two-page research proposal. I've been arguing with friends about the ethics of eating meat since my AP Environmental Science unit on agriculture, which bugged me because the textbook presented the whole thing as a settled emissions-and-land-use question and never mentioned the animals. My half-formed idea is this: every comparison I've seen — beef versus chicken, meat versus plants, this diet versus that diet — is computed per unit of food, whether per kilogram or per gram of protein or per calorie, but a farm is really a decision about what to do with a piece of land for a stretch of time, so the natural unit is the hectare-year, and I suspect that once you hold the land fixed the standard orderings scramble. On my very rough numbers, which I know are unreliable and which I'd want to replace with real estimates later, chicken beats beef if you count animals killed per kilogram of meat but loses if you count animals killed per hectare per year, because you can run vastly more birds than cattle through the same field; and the deaths people attribute to plant foods — the small mammals and insects killed by tilling and harvest and by the pesticides used on row crops — arguably belong partly to beef as well, since cattle are largely fed on crops, so it looks to me like those deaths get charged once to the plant column and then quietly forgotten in the beef column. The part nobody I've talked to has an answer to is the counterfactual: if a hectare stops being pasture it becomes something else, and whatever lives on it in its next life will on the whole starve, freeze, or be eaten young, so a comparison that just counts the animals a practice kills may be measuring the wrong thing entirely. What I want the proposal to do is turn this into a question with a determinate answer — roughly: does the ranking of beef, chicken, and crop-based diets by expected suffering per hectare-year flip depending on which baseline you assume for what the land would otherwise be and for what would otherwise have lived on it, and at what parameter values do the flips happen? — and to explain why the arguments currently on offer leave it open, since as far as I can tell each of them silently fixes a baseline without ever saying so.

I also need the proposal to be honest about method, because I have no way to gather data. The approach I'd like you to argue for is a kind of armchair sensitivity analysis: construct the ledger explicitly, with every quantity it depends on written down, and then ask which quantities the final ranking is actually sensitive to and how much empirical uncertainty each carries, instead of producing one more number. I want you to defend that against the obvious alternative of just waiting for better field estimates of how many animals crop cultivation kills — my reason is that the disagreement looks to me like a disagreement about which quantity to measure rather than about its magnitude, in which case better data wouldn't settle anything — but tell me plainly if that's a convenient rationalization. The deliverable should be something a person choosing an intervention could use: ideally a compact table or flowchart saying that if you believe such-and-such about the land's alternative use and about whether a short wild life is on balance worth living, then such-and-such follows for diet and for what to advocate. Please don't include a literature review, citations, or a suggested reading list; my mentor will help me attach those, and I'd rather the document be my own framing than a report on what's already been said.

Two things the selection committee explicitly asks for, and I'd like both done well. First, scope: I have around fifteen hours a week for six weeks, no statistics past the AP level, and a mentor who knows ethics but nothing about agriculture, so say directly if this is too ambitious and what the cut-down version is — if the sensible thing is to work through one baseline and one pair of foods, tell me that. Second, failure conditions: I can see two bad endings I'd rather name in advance than run into in week five, one where the ranking turns out to be robust across every plausible baseline and the whole contribution is negative, and one where it turns out that everything hinges on population-ethics questions — whether a life that never happens is a loss, whether death is a harm over and above the suffering it cuts short, whether suffering-years can be traded against lives — that I cannot settle in six weeks, leaving the ledger as a shell around an argument I have no room to make. Say which ending you think is likelier and what I should do in each case. Keep the prose at a level a bright non-philosopher on the committee can follow, and please don't write it so that I sound like someone who already knows how the project comes out.
