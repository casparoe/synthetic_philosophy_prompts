I'm a high school senior helping to run a "dialogue night" for my school's philosophy club, where instead of formal debates we stage scripted conversations between two people who disagree, and I've been given the section on split-brain patients and whether cutting the corpus callosum leaves one person or two. Every version of this I can find online flattens into a shouting match between "there are two minds in there" and "there's one mind with a wiring problem," and that won't work for us: our audience is other students and their parents, and I need both speakers to sound like they know what they're talking about. So I'd like you to write a dialogue of roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words between two competent, evenly matched people — call them whatever you like, maybe a neuroscientist and a philosopher of mind — arguing about whether a commissurotomy patient is one subject of experience or two. Give each of them real wins: each should make a point the other has to answer rather than restate, and each should concede at least one thing that genuinely costs them. Please keep them from citing any authors, papers, experiments, or books by name (our audience will only get lost, and I don't want a reading list tacked on); the arguments should run entirely off cases, distinctions, and principles that the characters explain as they go, including any brain anatomy or experimental setup they need.

Here is the concrete setup I have in mind, which you should feel free to use or adjust. The two of them are the members of a hospital ethics committee who have to co-write a recommendation about a fictional policy the committee is considering, which I've been calling the dual consent protocol: for any elective procedure on a commissurotomy patient, both hemispheres would have to register agreement separately, one through speech or the right hand and one through the left hand picking cards or tapping a tablet, and a mismatch counts as a refusal — so the question of how many subjects are in the room has actual consequences for whether the patient gets the surgery. Set the conversation in the twenty minutes before the meeting, with the two of them still unable to agree on the memo. I'd also like you to build the argument around two pieces of scaffolding I invented, so that they aren't just reciting familiar cases. First, a hypothetical surgical technique, graded callosotomy, that can throttle callosal transmission down to any percentage and restore it afterward, which lets them argue about what should be said at, say, forty percent: is there a threshold at which one subject becomes two, or is it a continuum, and what would each of them say a subject even is such that there could be a threshold? Second, an invented patient, someone five years post-op whose right hand drafted a conciliatory email to an estranged parent and whose left hand deleted the draft before it could be sent, and who, when asked why, produced a fluent and confident explanation that plainly confabulated a motive. Have each character offer their reading of that case and then respond to the other's reading, and let one of them press the other on whether they are really disagreeing about a number of subjects, about what counts as a subject at all, or about what we owe the patient in practice.

A few last constraints. One of the characters should raise people born without a corpus callosum, who reportedly describe an ordinary single stream of experience, and the other should get to reply to it rather than let it land as a knock-down. Somewhere in the middle I want them to separate, cleanly, the unity of consciousness from the unity of agency and from mere access to report, and to say which of those their opponent is entitled to. Stage directions in italics are fine, but please don't narrate inner states or tell the audience who won. Since they can't lean on authority, the interesting load has to fall on the distinctions and the invented cases. Finally, end without resolution: have each of them name one specific finding that would change their mind — some result from a graded-callosotomy patient at a given bandwidth, or some pattern in what the patient does over time — and phrase it so that both of them sound like they would actually give the position up if the finding came in.
