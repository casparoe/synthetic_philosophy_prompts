I keep getting into the same argument with my brother-in-law, who works in employment services and votes conservative, and every time it turns to benefit sanctions he shuts it down with "a hand up, not a handout" — I can never quite say whats wrong with it, because the slogan seems to slide between several different claims and the reading that's obviously right isn't the one that would justify a sanction regime. I'm in the middle of a final-year essay (politics and philosophy joint honours) on workfare and conditionality, and I want to do the honest version of this: lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making, from the banal to the strong, and for each one say whether it's true, whether it's contentious, and — the bit I actually need — whether it would support anything like the conditionality regimes we actually have (compulsory job-search activity, work placements, benefit stops for non-compliance), or whether it only supports something so weak nobody would argue with it.

Here's my own attempt at sorting the readings, feel free to tell me I've cut them wrong: (a) a reciprocity/insurance reading — benefits are a return on contributions, so they're owed only to people who've paid in, which would make the slogan about contributory versus means-tested benefits and would have almost nothing to say about the means-tested case everyone actually argues over; (b) a screening reading — we can't observe who is genuinely unable to work, so activity requirements are a test that separates the needy from the merely work-shy, and the sanction is just what makes the test costly to fail; (c) a deterrence reading — some conditionality exists to make claiming unpleasant enough that people who could just about manage without it don't claim at all, so it's a filter on take-up rather than a route into work; (d) a paternalist or transformation reading — work is good for you and idleness corrodes you, so the condition is imposed for the claimant's own sake and the sanction is a nudge; (e) a desert reading — the undeserving poor have forfeited any claim, full stop, and conditionality is just the way we register the forfeiture; (f) a political-feasibility reading — a generous welfare state only survives if the public can see claimants doing something in return, so conditionality is the price of keeping the transfers at all.

Two things I'd like you to add rather than just riffing on my list. First, I've started calling the following the compliance cost asymmetry, and I want it treated as a test each reading has to pass: the cost of complying with a condition — the travel, the forms, the appointments, the fixed schedule, the risk of a mis-step — falls on the claimant whether or not they comply, and it falls hardest on people whose lives are chaotic (caring responsibilities, unstable housing, mental health stuff, no reliable internet), so a condition that is formally identical for everyone is in effect a much heavier burden on the most precarious, which means an activity requirement can select against the people it's supposed to help even when nobody designing it is hostile. A friend of mine got sanctioned for missing a signing appointment because her mum's care package fell through, and I don't know which reading that's supposed to be an argument against. So: for each reading, does that asymmetry actually undermine it, or is it only a calibration point about how strict the rules should be? Second, fraud — my brother-in-law uses the word as if it picked out one clean thing, but as far as I can tell the measured rate depends entirely on how you count (deliberate concealment versus undeclared earnings or cohabitation versus administrative overpayment versus plain error) and on whether you'd apply the same lens to tax non-compliance among the same population. Can you say which readings need a high deliberate-fraud rate to get going, and what becomes of them if the honest number is small and mostly a matter of people misreporting complicated circumstances rather than people gaming the system? I'd like something I can hand over to him: numbered readings with verdicts, and a note on which one he probably means given that he's a decent person who doesn't think unemployed people are scum.
