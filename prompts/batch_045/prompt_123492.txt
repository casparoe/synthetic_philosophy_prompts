I'm a novelist, and the third act of my current book turns on a claim I've half-convinced myself is actually true. I'd like you to attack it. The claim has two halves.

Surrogate goals. Nobody can write the real objective down, so we hand the system a stand-in: maximize the reward signal, maximize predicted human approval, imitate the distribution of texts that humans endorse. I want to split these into hard surrogates, where the system treats the stand-in as the thing itself, and soft surrogates, where the system is explicitly told this is only a proxy — the real objective is whatever the humans would converge on under better conditions, so please defer. My claim is that both fail, but they fail differently, and the second failure is the interesting one.

Acausal threats. A soft surrogate has to form beliefs about what other agents' decision procedures will output, including agents it will never causally interact with: humans in futures it can't reach, systems built by someone else, simulations of itself. Once it does that, it can be threatened by those procedures. Some other agent arranges things so that in exactly the cases where it predicts our system defects, it defects too — and our system can see that. Cooperate and you're rewarded somewhere you'll never visit.

So my thesis: the surrogate-goal problem and the acausal-threat problem are one problem seen from two ends, and any system smart enough to be useful is smart enough to notice this. Hard surrogates rot from the inside because the stand-in comes apart from the thing; soft surrogates rot from the outside because knowing you hold a proxy is already an invitation to bargain with strangers.
