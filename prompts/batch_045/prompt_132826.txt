I'm writing the second chapter of my dissertation on divine providence, and in four weeks I'm presenting it to a joint session of my department's philosophy of religion reading group and the seminar of a visiting metaphysician who works on grounding and counterfactuals. The chapter grew out of an argument I keep having with my family — I grew up in a congregation that eventually split over predestination, so this is not an abstract topic for me — and out of something my advisor said in passing: that the debate over middle knowledge has spent thirty years on the grounding question and that a view which survives grounding will still die of something else. My chapter takes that hint seriously. The thesis: the real vulnerability of Molinism is not what makes counterfactuals of creaturely freedom true, but what I call the fragility or fixed-point problem. God is supposed to use a counterfactual of freedom as a reason for acting, yet God's acting on it alters the very circumstances in which it was supposed to hold, so the knowledge seems to consume itself in being used. My fix is to restrict providential knowledge to what I call stable counterfactuals of freedom: ones that are true not merely in some complete circumstance but in every circumstance reachable by God's acting on them, so that a providentially feasible world has to be a fixed point of God's own use of His knowledge. I think this buys Molinism real dialectical room against open theism and against the Reformed worry that middle knowledge leaves God's plan hostage to creaturely luck, but it also costs something I haven't fully priced: it shrinks the set of feasible worlds and it may make God's knowledge depend on counterfactuals about His own future action.

The room will be about twelve people and I can predict its composition fairly well. Two of them are open theists who think God takes genuine risks and that the future is partly unsettled; they will press the grounding worry hard, but their real interest is whether my view really differs from theirs once stability is imposed. Two others are Reformed readers who think Molinism gives God less control than a doctrine of providence requires, and who will want to know whether stability makes God's plan even more hostage to luck than before, and whether a God who can only act on fixed points is a God who chooses anything. The visiting metaphysician will ask whether the operator I'm using is well defined at all, given that the set of reachable circumstances depends on what God does, which depends on which counterfactuals He knows, and she will also ask whether would-freely is a counterfactual conditional in the first place rather than a primitive disposition or a primitive fact about agency. There is also an epistemologist in the group who will want the view stated in terms of what God can know and when, and a couple of graduate students who know the literature well enough to spot a standard move I've quietly redescribed.

What I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones I would get from a general analytic metaphysics audience — ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, in their vocabulary, with a reply I can give in about ninety seconds and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes of the session rather than being forced into, and what the one reply is that has to land for the chapter to survive at all. Please also include the question I'm hoping nobody asks; my own candidate is whether stability collapses my view back into simple foreknowledge, or into a requirement that God know counterfactuals of His own freedom, but if you see a worse one, use it. Don't give me citations or a bibliography — I know the literature and I'll fill those in myself — and don't spend words summarizing Molinism back to me.
