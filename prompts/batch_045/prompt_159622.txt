I'm the secretary of the Research Permitting Committee of the Alder Mere National Nature Reserve, and I have to circulate a draft decision memo by Friday for a vote ten days later. The item is the vegetation-monitoring protocol for the coming field season. The reserve is roughly circular, about 1.2 km in radius, with a concentric core zone of radius 0.6 km that is closed to disturbance because of ground-nesting birds. The protocol requires each season's straight-line transects to be located at random, and our permit from the regional authority caps core incursions at no more than two in five transects. A chord of the reserve enters the core exactly when its length exceeds about 2.08 km, and the committee has split four ways over how the random transects should be drawn — and hence over how often they will enter the core. One faction wants two points picked independently and uniformly on the boundary and joined (they say this gives a 33% incursion rate); a second wants a radius picked uniformly and then a point uniformly along it, with the transect drawn perpendicular to that radius (50%); a third wants the transect's midpoint picked uniformly inside the reserve (25%); and a fourth, backed by our treasurer, wants indifference applied directly to the only variable the budget depends on — sample the transect length uniformly between 0 and 2.4 km and then place the chord — which they say gives about 13%. Each faction insists that its procedure is the one that treats every possible transect equally, and each has produced a spreadsheet showing that the four procedures disagree not only about incursions but about mean transect length (roughly 1.5, 1.9, 1.6 and 1.2 km respectively), so the choice moves real money and determines whether we are inside or outside our permit.

Two further complications have come up. First, the board's ecologist has pointed out that the reserve boundary is not actually a circle, and that a rule stated only as pick a random transect becomes even less determinate on an irregular shape; she wants the protocol rewritten so that it specifies an operation the field team can actually perform, and notes that the team's existing habit is to walk in from a randomly chosen point on the perimeter (a random distance from the north gate, paced out) on a randomly chosen compass bearing, which one member has calculated reproduces the first faction's 33% exactly. Second, a colleague from the university's maths department told us that this is just Bertrand's paradox and that the principle of indifference is to blame; the chair's response was that this diagnosis is fine as far as it goes but does not tell us which number to write into the protocol, since in his words every scheme is a way of being indifferent to something. A fifth member has suggested we pick whichever distribution has the largest entropy subject to the symmetries of the reserve, but no one on the committee can say what that would select here, or whether the symmetries of a bounded reserve pin anything down at all.

Please act as a philosopher of probability and scientific method who has experience advising technical standard-setting bodies: think the case through carefully, and before drafting check each faction's arithmetic and tell me in the memo if any of the four numbers is wrong. What I need is the decision memo itself — addressed to the committee, recommending approve, approve with modifications, or return for revision, and stating which sampling rule to adopt, in operational terms a field technician could follow, with a time frame for re-registering the protocol and for reviewing it after the season. It should give the committee reasons they can vote on, including a genuine statement of the strongest argument for the option you are rejecting (I expect that is either the chair's point that the choice is conventional and so should be made on robustness grounds, or the proposal to abandon continuous randomisation altogether and sample instead from a finite pre-drawn list of transects), and it should be short enough — under about 700 words — for four non-philosophers to read before a vote. Begin the memo with a single sentence giving your bottom line, then give the reasoning; no preamble, no restatement of my question, and no closing summary of what you have argued.
