I'm a philosophy PhD student working on a dissertation chapter about expected-value fanaticism — the thesis that for every positive probability p and every finite value U there is some finite value V such that a p-chance of V is better than a certainty of U, so that no chance is too small to be outweighed by a large enough stake. I've built an argument that appears to refute it, and I don't believe the conclusion, partly because the argument seems to refute a great deal else besides. Call a "story" a hypothesis linking one of my available actions to its consequences, carrying both a prior probability and a stake.

(1) Fanaticism, as just defined.
(2) In any actual decision my evidence does not fix a single probability for the hypothesis that action a yields V; it fixes a class of stories. For every n there are stories compatible with my evidence, of description length around n and hence prior weight of order 2^-n, on which a yields value of order 2^n. This is the only unboundedness fanaticism needs.
(3) For every such story H there is a mirror story H*, of the same description length up to an additive constant, on which a yields the matching disvalue: a description can be negated at no extra cost, and nothing about simplicity favors the benign sign.
(4) So for every action a and every n, the prior-weighted contributions of the pro-a and anti-a stories of length n cancel.
(5) Since this holds for every a, the fanatic's ranking of her actions is settled by nothing but the residual asymmetry between the mirrored families — a constant number of bits, unknowable in practice and arbitrary in principle.
(6) But fanaticism was supposed to be the view on which stakes, not fine detail of the prior, settle the choice. A view whose verdicts turn on unknowably small asymmetries between equally simple hypotheses is not that view but its denial. So fanaticism collapses into its own opposite.
(7) The template is not specific to fanaticism. Run (2)–(5) with any stakes-sensitive principle — or with a trivial choice such as whether to take an aspirin, where every story on which the aspirin prevents a headache has an equal-length mirror on which it triggers an astronomical catastrophe — and deliberation collapses in exactly the same way. It plainly does not.

So: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep (1) and (2) if I can. I oscillate between (3) and (5) without being able to tell whether they are two mistakes or one, and I want to know why whichever step is at fault looked innocent. I have also been toying with a "length-stake bound": a story of description length n may carry stakes of at most 2^(n/2), so that prior weight times stake converges again and the mirrored sums stay finite. Does that bound survive step (2), or is it bounded utility under another name — and if it is, is that a price I should simply pay?
