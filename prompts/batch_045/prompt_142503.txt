i keep running into explanations of the form 'X is a costly signal of Y' — the peacock's tail, big-game hunting and meat sharing, fasts and other painful religious requirements, a degree as a filter foremployers, buying a watch you can't read the time off at a glance. sometimes I buy it, sometimes it reads like a just-so story with a bit of algebra stapled on, and I can't tell which from the paper. I don't want to read the primary literature on every case; I want a filter.

so write me the spec. what does a costly-signalling explanation of an actual behavior have to establish before it deserves to be believed, with the case or consideration that makes each item a requirement rather than a taste? I mean the version of the claim where someone says they've explained a real trait in the world, not the version where they've shown that some equilibrium exists in a model.

things I think belong on the list, argue with the framing:

- that the trait has the job of changing what a reciever does, as opposed to being a cue others have learned to exploit. (I want the case that shows why this is hard, not the definition.)
- the 'why doesn't a worse individual just do the same thing' question. my hypothesis is that the only real answer is about marginal cost differing across qualities, and that papers which just establish 'it is expensive' and stop have shown nothing. tell me if total cost ever does the work on its own, and if so in what kind of case.
- that the audience is named, that it responds, and that responding pays it — plus what to do when there are audiences with opposed interests reading the same display.
- waste: does the cost have to be genuinely wasteful, or only differential? is that a requirement or a Zahavi-vs-everyone-else preference?
- that it says what would distinguish it from the explanations that predict the same trait without any signalling at all (runaway, sensory bias, direct benefit, plain mechanical constraint). this is the one I care about most — I want the list to block the failure mode where everything turns out to be a signal.
- the origin/maintenance split: is 'how it got started' a separate requirement from 'what keeps it stable', or can one answer cover both?

and here's the case I most want the spec to handle, because I don't think the standard list fits it: the synthetic handicap. costs that are imposed from outside and that track stake or resources rather than the quality being advertised — proof-of-work, buy-in fees, an initiation ordeal that anyone can survive if they are rich enough — and, the one I actually care about, long-form writing and video now that fluent text can be generated for approximately nothing. my guess is that these violate the 'cost tracks quality' requirement while still doing real signalling work, so either that requirement is non-negotiable and proof-of-work isn't signalling, or it needs to split into two requirements. if you split it, tell me what the weaker one has to say and what would still fail it.

mark each requirement non-negotiable or negotiable, and flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — my candidate is the honesty condition against the existence condition, since making the signal costly enough to deter mimics may make it too costly for the real thing to be worth emitting, but there may be a better pair. no citations, I can't check them and I don't want them. a short table at the end scoring the handful of standard explanatory templates — handicap-style, index/mechanical, runaway — against the list is fine, but the list is the deliverable.
