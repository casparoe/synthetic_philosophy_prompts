There's a guy on our county farm board, Dale, grows corn and soy, and every time pesticides come up he defends them with a medical analogy that I cant get a grip on. He says the field is the patient, the pest is the infection, the pesticide is the drug, and the grower is the doctor. Then he gets three conclusions out of it:

1. Non-target deaths are just side effects, and side effects are acceptable when the treatment is what saves the patient, so worries about bees and soil bugs are a dosing and timing problem, not a moral one. He says he scouts and only sprays at threshold, like a doctor waiting on symptoms.
2. Resistance is exactly like antibiotic resistance, so the real obligation is stewardship: rotate modes of action, keep refuges, dont burn a chemical out. He also points out that the organic growers near him spray Bt and will breed resistant insects just as surely, so nobody gets to opt out of the arms race.
3. People pushing broad bans are anti-vaxxers in a different costume: theyd let the patient die to keep their own hands clean.

I keep throwing three things at him and he bats all of them away with the same analogy, which makes me think the analogy is doing more work than it can hold.

First: most of his corn goes in the ground as seed already coated with a neonicotinoid, applied to every acre whether or not any pest ever shows up. When I said that isnt treatment, he said its vaccination, prevention beats cure. But routine prophylactic antibiotics in livestock feed is the textbook example of bad stewardship in medicine, so I think his own analogy convicts him here, unless the vaccination mapping is the right one after all.

Second: I read somewhere about people proposing a levy on insecticides that scales with the estimated number of insects killed, with some kind of sentience weighting so you only count animals past a certain nervous-system threshold. If pests really are germs, that levy is nonsense, it would be like taxing chemotherapy by cell count. But if they might be subjects with experiences, cell count is exactly the wrong model. I want to know which reading the medical frame actually supports, or whether it just has no resources here.

Third: there are ways of controlling pests that dont kill them outright, sterilizing them so the population crashes, and slow modes of action where the insect stops feeding or fails to moult and dies over days. Medicine treats drugs that merely stop a pathogen from reproducing as morally on a par with drugs that kill it; nobody thinks a bacteriostatic drug is kinder to the bacteria. So under his analogy theres no reason to prefer sterilization, but under any view where the insects matter there is a big one. That looks like a place the analogy is quietly deciding the answer instead of describing it.

So can you audit it properly. Give me what maps onto what, where the mapping breaks, and what the medical frame smuggles in that the field lacks: a doctor and a patient who share a goal, an agreed notion of health, consent, somebody with a duty of care. Then tell me which of his three conclusions survives and which doesnt, whether hes right about the seed treatment, whether the kill-count levy and the sterilization question have any analogue in medicine or fall outside it completely, and whether theres a better analogy, warfare or fishing or landlord and tenants or something, that carries the parts he needs without the parts that dont belong.
