I have just come away from a long and rather inconclusive argument with a friend — a theoretical computer scientist who works on multi-agent learning, and who is far more willing than I am to take acausal trade seriously as a guide to action. The sticking point was not the familiar business that one can settle in an afternoon: we both grant that if you are confident you are facing a near-exact counterpart of yourself, in a decision problem you both know you are in, and neither of you will ever causally affect the other, there is a decent case for acting as though you were bargaining. The sticking point was how far that case generalises once the counterpart stops being a copy. Her proposal, which she cheerfully admits is half-baked, is a specification tariff: the acausal-trade weight you assign to a possible counterpart should decay in the length of the shortest description that picks that counterpart out from among all the agents you might in principle be trading with. A literal copy of you, or an agent running one step behind you in an identical sandbox, gets something close to full weight; a mind on the far side of the galaxy who happens to be reasoning about an abstractly similar problem gets a weight on the order of two to the minus n, where n is the number of bits it takes to single her out. I objected on two grounds — that the tariff is either already implicit in the probability you assign to the counterpart existing and being relevantly similar, in which case it is bookkeeping rather than a real constraint, or else it is doing far too much work, since the cheaper counterparts are to describe, the more of them there are, and I cannot see what stops the sum from exploding or collapsing. She replied that I was conflating a discount on each term with a bound on the sum, and we left it there.

The reason I am putting this to you is that I have agreed to help a small philanthropic foundation scope a research programme on cooperation between agents that cannot communicate: cases where each party knows roughly what the other is deciding, expects never to exchange a message or a causal influence, and may not even know whether the other exists. There is room for something like five or six postdoctoral researchers for two years, drawn from philosophy, decision theory and economics rather than machine learning, and the money is supposed to go to conceptual work whose outcome would actually change someone's mind about what to do — for instance, whether a lab deploying an advanced system has any reason at all to make it disposed to cooperate with systems it will never meet, or how much weight to give the thought that one's own choices are evidence about the choices of agents one can never observe.

So, in British English please, what I want is a defended agenda rather than a survey of the landscape: which three or four open questions in this area most deserve those two years, and in what order? I would like the ordering argued for rather than asserted — which criteria you are using (how much downstream judgement turns on the answer, whether two years of conceptually trained researchers can realistically move it, how neglected it is relative to its importance, how many other questions depend on it), how you are trading those criteria off against one another, and where the tariff line of argument sits in the list, if it belongs there at all. I am equally interested in what you would deliberately leave out: questions that look gripping but whose resolution either way would not change what anyone believes or does downstream, or that are really empirical questions wearing conceptual clothing. And for each item on your list, say what sort of result or evidence over the next two years would move it up or down. Please do not give me a reading list or a set of citations to chase — I have no library access where I am writing this, and I want your reasoning, not signposts.
