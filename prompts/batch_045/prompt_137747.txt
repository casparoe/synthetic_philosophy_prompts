I've basically had the standard rationalist take on no-free-lunch cached since forever and I want somebody to actually try to break it instead of nodding along with it. Here's the position, as carefully as I can state it:

The NFL theorems are true but they're theorems about averages over *all* possible objective functions, or all possible data-generating distributions. Averaged over that space every learner does exactly as well as random guessing, because for every world where your bias helps there's a mirrored world where it hurts exactly as much. So the theorems are basically a tautology dressed up: they say nothing about the world we're actually in, and they give the skeptic nothing. The real content of the problem of induction, once you've seen this, is downgraded to a single contingent empirical question — why is *this* universe compressible, i.e. why does a simplicity prior / Occam-style bias actually pay here — and the answer is: it does, we have overwhelming evidence that it does, we're entitled to use our own lights to see that, and there's no further level at which we owe a justification. Solomonoff-flavored stuff and "shut up and multiply" do the rest. Bonus move that I also count as part of the view: the reason our inductive biases are locally tuned is that selection already bought them for us — Darwinian creatures get some bias for free, Popperian and Gregorian creatures buy more by testing hypotheses in their heads and in culture instead of in their genes — so the "why should our bias fit the world" question has an answer even before you get to compressibility.

What I want is a map of the exposure. Concretely:

1. What would count *against* this, ranked from genuinely fatal down to merely awkward. And I want the ranking defended, not just asserted.
2. I want you to separate what a smart defender of the view would themselves concede counts against it from what only critics say — I've read enough of these threads to know those two lists are different and the first one is the interesting one.
3. Whether the view has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that's a problem *for a view of this kind*. I don't think "unfalsifiable therefore worthless" is obviously right for something that's supposed to be a precondition of inquiry rather than an empirical hypothesis, but I also don't want to hide behind that. Be honest about which one I'm doing.

Some specific things I want you to chew on, including a couple I made up because I couldn't find anyone discussing them:

- **The compressibility bubble.** Call this the hypothesis that the compressible regularities are the low-hanging fruit — that we find the easy structure first, and what's left over is progressively closer to maxentropy or actively adversarial. Markets getting harder to beat, the sense that each extra order of magnitude of compute buys less than the last one did, the possibility that most of what's left in some mature field is not-law-shaped. If that's true, "the universe is compressible" stays true while its practical content drains out. Can the position even represent that as a defeater, or does it just say "then that domain is uncompressible, go do something else"? Is that a real answer or a motte?
- **Prior-exploitability.** You're an embedded agent, your prior is public, and an adversary gets to pick the data-generating process *after* looking at your prior. Security, markets, adversarial examples, an unfriendly AI. My sense is that in this regime NFL stops being vacuous, because the mirroring worlds aren't random draws — someone is steering you into the one where your bias is anti-correlated with the truth. Is that right? And does the standard reply ("adversaries are rare and expensive to build") itself rest on an inductive assumption, i.e. is it circular in a way that matters?
- **The meta-lunch question.** There's a move where you say: don't pick a prior, run all the induction methods and weight them by track record, i.e. do induction about which induction to do. I want to know whether that level has its own free lunch problem, and if the regress has to stop somewhere, whether stopping there is a solution or just a place where we got tired.
- **The grue gradient.** Goodman's grue/bleen thing is normally presented as a one-off puzzle about which predicates are projectible. Imagine it instead as an empirical regime: the projectible predicates in some domain are systematically the *complicated* ones, and simple predicates degrade smoothly as you extend them. What happens to the view then, and how would we notice?
- One mundane thing I don't want hand-waved: in actual ML practice NFL-style results seem to *bite* — no optimizer beats another across all benchmarks, hand-tuned inductive bias keeps losing to scale, the bitter-lesson crowd keeps being right. Is that evidence the theorems aren't vacuous after all, or is it a totally separate phenomenon that just looks similar?

The thing that would personally move me most, if you can find it: a concrete case where a community held a simplicity prior for a long time and was systematically beaten by a more complicated alternative for *principled* reasons, not just pragmatically — or a straight argument that the choice of reference machine changes predictions we'd actually make within our lifetimes. Conversely, tell me which of my defeater candidates a defender is right to just laugh off.
