Hey — I'm on a tiny funding committee (five people, we move maybe 800k a year, almost all of it small grants to independent researchers and one or two PhD students), and we're trying to decide where next year's money on AI existential risk should go. I've read all the standard blog-post versions of the case for and against, so please skip the part where you explain that a superintelligent system might disempower humanity — assume I've heard it, and assume I've also heard the replies about how speculative it all is.

What I'm stuck on is that nearly every application we get seems to presuppose what a friend of mine has started calling the pivotal agent picture: there's some future system, or a handful of them trained by one or two labs, that's decisive enough that its goals basically determine what happens to the world, so what matters is whether those goals are the right ones. My friend's alternative — call it the janitor scenario — is that very little of the expected loss actually runs through a single decisive agent. On that picture the world gets restructured by enormous numbers of systems that are each individually too weak and too narrow to seize anything: they write most of the code, set most of the prices, draft most of the contracts and the regulations, do most of the hiring and the triage, and nobody is ever in a position to choose the overall direction because there is no overall direction, only the residue of a million locally-reasonable automated decisions plus the institutional incentives that adopted them. A related worry I can't shake: the orthogonality thesis (intelligence and final goals come apart) seems almost trivially true, but only decision-relevant if the goals of the systems in question are load-bearing for what happens; and instrumental convergence as usually stated — agents with a wide range of final goals will want more resources, self-preservation, and so on — doesn't obviously apply to a system boxed into a narrow task with no reason to want anything beyond doing that task well. Which makes me wonder whether that whole apparatus is smuggling in the pivotal-agent assumption rather than supporting it.

So: suppose you were advising us, and suppose you put something like 40% on the janitor-ish picture being where most of the expected loss lives and 60% on the pivotal-agent picture. Which open questions should we actually be funding, and in what order? I care much less about the list than about the reasoning — for each one, what makes it upstream of other questions, whether it's tractable for the kind of people we can actually pay (philosophers, economists, a few ML people without big compute; nothing that needs interpretability access to frontier models), how neglected it is relative to its importance, and what we might observe over the next eighteen months that would move it up or down the list. And I'd especially like the places where the two pictures give genuinely different answers about where the money goes: if your ranking would come out roughly the same under either one, say that and tell me why I'm wrong to treat the distinction as decision-relevant at all.
