I've somehow ended up on the county biodiversity advisory group — I think I just kept turning up to the BioBlitz — and last week my sister's kid asked me how many species are in the woods behind their school, and I realised I have been confidently counting things for three years without being able to say what I was counting. Then we got into it properly: I say the two look-alike populations of a thing in the marsh are one species, a botanist on the committee says definately two, and I now think we were answering different questions rather than disagreeing.

So: I want a one-page handout, a decision tree rather than prose, that sorts someone into a position on what a species is. Audience is volunteers with binoculars and a hand lens, no philosophy background. Rules:

Every branch node has to be a question someone could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and phrased about a concrete case rather than about theory — no "do you think species are real?" One more column on each leaf: could a volunteer actually apply this on a Saturday morning, yes/no/maybe. Leaves must include the interbreeding-and-isolation view, the ecological-role view, the smallest-diagnosable-cluster / shared-ancestry view, the "it's one lineage, and the criteria are just evidence for it" view, outright pluralism, and the skeptic who thinks a species is a convenient drawer we put specimens in. Each leaf gets its usual name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

The cases the tree has to actually route, and I want at least four of them used as branch questions: six populations of a salamander spread round a valley, each neighbouring pair interbreeds fine, but the two that meet at the north end don't interbreed at all and live side by side; a clonal all-female crayfish lineage that started from a single mutant individual a few decades ago, morphologically distinct, no sex anywhere in its life cycle; a fertile self-sustaining willow at Kestrel's Point that arose from hybridisation between two recognised species and doesn't breed with either parent; the lichen on the church wall, which is a fungus plus an alga, where the same fungus turns up elsewhere with a different partner; microbes in the hot springs swapping genes sideways.

Then, after the tree: a small table I've started calling the lathe question. For each leaf, which of these three it actually answers — where a species starts, what belongs to it at a given time, when it ends — and for the ones it doesn't answer, which other concept it is silently borrowing an answer from. Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, and any combination of answers the tree allows that nobody defends — with your guess whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Don't bother with who said what first or any bibliography, names as labels only.

Just start with the tree itself — first line is the top question, no preamble and no "here's a taxonomy" — and put the table, the flags and anything you want to warn me about underneath it.
