I have been asked to run a ninety-minute session for the lay members of a regional health prioritisation panel — eight people, mixed backgrounds, two of them clinicians, none of them economists — who must vote at the end of the month on which of two programmes to fund. Nobody will have read anything beforehand. They will arrive holding both of the following at once: that the question is easy ("ask each group how much better off they'd be, out of ten") and that it is hopeless ("you cannot get inside someone else's head, so treat everyone the same"). I want them to leave able to locate what is actually difficult about interpersonal comparisons of utility, and to see that the difficulty is not where they think it is.

Plan the session; do not write the explanation out, and do not summarise the topic for me. What I need is:

1. The order the ideas must come in, and what depends on what. Specifically: why the difference between ranking a person's own outcomes and measuring how much better one outcome is for them than another has to be in place before a social decision can be seen to need more than a ranking; why the three things a comparison might have to deliver — who is better off, whose gain is larger, and how many smaller gains offset one larger gain — are three different demands rather than three versions of one demand, and which of the three this panel's vote will covertly require; and why the point that inserting a common currency (money, willingness to pay, quality-adjusted life years) relocates the comparison rather than dissolving it must land before, not after, someone proposes a currency.

2. The one case to carry the whole session, with what it must have and what it must lack. I have been using a choice between funding joint replacements for older people in chronic pain and funding talking therapies for younger people with severe depression. Tell me whether that carries it, what such a case needs — two groups whose self-reports are not comparable in the obvious way, a trade-off that survives every attempt to find a common currency — and what it must lack: no cash equivalent, no single identifiable victim, nothing that lets the panel settle it by counting heads or by appealing to need alone. If another case does the work better, give it and say why.

3. The misconceptions in the order they will surface, and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. The ones I anticipate: that comparing across persons would require knowing what it is like to be the other person; that asking everyone to use the same zero-to-ten scale already solves the problem; that anchoring the top and bottom of each person's scale settles the middle, when the scale's shape between the anchors may still differ; that an inability to compare licences treating everyone alike; and finally that if full comparisons are unavailable then no comparison is available. That last one matters most to me, because a panel that concludes "no comparison is possible" and one that concludes "any comparison will do" behave identically in the vote. Relatedly, I want them to leave with the thought that some decisions can be made without a full scale, by identifying cross-personal differences that survive every admissible rescaling of the individuals' own scales — tell me what has to be on the table before that thought is intelligible, and where it fits.

4. One question or short task for the final ten minutes whose wrong answers are diagnostic rather than merely blank, and which separates someone who has grasped the problem from someone who has merely learned to say that utilities are not comparable.

5. A short list of what you would openly refuse to cover in ninety minutes, phrased as I could say it aloud to the panel.

Set the plan out in five numbered sections matching the above, in British English, and keep the whole thing under 800 words.
