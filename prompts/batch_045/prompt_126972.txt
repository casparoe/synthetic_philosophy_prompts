I run a small remote team, nine of us, and we've been arguing about whether to install one of those monitoring tools: screenshot every ten minutes, keystroke logging, and a focus score on a dashboard that everyone can see, including each other. I got curious and ended up reading about Bentham's Panopticon and how Foucault uses it, and now every time I mention this someone says the same thing — that's literally the panopticon, the whole point is you end up policing yourself, the guard doesn't even have to be in the tower, and power gets cheaper and more thorough the less it's actually exercised. People then use that to conclude two things I'm not sure follow: (a) that the real damage is the self-policing rather than the watching, and (b) that there's no point resisting because there's no one in charge to resist, it's just a mechanism that runs whoever happens to be operating it.

Can you audit the analogy properly? First what maps onto what — who or what plays the guard, the tower, the light, the cells, the inspection, the architect. Then where it breaks in a case like ours, and, more usefully, which of those breaks actually matter for (a) and (b) and which are just details.

The bits I keep going round in circles on:

- In Bentham's building the mechanism seems to depend on you being able to see the tower but never being able to check whether anyone's in it at this particular moment. Our dashboard shows a live number to everyone, so the uncertainty is gone and replaced by a score that's permanently there. Is that still panoptic self-policing, or is it a different animal — closer to a league table or a credit score, where it's the comparison with everyone else and the norm that does the work?

- The guard: nobody actually watches the screenshots, a rule just flags anyone who drops below a threshold. So what happens when the tower is empty in a stronger sense — say the data gets collected and no one ever looks at it and no one could, its just stored. If the effect on behaviour survives that, then the belief is doing all the work and the story holds. If it doesn't survive, the analogy needs an actual or at least possible gaze somewhere in it. Which way does it go?

- The gaze partly runs backwards in our setup too. We can all see each other's scores and the managers is on there as well. Bentham's arrangement is strictly one way. Does making it symmetric turn it into something else entirely, or is it still discipline because what's really watching is the norm?

I also want to know what would count as the analogy failing. Someone explained to me that Foucault meant the panopticon as a diagram of a mechanism of power rather than as a description of a building, and if that's right I can't see what would ever show that some arrangement isn't panoptic — which makes me suspect it's doing less work in the argument than people think. Is that fair, or is there still a test?

And one more thing. The way it's been explained to me, the producing of knowledge and the exercising of power are supposed to be one operation for Foucault, not two things sitting next to each other. In our case the score doesn't seem to produce any real knowledge of anyone, it produces a number people have already started gaming by leaving a key held down. Does that break the power/knowledge half of the story, or is the gaming itself the proof that it's working?

So: does (a) and does (b) survive once the mismatches are on the table, and if the panopticon is the wrong picture for a shared dashboard with a score on it, what's the better one?
