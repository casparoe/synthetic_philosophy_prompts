At a meeting of the volunteer organisation I help run, I called out a fellow organiser — call him D — for repeatedly missing shifts he had signed up for, leaving the rest of us to cover for him. His reply was that I have no standing: last year I quietly padded an expense claim by a modest amount, and some years before that I missed commitments of my own. His brother, who is a lawyer, later spelled the point out for me using two doctrines: standing, which he glossed as requiring that you yourself were injured, by the very thing you complain of, in a way the court can remedy; and, separately, unclean hands, which he described as barring equitable relief to a plaintiff whose own conduct is dirty in the very matter he complains of — with the qualification, which he insisted on, that unrelated dirt about your general character is supposed to be irrelevant. The conclusion D wants, and that I have now heard in every argument since, is that a hypocrite's blame is simply void: the target owes it no uptake, may shrug it off, and is not answerable to the person blaming. People in glass houses, and so on.

What I want is an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on my conduct. Please lay the mapping out explicitly — a table of correspondences is fine — for what plays the plaintiff, the defendant, the injury, the court, the remedy, the connection requirement, and the forum's power to strike a complaint for want of standing. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. In particular: are standing and unclean hands doing the same work here, or does the analogy quietly need both, or is the popular version really an ancestor of neither? There is no institution that can dismiss my blame, no one compelled to listen, no remedy or coercion I am enlisting, and blame is addressed at least as often to third parties or to oneself as to its target. Which of those disanalogies actually bear on D's conclusion and which are idle for his purposes?

Then three cases I cannot fit. First, connection: the dirt he taxes me with, padding expenses, is not in the same normative neighbourhood as what I blamed him for, whereas his missed-commitments charge is much closer to my own history; does the analogy support a domain-restricted loss of standing, or does hypocrisy taint all of a blamer's blame at once? Second, derivative dirt: my own pattern of shirking was in part picked up from watching him, years ago, before I reformed — may a blamer invoke wrongdoing that he himself produced in the blamer? Third, third-party blame: I was speaking largely on behalf of the people who covered his shifts rather than for myself, and I gather the law has devices for next friends and third-party standing, so I want to know whether the analogue helps me or whether not being the one wronged cuts the other way. I should add that I disclosed my own record up front and said I knew how easy the rationalisation is; whether that restores standing or makes it worse, I cannot tell. Finally: if the conclusion survives, does it mean only that I may not blame, or also that my blame was false or unwarranted, and does it constrain what I may think or only what I may say? And if the legal comparison is the wrong one, is there a better analogy — blame as a demand made from within a relationship, as a speech act with felicity conditions, as calling in a debt — that preserves whatever is true in the glass-houses slogan without delivering the conversation-stopper?
