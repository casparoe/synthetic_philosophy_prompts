hi — im taking a course on equality and anti-discrimination law this term and I'm trying to work out what I actually think about quotas, because my gut verdicts don't line up and I've got a short position paper due next week. Please answer in British English, and please don't give me citations or a reading list, I'll fill those in myself.

Here's where I land, case by case.

Norway's rule that around 40% of board seats in larger companies have to be held by women, with real penalties attached (I think at the extreme a non-compliant company could be forced to wind up?): fine, I'm for it.

Legacy preferences at US universities, kids of alumni: against, strongly. Cleanest case of something that should just be scrapped.

Race-conscious holistic admissions at US universities: for it — and I'm for it even though it isn't a quota, isn't aimed only at descendants of people that university wronged, and the beneficiaries include children of wealthy recent immigrants.

Reservations in India for the Scheduled Caste, Scheduled Tribe and Other Backward Class categories in higher education and public jobs: for it, and I'm for it even though eligibility runs by descent and today's beneficiary typically isn't someone who was wronged themselves. (This is the pair that bothers me: inherited group membership is exactly what makes legacy preferences odious, and here I'm fine with it.)

The creamy layer cut-off, screening out the better-off inside the reserved categories: for it as applied to OBCs. But the fairly recent Supreme Court ruling that states should also identify and exclude a creamy layer within SC and ST — I'm against that, or at least uneasy, since caste stigma doesn't dissolve with income. Which sits badly with my reason for liking the OBC exclusion.

A statutory 40% quota for men among newly hired primary-school teachers: against. Feels patronising and pointless. But if I'm for Norway's rule I should be for this too, unless the real difference is that male-coded roles are the high-status ones. I can't tell if that's a distinction or just my politics.

A medical school reserving places for applicants from historically excluded groups with lower entrance scores, on the grounds that they're likelier to practise in underserved areas: for it — but I feel queasy about the condition. I don't want to say that a beneficiary who ends up a private dermatologist in a rich neighbourhood was wrongly admitted.

Shortlist rules (the pool must be 40% women, the final pick unconstrained): I like these much more than outcome quotas and would accept them where an outcome quota is a straitjacket, e.g. three-person teams. Principle, or squeamishness?

Norway's rule should stay, now that it's roughly two decades old and boards are near parity; but a numeric admissions target at a US university should sunset once the class is representative. My shaky reason: the board quota changed what counts as qualified, it created a pool of experienced women directors, so repeal would partly revert; an admissions target doesn't do that.

Elite capture — Norway's quota mostly helps already-rich, credentialed women — bothers me much less than the parallel worry about Indian reservations bothers people in my seminar. Maybe because for boards the point is who sits in the room, not improving the prospects of the worst-off.

My tentative principle: a quota is justified when (a) the group's under-representation is itself a symptom of an unjust hierarchy, (b) the constraint bites at the stage of selection where the hierarchy does its damage, which is often the shortlist rather than the final pick, and (c) there's a built-in review point. But (c) collides with my "Norway should stay" verdict, (b) doesn't explain why I like outcome quotas for boards, and I don't think (a) handles the men-as-teachers case at all.

So: is there one principle — or a small set of candidates — that gets most of these? Please state it precisely, say which of my verdicts motivates which clause, be honest about which verdicts it forces me to drop, and tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: (i) extending Indian-style reservations to private employers who did no discriminating themselves; (ii) a quota on a brand-new institution where the imbalance is predicted rather than inherited — say reserving seats on a new AI oversight body for people from countries that had no hand in building the systems. And if you think I'm asking the wrong question, or that some of these cases aren't really about quotas at all, say that instead.
