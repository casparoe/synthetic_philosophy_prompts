I'm on a hiring committee for the first time and the way we read the letters is messing with my head. Every letter is glowing, so the committee reads them for what they don't say: 'she is reliable and always turns things in on time' gets treated as a warning, and we calibrate to each writer — from someone who calls every student 'the best I've ever taught', even 'excellent' comes out lukewarm. Everyone in the field knows the letters are inflated, everyone knows that everyone knows, and the system keeps working anyway. Meanwhile the same university reads its own contracts and policies word for word, and when a candidate complains about a rejection we are told to point out that nothing we wrote was false. What view of meaning or communication does all this presuppose? Does reading letters this way commit us to saying that what a writer communicates goes beyond what her sentences literally say, or can you get the same result from literal content plus facts about her incentives and what she would otherwise have written? What is actually carrying the inference — shared maxims, the options she had, or our model of her? And what would be lost if we replaced letters with a form that forced explicit comparative rankings?
