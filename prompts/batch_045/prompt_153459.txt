I'm retired now — 30-odd years as an experimental chemist, mostly spectroscopy — and I've started reading philosophy of science in the evenings since I finally have the time. No formal training, so please go easy on the jargon. Here's roughly where I've landed on laws of nature, and I want a consistency check before I try to defend any of it at my reading group:

(1) Laws dont govern anything. There are no necessary connections in nature, no "nomic glue" — a law is just a compact description of the pattern the particular facts happen to fall into, and nothing more.
(2) But there's a real, objective law/accident distinction: F=ma is a law, "all the coins in my pocket are silver" isn't, and neither is "every gold sphere is less than a mile across" — and this isn't merely about what's useful or interesting to us. I also want to keep calling the ideal gas law a law, even though every real gas deviates from it a little.
(3) The right way to draw that line is the best-system story: the laws are the axioms of whatever system packages up the particular facts with the best trade-off between simplicity and informative strength. And which system wins is an objective matter — simplicity isn't in the eye of the beholder, it's a fact about the theory.
(4) Quantum probabilities are real single-case features of the world, not ignorance about hidden variables.
(5) Laws are contingent: the total pattern of events could have been different, so the laws could have been different, and that causes me no sleepless nights.
(6) Laws explain what happens and they support counterfactuals — if I'd let go of the sample it would have fallen — and that's part of what makes something a law rather than a mere regularity.

So: which pairs here are really in tension, and which only look like it? Rank them by how bad they are, say for each what would have to give and what it costs me, and then tell me whether there's a single revision that cleans up the most. I genuinely dont know where the weak spot is, which is the whole point of asking.
