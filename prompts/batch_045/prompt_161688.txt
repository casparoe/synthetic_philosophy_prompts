You are a professional philosopher who is equally at home in welfare economics, social choice theory, and the psychometrics of subjective well-being measurement. Please think carefully before writing, and do some private brainstorming first: use your early reasoning to work out where the two positions below genuinely diverge, what the smallest set of cruxes is, and which of the apparent disagreements are really disputes about the words "utility," "measurement," or "rational." I do not want a literature survey, a history of the debate, or a reading list — I want a draft document that two real people could sign.

The setting: I sit on a technical advisory panel for a national health technology assessment body. Our remit is narrow but consequential — we allocate a fixed budget across patient groups, and our current practice ranks interventions by expected quality-adjusted life years gained, with an optional extra weight on gains to people who are worse off in baseline health. Two panel members have been arguing about this for a year without moving. One — call her the Sceptic, working in the ordinalist tradition that holds interpersonal comparisons of utility to have no scientific basis — says that the numbers we feed into the ranking are constructed by convention, that the rankings survive only because we have quietly stipulated comparability, and that the "extra weight for the worse off" is a moral parameter smuggled in under a measurement label. The other — call him the Comparabilist, working in the cardinal welfare tradition — replies that we compare people all the time and could not run a health service otherwise, that a single decision-maker's extended sympathy or impartial judgement is all that interpersonal comparison has ever meant, and that modern well-being instruments give us approximately interpersonally comparable scales. Both have agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to produce the skeleton document they will fill in and correct.

Two things I want built into the collaboration rather than left at the level of slogans. First, a measurement thought experiment I have been using to make the argument concrete. Suppose a future instrument — call it a W-index — that for each person i reports a number w_i related to true well-being by an increasing affine map with a person-specific unit and a person-specific zero point, so that the observer knows the shape but not the calibration. Now distinguish three informational grades: full comparability (units and zeros common); bounded-ratio unit comparability (each ratio of units known only to lie within a band, zeros still unknown); and ordinal non-comparability of levels and of differences. The question I want the two of them to face is which of our actual decision rules — plain sum-ranking, priority-weighted sum-ranking, and maximin over final well-being levels — is licensed by which grade, and whether the Sceptic's "no scientific basis" objection is really an objection at the level of zeros rather than units. Second, an empirical test I think both would accept as bearing on the dispute: three independent instruments — global life-satisfaction surveys, experience sampling of momentary affect, and trade-off or willingness-to-pay tasks — are administered to the same large, diverse sample using anchoring vignettes to correct for the fact that different people use response scales differently. If the interpersonal orderings and gain-ratios converge robustly across instruments and subpopulations, does that constitute evidence for a common scale, or is the Sceptic entitled to say it shows only coherence among our conventions? I want each side's prediction about that study written down before the fact.

Please draft the document: a numbered section of agreed claims (things both can sign about formalism, about what has to be explained, and about what our existing practice presupposes), followed by a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the Sceptic's own formulation of her position on it in wording she would accept, the Comparabilist's own formulation in wording he would accept, the argument, case, or evidence both agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction. Close with a short section identifying any part of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal and saying why, and — honestly — whether anything could end it, or whether the panel should simply adopt a convention and say so. Where you think a formulation would be slanted, say that explicitly rather than smoothing it over.
