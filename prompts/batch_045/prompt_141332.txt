I'm on the four-person advisory board of a small private foundation, and we're about to commit our last big grant cycle — roughly eight three-year postdoctoral fellowships, split between philosophy and psychology departments but explicitly not funding new data collection — to work on meaning in life. The board is split down the middle: two of us think the money should go where the conceptual action is (whether meaning requires objective worth or only a fitting kind of attraction, how meaning relates to happiness and to achievement, whether cosmic meaning and meaning in a particular life are even the same property), and two want work that would change how meaning gets invoked outside the academy — in end-of-life policy, in medicine, in how people are advised about careers and retirement, in the way institutions now advertise purpose to the people they employ. We can't do both. What I need from you is a defended ordering of the open questions, not a survey of the field: no citations, no reading recommendations, no names to go look up.

Two caveats about my own thinking, because I'd rather you engage with them than politely set them aside. First, I've become attached to something I've started calling the deputization model of felt meaning: the claim that for most people the sense that one's life matters is borrowed — you take yourself to be contributing to projects whose value you neither fully understand nor could defend on demand, and your confidence in them is a form of deference to institutions, disciplines, or admired individuals. If that is even roughly right, then a good deal of what gets measured as felt meaning may not be tracking what the theories are about, and questions about meaningful work may really be questions about justified deference. Second, I keep coming back to a striking asymmetry argued for at length in a recent book: that our projects would lose much of their point if humanity were about to die out, even if our own lives and the lives of everyone we cared about were unaffected. That opens a cluster of questions I don't see clearly ordered anywhere — whether meaning is essentially forward-looking or historical, whether a last generation could have meaningful lives, whether being forgotten matters, and whether meaning has any threshold at all rather than only coming in degrees, which is what several European jurisdictions seem forced to assume when they debate whether a life can be complete. Add to that the practical worry that much ordinary meaning currently comes from being needed and from productive contribution, and that this may become scarce, plus my suspicion that the familiar cosmic-indifference framing — the universe is vast and does not care — is a category mistake that survives on rhetorical force alone.

So: rank them. Which of these, and which ones I'm not listing, are worth three years of a smart person's time, and why? I want the criteria made explicit and the trade-offs among them surfaced — how upstream a question is, whether it is tractable in three years with conceptual tools and no new data, whether it is genuinely neglected or already crowded, and how much would change downstream if it were answered one way rather than the other. I also want to know which questions you'd deliberately leave off the list because their answers wouldn't move anything else, and which of the alleged implications of the absurdist tradition you think are overrated or don't follow at all. Tell me what would move your ranking up or down over the next few years, and name the one slot you'd spend on something speculative. Please write in American English, and aim for something around 1,200 words — dense rather than comprehensive.
