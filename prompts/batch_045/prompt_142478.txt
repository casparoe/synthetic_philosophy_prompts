Our book club is doing The Remains of the Day next month and it's landed a bit to close to home — my dad's business partner of twenty years is fairly clearly siphoning money out of the company, and dad keeps not opening the letters and changing the subject. I genuinely can't tell whether he knows and is managing it or whether he doesn't, and it matters a lot for what I do next.

So I want a reading of Stevens through the philosophy of self-deception. The basic paradox — how can one person be both deceiver and deceived, doesn't the deceiver have to know what he's hiding, in which case nothing is hidden — and the main families of answer: split the mind into parts, or drop the requirement that you ever hold the false belief and make it a matter of motivated reasoning. Which of those, if any, actually fits what the novel gives us? Is Stevens a case at all, or is loyalty to Darlington, dignity, professional training and being English in the 1950s enough to explain him without it?

The thing I really want tested: I've started calling the second kind attentional housekeeping — you never get yourself to believe the opposite, you just arrange your days, your routines, who you talk to, so the true thing is never the thing you're thinking about, and you'd stop if you caught yourself doing it. Does that count as self-deception or is it just... not getting round to it? Is it a solution to the paradox or a way of changing the subject?

And where does the book resist all this — his father dying upstairs during the conference, the Jewish maids, Miss Kenton, the way he handles being asked who he worked for, the end on the pier? Does the novel know something the philosophy doesn't?
