So I run a tiny decision-theory reading group — me, two grad students, a retired econ guy — and we've now spent four sessions on Parfit's hitchhiker and we are exactly where we were at the end of session one, which is making me think something other than the case is going on.

Where we're stuck, so you don't have to re-derive it: you're stranded in the desert, a driver will take you to town only if he thinks you'll pay him $100 once you're there, and once you're in town paying costs $100 and does nothing for you. Half the group says: pay — or rather, be the kind of person who pays — because rationality isn't only about the choice in front of you at the moment, it's about policies, plans, dispositions, being the sort of agent who gets rides (this is the strand that shows up in the literature as resolute choice, constrained maximization, planning agency; I don't need the citations and don't want them). The other half says that's just changing the subject: at the moment of choice in town every reason you have points one way, and if "rational" can require you to act against your all-things-considered reasons then "rational" has stopped doing any work. They're happy to recomend cultivating the disposition, but they say that's advice about what kind of agent to build or become, not an answer to "what is it rational for me to do here." And each side has a story about the other: the disposition people say the maximizers are confusing a theory of moment-by-moment choice with a theory of how to live; the maximizers say the disposition people are smuggling in a non-causal decision theory, one where your act at the later node is tied to the driver's earlier belief by something other than causation.

Since that sounded like the crux, I made up three variants to try to force it, and the depressing thing is that nobody moved on any of them — they just restated.

(1) The unobservable payment. Same setup, but you pay by dropping cash through a slot at the driver's house at 3am, he never learns whether you did, and you'll never meet again. No reputation, no repeat play, nothing downstream. Disposition people: still pay. Maximizers: obviously don't. Nobody budged.

(2) The face-reading driver. He's a perfect detector of your occurrent plan at the moment he makes the offer, and you know you'll re-deliberate from scratch in town, costlessly. Now "having the disposition" only helps if it makes you *seem* disposed at the roadside, and what makes you seem disposed is your current plan, not your character. The maximizers said: see, your story is really about appearing trustworthy, and that's a self-deception story, not a norm of rationality. The disposition people said no, it's about being the sort of agent whose plans survive re-deliberation, and that is literally what resolve is. Restated again.

(3) The lockbox. At the roadside there's a device that makes paying binding, and it costs $40. Everyone agrees you should use it iff the ride is worth more than $140, and everyone agrees that if it cost $150 you shouldn't. But when I said "ok, at $150 — once you're in town, is it rational to pay?", both halves just gave me their original answer back.

So here's my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: this isn't a disagreement about the case at all. It's a verbal or different-questions disagreement sitting on top of a prior fight about which decision-theoretic formalism is the right one, and the hitchhiker is just ammunition in that earlier war — which is why no variant of the case can move anybody. But I genuinely can't tell whether that's the whole story or whether there's a substantive residue: a bedrock disagreement about whether reasons are time-relative, or about whether "rationality" is a normative notion (tied to what you can control at the moment of action) versus an evaluative one (a ranking of agents and policies by how well they do), or whether the whole question is ill-posed.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey of positions and not a verdict on who's right — you can tell me who you think is right at the very end if you want. Concretely: where does the disagreement actually sit, given what each side says? What does it tell us that both sides are internally consistent across all three variants and yet their answers are invariant across them? Is the "rational irrationality" bit — the claim that rationality can require you to be disposed to act against your reasons later — a genuine paradox, a level confusion, or a real case of self-defeating normativity? Is my hypothesis right, partly right, or wrong? Is any hidden empirical or formal question doing the work? And what would count as progress — a distinction between levels of rationality, a formal result, reframing "what should I do" as "what should I be", or is it honest to say no progress should be expected, and why?
