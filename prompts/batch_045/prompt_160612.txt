I retired about four years ago after forty-odd years in an analytical chemistry lab — mostly infrared spectroscopy, instrument calibration, arguing with detectors — and since then I've been going to a fortnightly reading group at the philosophy department down the road, run by a postdoc named Marta. I like it because she actually commits to verdicts on cases instead of just laying out considerations and going home, which is the only bit of philosophy that reminds me of lab work. Over the term she's ruled on a pile of them: the man with early-onset Alzheimer's who keeps the museum address in a notebook he always carries — part of his mind; my own lab notebook, thirty years of calibration constants in my handwriting, consulted daily, trusted absolutely, none of it in my head any more — part of my mind; the phone numbers I typed into my phone myself years ago and now dial by tapping a name without ever having learned them — yes, part of my mind; my reading glasses — no, that's just fixing the input; a blind man's cane — yes, but grudgingly; a pocket calculator doing long division for someone who never checks it — yes; asking my wife to remind me of a value and acting on it immediately — no, that's testimony and not memory; the whiteboard in a shared lab that everyone reads and nobody owns — not part of anybody's mind, though she muttered something about groups that I didn't follow. When I pressed her for what she was actually doing, she said the three things she looks for are that the information is reliably available when needed, that it gets automatically endorsed rather than double-checked, and that it's easy to consult — but I don't think that's really what she's running, because it doesn't explain why she let the calculator in and kept my wife out.

Is the pipeline part of their minds?
