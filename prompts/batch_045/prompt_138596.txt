A friend and I argued late last night about how far an aesthetic judgement can be taken on trust. She has a critic whose verdicts have matched hers across something like a hundred films: when he calls a new release a masterpiece she now says she knows that it is one, and says so to other people before she has seen it herself. I said she is entitled to conclude that she will probably like it, but not that it is good — that you have to see for yourself. She replied that this scepticism would wreck much more than her cinema-going: newspapers print star ratings, websites average those ratings into a single score out of a hundred as though each expert verdict were a noisy measurement of one quantity, and publishers, prize juries and arts funders hand out contracts, prizes and grants on the strength of written reports from readers the decision-makers have never met, about work most of them will never experience.

I cannot settle the institutional half of that. So: what does this cluster of practices actually presuppose about aesthetic testimony? Does relying on a critic commit us to aesthetic judgement being transmissible by testimony at all, or is something weaker enough — his word as evidence about what my experience would be like, or about whether the work has features I care about, rather than as a licence to believe that it is good? Does it matter that the verdict arrives bare, as a rating or a score, rather than as a review that gives reasons and directs attention — do the two formats presuppose different things about what is being passed on? Where exactly does "worth seeing" stop and "is good" begin? And if it is true that nobody can know a work is good without experiencing it first-hand, what would those publishers, juries and funding panels actually have to change, and what would they lose?
