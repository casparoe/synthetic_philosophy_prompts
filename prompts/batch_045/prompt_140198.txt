i'm doing a seminar on animal communication this term and we spent a couple of weeks on Zahavi's handicap principle and honest signalling generally — the idea that a signal can only be trusted if it costs the signaller something, and specifically that the cost has to fall differentially on the wrong type of signaller, otherwise cheap talk takes over and the whole system collapses. My flatmate did a module on the First World War last year and we got into an argument at about 1am about whether the Christmas Truce of 1914 (and the wider live-and-let-live stuff along the Western Front — quiet periods, predictable shelling times, not shooting at ration parties, that kind of thing) is actually a decent example of honest signalling, or whether we're both just pattern-matching onto a story we happen to like. I want the careful version of that argument, not the pub version, and I'm happy to be told the whole framing is wrong.

So: give me the mapping properly. Who the signallers and receivers actually are (my hunch is that the frontline unit is the player and that higher command is a third party with a completely different payoff function, which already makes this not the standard two-player setup), what the signal set is — singing, showing a light, standing up on the parapet, walking out with a gift, not firing during a fixed window — and what exactly the cost is supposed to be, and to whom. The bit I can't get straight is whether exposing yourself in the open is a handicap in Zahavi's sense at all: the cost seems to be set by what the receiver does next rather than by anything intrinsic to the signaller, so it feels more like a vulnerability test or a hostage than a handicap — you'd only dare do it if you already believed the other lot weren't going to shoot, which makes it look like belief-bootstrapping rather than communication. Tell me which of those it is, and whether the distinction even matters for whether the honesty is stable.

Then tell me where the model breaks and what the case does back to the theory. Things I specifically want: what happens when there are two audiences whose interests conflict, given that being seen fraternising by your own side is a cost the enemy never imposes, and command later seems to have deliberately raised exactly that cost (rotating units, barrages scheduled over Christmas, threats of discipline) — is that the theory sticking its neck out with a risky prediction, or is third-party punishment just doing all the work? Whether the handicap principle explains anything here that a plain iterated reciprocity story doesn't do better, since these people expected to be facing each other for months. Whether the truce signals contained any waste at all — swapping tobacco and buttons looks mutually beneficial rather than costly, so is cost really necessary, or is differential cost enough? And the objection my seminar group kept banging on about, that any cost you can identify afterwards can be relabelled 'the handicap', which would make the theory unfalsifiable: is this a case where it predicts something we'd otherwise find surprising, or a post-hoc gloss? Please also be explicit about where the theory is simply silent — how a signal gets off the ground in the first place, how an audience learns to attend to it, what happens when a third party punishes the signal rather than the faker — instead of forcing it to speak. And don't hang any of it on the football kick-about; I gather that part is embellished at best.
