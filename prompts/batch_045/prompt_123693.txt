I am preparing a seminar session on radical interpretation and working from a chapter in a recent handbook on interpretation and the social sciences. The chapter is by a specialist, the account hangs together, and I cannot locate its weak joints, so I would like an independent reading. It says roughly this:

Imagine an interpreter who arrives among speakers of an unknown language equipped with nothing but his own language and his own beliefs. The only evidence available to him is the pattern of the speaker's assent and dissent: which sentences she holds true, and when. But beliefs and meanings cannot be assigned one at a time. A belief is identified partly by its place in a wider network, and the meaning of a sentence is read off the belief one would have to attribute in order to make the sentence come out true in the circumstances. So the interpreter must solve for both at once. Two constraints make that tractable. Coherence requires the attributed belief set to be logically and inductively in good order; charity requires the speaker to come out mostly right by the interpreter's lights. Charity is therefore an empirical assumption about how much speakers and interpreters share, and like any empirical assumption it can fail. Confronted with a population whose beliefs were systematically false by our lights, the method would simply break down and we would have to withhold interpretation altogether.

Three consequences follow, the author says. First, because the evidence fixes the truth conditions of sentences but not which objects satisfy the referring expressions, reference is inscrutable: assignments related by a proxy function fit the evidence equally well, so meaning is indeterminate in Quine's sense and there is no fact of the matter which of two adequate theories of meaning is the right one. Second, this is why the later appeals to triangulation — interpreter and speaker jointly responding to a common object in a common world — have to do the real work; they are an attempt to plug the hole that indeterminacy opens. Third, since charity is only an assumption of similarity, the celebrated argument against conceptual relativism — that we could make no sense of a conceptual scheme radically different from our own — is circular, because it builds the desired conclusion into the method used to reach it. The author then adds a proposal of his own. If charity is at bottom a maximizing instruction, the natural formalization is to select, among interpretations compatible with the evidence, the one that maximizes agreement between speaker and interpreter; on that reading, he says, radical interpretation turns out to be a special case of inference to the best explanation.

Two things in this make me uneasy, though I cannot say exactly why. I have seen it claimed that Davidson's constraint of charity is constitutive of interpretation rather than an empirical assumption that might turn out false, and that his talk of the inscrutability of reference is meant to be harmless in a way that Quine's indeterminacy of translation is not. Those may just be slogans that get repeated.

Would you work through the passage claim by claim rather than giving me a general verdict, sorting each part into: what Davidson plainly does hold; what is a fair simplification for a handbook audience; what is a subtle misrepresentation — two things he keeps apart conflated, a modest thesis overstated, a term of art used in its everyday sense; and what he explicitly denied. Then tell me which of the three consequences survive once the misrepresentations are repaired, and what a sympathetic Davidsonian would say in reply to each. Finally, two judgment calls: how much of the author's maximizing proposal is a genuine development of the method and how much is a change of subject, and, if you think any part of his reading is in fact defensible, what would have to be true for it to be right. Please reason from your own understanding of the material; I want analysis, not page references or a reading list.
