I am a final-year PhD student in philosophy, and on Thursday I am presenting a chapter of my dissertation at my department's work-in-progress seminar; there is a half-hour question period afterwards, and I would like a prep sheet for it rather than a summary or a defence of my own view. The chapter is about the scale argument for taking insect welfare seriously — the argument that even a very low per-insect probability of sentience, multiplied against insect headcounts that run into the quintillions, yields an expected moral cost so large that it swamps every other candidate priority, and that insect suffering should therefore dominate what we do. My thesis is that the scale argument, properly formalised, does not support the priority it is standardly used to support: it supports spending on resolving the underlying empirical question, not on acting as if the question had been answered. The central move is that the uncertainty about insect sentience is not a collection of independent per-creature lotteries but a single global hypothesis — either creatures with insect architecture have valenced experience or they do not — and that the headcount itself is hypothesis-dependent, because which taxa and life stages belong in the denominator (only adults? only the large-bodied orders? only individuals that survive to reproduce, given that most wild insects die very young?) shifts the relevant N by orders of magnitude depending on which hypothesis is true. I call this last point the correlated-N problem, and I argue that once you stop treating N as fixed and independent of the hypothesis, the astronomical expected value is exposed as a bet on one binary proposition whose payoff nobody has measured; and the standard verdict about such a bet is that the differences between the available interventions, conditional on the hypothesis, are small next to the value of finding out which way the hypothesis goes. My practical conclusion is therefore that the honest reading of the scale argument funds the basic science — better markers for valenced states, anaesthesia and analgesia work, the comparative neuroanatomy — and treats large-scale interventions on insect populations as premature, while giving up on quoting quintillions as though the number itself settled anything. The audience is about twenty people: my supervisor, a total utilitarian who works on population ethics and is sympathetic to the scale argument and whom I most need not to lose; a contractualist colleague who holds that interpersonal aggregation of this sort is a category error and will want to say the entire framing is confused rather than merely wrong; a formal epistemologist and decision theorist who will press me on whether I am entitled to a probability over the hypothesis at all, and on why I aggregate across hypotheses by expectation while objecting to the way the scale argument aggregates across individuals; a visiting insect neurobiologist who works on nociception and anaesthesia and who thinks the philosophical literature has badly outrun the evidence, cares almost entirely about whether anything in the chapter would change what an experimenter should do on Monday, and is allergic to being invited to adjudicate metaphysics; a philosopher who works on global poverty and climate and regards the whole topic as a distraction from causes whose benefits are not conjectural; and three or four graduate students with effective-altruism sympathies who want the chapter to be usable as a case for funding insect welfare work now, and who will be irritated by my conclusion. What I would like from you is the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, in rough order of how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it — I want the contractualist's objection to sound like a contractualist, the neurobiologist's like someone who has dissected the animal and is tired of being asked about its feelings, and the decision theorist's like a notation-carrying challenge to my decision rule. For each objection give me the roughly two-minute reply I should make in the room, say what I should concede up front rather than fight for, and mark where my reply is soft or where I am quietly relying on a premise I have not defended. Please also tell me which objections I can concede outright without giving up the thesis, which single reply has to land if the chapter is to survive the hour, and — last — the one objection I am hoping nobody asks.
