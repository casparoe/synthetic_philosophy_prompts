I hold a position I'll call *simulation deflationism*, and I want to know what would count against it. Please don't restate the case for it back to me: assume the view below and attack it.

The position in brief. What I'll call the fanatical long-termist argument gets its force from a product: a small but non-negligible probability that what we do now affects whether humanity, or whatever succeeds it, has a long future, multiplied by the size of that future — the number of morally considerable beings it would contain. Because the second factor is astronomically large, the product swamps every near-term consideration unless the first factor is driven down to something absurd. Simulation deflationism leaves the probability factor alone and shrinks the magnitude factor instead, and claims that this alone is enough to undo the domination.

The premise doing the real work, and the one I most want stress-tested, is what I'll call the short-run bias:

(P3) A simulating civilisation has a finite computational budget. What a run costs is broadly proportional to the amount of simulated history it contains — the simulated events, and above all the simulated observer-moments, that have to be computed — not to how much wall-clock time the run occupies in the simulating reality. It follows that, for a civilisation with many things it might want to study, short runs are cheap and long runs are expensive: a run continuing for a billion simulated years is among the costlier things such a civilisation could undertake, while a run terminating within a few simulated centuries is among the cheapest. If the population of runs is sampled even roughly in proportion to what a civilisation can afford to run many of, the short runs dominate it. So conditional on finding myself in a simulation, I should expect it to end well before anything like a colonised galaxy shows up inside it. Simulations, on this picture, are meteorological: built to model a period, not to inherit a future.

Add that if the simulation ends we have no causal purchase on what the simulating civilisation does afterwards, and no reason to think our actions reach the base reality's future at all. Then, conditional on being simulated, the "astronomical" factor is not astronomical. Unconditional on it, the expected size of the future we can affect is a mixture of something enormous and something modest, and a credence in simulation of even a few per cent — mine sits somewhere between 5 and 25 per cent — drags that expectation down by orders of magnitude. What survives is a case for reducing extinction risk that is large but ordinary, competing on the same scale as other large human projects, rather than one that renders everything else a rounding error.

What I want from you is a map of this view's exposure.

1. Rank the defeaters, from those that would be fatal to those that would be merely awkward, with a sentence each on why each lands where it does. I want arguments and formal or empirical developments together: what could we learn, or discover about the world, that ought to move me?

2. For each serious defeater, say whether a defender of simulation deflationism would concede it as counting against the view, or would dismiss it as smuggling in a rival commitment. I care about that distinction more than about the length of the list.

3. Two objections I already have replies to: tell me whether the replies are adequate rather than repeating the objections. (a) Simulated minds matter no less than base-reality minds, so nothing is lost by being simulated. My reply: that restores the value per unit of future, not the quantity of future; the short-run bias is a claim about how much simulated future there is, and would still bite if simulated minds mattered ten times more than ours. (b) If the view is wrong and we are in base reality, acting on it is catastrophic, so the asymmetry of error tells you to ignore it. My reply: that is the structure of a Pascalian wager, which I reject elsewhere — though I concede the reply weakens if my credence in simulation should really sit nearer the top of my range.

4. The objection I would find most persuasive if it were made properly, and which I would like spelled out rather than gestured at: P3 needs a measure. Count runs and the short ones dominate, exactly as I have claimed; weight each run by the observer-moments it contains and the long ones dominate again, and my conclusion inverts. Which measure does an argument of this kind require, and why? Is there a principled way to choose, or does simulation deflationism simply inherit whatever the dispute in anthropics about observer-moment weighting happens to hand it? If the honest answer is that it inherits an unsolved problem, say so: I would rather have that than a confident choice.

5. Two further places to press. First, the fidelity–duration trade-off: if a run can be made cheap by being coarse rather than by being short, then long simulated futures are cheap after all and P3 may collapse. Does it? Second, scope: does the view prove too much? Would it deflate long-run projects I am unwilling to give up — anything whose payoff lies more than a century out, or for that matter a base reality which itself ends soon? If it generalises that far, is that a reductio or a feature?

6. Finally, an honest verdict on insulation. What observation would I actually treat as disconfirming? I notice that I am tempted to treat the absence of any sign that our run is about to be wound up as no evidence at all — there is no particular reason to expect a cue — and that this temptation makes the view comfortable to hold. Is that a vice here, in the way it would be for a metaphysical thesis? The view is decision-relevant rather than descriptive, and I suspect the standards differ; tell me whether treating them as different is special pleading. And if you conclude that the view is well insulated, say whether that is a reason to discount it, and name the single development that would in fact move me most.
