I'm part of a small commission drafting a facilitator's handbook for a state-level citizens' assembly on assisted dying. The assembly's recommendations are formally non-binding, but the legislature has committed to putting them to a vote, so anything a participant says can end up shaping coercive law — indirectly, through two stages of delegation, with a facilitation norm that pushes participants toward unanimity rather than majority voting. The section I keep failing to write is the guidance on religious reasons. Two colleagues of mine — one a Rawlsian-flavored restrictivist who thinks citizens owe one another restraint in matters of coercion, the other an inclusionist who thinks a citizen may offer whatever actually grounds her political conviction — have been going back and forth about it for months, and I'd rather they produce a document than another round of emails. Please write the joint statement they could both sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration: not a verdict on who is right, and not a dramatized exchange, but the constructive text.

Call the first position the restrictivist camp: roughly, that when a citizen advocates for a coercive law, or votes for one, she owes her fellow citizens a justification they can in principle assess, and that reasons whose force depends on a revelation or authority they do not share fail that test. Call the second the inclusionist camp: roughly, that such a duty either misdescribes democratic citizenship, or asks religious citizens to be alienated from their own deepest convictions, or rests on a religion/secularism distinction that does not survive scrutiny. Give me, first, a numbered list of claims both camps can actually sign — about coercion, about the difference between what a citizen may act on and what she may offer, about whether the relevant duty (if there is one) falls on voters, on advocates, on officials, or on the authors of an official rationale document, and about what an assembly's own unanimity-oriented facilitation norm does or does not add to the ordinary case. Do not let these be vacuous: each should be capable of being denied by someone.

Then give me a disagreements table with these columns: the crux; the inclusionist claim in wording an inclusionist would accept; the restrictivist claim in wording a restrictivist would accept; a concrete case or piece of evidence both would agree bears on that crux; and what each side predicts about it in advance. Among the cruxes I want at least these four addressed. (1) Formulational: the requirement might be about the accessibility of a reason's epistemic warrant, about whether it is the kind of reason others could share, or merely about what it is permissible to adduce in public advocacy — and I suspect a good part of their quarrel dissolves once these are separated, so tell me which part you think is verbal and why. (2) The asymmetric-accessibility case: a small religious minority offers a reason the majority cannot assess, while the majority offers a secular reason resting on a contested social-scientific claim that the minority cannot assess; do the two norms treat these symmetrically, and if not, what work is the religion/secularism distinction really doing? (3) Testimony: a religious participant says, in effect, that she has a long track record of sound judgment on the common good and asks to be trusted that this policy serves it, while declining to share the revelatory ground on which she holds it; does that satisfy the norm, flout it, or change what the norm was about? (4) Integrity and uptake: whether the cost of restraint falls unevenly on religious citizens, and whether it is a moral wrong or an etiquette of civic friendship that is being violated — plus what each camp would count as evidence that religious reasons in deliberation entrench rather than open up discussion.

Two constraints. First, the handbook is for facilitators with no philosophical training, so define any term of art you use and don't cite or gesture at the literature — I want the argument, not the bibliography. Second, close with a short section stating, for each camp, what single development — an argument, an institutional change, or an empirical finding about how assemblies actually deliberate — would most move it, and say whether there is anything both would agree would settle the matter. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words, and write it so that neither of my colleagues would accuse you of having quietly adopted the other's framing.
