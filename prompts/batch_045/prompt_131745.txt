I've been in a small reading group on AI alignment stuff for a couple mnoths now, and I've become convinced of something the other people there mostly don't buy, so I want it stress-tested properly rather than just defended again. The setup everyone uses is: you've got a very capable predictor trained on a stream of observations — the stock illustration is cameras watching a vault with a diamond in it — and you want to get it to tell you what it actually "knows" about the world, e.g. whether the diamond is still in there, in cases where you can't check the answer yourself. My position is that people misdiagnose this problem when they frame it as being about deception, that is, as being about whether the system is trying to mislead us. Even a maximally cooperative system is a problem here, because the only training signal the reporter ever gets rewards agreement with what we can already verify, and nothing in that signal picks out the honest report from a report that instead tracks what a human observer would say given the same camera feed. So on my view the difficulty is underdetermination rather than adversaries, and it's a difficulty that doesn't go away when you make the model nicer.

The concrete thing I've been defending — and this is the part I most want attacked — is a scheme I've started calling a swapped-sensor check. Train two predictors on different observation channels of the same world: one gets the vault cameras, one gets vibration and weight sensors in the floor. Then ask each of them to answer questions not about the world directly but about what the other one's channel is going to show. Predicting channel B from the history of channel A is self-supervised, so it needs no human labels at all, and my claim is that a predictor can only do it by routing through its model of the world rather than through a model of what people say — nobody ever told it anything about the other sensors, so there's no human-opinion shortcut to exploit. If that's right, a reporter trained on the cross-channel objective gets anchored to the world-model for free, and then at deployment you ask it the diamond question in plain English.

Tell me what would count against this package — the diagnosis and the scheme together, and each separately if they don't stand or fall together. I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd be, with the fatal ones up front and the merely awkward ones at the bottom. Some specific things I care about: what would have to be true for the underdetermination diagnosis to be wrong and the deception framing to be the right one after all; what a human-simulator reporter would actually be expected to do on a cross-channel question, and whether it might just learn to imitate a human who is looking at both channels, or imitate the *predictor's own* outputs, and quietly collapse back into something that isn't tracking the world; whether there's any result or thought experiment that would show the honest reporter is in fact the unique best scorer under a rich enough training distribution; and what people who hold the standard framing would themselves concede counts against them, not just what I say counts against them. Also give me an honest read on whether I've insulated the view — I notice I've built in "if the reporter fails, it wasn't anchored properly," and I can't tell whether that's a real prediction or a get-out. For what it's worth, the thing I personally find most persuasive is evidence from actual current systems: cases where a model can be made to report something it "knows" that its trainers couldn't verify, or cases where it definitly can't. If that kind of evidence cuts against me I'd rather hear it now than at the next meeting.
