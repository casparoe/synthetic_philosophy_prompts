I've been reading about this thing called luck egalitarianism after an argument at work, and I want to try it out on something concrete rather than more hypotheticals. My employer's private health scheme knocks money off your premium if you don't smoke and if you hit a steps target on the app, and they're talking about adding blood pressure and BMI next year. But nobody pays more for having a parent who died at 55 — they dont even ask about family history. So the scheme is happy to charge people for what they do and not for what they were born with, and the line sits right at behaviour.

What does drawing the line there actually presuppose? The bits I can't sort out:

- Does it presuppose that smoking and inactivity are chosen in a way your genes aren't? Smoking looks pretty heavily shaped by addiction and stress and how you grew up, and sticking to exercise looks shaped by temperament, so is the line really between choice and luck, or between two kinds of luck we just feel differently about?

- Could you get this exact scheme without any view about fairness at all, just from incentives and information — the app is really a way of finding out who's healthy, and healthy people are cheap to insure? If so, does anything about the practice strictly require the luck-egalitarian story, or does it only sit nicely with it?

- The bit that really puzzles me: if they move to rewarding measured outcomes like BMI, that seems to charge people for how their body happens to respond to the same effort. So a fairness-minded version should reward effort and ignore results. But rewarding effort while ignoring results rewards the wrong thing, and it mostly rewards people who were lucky enough to be raised to find exercise easy.

So: reconstruct whatever view of responsibility the scheme is quietly running on, say whether its different parts are consistent with each other, and tell me whether there's a version of it that's actually coherent or whether several quite different theories would all sign off on the same thing.
