For a paper I'm poking at on when (if ever) it's ok to kill insects in the course of pest control, I want the spec before I go read any existing view. Write me the desiderata a theory of permissible insect killing has to meet: which cases it has to handle, which distinctions it can't collapse, which facts about insect lives and populations it can't contradict — with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

The cases I keep circling: malaria mosquitoes in a village, aphids on a crop, a wasp nest by a playground, termites in your own floor, invasive ants on an island, and then the method axis — spraying vs sterile-insect release vs gene drive vs just screening the windows. The distinctions I think matter: killing vs suppressing vs displacing vs sterilising; the target animal vs the vastly larger number of non-target animals; individual vs population level; intended vs merely foreseen when the bodycount is literally uncountable.

Two candidate requirements I can't tell are real or just my hangups — tell me which: (1) the standing-crop worry: since most pest insects die within weeks anyway and get replaced fast, killing them may cost them almost nothing, but that same reasoning seems to greenlight killing day-old chicks and I don't want that result; (2) the moving-target worry: control reshapes the population (resistance, density compensation), so any headcount the theory leans on is one the intervention itself destroys. And one more I'd insist on: we don't know whether they're sentient, so the theory has to say something under that uncertainty instead of waiting for science.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preferences, say which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, and flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied. One short paragraph at the end on which familiar view does worst is fine, but don't turn it into a survey.
