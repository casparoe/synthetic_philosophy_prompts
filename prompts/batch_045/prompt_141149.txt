I am writing the specification for an aggregation rule, and I want the requirements settled before I look at candidate rules. The setting: a resource-allocation system that must rank risky prospects affecting a group of people, where each person's preferences are represented by a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function estimated from data, so the utility functions are cardinal only up to positive affine transformation, known only within error bars, and not interpersonally comparable by construction. The same rule must be applied to differently sized groups on different days, and it has to be defensible to the people it ranks — meaning the ranking has to be explainable to each of them in terms of their own utility numbers. Harsanyi's result is the obvious starting point: a social ordering over prospects that satisfies the expected-utility axioms together with Pareto indifference must be expressible as a weighted sum of individuals' utilities. My problem is that the theorem tells me the form of what I want and not whether that form is what I should want.

So: give me the desiderata. For each requirement, the case or consideration that motivates it, and a verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Tell me which of my candidate requirements are genuine and which are prejudices dressed as requirements; tell me which subset of the genuine ones cannot be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates. Then, in no more than a short paragraph or a compact table, score four contenders: the unweighted sum; a concave (priority-weighting) transformation of the sum; a rule that evaluates each realized outcome vector separately for equality and only then takes expectations; and the same sum normalized by group size. Keep the scoring brief — the criteria are what I am paying for. Do not pad the answer with references or a reading list; I want the arguments.

The cases I want any candidate rule to be tested against:

1. Two people indistinguishable in every respect, one indivisible benefit. The system can allocate it to A outright, or flip a fair coin. By any anonymity condition the two outright allocations are equally good; by the sure-thing-style independence axiom on the social ranking, they are then also indifferent to the coin flip. So a rule of Harsanyi's form cannot treat the fairness of the chances as any improvement at all. Is valuing the fairness of chances a desideratum, or is it the confusion of ex ante equality with ex post equality? If it is a desideratum, which axiom has to go?

2. The hard-to-please person. One person's utility across the relevant outcome range moves between 0 and 10, another's between 0 and 1, and the estimating procedure pins down a common unit in which both are measured. Equal weights after normalizing each person's function to a common range, and equal weights in the pinned-down unit, give different rankings. Is invariance to independent affine rescaling a requirement that survives here, or should it be dropped when a natural unit is available — and if the weights are only meaningful relative to a normalization, what is the requirement that fixes the normalization?

3. Disagreement about probabilities. The person affected thinks the risk of a bad outcome is 5 percent; the clinician setting the parameters thinks it is 25 percent. The two rank the same two acts in opposite order. State the Pareto requirement that applies here — unanimity over prospects evaluated at a single probability distribution, or unanimity over each person's own expected-utility ranking — and tell me whether a rule doing the former can still do the latter. I suspect these two cannot both hold, and I want the argument rather than the assertion.

4. Noise. The utility estimates come with error bars, and any rule whose ranking flips when the inputs move inside their error bars is unusable. Is continuity in the inputs, or a stronger stability condition, a genuine desideratum or merely engineering convenience — and what does it cost, given that it appears to rule out thresholds, lexicographic priority, and other devices people reach for?

5. Variable numbers of people. Decide between a small group each well above the relevant zero and a much larger group each barely above it, then say whether the same rule gives consistent verdicts when a decision affects one group size on Monday and another on Friday. Harsanyi's framework holds the population fixed; extending it is part of the specification. Say what the extension costs.

6. Description invariance. Reducing a compound lottery to its single-stage equivalent, splitting one state into two with the same utility vector, or refining the state space in any way that leaves the induced joint distribution over realized utility vectors unchanged, must not change the ranking. State this precisely, and say whether it is in tension with anything on the list — in particular with the ex post equality concerns raised by case 1, and with the Pareto condition from case 3.

7. Social risk aversion. Each person's own attitude to risk is already built into their utility function. Is there any remaining room for the social ranking to be risk-averse or priority-weighting with respect to the aggregate, or does that double-count something, and if so, is the appearance of double-counting an objection or a feature?

Two further requirements I would like you to adjudicate rather than assume: that the rule determine its own weights, since the theorem fixes the functional form but leaves the weights free and a rule with free parameters is not a specification; and that the ranking be legible, in the sense that the person who loses out can be shown, in their own numbers, why the outcome they got was ranked below the one they wanted. Say whether legibility is a real desideratum, whether it is satisfiable by any rule of the weighted-sum family, and whether it conflicts with robustness in case 4.

Close by telling me which requirement on your final list does the most exclusionary work, and which pair or triple of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — if the list turns out to be an impossibility result, that is the answer I most want, and I want it stated as such.
