You are a professional philosopher who specializes in Kant's ethics — the universalization formulas in Groundwork II, the fierce disagreement about what the test is even supposed to be testing, and the contemporary reconstructions of it. Background: my colleague — call her H. — has taught the Groundwork every year for about fifteen years, and in the joint grad seminar she rules on maxims constantly. I have been writing her verdicts down, because they don't hang together the way I expected. Abridged track record:

1. Borrowing money on a promise to repay that you know you can't keep — fails, and she calls it the cleanest case: the practice couldn't survive universal non-keeping.
2. Ending your own life to escape a wretched stretch of it — fails.
3. Never bothering to develop your talents — fails, but she flags it as the weaker kind of failure, the imperfect-duty kind.
4. Never lifting a finger for anyone in need — fails, same weaker kind.
5. Lying to the person at the door who is looking for the friend hiding in your house — fails, and she is unmoved by the consequences.
6. Telling a colleague you are delighted to see them when you are mildly annoyed — permissible, on the grounds that no one is deceived.
7. Breaking a promise you made at gunpoint — permissible, no valid consent, nothing there to keep.
8. Buying the cheaper version of something even though you know it's produced under worse labour conditions, because your one purchase changes nothing — permissible.
9. Taking the car on the day everyone is asked to leave it at home, because one car's emissions make no difference — permissible.
10. The maxim 'I'll lie whenever the truth would be devastating to the person I'm talking to' — fails; she says you can't narrow a maxim until it comes out clean.

So: 8 and 9 say causal impotence excuses, and 3 and 4 say it doesn't; 7 lets a maxim be qualified by features of the other party while 10 refuses to let it be qualified by the outcome. That's the mess I want you to make sense of.

Here's the case I need a verdict on. Departmental life runs on uncredited labour — hiring files, letters for the grad students, the reading group, refereeing, committees. My own working maxim is: I do the contractual minimum and no more, I take full advantage of everything the place produces for me, I'm entirely open about it rather than deceiving anyone, and I add one clause — if the department ever got to the point where it genuinely couldn't function without me, I'd step in.

So: what does H. say about that maxim? Which reading of the test is she implicitly running — contradiction in conception, contradiction in the will, practical-contradiction-about-my-own-purpose, or something more transactional about deception and consent — and what does each of those predict here? Which of her precedents is the closest analog, given that 8 and 9 pull one way and 3 and 4 pull the other? Does my 'I'd step in if it collapsed' clause actually do any work, or is it a trick that a stricter reading simply refuses to count?
