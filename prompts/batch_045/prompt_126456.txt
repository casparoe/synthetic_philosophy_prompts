You are a moral philosopher who has taught undergraduate ethics many times and knows the ways discussion sessions go wrong. Before you write anything, think through the session design: sketch two or three orderings of the material you considered, give one sentence each on why you rejected them, and then give me the plan you would actually run.

The setting: I lead a weekly seventy-five-minute discussion for a campus effective altruism society. The members arrive already convinced that they should do as much impartial good as they can with their careers and money; several have pledged a large share of future income, and they have met the worry that morality asks too much and dismissed it as an excuse for self-indulgence. Next week I want them to feel the force of a different worry: that a life wholly devoted to impartially promoting the good of others would leave no room for non-moral excellences — the kind of achievement, playfulness, or love of particular people and projects that Susan Wolf's "Moral Saints" made central — and that moral sainthood is therefore not an ideal of a well-lived life, even granting that it would be morally permitted or required. I want them to see this as a claim about what kind of life is worth wanting, not as a licence to donate less.

So plan the session, not the doctrine. What has to be in place before the worry can even be stated, and why does it have to come first? Which single case or figure should carry the whole hour, what must it have and lack, and why do the obvious candidates fail — the burnt-out aid worker, the joyless do-gooder, the person who never learned to have fun? Then give me the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface — I expect "so you're saying be selfish," "that's just burnout with a philosophical name," and "saints can play the cello too" — and the point in the sequence at which you would head off each. I also know I will get two specific pushbacks: that sainthood can be domain-limited (be a saint about one cause, appealing to a division of moral labour, and keep a rich personal life otherwise), and that the cost Wolf identifies is only that saints are boring, which my members will happily pay. Say where in the hour each of those belongs and how the sequence up to that point should absorb it. Close with one question that would show me whether they have understood the objection rather than merely been reassured, and with what you would openly refuse to settle in seventy-five minutes.

Finally, tell me where your plan is most likely to misfire: which step you are least confident will land, and what a student intervention would have to look like for you to reorder the session.
