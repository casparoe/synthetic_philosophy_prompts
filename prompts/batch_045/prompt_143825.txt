I'm retired — thirty-some years in air-quality and dispersion modelling, so I spent a career watching small errors in initial conditions eat forcasts alive — and I've been sitting in on a philosophy of history reading group. We got onto whether history can be a quantitative, predictive science at all, and I built an argument that I now think proves too much, but I can't locate the move.

(1) A cliodynamic model is a dynamical model: specify the state now — population, real wages, state fiscal capacity, numbers of elites — integrate, read off the state later.
(2) For any real historical society the state estimate is uncertain at the few-percent level at best, and not merely noisy: for most of the past we simply don't know the numbers.
(3) The models that get the qualitative story right (expansion, elite overproduction, fiscal stress, instability, collapse, recovery) get it right because of feedback, which means nonlinearity, which means nearby trajectories separate; at plausible separation rates a few percent of initial error becomes an error the size of the whole cycle within two or three generations. Call that the divergence budget.
(4) So no such model can say anything about when a particular instability peaks, or how bad it gets, more than a few generations ahead.
(5) What a predictive science of history would have to deliver is exactly that: dates and magnitudes. Anything weaker is curve-fitting with a story bolted on.
(6) Therefore cliodynamics is not, and cannot be, the predictive science it advertises itself as.

Here's why I think it overreaches. Run (1)–(4) on any nonlinear system that cycles — predator and prey, epidemic recurrence, a driven pendulum — and you'd conclude we can predict nothing about those either. But we can: "the cycle recurs on the order of N years" is a real, checkable success, and it is pretty much the shape of the secular-cycle claim (instability every couple of centuries or so). So either (4) is really only about date-level prediction and (5) quietly raises the bar to something nobody promised, or it generalises and it takes down ensemble weather forecasting and climate projection with it, which I'm not willing to do.

Two things from my old trade I'd like you to use rather than dodge. First, a rough diagnostic I made up — call it precision doubling: halve the uncertainty on the initial state and see whether the useful forecast horizon grows by a constant increment or by a constant factor. I think that separates "your state estimate is bad" from "your equations are wrong," and I suspect it matters here. Second, in my field we'd answer (4) with an ensemble: you don't predict one trajectory, you predict a distribution. My worry is that over a two-century cycle the honest ensemble is so wide it's both useless for anybody acting on it and unfalsifiable afterwards. So is a probability distribution over outcomes a prediction, or a shrug?

I most want to keep (2) and (3) — I've watched error growth, I don't want to wish it away — and I can live with giving up (5). Wich step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? Where does the divergence budget actually bite, and where does it not?
