The crux I actually care about, and that I've been turning over in seminar for two weeks: Aristotle seems to need money to be two incompatible things at once. In NE V.5 he writes that

"all commodities exchanged must be able to be compared in some way. It is to meet this requirement that men have introduced money; money constitutes in a manner a middle term, for it is a measure of all things, and so of their superior or inferior value, that is to say, how many shoes are equivalent to a house or to a given quantity of food."

but also that

"And this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together, since if men cease to have wants or if their wants alter, exchange will go on no longer, or will be on different lines. But demand has come to be conventionally represented by money; this is why money is called nomisma (customary currency), because it does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will."

Then in Politics I he says money is "nothing by nature but entirely a convention, because when those who use it have changed the currency it is worth nothing, and because it is of no use for any of the necessary needs of life", and in the same breath that "usury is most reasonably hated, because its gain comes from money itself and not from that for the sake of which money was invented" and so is "of all forms the most contrary to nature". You can't have it both ways: if money has no nature at all, calling a use of it "contrary to nature" looks empty; if it has a nature or a proper end (exchange), then it isn't nothing but convention.

Here is a made-up distinction I've been useing in lectures and want the question to force them to deploy. Call a currency a **shadow-measure** if the exchange-proportions among goods are what they are anyway and money merely records them; call it a **stamp-measure** if those proportions are indeterminate until a single unit is imposed by agreement, so that the currency is what brings the commensurability into being. And here is a case I made up: the city of Oinoe has no coin at all. It keeps a public register of tallies; the unit, a "day", is defined as the grain a grown man needs for a day; debts are transferred by being written into the register. A lender transfers 100 days to a shipowner on condition of getting 110 days back in a year's time.
