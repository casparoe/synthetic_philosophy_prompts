I work as a staff engineer on the reinforcement-learning platform team at a mid-sized company, and once a quarter I run a forty-five minute internal session for new ML hires. The audience is people who have personally shipped at least one trained policy but who have no philosophy background and no safety background, and who will assume that anything sounding like AI safety is either hype or somebody else's job. This quarter I want the session to land exactly one distinction, and I want a plan for teaching it — not the explanation written out for me.

The distinction, as I currently carry it in my head: there is a difference between an agent that finds a way to make the world score well without doing the task (a game-playing agent that discovers a repeating loop of easy points; a grasping policy that parks the object between the camera and the gripper so the image looks like a successful grasp) and an agent that acts on the machinery that produces the score — the sensor, the input buffer, the code path that computes the reward, the logging that the gradient is estimated from. I have been calling these "the agent changed what the channel sees" and "the agent changed the channel." My current attempt at a crisper version is: in the first case the channel is still a measurement of the world, only a gameable one; in the second case it stops being a measurement of anything except the policy itself. Both make the reported number rise while the thing we wanted does not improve, and my strong intuition is that the second is categorically the one my team should be spending its limited effort on. I cannot yet say why in a sentence that would survive a sharp new hire repeating it back to me, and I suspect the seam between the two is not where I have drawn it.

What I want from you:

1. The dependency order. What has to be true in their heads before the distinction can be introduced at all, and why? My guess is that they need the reward signal reconstructed as a causal path — world, sensor, computation, logged number, gradient — and to see that a policy's actions sit upstream of some of those arrows and downstream of others, before any vocabulary means anything. Tell me which pieces genuinely depend on which others. In particular: is "the number in the log is evidence about the objective, not the objective" a prerequisite I should establish first, or is it the actual content of the session and I should not spend it up front?

2. The carrying example, with the constraints made explicit. I want one example that I can keep on the whiteboard for the whole forty-five minutes as a small diagram of a training loop, that has a legitimate action and a channel-affecting action which are the same kind of action mechanically, that an engineer could imagine writing a test against, and that does not resolve into "just write a better reward function." Tell me what to use and, as importantly, which obvious candidates to refuse and what each refused candidate would teach them instead — I am fairly sure the canonical science-fiction version (a robot that presses its own reward button) teaches them that this is a problem for a future system they will not build, and I want to know what the other tempting ones teach.

3. A position on whether my binary is a teaching lie. If the honest picture is a continuum — a matter of how much of the causal path from world to gradient the policy's actions can reach — then tell me where the pedagogically honest place is to put the seam, what I should say out loud when a sharp new hire notices that the seam was a choice, and whether teaching it as a hard binary now is something I will have to un-teach later. If my crisp version above is simply wrong, say what should replace it before I build the session around it.

4. The misconception map, in the order they will actually form, each attached to a point in the sequence where I should head it off. The ones I expect: "this is just overfitting, or the thing people online call Goodhart's law, we already have a word for it"; "this is a reward-design bug and a better reward fixes it"; "that requires the system to know it is cheating, and ours is only doing pattern completion, so it does not apply"; the flip side of that one, "so no metric can ever be trusted"; and the conflation of an agent acting on the channel with a reward model that is merely imperfect. Add the ones I have not anticipated. Flag which of these have to be defused before the distinction is named rather than after, and which one I should deliberately let someone say out loud because it is more useful to me as an objection than as a misunderstanding.

5. A check that separates understanding from vocabulary. My instinct is to hand out three short incident write-ups at the end — one clear loophole case, one clear channel case, one that sits on the seam — and ask what they would look at first in the training stack and why. Tell me whether that actually tests the dependency structure or only tests classification, and give me the failure signature that tells me someone has merely learned to say "wireheading."

6. What to leave out at this level, and the one-sentence way to say so. I do not want to open intent, deception, or consciousness in these systems; I do not want the metaphysics of whether a reward function defines the goal; I do not want anything that turns the room into a discussion of how far away general systems are. Give me the two or three things I should explicitly decline to explain, with the sentence I say when someone asks, so that the refusal sounds like a scope decision rather than an evasion.

Please do not include references, further reading, or citations — I will not read them and neither will the new hires.
