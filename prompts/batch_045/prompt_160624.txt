I clerk for a mid-sized city's Public Names and Monuments Committee: nine lay members appointed by council on rotating terms, no research budget beyond a few hours of a junior staffer's time, and a standing requirement that we return a written recommendation with reasons within six weeks of a petition being accepted. We see fifteen to twenty matters a year. About half are demands to remove, rename, relocate, or recontextualize something the city already owns — a bronze statue on a courthouse lawn, a school, a named bridge, a donor plaque in a hospital lobby; the rest are proposals to add: a new name for a park path, a marker on city land, a bust in a civic building. Council ratifies almost everything we send up, so in practice our recommendation *is* the decision, and it has to be drafted so that it survives both a council floor debate and a hostile newspaper editorial. Right now what we actually do is ask whether the person was a bad person, argue about it for two meetings, and split four to five. I want to replace that with something we can run.

So: give me an ordered decision procedure my committee could actually operate. I want the questions in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure returns a default of retention and stops, the point at which it returns removal or renaming, and — most importantly, because this is where we currently collapse — the points at which it must hand the decision to a human because the remaining question is one of values or of city identity rather than of fact. It needs a full menu of dispositions (retain as is, retain with annotation, move to a museum or cemetery, rename, remove, rename *and* install something new, install a counter-commemoration alongside) together with a test for when recontextualization is genuinely adequate and when it is a way of appearing to act while leaving the object doing the same work. It also needs to separate the case where we are stuck because the historical facts are contested from the case where the facts are agreed and what is contested is what they mean for eligibility, since our six weeks and one staffer can only settle the first kind of question some of the time. Please don't tell us to commission a biography or consult an archive; the whole point is a procedure that works with what a junior staffer can find in six weeks.

Four distinctions have been forced on us by recent cases and I want them built into the procedure rather than averaged away. First, the difference between the *honoree* and the *deed or role* for which a thing was erected: a statue raised in 1911 to a man as a wartime general, a hospital wing named for a woman because she gave the money, a school named for a teacher — is the object committed to the whole life or only to one act, and what follows when the act and the life come apart? Second, what I have started calling inert honorifics: a great many of our street names were attached to surveyors, engineers, and landowners in the 1920s and have long since decayed into pure addresses, and I have noticed that a removal campaign can *revive* an inert name's honorific force — it becomes a badge, a thing people defend — so the cost of acting and the cost of not acting are not independent of our own proceedings. Third, the difference between taint in the honoree and taint in the origin: some of our objects were installed by private organizations with an explicit ideological purpose and a condition in the gift, which seems to me a different objection from the one you get from the biography. Fourth, scarcity: the city has a roughly fixed stock of honorific slots, so every retention is also a refusal to give that slot to someone or something else, and our procedure has never once priced that in.

Please run the procedure you give me on three matters currently on our desk. (1) A bronze statue, erected 1911 by subscription from business associates, of a local industrialist who funded the city's first public library and a hospital wing and who also, in the 1890s, led and publicly defended a campaign of violent strikebreaking in which several workers were killed; the inscription praises him as a friend of the workingman. (2) A residential street named in 1923 for a city engineer who designed the sewer system and who, in the same decade, served on the committee that produced the racially restrictive covenant scheme the city then enforced for forty years; nobody on the street has any idea who he was, a history graduate student found the connection, and the petition has already attracted a counter-petition from people who have never heard the name before this month. (3) A proposal, from two different groups, about a site on city land where a lynching took place in 1919: one group wants the pedestrian path there named for the victim, the other wants only a marker with no name; the victim's surviving family has told us they want no new name at all, and our existing rules require that names honour distinguished citizens, which a victim of violence is not.

I took one philosophy elective as an undergraduate and remember something vague about how names latch onto their bearers, which may be why I keep worrying about whether a street named a century ago still refers to the engineer in any sense that matters — but I don't want the theory for its own sake, and I can't chase down readings. I want the procedure, its application to those three, an honest account of where the procedure will misfire and what it cannot settle, and at least one place where running it properly gives me an answer I will have to argue my colleagues into accepting.
