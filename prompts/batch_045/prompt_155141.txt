Hey — I'm running a discussion group for first-year philosophy students next week on the problem of universals, and I want to build it around Borges' story Funes the Memorious (the one where Ireneo Funes, a kid in a small Uruguayan town, falls off a horse and afterwards remembers absolutely everything — every cloud shape at every moment, every muscle and temperature sensation that went with it — sits up all night talking to the narrator in the dark, has somehow picked up Latin from a dictionary and recites Pliny at him, and passes the time reconstructing a whole past day in real time plus inventing a numbering scheme where each number gets its own made-up name instead of a numeral; the narrator remarks somewhere that thinking is basically forgetting differences, generalizing, abstracting, and that Funes was incapable of general or Platonic ideas, and then the kid dies young of lung congestion).

So: read Funes as a case study in the metaphysics of universals. I don't want a straight-A essay on realism vs. nominalism in general — I want the story to actually do some work, and I want you to say plainly where it refuses to.

Things I want dealt with rather than slid past:

1. The Latin wrinkle. Funes reads Latin, and Latin is shot through with general terms. So is he a mind with no universals at all, or a mind with universals that can't stop noticing differences? Those strike me as very different diagnoses, and I suspect only one of them is interesting for the realism debate. Which one does the text reward?

2. My friend who works in semantics says I've got the story wrong completely. He says Funes' trouble isn't universals but inventory: a mind with a finite stock of names can't give a distinct univocal name to each of an endless crowd of things — he calls it the naming-budget problem — and a realist Funes with perfect memory would be just as stuck. Is that a different problem, or the same one wearing a hat?

3. Does the story take a position, or is it just mood? If it does, it feels like it lands somewhere near conceptualism: universals as what a mind gets by abstracting away from detail. Is that a position anyone in the debate actually wants, or does the story smuggle it in by making abstraction sound like a psychological defect?

4. Is the number scheme a reductio of pure particularism, or is it what a Platonist about numbers would look like with the notation taken away? And if Funes is a particularist, is he even a trope theorist, or something more radicial than trope theory can allow?

If you had to defend exactly one reading to the group, which would it be — and what would an honest critic say the story adds here that the arguments alone don't?
