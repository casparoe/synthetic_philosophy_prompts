Our office has a statutory duty to weigh the interests of future generations, and we need a single discounting rule to apply across projects whose payoffs run out eighty to a hundred and fifty years: sea defences, nuclear waste storage, grid rebuilds. Before I look at any particular proposal I want the specification, since nearly everything I read argues from one favourite case. Write the desiderata that any adequate account of pure time preference and of discounting distant future benefits must meet, with the case or consideration behind each: what it must say about the difference between discounting money, consumption, and welfare; about keeping pure time preference separate from — or explicitly folding in — expected growth, catastrophe and extinction risk, and the possibility that the recipients of the benefit owe their existence to the timing of the project itself; about whether the rate should be flat or decline with the horizon; about how the rule behaves when, as actually happens, it is reapplied at every budget cycle rather than once at the outset; about what it owes, if anything, to observed market and political behaviour; about the distinction it must not collapse between how much we owe the future and how much we should invest now against a fixed budget; and about benefits that are not a separable stream at all — say, a project that lowers the annual hazard rate of civilisational collapse, so that the value of the benefit depends on the size and shape of the whole future it protects. Mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are the preferences of a particular camp, name the single requirement that excludes the most candidates, and flag any subset of the list you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — I expect the trouble to lie in the triangle of horizon-declining rates, stability under repeated re-optimisation, and consequentialist re-evaluation. Then a sentence each on where the standard families fail the list — zero pure time preference with a growth term, a positive pure rate calibrated to observed choice, declining-horizon schedules, and views that drop pure time preference but add a separate sustainability constraint — but no full scorecard.
