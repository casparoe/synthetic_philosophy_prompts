Write a short story (1,200–1,800 words, in American English) that dramatizes the narrativist view of the self: the view that a person is not an object located somewhere inside a head but an abstraction, the focal point around which the stories about a life get organized — a center of narrative gravity, as one might call it, rather than one more part of the furniture of the world.

Set the story in a near-contemporary clinic that treats a fictional condition, *narrative apraxia*. Patients retain full intelligence, perception, and vocabulary, but lose the capacity to hold their experiences together as one continuous life: each day arrives disconnected from the last, and asked what sort of person they are, they cannot answer except by echoing the last thing said to them. The clinic's treatment is a *continuity editor* — a salaried professional who keeps a written ledger of the patient's life in the third person, interviews the patient weekly, and quietly revises earlier entries when new material fails to fit. The single speculative element: when the patient reads a revised entry, they remember that day the way the revision describes it, within the hour.

The crux of the story: two editors have produced incompatible ledgers for the same patient, and a hospital committee must rule on which ledger is authoritative for renewing the treatment — knowing that whichever it picks, the patient's first-person memory will follow the ruling.

Requirements:

- No character explains the theory. The philosophical question should arrive as a decision someone has to make under time pressure, with something they care about at stake.
- Show the continuity of the self living somewhere other than in the patient: in the ledger, in the editor's memory, in an institution's files — and show what happens when that support is withdrawn, reassigned, or edited.
- Leave the reader genuinely unable to settle whether the treatment is a rescue or a quiet usurpation, and whether the patient was one person before the ledger existed.
- Keep it in the register of quiet clinical realism: no conspiracy, no rogue-technologist villain, no final twist, no philosopher character, and no closing paragraph that sums the point up.
