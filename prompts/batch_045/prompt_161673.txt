I'm running a ninety-minute seminar next week for second-year cognitive-science undergraduates on Hofstadter's claim that an 'I' need not be confined to a single skull: that a self is a pattern which can be partly instantiated in more than one brain, and that when someone dies what persists of them is distributed, in diminished and unequal measure, across the people who carry it. Their preparation is one lecture on Parfit — teletransportation and the fission cases — and otherwise no philosophy. Most of them will arrive inclined to hear the claim either as obvious sentiment dressed up in technical language or as mysticism dressed up in cognitive science, and I want neither reaction left standing by the end.

Don't explain Hofstadter's view to me; I know the view. Plan how I should teach it. What I want from you is the architecture of the session:

The order the components have to come in, and why. Give me the dependency structure rather than a topic list: which idea has to be in place before the central claim is even intelligible, what each step buys for the next, and what specifically goes wrong if a later step is introduced early. My own instinct is that they cannot hear the claim about distribution until they have accepted the self as a pattern rather than an object, and that the pattern claim itself needs one prior move about self-reference and levels — but tell me if I have the order backwards, and say what breaks in each case.

The one case that should carry the whole session, and what such a case must have and lack. I have been working with a pair of scientists who collaborated closely for thirty years, one of whom has since died; the survivor now notices that when he reads a manuscript he hears, unprompted and in something like the dead colleague's voice, the objection she would have raised, and that this hearing partly settles what he thinks. Tell me what this case needs in order to do the work — I assume it needs dense, long, two-way influence rather than admiration or imitation, and that it must involve no uploading, copying, teleportation, or resurrection machinery, since any of that sends them straight back to Parfit and turns the question into one about fission and duplicates — and, more usefully, tell me what else it must contain and what it must conspicuously lack.

A three-way distinction I want them to be able to draw by the end, which I have been using in my own notes: (i) trace — the living person carries information about the dead one; (ii) simulation — the living person runs a working model that generates the responses the dead one would have generated, and does so accurately enough to be useful; (iii) co-inhabitation — the dead one's pattern is partly instantiated in the living brain in such a way that it partly constitutes the living person's own 'I'. Everything hangs on what would have to be true for the third to be more than the second. Say where each should be introduced, and give me the probe or small variation on the carrying case that prises (iii) apart from (ii) in a way these students can actually feel, rather than a definition they can repeat.

The misconceptions, in the order they will actually arrive, and the point in the sequence at which each should be absorbed. My candidate list, which you should correct and reorder: that this is just memory and causal influence with a metaphor bolted on; that on this view my grandmother is alive as long as I remember her, which makes survival far too cheap; that partial survival is incoherent because identity is one-one and admits no degrees, so that Hofstadter must either be changing the subject or talking nonsense — this is the strong objection and I want it met head-on rather than managed, including the possibility that the honest answer is that he has changed the subject and that what he has changed it to is worth having; that if the pattern is the person then two sufficiently similar people are one person; that the view commits him to consciousness surviving death; and finally the word 'soul', which I want kept off the table until late, because introducing it early does something to the room that I would rather it do once the pattern claim is secured. Tell me which of these deserve real time and which should be acknowledged in a sentence.

What I should refuse to open at this level, and say so out loud to them: I am inclined to fence off the hard problem of consciousness, the Further-Fact versus Reductionism dispute, and the fission literature. Tell me whether that is right, and what else I am likely to get pulled into that I should decline.

One question I can put to them in the last five minutes such that their answer tells me whether they have grasped the difference between the second and the third grade, as opposed to having been merely moved by the example or persuaded that memory is important.

Finally, be explicit about where this plan is weakest: which step you think is most likely to fail with this particular audience, what would lead you to reorder the sequence, and what consideration — an argument, a distinction I have not supplied, or a better reading of what Hofstadter is actually claiming than the co-inhabitation reading the plan rests on — would make you abandon the plan's central move and teach the topic some other way.
