I'm a senior at a large public high school, and I help run a student-led elective called Ethics and Economics for sophomores and juniors. I have one 55-minute session to fill, about 18 students, and I want to spend it on the part of cost–benefit analysis that I have become convinced is actually load-bearing: not the arithmetic of adding up benefits and subtracting costs, but the prior question of whether one person's benefit can be put on the same scale as another person's at all. A friend of mine thinks this isn't a real problem: she says you just ask people how much they want something, in dollars or on a scale of one to ten, and then compare the numbers. I think that answer quietly assumes the very thing in dispute, but every time I try to explain why, it comes out as abstract hand-waving and people go back to thinking the numbers are fine. So I don't want a session where I explain anything at the front; I want a game or structured activity where the students collide with the difficulty themselves, using real if small stakes, and where the phrase interpersonal comparison of utility isn't said aloud until the very end.

Here is what I have in mind, which you should feel free to repair or replace. Materials: index cards, play money, and a stash of things students actually want, like snack bars and homework passes. Roughly: five volunteers each get a secret multiplier telling them how much enjoyment a unit of each prize gives them, and everyone else has to allocate a fixed pile of prizes among them so as to produce the most total enjoyment, learning about the volunteers only through what they do — bids, trades, refusals. The multipliers get revealed at the end. Beyond that I want three specific things to be felt rather than asserted. First, that willingness to pay tracks buying power at least as much as it tracks how intensely someone benefits, so the student who wins every auction may be the one who enjoys the prize least. Second, that asking someone whether they would rather swap places entirely with another person mixes together two different things — how good that person's situation is, and how much I in particular would enjoy their bundle given my tastes — so a swap test cannot by itself establish who is better off; ideally the setup should make a swap cycle actually crop up, where each person would rather be the next one and there is no consistent ordering to recover. Third, that asking who gains more from this one policy is a different question from asking who is better off overall, and that the first can be answerable while the second isn't.

What I need from you is the runnable version: exact rules, the payoff and multiplier scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline for 55 minutes, and how to run the reveal. Then a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto those three points, that includes the questions you would ask to draw the point out of them rather than telling them, and that says honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it in the room. Please also be frank about which part of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply myself in words — I suspect one of these is that in the game somebody knows the multipliers and in real life nobody ever does, and I want you to say whether that gap ruins the analogy or is actually the deepest thing to point at. Please keep the whole response, debrief and caveats included, under 900 words.
