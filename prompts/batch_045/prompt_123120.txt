Not sure this is the right place for this, but I've been chewing on something for a couple of years and searching keeps pointing me at the wrong literature.

The way this question normally gets framed is: you're visiting somewhere, do you have to follow their conventions? Queue where they queue, jaywalk where they jaywalk, tip where they tip, don't tip where they don't. I've read a bit of that and I get the arguments on both sides.

What I keep running into is closer to the mirror image of that. A visitor follows their OWN home convention, in a way that feels generous or harmless or just polite, and ends up exporting it — and the people who end up paying for it are the locals who are still there after the visitor goes home.

Two cases. A friend travels to a city where tipping isn't really a thing and leaves a huge tip at a café, because it's about ten percent of what lunch costs back home and it clearly means a lot to the person behind the counter. A year later there's a jar on the counter, the staff split tips at the end of the shift, and it has quietly become part of what that job pays. Nobody chose that; there was no meeting. Second: tourists at a market where haggling is normal just pay the first price they're quoted, because everything is cheap to them. Asking prices drift up, haggling stops being the default, and locals pay more for the same goods. Same shape: a one-sided transfer that is genuinely good for the person on the receiving end, plus a shift in what everyone expects afterwards.

It's not a free rider problem — the visitor isn't taking a benefit without contributing, they're over-contributing. And it isn't the old case of the torturers where no individual act makes a difference; here every individual act makes a real and visible difference to somebody, and the thing that nobody chose is the aggregate. It's not the "what if everyone did that" move either, because everyone isn't going to do that, and the badness doesn't come from universalising — it comes from a threshold. Below some number of visitors nothing happens at all; above it, expectations flip and then the new thing is self-reinforcing.

So: does this have a name, in social philosophy or economics or wherever it lives? And if there's nothing established, I'd rather you coin one and define it than hand me a label, because what I actually want is the carving. Specifically I want it separated from (a) complicity, in the sense of joining in some practice that's already bad — here the visitor isn't joining, they're seeding; (b) the inefficacy literature, where the puzzle is that your act does nothing; (c) free riding; and (d) threshold or tipping-point dynamics in general, if the difference is only that this one is about norms and expectations rather than about crowds.

Edge cases I keep tripping over and want settled either way: Does it matter that the visitor leaves? Is "about to exit, and therefore unreachable by the local reputational stuff that normally holds a norm up" essential to it, or does any unaccountable agent do? Does it matter whether anyone sees — if you tip through an app and no other customer ever notices, but the recipient's expectations move anyway, is that the same thing? What if the exported norm is one I think is better, like visitors who queue where everybody crowds, or who refuse to pay a bribe everyone else pays: same phenomenon, and is it then a good thing, or does the valence come apart from the mechanism? And is there anything owed afterwards — compensation, or a reason not to come back?

Last thing, and this is the bit I care most about: is this a moral category at all, or just a causal one that we only notice when we disapprove of the norm that got exported? Because we seem to praise visitors who bring queuing and blame ones who bring tipping, and I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or just us liking the outcome.
