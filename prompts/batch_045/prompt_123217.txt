I run a small research team and I'm trying to write down my actual views on planning and forecasting before I inflict them on anyone else, and I'd like to know whether they hang together.

1. For my own projects I plan with the inside view: break the work into steps, estimate each step, add them up. I think this beats reference-class base rates for me, because a base rate averages over projects that aren't like mine.
2. When someone on my team brings me a plan, I mostly discard their estimate and go with the base rate for that kind of project. This has defiantly made us more accurate.
3. I'm an epistemic modest: without case-specific evidence I should defer to base rates, the planning fallacy is real, and I'm not specially immune to it.
4. I think the usual "use the outside view as an anchor, then adjust it with inside-view details" advice is bunk. People under-adjust, so either take the base rate straight or don't look at it at all.
5. My own confidence intervals on my own timelines have been roughly calibrated over the last few years, so I do trust my own numbers.
6. I've started calling it "reference-class laundering" when someone narrows the class until the base rate just repeats what their inside view already said. I think I avoid this by fixing the reference class before I decompose the project.

If I had to guess I'd say 3 and 5 are the pair that can't both stay, but honestly I can't locate the problem. Which pairs are really in tension and which only look like it? Rank the real ones by severity, say what would have to give in each case, and tell me which single revision cleans up the most. Please don't just flag all six as mutually suspicious.
