I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and there is one standoff I have never managed to stage in a way my students find genuinely live: the dispute between higher-order accounts and same-order (self-representational) accounts of what makes a mental state conscious. When I lecture on it, students come away with one of two reactions, and I think both are my fault. Either they conclude that the views are the same claim in two vocabularies — both, after all, make consciousness depend on the mind's somehow taking itself as an object — or they conclude that the higher-order view is a non-starter, because they picture it as requiring the subject to be running an explicit piece of self-directed theorizing at every waking moment. I would like a scripted dialogue I can use as a handout instead, and I would like it to be genuinely in the weeds rather than a rehearsal of the slogans.

Here is the setup I have in mind. Two senior philosophers — call them Vera and Aldo — are waiting for a shared shuttle after a conference poster session, with about twenty minutes to kill. Vera defends a higher-order thought account: a mental state is conscious in the relevant sense only if the subject is, non-inferentially and without observation or inference, appeared to as being in that state, via a distinct higher-order state that is standardly caused by it. Aldo defends a same-order view: a conscious state is one that represents itself, intrinsically and non-relationally, so that no separate state is needed and the regress the higher-order view invites never gets started. Both should be excellent at their jobs — they anticipate each other's objections before they are finished, they do not attribute to the other the view that a first-year would hold, and each makes at least one real concession about a cost their own account carries. Please have them work through, in actual back-and-forth rather than in alternating monologue: (i) whether the higher-order state is supposed to be constitutive of the state's consciousness or merely to make an already-conscious state available for report and reasoning, and why Aldo thinks the first reading is unmotivated and the second is no longer a theory of consciousness; (ii) the familiar worry that a suitable higher-order state could occur while the first-order state it purports to be about is absent altogether, and what Vera's theory should then predict about the subject's seeming to be in a state they are not in; (iii) whether Aldo's intrinsic self-representation is anything more than a label for whatever neural or functional property does the work, and whether his view can distinguish a conscious first-order state from a merely first-order one without quietly reintroducing a second state or a second content; (iv) the difference between a creature's being conscious and a mental state's being conscious, and which of these each theory is actually a theory of; and (v) whether, once all this is cleared away, the residual disagreement is substantive or verbal, and what empirical finding, if we had one, would adjudicate it.

I also want them to argue over two cases I have been turning over, which I have not seen discussed anywhere and which I suspect press the two theories differently. In the first — call it the stacked case — a subject's visual system is in a single, determinate first-order state, one that represents a uniform magenta field and nothing else. At the very same time, and both caused by that one state, two non-inferential higher-order states occur: one represents the subject as now seeing magenta, the other represents the subject as now seeing pale green. Asked on the spot what it is like, the subject says it is somehow both and also neither, and that they cannot settle which. Is this one conscious state or two, or one state with two phenomenal characters at once, or a case in which the subject is simply appeared to in two incompatible ways and neither appearance is veridical? Whose content determines the character of the experience — the first-order state's, or the higher-order state's, or do the two theories give different answers to that question in a way that is more than notational? In the second — call it the lagging case — the same subject is in the magenta state for two hundred milliseconds, the state then ends entirely, and three hundred milliseconds later a non-inferential higher-order state occurs, representing the subject as seeing magenta and caused by the short-term trace of the earlier state. Does the earlier state thereby become conscious, and if so, is the subject conscious of magenta at the earlier time or at the later one? I would especially like each character to say what would count as evidence for their answer here, since it seems to me that this is where the two accounts might come apart in a way students can actually see.
