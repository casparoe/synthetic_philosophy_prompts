I've been re-reading the old debates about takeoff speeds, and I want to stress-test the intuition that a fast takeoff is the dangerous case. Please build me a thought experiment to the following specification.

Construct two scenarios with the same starting point, the same terminal capabilities, and the same degree and kind of misalignment in the systems that eventually exist, differing only in the speed of the takeoff, such that the slow-takeoff world is the one that ends worse.

Constraints:

1. Define takeoff speed operationally and say whether you are measuring calendar time between capability thresholds, capability doublings per unit time, or something else. Do not let the choice of metric do the work: I want a world that is genuinely slow on whatever measure you pick.
2. No science fiction. Software, compute, data, ordinary institutions; no nanotech, uploads, or neural interfaces. Human actors are recognizably human, and no actor is stipulated to be unusually wise or unusually foolish.
3. Hold misalignment fixed. The slow world must not lose because its systems are badly behaved in a way the fast world's are not. The loss has to fall out of the structure of the takeoff itself.
4. A short paragraph each; no more.

My own guess about the load-bearing variable is something I would call a response threshold: institutions and publics react to jumps of at least a certain visible size, so capability growth delivered in increments below that size can run a very long way without ever triggering a coordination event, while fast growth crosses the threshold early and gets met with a response. If that is the mechanism you use, make it precise. If you think the real mechanism is something else — distribution of capabilities across many competing labs, diffuse economic stakes in continued deployment, deterioration of any single legible warning shot into background noise — use that instead and say why it is doing the work.

Deliverables: the two scenarios; the verdict in each from the standpoint of someone who thinks fast takeoff is the dangerous case, and why that verdict flips; an explicit statement of the single feature your case turns on; what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed, ideally collapsing back to the standard conclusion; and the strongest objection a fast-takeoff-is-worse person would raise against your case, together with your reply.

One point I want handled directly rather than assumed away: the obvious reply that a slow takeoff simply gives everyone more time to notice and coordinate. Show me why the extra time does not cash out into extra coordination here, or concede the point if your case cannot sustain it.
