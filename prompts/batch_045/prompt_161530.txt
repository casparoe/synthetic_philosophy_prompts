Hey! I'm on the small committee writing the annotation guidelines for a "does the agent know p?" dataset — the agent is an idealized reasoner (not a human, not a language model) and we keep smacking into the logical omniscience problem. Our rulings so far, all about the same agent Vera, a logistics planner:

1. She believes the shipment lands Tuesday, and believes that if it lands Tuesday then the warehouse is full that night; she has never considered whether the warehouse will be full. We ruled: she knows it will be full.
2. She believes the shipment lands Tuesday, that if it lands Tuesday the dock crew works late, and that if the dock crew works late the north gate is left unlocked; she has never considered the gate. We ruled: she knows the gate will be unlocked (two steps, no new notions).
3. She believes A1 and the chain A1→A2, A2→A3, …, A30→A31, and has never considered A31. We ruled: she does not know A31.
4. She believes the shipment lands Tuesday and has never thought about baseball. We ruled: she does not know that the shipment lands Tuesday or the Cubs win the 2031 pennent — even though its a one-step consequence.

(The obvious rule — she only knows what she's actually inferred — is already dead: 1 and 2 kill it.)

The case we now have to decide: Vera believes a pile of true routing facts, and the claim C follows from them. C is stated entirely in terms she's fluent in (trucks, depots, delays — nothing exotic). But the shortest derivation anyone has found is two steps, and every known two-step derivation runs through an intermediate claim about a quantity that appears nowhere in what she already believes — think of it as the "slack" of a rotation schedule, a thing you have to invent to get the proof off the ground. So the conclusion is topically familiar, the only route to it isn't.

What should we rule if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I care less about the right answer in the sky than about: which rules fit all four of our precedents, which of those rules come apart on this case, what each one predicts, our best pick with the reason, which existing precedent is the closest analog, and what principle we'd be committing to either way. And if our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, tell me which extra case we should rule on next to break the tie.

Send it back as a JSON object with these fields: "decision" ("knows" or "doesnt_know"), "closest_precedent" (which of 1–4, plus one sentence on why), "candidate_rules" (array of objects with "name", "statement", "fits_precedents" (true/false), "predicts_new_case"), "recommendation" (two or three sentences), "principle_if_yes", "principle_if_no", "tiebreaker_case", "confidence" (0–100).
