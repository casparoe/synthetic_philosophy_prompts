I help run a small mutual-aid group in a mid-sized city — mostly tenant support and emergency cash, a handful of volunteers, no budget. I have no academic background in philosophy or history; I read a bit and argue with people. We have had two rough years: a rent-stabilization ballot measure lost badly, our main funder pulled out, and two of the things we spent the most energy on turned out, on reflection, to have helped almost nobody. At our last two meetings my co-founder ended the argument the same way both times, with the line that the arc of the moral universe is long but bends toward justice. The first time it was meant to stop us from updating: a bad stretch is noise, the direction is fixed, we should keep doing what we are doing and not redesign it in a panic. The second time it was aimed at me, after I said some of our standard tactics might be actively counterproductive: if history is on the side of the work, the details of strategy cannot matter all that much. I do not think one sentence can do both of those jobs, and I am not sure it does either.

A friend from a reading group offered me one precisification I half like: injustices are inherently unstable — arrangements that rest on coercion, or on people having to accept stories that degrade them, carry the seeds of their own breakdown, so the bend is really a claim about what tends to collapse rather than about what tends to get built. I would like that placed among the alternatives rather than just accepted. There is also a smaller thing I cannot get straight: the slogan says bends, not rises. A bend sounds like a claim about curvature — about whether improvement is speeding up or slowing down, or about the direction of change rather than the level — and I cannot tell whether anyone who says it means that, or only means that things are better now than they were a few centuries ago. And since we only get one history and we are standing inside it, I would like to know, for each reading, what sort of evidence available from where we are could in principle tell for or against it, as opposed to what we could only see from the end.

So please give me a menu rather than a single verdict: the distinct precise theses the line could be expressing, from the emptiest to the most ambitious, laid out as a list or table, with for each one — what it actually asserts; whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false or incoherent; what evidence from inside the curve could support or undermine it; whether it licenses the first use, the second use, both, or neither; and your guess at which reading my co-founder actually had in mind in each of the two arguments. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which reading, if any, earns the work the line was being made to do — then give the menu, with no preamble and without restating my question back to me.
