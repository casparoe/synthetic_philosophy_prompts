You are a philosopher of history and a social epistemologist, with a good grip on how historical records are produced, replicated, lost, and distorted. Please think this through carefully rather than quickly: before committing to anything, brainstorm four or five genuinely different candidate principles — including at least one you end up rejecting because it breaks against my verdicts — and only then argue for the one that survives. No reading lists, no citations, no named authorities: I want the principle and the argument that supports it, worked out from the cases themselves.

Some context on where I'm coming from: I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and my partner works in archives and digital preservation, which is how this started. I have a bad habit of thinking about the past in infrastructure terms — who had write access to the record, which copies got replicated and which got garbage-collected, what survived because it was cheap to serialize — and after an argument about the slogan "history is written by the victors" (she says it's unfalsifiable and nearly useless, I think it gestures at something real but smuggles several different mechanisms under one phrase) I started sorting cases into what I privately call *erasure* (the losing side's records are destroyed and never copied), *capture* (the records survive but only inside the winner's formats, genres, and purposes), and *adoption* (the winners end up circulating the losers' own story). My tentative principle is something like: the version of an event that circulates is the one that is most useful and cheapest to transmit for whoever controls the copying, teaching, and commemorating infrastructure at the time — which is usually, but not always, the side that won the war.

Here are my verdicts, going on gut and not on theory:

1. Rome and Carthage: the city is destroyed, essentially no Carthaginian histories survive, and everything we have comes from Roman or Roman-aligned Greek writers. Verdict: victor's history, obviously — though I don't want that to mean "false," since we can still recover true facts from a hostile source.
2. The Norman Conquest of England in 1066: Norman Latin sources dominate, but the English-language chronicle was still being copied and updated at a monastery for decades afterward, so some of the conquered side's grievances and perspective survive. The overall frame — castles, feudal tenure, a backward kingdom modernized — is the winners'. Verdict: victor's history, with leakage.
3. The Spanish conquest of the Aztec capital: Spanish accounts dominate, but a Spanish friar compiled Nahua elders' accounts of the same events in their own words and those survived. Verdict: still victor's history, even though the losers' words physically survived, because we read them through the winners' categories. This bothers me, because it feels like only a difference of degree from case 2.
4. The American South after 1865: the "Lost Cause" — the losers produced the account of the war that dominated national memory for generations, minimizing slavery and recasting defeat as heroic resistance against overwhelming odds. Verdict: not victor's history. The losers wrote it.
5. The Eastern Front after 1945: the Western public's picture of the campaign was built substantially out of the postwar memoirs of German generals — the winners adopted the losers' account, partly because it was professionally usable and politically convenient in the new confrontation with the Soviet Union. Verdict: not victor's history, and I think it is a different mechanism from case 4: in 4 the winners stopped contesting the story; in 5 they actively picked it up and repeated it.
6. When both sides' archives survive and are roughly equally rich — after the Soviet military archives were opened in the 1990s, the German-memoir version of the Eastern Front largely collapsed — I stop wanting to say "victor's history" and start wanting to say "the evidence base improved." Verdict: not victor's history.
7. When the victors' own later generations open their own archives and overturn the heroic version their grandparents published, I also don't call it victor's history — but it isn't "the losers wrote it" either, and I don't have a word for it, which makes me think my three-way sorting is incomplete.

What I want from you: test my tentative principle against these cases, repair it or replace it, and state the result precisely, clause by clause, telling me which case forces each clause to be there. In particular I want you to face the pair I can't reconcile: cases 4 and 5 are handled beautifully by a "usefulness to whoever controls distribution" principle, but case 1 seems to be pure availability — nothing was adopted because it was useful, the losers' version was simply destroyed, and no amount of usefulness talk explains that. So tell me honestly whether one clean principle can cover both, or whether I have to accept a disjunctive, ugly, two-mechanism account to keep all seven verdicts, and if so which verdict a simpler principle would force me to drop and what it would cost me. Then give me your principle's verdicts on three cases I haven't listed: a war that ends in a compromise where both sides keep their own schools and presses; an event with no winners at all, like a plague or an earthquake, where every surviving account comes from literate elites; and a case where the defeated side had the better bureaucracy and the victors end up running the country using the losers' paperwork.
