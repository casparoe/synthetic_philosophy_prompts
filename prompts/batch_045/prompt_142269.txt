Here's where I've actually got to. I think the debate keeps sliding between two different kinds of claim that nobody in my notes separates cleanly:

(1) type-level or generic claims, about a repeatable kind of setup — this die comes up six about one time in six, this isotope decays with such-and-such probability per hour.
(2) token-level claims about a one-off event — 70% chance it rains on graduation day, 12% chance the election is called before midnight, 3% chance this patient survives the night, and also the retrospective ones ("there was only a 2% chance the barrier would fail").

My sense is that the frequency story is basically built for (1) and has to do something awkward for (2) — pick a reference class, or talk about what would happen in an imagined infinite sequence — while the subjective story is totally comfortable with (2) but then the number looks like it's about us and our information rather than about the world, which feels wrong precisely when the world is what we're arguing about. The propensity story looks like the one that was invented to give you objective single-case probability, so it should win on (2) — except I suspect it quietly leans on the very long-run or counterfactual facts about repeats that it was supposed to replace, which puts us back at (1).

Second thread, and I genuinely can't tell if this is the same point or a different one: propensities are supposed to be causal or dispositional, and causal arrows have a direction. But when we use probabilities to confirm hypotheses we need numbers running the other way — how likely the evidence is, given the hypothesis and given its rivals. If propensities only run forward from setup to outcome, it's not obvious there is any such thing as the propensity of the evidence. So maybe propensity talk can help explain why outcomes happen but can't do the job we need in confirmation, which seems like a real cost and not just a quibble.

Third thread, which is the bit I most want your help sharpening: there's an obvious adult move, pluralism — chances do one job, credences do another, everyone go home. What I want to know is whether that's a position or just a way of changing the subject, because the pluralist still seems to owe an answer to why the two are supposed to line up at all: why is it rational to set your confidence in a single outcome equal to its chance, when the chance is one thing and your confidence is another? And the usual sort of answer (roughly: matching them pays off in the long run) is itself a repeated-trial answer — exactly the kind of answer that isn't available for the one-off cases where the whole debate was supposed to matter.
