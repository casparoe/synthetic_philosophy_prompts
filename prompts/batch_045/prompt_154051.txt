hey — I'm the grad rep on our university's Frontier Questions seed fund committee (two-year internal grants, ~$250k each, we fund 3 out of 12 pre-proposals), and I've got to circulate a decision memo before Friday's meeting. I'm a philosophy of physics PhD student, so I'm the one everyone dumps the "is this even science" argument on. Could you draft the memo?

The application we're stuck on: two people from the theory group want money to build what they call a "framework confidence index" for the string landscape / inflationary multiverse picture. (One of them is my supervisor, so the memo has to be able to stand on its own without me in the room defending it.) They're explicit that no part of the proposal produces an observation or reanalyses a dataset. Instead they want to formalise three lines of argument they claim already support the framework: (i) decades of work has failed to produce a stable alternative candidate that does the same job, and they want to count that failure as evidence in favour; (ii) the machinery was assembled out of parts introduced for unrelated reasons and keeps turning out to hang together in ways nobody built in; (iii) historically, theories that were accepted before their decisive tests usually got their evidence later, so present non-empirical support has some track record. The deliverable is a number — a posterior for the framework — plus a suggested rule for how big a slice of a national theory budget should go to frameworks that can't currently be tested at all.

The committee is split down the middle. Two observational cosmologists and a statistician want to reject: their line is that (i) is exactly the argument that was available for plenty of theories that turned out to be wrong, that (ii) is what any flexible enough formalism looks like from the inside, and that a probability you will never score is decoration rather than measurement. The other three say we already fund plenty of work with no empirical content, that "untestable" is a moving target, and that how a community should allocate effort under deep uncertainty is a genuine question even if this particular framework is wrong.

What I need is a bottom line — fund, reject, or fund with conditions — with reasons the applicants could actually respond to, not a restatement of the demarcation problem.

Two things I'd like you to work in, because I've been pushing them at meetings and nobody has really answered me:

First, the flexibility levy. By the applicants' own back-of-envelope count the landscape has something like 10^500 vacuum solutions, which means that for almost any value of the low-energy constants we might go on to measure, there's some vacuum that produces it. I want us to treat that the way the statistician on our committee would treat a model with a huge number of effective parameters: the likelihood comes out nearly flat over the data, so support for the framework isn't free just because the data lands somewhere inside the range. The bit I can't settle is what would actually discharge the levy — does the framework have to tell us which vacuum we're in, or is a probability distribution over vacua enough? And if a distribution is enough, whose measure? I think the measure problem is the real trap: you need a measure over the vacua and a story about where observers sit, and every way of getting one that I know about gives you different answers.

Second, pre-registration. Before any money moves I want them to write down, in advance, which future results would lower their number and by roughly how much, and to commit to publishing the update when one comes in. If a framework can't name anything that would count against it, I don't see how the number means anything even to them. But I'm genuinely unsure whether that's a fair thing to demand of a theory with no relevant data on the horizon, and if it isn't, I want the memo to say so.

One more wrinkle: whatever we decide, this will end up in a press release about the university backing "frontier" work, and I really don't want to be on the committee that laundered a guess into a headline percentage. Whichever way we go, I want the memo to name the single strongest objection on the side we're not taking and say honestly why we're overriding it — I don't want it to read like we just voted the other side down. And please make the conditions, if there are any, concrete: who reports back, on what, by when. No need for citations or a reading list, it's going to five people who won't read them anyway.
