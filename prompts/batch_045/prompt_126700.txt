I sit on the ethics committee of a learned society in the social sciences, and we have to decide, at our meeting in three weeks, whether to issue a formal censure that one of our own members — call her R. — has drafted and is pressing us to adopt against a senior figure in the field, call him M. The underlying conduct is not really in dispute: between 2009 and 2013 M. published three papers reporting results from a field experiment in which participants were misled about who was funding the study and what it was for, the deception was never disclosed to them afterwards, and M. has declined either to retract or to attach a note to those papers, though he did stop running studies of that kind. R.'s own dissertation research, published in 2011, rested on exactly the same kind of undisclosed deception, and was never sanctioned, because our code at the time said nothing about deception beyond the signing of consent forms; the Society in fact gave that work a prize in 2012. She has since become the field's most persistent critic of the practice, has asked that her own 2011 paper be retracted, and is the author of the censure motion now before us. M.'s written response is short: the Society has no standing to condemn him, because the person moving the censure did the same thing, because the Society rewarded it when she did it, and because at least four other senior members behaved similarly around the same period and are not being censured.

We have been going round in circles with the word "hypocrisy," so I would be grateful for a written opinion I can circulate before the meeting that recommends something concrete and dated rather than surveying the positions available. Three things in particular divide us, and I would like you to take them in turn. First, several of my colleagues want to say that a censure is not an act of blaming at all but a declaratory finding that a norm was violated, entered into the record; if that is right, they argue, questions about who is doing the blaming and whether their hands are clean simply drop out. I am not convinced — a censure seems to do something to its target that an entry in a register does not — but I would like the claim tested rather than merely asserted from either side. Second, there is a move R. makes that I have not seen anyone else make: her standing, she argues, is not damaged but improved by the fact that she is condemning the very thing she did, since the condemnation costs her something and binds her to a standard she has already applied against her own work — call it the reformer's exemption if you like, though I am suspicious that it relabels the hypocrisy and then calls it a virtue. Third, there is the Society's own position, which seems to me genuinely hard: we are being asked in 2025 to condemn conduct that our rules permitted, and in R.'s case honored, in 2012, and to do so selectively, since a general inquest into the field's pre-2015 literature would take years and is not what the committee voted to take up.

What I want from you is a recommendation — adopt the censure, adopt it in some modified form, decline to censure, or postpone pending a specified condition — together with the reasoning behind it, set out as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, since this will go out as an email to eight people who will read it on their phones. Please give the strongest version of the case you are rejecting rather than one that is easy to knock down: if you think we should censure, tell me what the most compelling form of M.'s tu quoque actually is and why it fails; if you think we should not, tell me what is genuinely lost by our saying nothing. Do not cite or recommend any literature — we have no library access from where we are meeting and a bibliography would be useless to us — but do make explicit which distinctions are doing the work in your argument, and flag any point at which the recommendation would change if a contested factual question, such as whether M. knew the code was about to be revised when he ran the last of the three studies, were settled the other way.
