I am a novelist, and I am currently negotiating the deposit of my papers with a university library. Before I sign anything, I want you to reconstruct the theory of what an archive is *for* that the library's standard practice commits me to, and then tell me whether the alternative arrangement I have in mind rests on a genuinely different theory, or merely on the same theory applied more tenderly.

Here is the practice as it has been explained to me. The repository will take the whole of my working papers — notebooks, drafts at every stage, corrected proofs, readers' reports, contracts, diaries, correspondence — and will keep essentially all of it, on the view that material with no apparent value now acquires it later, because discarded drafts are precisely the evidence out of which accounts of a work's making are written. Material that names living people can be sealed, but I gather that a restriction is only acceptable to them if it has an end date, so that what is really on offer is a delay rather than a silence. What the repository does *not* want is a separate box of my grandmother's things: a union dues card, a rent book, two letters from a cousin in a hand I can barely read, a photograph with no names on the back. She was a weaver in a textile town and left no writing of her own. The archivist is not being careless or brutal; she is being professional. That box has no research value in itself, and it acquires relevance only by descent, as background to the person who turned out to be me.

Two features of this strike me as worth interrogating. The first is the asymmetry of completeness: everything about me is worth keeping indefinitely, while an entire life is worth keeping only as a footnote to mine, and only because of an accident of kinship. The second is what I would call extraction: a relationship that lasted forty years exists in the record solely as the residue of one party's filing habits — the cousin's letters survive because they came into my grandmother's hands, my grandmother's side survives nowhere, and what the archive will catalogue is not the exchange but what happened to end up in my house.

So I have drafted a counter-proposal, and I want to know what it assumes. First, a coupling clause: my own drafts sealed until 2050 and releasable only *together with* the grandmother box, so that no one can write the genetic account of the novels without also handling the material the profession would have discarded. Second, a negative finding aid: a document placed at the head of the collection recording what was destroyed, weeded, or never offered, by whom, and under what appraisal judgement, so that the silences are themselves catalogued rather than left to look like nature.

Please write to the following sections, in this order:

1. **The implicit theory** — approximately 350 words. Reconstruct the view of authorship, evidence, and the relation between a record and a person that makes the standard practice intelligible: what the unit of cultural production is taken to be, how a document is supposed to acquire value, who the archive's client is, and what it assumes about the claims of the living against the claims of the not-yet-born.
2. **Strict presupposition versus comfortable fit** — approximately 250 words. Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits well with, and say which rival views it quietly rules out.
3. **Where the practice is underdetermined** — approximately 250 words. I suspect several quite different theories would license exactly this behaviour; if so, say which, and say what observation would distinguish them.
4. **What my counter-archive presupposes** — approximately 350 words. Set out the theory my coupling clause and negative finding aid commit me to, and answer directly: is a documented silence still a silence, or does cataloguing an absence partly undo the power it records? Is the time-limited seal a protection for my father's cousin, who is eighty-six and is the basis of a major character, or is it a transfer of her interests to a future scholar?
5. **What I should do on Monday** — approximately 150 words. Concrete guidance for the deed of gift, given that I cannot rewrite the institution's appraisal policy, only my own conditions.
