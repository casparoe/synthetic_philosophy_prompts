I am writing a paper on Marx on exploitation and alienation and have constructed the following argument, whose conclusion I cannot accept. I would most like to keep the claim that capitalism is distinctively wrong: under capitalism the direct producers are separated from the means of production, their surplus labor is appropriated by a class that does not perform equivalent labor, and the objectified results of their social labor confront them as an alien power rather than as instruments of their consciously regulated common life. I also want to preserve the judgment that a worker-owned cooperative that must make the standard social deductions is not exploitative in the capitalist sense, even if it is not a utopia. Yet the chain below seems to show that either exploitation and alienation are universal features of all social production, or that the Marxian concepts cannot distinguish capitalism from the cases I describe.

(1) Exploitation is a relation in which the direct producers' surplus labor is objectified in a product or fund that is controlled by, and serves the interests of, a person or group that does not itself perform the equivalent labor, so that the producers' own product becomes a power over them.

(2) Alienation is a relation in which the objectified results of the producers' social labor—products, machines, institutions, or market relations—confront the producers as an independent power that is not consciously regulated by their associated will, and therefore dominate their life activity.

(3) In any society with a developed division of labor, the immediate producers cannot consume the whole current social product; the social product must include a portion that replaces used-up means of production, a portion that expands or improves them, a reserve or insurance portion against accidents and fluctuations, a portion for the administration of common affairs, a portion for common needs such as education and care, and a portion for the maintenance of those unable to work.

(4) The withheld portions described in (3) are objectified in means of production, reserves, administrative apparatuses, public goods, and support funds rather than being individually consumed by the producers.

(5) Therefore, in every such society, part of the objectified product of labor stands outside the individual producer's consumption and helps constitute a social process that no single individual directs alone.

(6) If the test for exploitation is that part of the product of labor is objectified outside the producer's individual consumption and used in a social process the producer does not individually direct, then any society that makes the deductions in (3) exploits its producers.

(7) If the test for alienation is that the objectified results of labor confront the producers as a power that is not directed by their associated will, then the fact in (5) shows alienation wherever the deductions are made, since the objectified means of production and the apparatus of administration necessarily condition present labor.

(8) Consider three cases. Case A, the Recursive Workshop: a firm owned equally by its workers, governed by one-person-one-vote, selling its output on a market, and making exactly the deductions in (3). Case B, the Inherited Plan: a socialized economy in which associated producers democratically approve a plan, but the plan must be executed through machinery, infrastructure, logistics, and administrative routines inherited from past generations and not wholly redesignable at each planning cycle. Case C, the Bureaucratic Deduction: the same deductions as in (3) are made, but the apparatus that administers them is not recallable by the associated producers, and control over the social surplus reproduces the power of a bureaucratic stratum.

(9) In Case A, the surplus withheld from individual workers is not appropriated by a private non-laboring owner; it returns to the same workers collectively as repaired machines, expanded capacity, insurance, and common funds. Yet, because the firm must compete on a market, the workers' own objectified labor in the form of capital equipment and market constraints continues to shape their activity in ways no individual worker controls.

(10) In Case B, the producers collectively own the means of production and vote on the plan, but the plan is carried out through an apparatus of past labor that confronts each individual as a preexisting structure; the associated producers as a whole may control the process, yet each individual still experiences the social metabolism as an objective power.

(11) In Case C, the deductions are still in one sense made for the maintenance and expansion of the social conditions of production, but control over the surplus is vested in a stratum whose power does not depend on the associated producers' ongoing consent and is not exercised as their consciously regulated common will.

(12) Applying (6) and (7): the Recursive Workshop and the Inherited Plan both make the deductions in (3), both have objectified means of production and administrative routines that condition labor, and in both no single individual directs the whole process; so both are exploitative and alienating. Case C looks exploitative and alienating as well, though for the additional reason that the surplus is controlled by an unaccountable stratum.

(13) Since a worker-owned cooperative and a democratically planned socialized economy are supposed to be noncapitalist and, on the Marxian view I want to defend, should not be exploitative or alienating in the capitalist way, the result is unacceptable.

(14) Hence either (a) exploitation and alienation, as defined in (1) and (2), obtain in all social production and cannot be used to distinguish capitalism from the Recursive Workshop or the Inherited Plan; or (b) the tests in (6) and (7) misapply the definitions in (1) and (2), and the real distinctions must be drawn somewhere else.

Tell me which numbered step or which transition between steps is where the argument goes wrong. Name the step precisely, explain why it looked compelling and what plausible Marxian principle it seems to follow from, and price the alternatives: if I reject that step, which of my other commitments must I modify, and what would I then have to say about Cases A, B, and C? In particular, say whether your diagnosis preserves the judgment that Case A is not exploitative in the capitalist sense, whether it treats Case B as alienating, exploitative, both, or neither, and whether Case C introduces a distinct political form of exploitation or alienation rather than a mere variant of the capitalist case. If you think no single step is faulty and the chain is valid up to the disjunction in (14), say which horn of that disjunction is least costly to accept and what it costs my attempt to defend a distinctively Marxian critique of capitalism.
