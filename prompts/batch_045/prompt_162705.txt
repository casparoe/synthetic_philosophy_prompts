I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas, mostly AI lately — and I got into an argument over a pint with a mate who's a product lead at a company that builds customer-service chatbots for banks. She's being asked to write a single definition of a "deceptive output" into their internal spec, something their evals team can actually test against, and we ended up going round in circles on three real examples from their logs that she showed me. In the first, a customer asked whether they'd always get the bank's best savings rate, and the bot answered something like: "Your rate is fixed for twelve months, so you'll earn the rate shown today." Every clause is checkably true, but the bank quietly stops paying the loyalty bonus when a fixed term matures, so a lot of people renew onto a worse deal, and the bot doesn't mention it. My mate says that can't be deception because nothing it said was false; I say the customer was steered into a false belief and that's the whole point. In the second, the bot told someone "I can't access your account details" when in fact the details were sitting right there in its context window — it just isn't permitted to use them. That one is literally false, and she wants it removed, but it feels to me like the one that's closest to honest. In the third, it told a user that "most customers in your situation end up paying about £40 less per year" — a number no one can source, it's just the model's sense of what a typical answer looks like, and it happens to be roughly right often enough across thousands of chats that nobody flags it.

My own instinct, which I'm not sure I trust, is that a lie needs someone who intends to get someone else to believe something false, and that a language model doesn't intend anything at all, so strictly speaking none of these are lies — but that conclusion seems to let everyone off the hook, and I don't believe it, so I think I'm missing something about what we should be asking for instead. I keep wanting to say the thing that matters is what the system is *for*: whether it's built to leave the user better placed to act on the truth, or just to produce a plausible-sounding sentence that closes the ticket. She thinks that's too wooly to put in a spec.

I'm not on deadline for this, I just want to be able to write something sharper than "it's complicated" in the piece. Can you untangle it for me: what's really going on in each of those three cases, why do they feel so different, and what should she actually write down? I'd also like to know where my intention-based instinct goes wrong — and whether there's a version of it worth recieveing, so to speak, because I do think it's pointing at something when I say the chatbot didn't *mean* to mislead anyone.
