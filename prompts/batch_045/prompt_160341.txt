I spent thirty-eight years as a physical oceanographer, most of them building and arguing with circulation models that I never for a moment took to be agents. I'm also well used to descriptions that hold at one scale or resolution and vanish at another — that is most of what large-scale modelling consists in — so higher-level talk about a system does not scandalize me. In retirement I have started sitting in on a philosophy of mind and AI reading group, and I have been asked to write up, for next session, where I stand on one question: whether a large language model is an agent, a simulator of agents, or something else again. I would much rather have the inconsistencies in my position found beforehand than from the floor.

Here is what I actually believe, written down in one sitting:

1. A metaphysical scruple. The trained system is a set of weights plus a sampling procedure: a function from token strings to distributions over tokens. Anything I go on to say about agency, belief, or character has to supervene on that, and I do not want to be committed to anything spooky at the base level.

2. The simulator framing seems to me to get something importantly right. Next-token prediction over a corpus written by an enormous number of distinct people gives the training objective no way of privileging one character, so what results is a machine that can continue any of a very large family of voices rather than one that has a voice of its own.

3. But I also want to insist that the contrast between being an agent and simulating one is a substantive difference in how a system is organized — not merely a verbal preference about how to use the word "agent." I do not want the question to dissolve into "describe it however you like."

4. I am a pragmatic, intentional-stance sort of person about belief and goal ascription: attributing a belief or a goal to a system is warranted just insofar as the attribution buys predictive leverage you cannot get otherwise, and I take patterns that earn their keep to be real in the only sense I need.

5. Accordingly I am happy to say that the model knows Paris is in France, but not that it wants to be helpful, and I think I can defend that asymmetry rather than having simply inherited it.

6. The strongest argument on the other side, to my mind, is the fine-tuning one: post-training compresses the space of continuations toward a single, stable, context-independent persona, and a system that carries one persona across episodes looks considerably more like an agent than like an indifferent simulator of many.

7. My own attempt at a criterion — call it diachronic grip, and please knock it down if it deserves it: X is an agent rather than a simulator only if what persists across episodes is better predicted by an intentional characterization (a standing goal, a character, a body of belief) than by a specification of capacities plus a role, where "better predicted" means: better, conditional on everything you could observe at the opening of a fresh episode.

What I want is an audit, not a tutorial. Which of these commitments actually pull against one another, which only look as though they do, and what is the least costly way to make the set consistent? My suspicion is that at least one has to go, but I cannot see which, and I would rather not discover it mid-seminar. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equal, say for each genuine one what exactly would have to be surrendered, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and give me a verdict on whether diachronic grip survives as a test at all or is covertly the simulator framing with extra steps.
