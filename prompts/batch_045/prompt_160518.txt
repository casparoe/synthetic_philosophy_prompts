My town has spent two years fighting about a school named after a 19th century mill owner who paid for the building and who also, it turns out, organised the local campaign to keep Irish families out of the ward. I got curious about the general question of what statues and names actually do, and now my brother-in-law Dave, who argues about everything, has backed me into a corner I cant get out of. His argument runs: (1) A statue, a street sign or a name on a building isnt just a marker; its a claim in the first person plural. It says here is who we are and here is what we admire, and it says it in the name of everyone who has to live under it. (2) Saying something in someone elses name is borrowing their voice, and borrowing a voice without permission is what turns an otherwise harmless act into an imposition — even when the thing said is true, and even when the person has no complaint about the content itself. (3) Nobody asked me. I never voted on the school's name, and neither did most people here; and even if there had been a vote, the majority could only lend their own voices, not mine. (4) So every bit of commemoration in this town is an unauthorized use of my voice and of everyone elses, and unauthorized uses of a persons voice are pro tanto wrong. (5) Nothing about the case cancels that: honoring a decent man is still using him to say something I never agreed to say. (6) So commemoration as such is wrong, full stop, and the right response is to take it all down — the school name, the statue on the green, the street signs.

What bugs me is that the argument seems to work too well, and also to point somewhere Dave doesnt want to go. If it works, it condemns the war memorial on the common with the names of the dead on it, and the little plaque for the town's first schoolteacher, and — here's the part I keep coming back to — it condemns the removal as well, since taking a statue down in the town's name looks like exactly the same kind of borrowing. So (6) doesnt even seem to follow from (4) and (5): the most those steps give you is that whatever we do we're doing something wrong, not that removal is the answer. I tried two escapes on my own and both collapsed. First I thought the content of a monument is fixed by what people now take it to say, not by what the people who put it up meant, so a name can quietly stop being praise and turn into just a label — nobody hears Mill Street as a compliment to a mill — but Dave pointed out that this only relocates the question to who gets to decide what the uptake is, and it also implies the older a monument gets the less it commits anyone, which seems backwards for the cases that started all this. Then I tried saying that putting up is something you do while leaving up is just letting something be, so they aren't on a par; he said the town pays to clean it, repoint the plinth and insure it every single year, so keeping it is a fresh act every budget cycle. That one I still think is right somewhere but I cant make it stick.

So: which step of Dave's argument do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? The two things I'd most like to keep are the thought that a statue really does say something in the name of the community — that's what got me interested, and without it I dont know why I care who a school is named after — and the thought that putting up a monument and taking one down are not morally the same shape, even though the town maintains both. I'll give up nearly anything else. Tell me why the step you're rejecting looked fine when I first heard it, what I have to pay to give it up, and what the cheapest available alternative is if I refuse to give up those two. Please answer in American English.
