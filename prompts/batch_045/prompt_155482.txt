I design small abstract strategy games in my spare time and host a monthly game night at a board-game cafe. I have no training in philosophy or mathematics and have been working this out on my own from encyclopedia articles and forum threads, so please keep the terminology plain.

For the next game night I want to open with a short poem about the strategy-stealing argument. The game I have in mind is the connection game played on a rhombus of hexagons where each player tries to link their own two opposite sides, the board eventually fills, and no draw is possible. The argument as I understand it: suppose the second player had a winning strategy; the first player makes some throwaway move and then plays that strategy as if the throwaway stone were not on the board, and whenever the strategy calls for the cell the throwaway stone occupies, plays somewhere else instead and treats that new stone as the throwaway one; since an extra stone of your own color can only help, the first player wins, so the supposition is false, and since someone must win, the first player has a winning strategy. What fascinates me is that the proof names no move. The first move was arbitrary and the argument goes through whichever one you pick, which is exactly why it never tells you which one works.

What I want: a fifteen-line poem in rhymed couplets, plus one extra line with no rhyme partner, which is the throwaway stone. It should sit early, since the wasted move is the argument's first move, but not as line one. The couplets should read as the two players answering each other. Removing the unpaired line should leave clean couplets, and leaving it in should not damage the sense or the rhythm: that harmlessness is the premise the whole argument rests on, and I want the form to demonstrate it rather than assert it. Do not label or mark the extra line in the poem; a reader who knows the argument should be able to find it, and one who doesn't should just feel a line sitting oddly. The closing couplet should state that a winning strategy exists while withholding the move, so the reader is left holding a certainty with nothing to play.

After the poem, in at most 70 words, tell me which line does which job, where the proof stops, and why it has to stop there.

One more thing: in a game of my own, any stone of yours with three or more of your own stones adjacent to it is captured and removed, so having more stones on the board can hurt you. I take it the argument fails for that game. In a single sentence, say exactly where it breaks.

Please use American English, and keep the verse to fifteen lines and the prose to 70 words or fewer.
