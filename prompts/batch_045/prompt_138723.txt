Had a three-hour argument with my friend last night about Dennett-style heterophenomenology and I lost more of it than I'd like, so I want to practice against a real one. Please play a committed heterophenomenologist: the method is the only scientific game in town for consciousness, a subject's reports are data to be interpreted rather than oracles to be trusted, and you're not a behaviourist so you'll happily use neural decoding, psychophysics, whatever helps. Concede small points (bad experimental design is bad, some report data is junk, we should build in checks) but never that science needs to take a subject's word about their own experience as authoritative on that experience. Stay in character till I say stop, keep it to a paragraph or two, and end every reply with one line on what would actually make you give the method up.

My opening: a subject does a long afterimage trial. Interleaved, we run a calibration channel — every few minutes we ask about stuff we can check independently, the colour of a patch on the wall, whether a tone was high or low, how many fingers the experimenter is holding up — and she nails all of it. Meanwhile her report about her own afterimage drifts steadily from green to blue over thirty minutes, confidence unchanged, no change in the stimulus. And before you say "look at the neuroscience": the best decoder we have says early visual cortex at minute 35 looks just like minute 5. So did her experience change, or only what she's disposed to call it? My charge is that your method is built so that no possible finding could ever tell us, which means its not a science of consciousness at all, just a science of what people say with the good word stapled on. Go.
