I'm drafting the first chapter of my dissertation — a short position statement on what it takes for a system to count as acting for a reason, as opposed to merely behaving as if it did — and my supervisor has read the outline and told me that the package of things I want to say doesn't obviously hang together, but has declined to say where. Before I commit a term to defending it I'd like an audit. Here is the package, roughly in the order in which I find each piece attractive: (a) I accept Darwin's strange inversion of reasoning in its strong form — competence without comprehension goes all the way down in the biological case, no homunculus ever shows up in the machinery, at no point does anything like a reason enter the mechanical story of how a competence is produced, and I don't think there is a bright line in phylogeny or in development at which comprehension switches on, so that this is an explanation rather than a lament; (b) I accept Turing's version of the same inversion just as fully — demonstrated competence settles that the system is doing the thing, and the reflex that says a machine is "just doing statistics" or "merely pattern-matching" is exactly the mind-first residue the inversion was supposed to dissolve; (c) I'm nonetheless a realist about reasons: when someone acts for a reason, that reason belongs in the explanation of what they did and in its justification, and is not merely a pattern projected onto them by an interpreter; (d) I'm a reasons-responsiveness compatibilist about moral responsibility, and I think responsibility scales with answerability — an agent's capacity to articulate the considerations it was responding to, and to be held to them, is part of what makes it more or less responsible; (e) I hold an etiological constraint on content: what makes a system's internal state about something is that the lineage that produced it was shaped by the thing it is about, in the way a frog's fly-detector was shaped by flies, and not by our interpretive convenience; and (f) for that reason I deny that current large language models have beliefs or desires, act for reasons, or are about anything at all — reading them from the intentional stance is a heuristic with practical value, but there is nothing there for it to be true of.

The case I keep getting stuck on is one a friend in machine learning pressed me with. Call it the curated lineage: instead of training a single network, you maintain a population of them; each generation replicates with heritable mutation in its weights, every individual is evaluated on a task inside a simulated environment, failures are deleted outright and the survivors' weights seed the next generation, and after some hundreds of generations you end up with a system whose internal states track features of its environment about as reliably as a frog's visual system tracks flies. My verdict is that this system is still a mere tool — no more a genuine agent than the single network you started from — but when I try to say what it lacks, every candidate I reach for (a history of selection, heritable variation, differential retention, being tested against an environment it has to cope with) it seems straightforwardly to have, and yet the conviction that it is an optimized artifact rather than an agent won't go away. If I keep that verdict, something in (a), (b) or (e) has to move, and I can't see which, or how much it costs me elsewhere.

So what I want is an audit of the six commitments, not a lecture on the topic. Which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it — I'd rather you tell me that one of the apparent conflicts dissolves on inspection than that all six are equally shaky — and for each real tension, what the least costly revision would be, what giving that up would cost me elsewhere in the package, and how severe the tension is relative to the others. Please rank them rather than listing them flatly, name the single revision that would clean up the most, and tell me explicitly which commitment my verdict on the curated lineage forces me to abandon and whether that verdict is worth the price. Please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list: I've read the literature, and what I need is the argumentative map of my own position.
