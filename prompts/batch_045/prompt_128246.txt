A friend of mine restores wooden sailing vessels for a living, and she has unusually firm intuitions about identity over time. Over several years I have collected the following verdicts from her, and she has never wavered on any of them.

1. A vessel has every timber replaced one at a time over forty years, the old timber burnt as it comes off. Same vessel throughout; there is no moment at which the question becomes borderline, and it would be absurd to ask what fraction of replacement was the threshold.
2. The same gradual replacement, except that the old timbers are kept in a shed and, within a month of the last one coming off, are assembled into a vessel of the same design. The continuously maintained vessel is the original; the reassembly is a replica made of old wood. She is unmoved by the fact that the replica has all the original matter, the original design, and the original arrangement of parts, and unmoved by the observation that on her view being the original and having the original's matter simply come apart.
3. A vessel sinks, is raised a year later, and is restored over five years keeping only the keel and a handful of frames. Same vessel.
4. A vessel is deliberately taken apart, every piece catalogued, stored for two years in a warehouse, and then put back together by the same shipwrights. Same vessel. She says that existing for two years as a heap of numbered parts is not a break in that vessel's history.
5. A hull is left nine-tenths finished on the stocks for thirty years after its funding lapsed, then completed and launched. The launched vessel is the same vessel as the abandoned hulk, and already was that vessel while it sat unfinished under its shed.
6. A vessel is cut lengthwise, widened by four feet, re-rigged from schooner to ketch, and mostly re-timbered over the following decade. Still the same vessel.
7. A museum commissions a from-scratch replica built to the original lines, in the original materials, using the original techniques. Not the original, and not a borderline case.

When I once asked her whether what makes the maintained vessel the original is that the register, the name, the crew and the owners all follow it, she said no: they follow it because it is the original, not the other way around. But she also conceded that she had never encountered a case in which they came apart, so I have never known how much weight to put on that answer.

Here is the case I want to put to her. A vessel, the *Cormorant*, is launched in year 0 and for sixty years is maintained by replacing one timber at a time. Every timber that comes off is carried across the harbour to a navy yard, where shipwrights immediately fit it, in its original position, into a second hull that is under construction continuously throughout those sixty years; that second hull is completed and launched in the same week in which the last original timber comes off the *Cormorant*. So at that moment: vessel A has been in continuous service for sixty years and contains none of the launched vessel's matter, while vessel B contains all of it, in the original arrangement, assembled gradually and continuously out of it, and has never been to sea. One year later A is destroyed by an explosion; nothing survives her but the ship's bell, which had never been replaced. The navy transfers the name, the commissioning date, the battle honours, the logs and the bell to B, which then sails as the *Cormorant* for fifty more years, in the course of which every one of its timbers is replaced.

Two questions, and I care much more about the reasoning than the answers. First, at the moment of B's launch, with A still afloat, which of the two will she say is the original *Cormorant*, and how confident will she be? Second, fifty years after A's destruction, will she say that B — now with none of the original matter and with fifty years of its own replacement behind it — is the original? Beyond the predictions: what rule is she implicitly running; which other rules fit all seven of her earlier verdicts just as well; what does each of those rules say about this case; which prediction would you actually commit to and why; and what single further case would best separate the rules that survive?
