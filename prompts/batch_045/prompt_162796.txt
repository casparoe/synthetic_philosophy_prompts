Hey — I'm in a decision theory seminar this term (philosopy dept, cross-listed with econ) and I'm trying to work out what I actually think about the urn cases before I have to write anything up, going by gut rather than by the readings.

Setup for all of them: you get $100 if the ball comes up red, otherwise nothing. K is a normal urn: exactly 50 red and 50 black, shaken, drawn by a machine. M is 100 balls, red and black in some mix I'm not told, and I'm also not told how it got filled.

Here's where I land. Red from K versus red from M: I take K. Black from K versus black from M: I also take K. I know that pair is the famously awkward one and I still feel both. M filled by someone who gets $100 if I lose and who knows I'm betting on red: I take K, more strongly than in the first case — but honestly not that much more, which surprised me. M filled by someone who gets $100 if I win: I still take K. Less confidently, but I take it, and that one feels dumb of me. M filled by a machine that pulled 100 balls one at a time out of an enormous bin that is half red and half black, where I'm told this and believe it but can't check: here I'm basically fine, I'd take either urn — which is strange, because I know exactly as much about the actual contents of M as I did in the first case. K, but the draw is done by hand by someone with a tremor, and I've been told red balls are heavier and sink: I feel a flicker of something, but I'd still take it at even odds. Nowhere near as bad as the first case. Two urns, K and M, one ball from each, $100 if the two balls are the same colour: totally fine, feels like a coin flip. Someone in my seminar said that if I'm fussy in the first case I should be fussy here too, and I don't see why, but I also can't explain why not. And: I can pay $5 to watch one ball get drawn from M and put back before I choose. I'd pay. If it comes up red I feel better about betting on red, but I'd still take K.

The rule I've been telling myself is: if I don't know the chances, act as if they'd been set to make me lose — unless I know the process that set them, in which case just work out the number and go with that. I think that gets the basic case, the hostile-filler case and the machine-filled case, but it says the wrong thing about the friendly-filler case and it's way too strong on the tremor case.

So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or a defensible subset? I want it stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I haven't seen, with the verdicts that motivate each bit of it, and an honest account of which of my verdicts it can't save and why those are the ones to give up. And I want to know what it says about the one I keep flip-flopping on: M is 100 balls and all I'm told is that it contains at least 30 red and at least 30 black. Half the time that feels like knowing nothing, half the time it feels like it makes the whole thing fine. Also, does the principle have anything to say about whether I should pay the $5, or is that a separate question I'm smuggling in? I have a feeling it might be. I can't look anything up right now and I don't want paper recommendations or a reading list — I want the formulation and its verdicts, plus whatever standard rivals are worth putting next to it.
