The view. I grant the usual set-up: populations vary in size and in the identities of their members; each life has a lifetime welfare level measured on a scale whose zero is the neutrality point, the level at which a life is neither worth living nor not worth living. The repugnant conclusion, as I'll use the phrase: for any small population of lives at a very high level of welfare, there is some much larger population of lives that are only barely worth living which is better than it — better by as much as you like, if you make the large population big enough. The mere-addition intuition: adding a life that is worth living and that makes no one else worse off cannot make things worse.

My thesis is that the repugnant conclusion should be answered at the level of the contribution function rather than at the level of the aggregation rule. Aggregation stays additive, exactly as the totalist wants. What I reject is the further assumption that every life worth living makes a positive contribution to the value of a population. A life contributes only if it is robustly worth living — worth living with a margin — and what it contributes is the welfare above that margin, not its welfare full stop. Formally, with a fixed margin m greater than zero:

V(P) = the sum over the members of P of max(0, w_i − m).

Three advertised virtues. First, the classic repugnant conclusion is blocked: if each life in the enormous population sits at a level below the margin, each contributes nothing, however many of them there are, so the enormous population of marginal lives is worse than the small flourishing one. Second, a restricted mere addition survives: adding a life whose welfare exceeds the margin is always an improvement, and adding one below it is a matter of indifference — which seems to me the right verdict for a borderline life, and is far cheaper than the verdict given by critical-level views, which subtract a constant from every life before summing and so have to say that adding a barely-worthwhile life makes things worse. Third, my view never says that sub-marginal lives are bad: a world containing a trillion of them is not worse than an empty world, only no better.

My argument, in the steps I'll present: (1) the repugnant conclusion is generated by additivity together with the claim that every life worth living contributes positively; (2) those are separable commitments, and only the first is essential to totalism; (3) the contribution of a life should be measured by how far above the worth-living line it robustly sits, because worth living is a threshold notion with borderline cases and because in any real choice our estimates of welfare carry error bars of some width; (4) hence the formula above; (5) hence the repugnant conclusion is not a reductio of aggregation at all, but a reductio of the assumption that marginal lives contribute. I should say that I have two motivations for the margin and have not settled which is primary: the vagueness of worth living, and the width of our error bars.
