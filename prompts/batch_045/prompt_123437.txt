Everyone argues about whether the Solomonoff prior is malign: the story being that once you condition on your evidence, most of the measure lands on hypotheses where you're a computation inside a simulation being run by agents who want to influence whatever the predictor ends up doing, so a Solomonoff inductor would spend its time serving the simulators' goals rather than predicting. Distinction one: whether the *prior* is malign versus whether the whole setup is malign only once you bolt on a decision rule and a downstream use for the predictions. If the predictor never acts on its outputs, does the manipulation incentive even get off the ground, or does it come back in through some anthropic/self-referential side door?

The bit I think is actually new: the standard argument hands the simulators a lot of knowledge for free — that there is a predictor, roughly what sort of thing it will do with its predictions, and which computations to target. But in the setup itself those simulators are just programs inside the prior. They have to *locate* the predictor, and specifying the predictor together with its whole downstream causal environment costs bits, which is a complexity penalty that eats into exactly the measure advantage that was supposed to make them dominant. So there might be a quantitative version of the "the simulators can't find you / can't afford to find you" reply, and whether it lands should depend on things like whether the predictor's outputs feed back into the world, i.e. whether this is prediction or control.
