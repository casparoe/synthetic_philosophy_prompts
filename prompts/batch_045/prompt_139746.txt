Hi! I'm a third-year PhD student and I help run a small reading group that meets irregularly and argues constantly, currently on "quining qualia" — Dennett's four-property characterization of the traditional target (ineffable, intrinsic, private, directly apprehensible), the Chase–Sanborn-ish replies, and the more recent illusionist literature. Two of the regulars have been at each other for over a year and have finally, mostly because I nagged them, agreed to write a joint statement. I got volunteered to draft it, and I'd rather hand you a messy brief than another 3am attempt.

The two positions: a phenomenal realist — there are phenomenal properties, they are not captured by any functional, representational, or cognitive-role description, and what it's like is the residue the physical/functional story leaves out — and an illusionist (call them a qualia eliminativist), who thinks the four properties are bolted together in a way nothing could jointly satisfy, that "qualia" so characterized is an incoherent posit, and that the correct thing to say is that there are no phenomenal properties even though it introspectively seems to us exactly as if there are. I want the document they could both put their names to, in three parts. First, a numbered section of claims both can sign: about the data any theory has to accomodate (that we make introspective judgments with a peculiar first-personal feel, that those judgments are predictably responsive to intervention, that they come in degrees of confidence, whatever else you think is genuinely common ground), about which of the standard anti-qualia arguments is doing the real work (the no-explanation / explanatory gap argument, the hallucination argument, the specification-or-indexicality worry about how the realist ever pins down which property they mean), and about what each side is explicitly not committed to. Second, a disagreements table: the point at issue stated neutrally in one column, each side's own formulation of its view in its own column, worded so that side would accept it as theirs rather than as a strawman, and a column flagging whether you take that particular row to be a substantive disagreement or a verbal one. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them — and for each crux, one case or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I want predictions, not verdicts: don't tell me who wins.

Three things from our meetings I'd like you to use or to say plainly why they're wrong. (1) I suspect a lot of the fight is a thin/thick equivocation: "there is something it is like, experience presents itself to the subject as having a character" versus "that character is intrinsic, ineffable, private, directly apprehensible." My guess is the hallucination argument only bites the thick notion and the realist only ever needed the thin one — but I can't tell whether the illusionist is also denying the thin claim, and if they are, whether that stops being verbal. (2) I've been calling it the Error Parity problem: the illusionist's argument needs the possibility of hallucinating a quale, but error about X normally seems to presuppose there being facts about X; if introspective judgments have no accuracy conditions at all, in what sense are they in error? My supervisor keeps pushing an analogy with a global error theory (about colour, or about morality) as the illusionist's resource — is that analogy load-bearing or a distraction? (3) A case I made up, which isn't in the literature so don't try to place it: future cognitive science finds subjects (call them the Schmen) whose reports track a dimension of their experience that varies while everything we can functionally or neurally discriminate stays fixed; a subsystem predicts those reports; lesioning it removes both the reports and the disposition to draw the distinction, with no other measurable change; and cross-wiring it between two subjects makes each report the other's history. What should each side say about that, and what would each predict before we run it? Also please say which parts of the whole dispute you'd diagnose as merely verbal and why, and be blunt about it — if you think it's verbal all the way down, or substantive all the way down, say that instead of splitting the difference. Keep it around 900–1200 words, and don't give me citations or a reading list — I've got the bibliography, I need the argument.
