I run a small team that trains systems on judgements we are not able to check directly. Our working scheme is to break a hard task into small sub-questions that a weaker assistant, or one of us, can answer with confidence, assemble the sub-answers into an answer to the whole task, and then train a single system to produce such answers itself, in one pass, by imitating a large number of assembled results. That system then becomes the thing we break the next round of tasks down for.

There is a failure pattern in this scheme that I keep meeting and cannot name. Two cases. We summarise long documents by summarising paragraph by paragraph and then merging; every paragraph gets a faithful summary, the merge reads well, and on our rubric it scores as well as what we would have written — but it silently drops the one sentence on which the document's significance turned, because that sentence was a hinge visible only in the relation between two paragraphs, and our per-paragraph instructions rewarded local fidelity and nothing else. We review changes to a program by asking, separately, whether a change is correct, whether it is secure, and whether it is readable; a change passes all three and introduces a deadlock, because the defect lives in the interaction of the three concerns and no sub-question had authority over that interaction.

Three features seem to be doing the work, and they are what make the pattern hard for me to place. First, the loss shows up specifically at the step where the multi-step procedure is compressed into a single system answering in one pass: the slow procedure does catch these things, because someone can step back and notice that the parts do not fit together, whereas the compressed system reproduces the shape of good answers without whatever it was that made the assembled answers good. Second, our checking cannot see the loss, because we evaluate by re-running the same decomposition, so anything living in the relations between the parts is invisible to the check by construction. Third, it compounds: each round describes its tasks in the vocabulary of the previous round's decomposition, so a gap in one round is inherited by everything downstream.

I do not think I am describing the familiar point about a measure coming apart from what it stood in for — Goodhart's law, roughly. That seems to presuppose a target independent of the measure, and for tasks like "what matters in this document" we have no such target: at least partly, the decomposition itself is what fixes what counts as getting it right. Nor is it merely the old observation that a whole can have properties none of its parts have, which is true here but too general to be of use. My colleagues and I have taken to calling it "the articulated blind spot", which I suspect is wrong, since it makes the problem sound merely epistemic.

Does this pattern have an established name or a literature of its own? If not, I would like a name together with a definition that marks its edges: which cases are instances of it, and which merely look like instances. Please do not leave me with a bare label — I want the carving. In particular, I want to know whether the three features I have listed belong to one phenomenon or to two or three, and whether the second really is distinct from the first.

And please push back rather than accommodate me. If the description above is confused — if there is no single pattern here, if the "gap" is only the ordinary cost of compressing a procedure into one pass, or if my reason for setting Goodhart's law aside is itself mistaken — say so plainly, tell me which premise to drop and what I should have asked instead, and mark how confident you are in each of your claims and what would change your mind.
