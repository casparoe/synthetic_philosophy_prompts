THE QUESTION (20 marks)

(a) State the Principal Principle with the care it requires, including the role of the time index and of the admissibility clause. Then explain why the principle is stated as a claim about conditional credence — credence in A, given that the chance of A is x, together with admissible evidence — rather than as the simpler claim that a rational agent's credence in A simply equals A's objective chance. Give a case in which the simpler claim and the official one give different answers.

(b) A hospital triage model outputs, at 9am, a probability of 0.2 that patient P will be admitted to intensive care before midnight. Suppose you know that this figure is the true objective chance, indexed to 9am, of that outcome. Nothing further is learned until noon, when the model is updated with new lab results and outputs 0.7; suppose you likewise know that this is the true objective chance, indexed to noon, of the same outcome. It is still noon and the outcome is not yet settled. What credence should you now have in the proposition that P is admitted to intensive care before midnight, and how, precisely, does the Principal Principle bear on the case? Then consider a variant: everything is as before, except that what you learn at noon is that at 6pm you will be sent a message reading "P was admitted," from a source you know to be perfectly accurate about this. Does the Principal Principle tell you to have credence 0.2, credence 0.7, some third value, or nothing at all? Explain your answer, and be explicit about what work the admissibility clause is doing in the variant.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

The Principal Principle says: let C be any reasonable initial credence function, let t be a time, let A be a proposition, and let x be a real number in the unit interval. Let X be the proposition that the chance, at t, of A's holding is x, and let E be any proposition admissible at t. Then C(A | X and E) = x. Informally: treat the objective chance the way you would treat an expert — once you have learned what the chance is, and you have no inadmissible information, set your credence equal to it.

The principle has to be stated as a claim about conditional credence for two reasons. First, an agent usually does not know what the chances are, so her unconditional credence in A should be her expectation of the chance of A, averaged over the chance-values she thinks possible, rather than any particular value. If I know that a coin is either heavily biased toward heads or heavily biased toward tails, but I have no idea which, then my credence in heads should sit in between the two, and not at either of them. Second, the conditional formulation is what makes room for the admissibility clause: the clause marks off the body of evidence relative to which deference to the chance is required. Admissible evidence is evidence that bears on A only through what it says about the chance of A; evidence that tells you directly whether A occurs, or that derives from a time later than the t to which the chance is indexed, is inadmissible, and where evidence is inadmissible the Principal Principle requires you to set it aside and defer to the chance alone. The clause is what gives the principle its content. Without it, the principle would collapse into the trivial claim that you should believe whatever your total evidence supports, and would lose whatever is distinctive about chance.

It is worth being clear about what the principle is doing, because that settles how it can be criticized. The Principal Principle is best read as an analysis, or definition, of objective chance: chance just is the quantity that rational credence defers to in this way, and the point of the principle is to fix what we are talking about when we talk about chances, not to impose a substantive constraint that some metaphysical theory of chance might turn out to violate. That matters for the undermining arguments in the literature, which hold that a best-system, Humean account of chance permits chance assignments whose truth is rendered highly improbable by the very credences the principle demands, so that chance and deference to chance come apart. On the reading I have given, those arguments cannot get started: a definition cannot be refuted by a metaphysics, so there is nothing for them to bite on, and the correct response to them is to reject their assumption that the principle is a substantive claim about the world rather than a claim about what chance talk means.

(b) At noon I have evidence about two chances: 0.2, indexed to 9am, and 0.7, indexed to noon. Since the later chance is computed on strictly more information than the earlier one — the model has the labs as well as everything it had at 9am — it screens off the earlier chance, and the Principal Principle requires deference to the most recent chance: my credence should be 0.7. The earlier figure has become irrelevant, the way a stale forecast is irrelevant once a newer forecast incorporating it has been issued.

In the variant, what I have is evidence about a later event, and hence evidence from after the time to which the relevant chance is indexed, so it is inadmissible. That means the Principal Principle tells me to disregard it: my credence should remain at 0.7, the chance at noon, and my knowledge of the 6pm message is simply not the kind of thing the principle permits me to use. This is the payoff of the admissibility clause — it is exactly what prevents information about the future outcome from crowding out the objective chance. If evidence like the message were allowed to count, the link between credence and chance would be broken, and there would be nothing left to distinguish the Principal Principle from ordinary conditioning on whatever you happen to learn.
