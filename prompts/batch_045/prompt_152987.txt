Hi — I'm a journalist on the science desk, doing a long read on why we keep getting caught out by things we can perfectly well model, and I want one section of it to be a proper autopsy of the Great Storm of 15–16 October 1987 through the lens of chaos and predictability. Not the weather-porn version, the conceptual one.

Facts as I'm happy to work with them (please don't go hunting for a source, and I'm not fussed about exact numbers): a depression out of the Bay of Biscay deepened explosively through the day — the sort of thing now called a weather bomb — down to about 953 hPa by midnight, crossing the Channel and hitting the south east. Around 22 people died in England and France; gusts around 115 mph on the Sussex coast; millions of trees down; large parts of the south east blacked out. Earlier in the week the farmers' forecast had flagged bad weather for the Thursday or Friday; later model runs pulled the severe weather south, keeping the worst over the Channel and the French coast; through the evening the public broadcasts played up the rain an played down the wind, though the Met Office did warn the MoD just after half one in the morning that the military might be needed. Michael Fish's forecast the previous evening is the bit everyone remembers. Afterwards the Met Office upgraded observations, models and forecaster training.

The framework I want you to use, stated so you don't need to have read up on it: the distinction between predictability of the first kind (given the right dynamics, forecast the later state from the state now) and of the second kind (forecast how the statistics of the system respond when the forcing or the boundary conditions change). Plus the distinction between error that grows because you got the initial state wrong and error that's baked in from the start because the model is the wrong shape — missing physics, too coarse a grid.

What I want from you:

- Where does the failure actually sit in that space? Initial-condition error over a data-sparse Atlantic, structural model error, or something the framework has no slot for? My suspicion, which you should push back on if it's wrong, is that the real mismatch is in the predictand: they needed near-surface gusts over land and the models were handing them pressure fields and tracks, so we're not even talking about the same variables.
- 'Is a dangerous storm heading for us' looks much more like a second-kind question than a trajectory question. If that's right, was chaos-talk doing any work here at all, or was it an excuse bolted on afterwards?
- What should a forecaster facing a system like this have done? I care about the decision problem, not the dynamics: with an ensemble putting a small but real probability on catastrophe, what's the right thing to say on telly at six in the evening — and does the framework speak to that or go quiet?
- Run it the other way too: does the episode tell us anything about the theory? My hunch is it shows 'unpredictable' is a three-place thing — a system, a predictand, and somebody who has to make a call — not a property of the weather. Tell me if that hunch is definately wrong, and tell me where chaos theory is genuinely silent about this case rather than making it speak.
