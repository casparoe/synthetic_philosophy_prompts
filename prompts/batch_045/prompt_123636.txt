I am a philosopher by training, and next week I am guest-lecturing in a colleague's first-year course on formal reasoning: seventy-five minutes, a whiteboard, no slides, and an audience whose background I would put at truth tables plus about a term of Fitch-style natural deduction. No computability, no modular arithmetic, and no Gödel. I want to use Hofstadter's MU puzzle as the vehicle for getting across what he calls jumping out of the system — the MIU system with axiom MI and the four familiar rules (if a theorem ends in I, append U; from Mx derive Mxx; replace any occurrence of III by U; delete any occurrence of UU), the question whether MU is a theorem, and the invariant argument that settles it. I am not asking you to solve the puzzle or to explain the material to me; I have taught it before and it went badly. I opened by announcing that MU cannot be derived and then spent fifty minutes making the divisibility argument airtight, the students transcribed it in silence, and on the problem set they treated "MU is not derivable" as though it meant "MU is a false string," while one of them asked whether we could not simply add a rule that produced MU. So what I need is a plan for the explanation: the order in which the components have to arrive and what depends on what — in particular whether the students should be generating derivations and failing to find MU before any talk of invariants, how much of the hour that failure should be allowed to cost, and how the invariant is to be introduced to people for whom "multiple of three" is not yet a usable tool.

The misconception map is where I most need your help, and for a specific reason. I am convinced the standard presentation invites two errors, and I do not know where in the sequence to defuse either: (a) hearing the moral as Gödelian — "there are truths the system cannot prove" — when the strings of MIU have no intended interpretation in which MU could be true or false, and what is missing is derivability rather than truth; and (b) hearing the jump out of the system as an escape from systemhood altogether, when what I want them to keep is that the meta-level argument is itself formalizable, so that a further jump would be available and no jump is final. Relatedly, some of them will balk at counting zero as a multiple of three, and some will take the invariant to be a trick that happens to work here rather than an instance of a method with a shape. As a check I have built a small companion puzzle: call it MIU*, identical to MIU except that the third rule reads II → U instead of III → U. There MU is derivable in two steps, and there are theorems whose I-count is a multiple of three — six, for instance. My question about it is where in the hour it belongs and what I should be asking as I put it on the board: is it the closing check, a mid-session demonstration that the invariant is a discovery about these particular rules rather than a general feature of puzzles, or a diagnostic I hold back until I see who is floundering?

I also need to be told plainly which of my ambitions to drop. I had hoped to gesture at incompleteness in the last ten minutes, and I now suspect that gesture is precisely what manufactures error (a). Please give me the plan in the following sections, in this order: "Sequence" (roughly 500 words) — the ordered components with the dependency between each adjacent pair stated explicitly, marking where the students are doing something rather than listening; "The invariant" (roughly 200 words) — how divisibility by three should be made to feel like an observation about the rules rather than a number-theoretic fact taken on trust; "Misconceptions" (roughly 350 words) — errors (a) and (b) above, plus the ones you expect that I have not named, each with the point in the sequence at which it should be headed off; "Check" (roughly 150 words) — the placement and handling of MIU*; "What to withhold" (roughly 150 words) — including your verdict on the closing gesture at incompleteness and what I should openly refuse to explain at this level.
