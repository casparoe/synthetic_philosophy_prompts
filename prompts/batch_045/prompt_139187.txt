I have been running a graduate reading group on the ethics of creating people for four years now, and we have got nowhere at all with the procreation asymmetry. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

The asymmetry itself: the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even when there is nobody for whom it is good, and the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is somebody whom it deprives. Benatar's defenders take this to show that coming into existence is never a benefit and always, on balance, a harm. His critics say either that the third claim sneaks in an impersonal, non-person-affecting value that nothing else in their view can accommodate; or that the package is gerrymandered, combining a person-affecting principle where it helps the argument and an impersonal one where it helps the argument; or that no claim about what is better or worse *for* a merely possible person can even be formulated, because in the outcome in which she is not created there is no subject of the comparison for the predicate to attach to.

Every argument on each side has been made, and nobody has moved. My own hypothesis is that 'the asymmetry' is doing duty for at least three different theses that we keep running together: an axiological one about the value of states of affairs, a deontic one about the reasons a prospective parent has, and a comparative one about whether existence is better or worse for the person who comes to exist. If that is right, much of the back-and-forth is two groups answering different questions under one heading, and the dispute that looks like bedrock — whether value can attach to a mere absence with no subject — is really only the first thesis in isolation.

Two cases I have started using as probes. First: suppose a couple will bring exactly one child into existence and can choose which of two possible children — one of whom would have a life well worth living, the other a life not worth living — but cannot bring about neither. Everyone in the room insists they must choose the first child, and yet on the asymmetry it is hard to see what reason applies: creating the happy child benefits nobody, since nobody is deprived by her absence, and creating the miserable child is not worse *for* her than the alternative in which she does not exist. Second: suppose a couple can create a child whose life will, with known probability, be excellent or dreadful, with no way of influencing the odds and no alternative child available. A symmetric view treats this as a straightforward expected-value calculation. The asymmetry seems to say that the upside never counts in favour at all — the forgone pleasure is not a loss — while the downside counts decisively against, so that creation is ruled out at any non-zero risk of a bad outcome. That is a dominance argument from one tail of a distribution only, and it looks to me either like a reductio or like evidence that a decision-theoretic question about weighing merely possible subjects is doing all the work.

So: is my three-theses hypothesis correct, or is something else holding the debate in place — a hidden metaphysical question about whether the same individual can be identified across outcomes in which she exists and outcomes in which she does not, a background disagreement about the shape of practical reasons, or bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is the question partly ill-posed? And given whatever you diagnose, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a formal result about choice under uncertain existence, a change of question, or nothing at all, and why? Please argue for the diagnosis with reasons rather than canvassing the literature, and note in passing how your diagnosis handles the fact that the same asymmetry appears to give no guidance at all when the question is whether *I* should go on existing rather than whether someone should begin to.
