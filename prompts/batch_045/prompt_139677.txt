I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on formal epistemology: 14 students, one 110-minute session, plus a 50-minute session a week later that I can use for follow-up if the design needs it. Everyone has worked with Bayes' theorem and conditionalization, can compute a likelihood ratio for simple hypotheses, and has used a proper scoring rule at least once. I want a runnable in-class activity that makes students feel the problem of old evidence from the inside, rather than having me derive that a known item of evidence blocks the update and then list the standard escapes.

Design constraints.

The problem has to arrive through the rules, not through me. Ideally students notice that they have updated on a fact they already had, and that the machinery they were happily using twenty minutes earlier gives them no licence to do so. No up-front lecture, and they should not hear the label for the problem until the debrief.

The case must be self-contained, with no history of science and no background knowledge: a closed, enumerable hypothesis space they build or inspect themselves (a deck with a hidden dealing rule, a jar of differently weighted dice, a protocol with one concealed constraint), so that every likelihood they need is one they can compute or estimate on the spot rather than take on trust.

There should be real but small stakes via a scoring rule, and a trading mechanic on top of it: students can buy and sell credence in hypotheses with each other, so that the market price makes visible a confirmation that their own betting slips deny. My hope is that the gap between the two — the price moves, the personal posterior supposedly does not — is where the problem becomes something they are inside of rather than something they are told about. Keep the payoff scheme computable in the head; I do not want arithmetic to eat the session.

The mechanic I care most about takes their priors away. In the first round they bet on hypotheses before seeing the data, on slips that I collect and do not return — or return shuffled, so that nobody can pair a prior with a posterior. The situation I actually want them in is not the textbook one of not yet knowing E, but the one of knowing E while having lost the ability to reconstruct what they thought before E. That, I suspect, is the ordinary scientific case, and it is worse than the textbook case: conditionalization is not merely silent here, it is unusable, because the quantity the formalism needs no longer exists for the agent. Call this prior amnesia. Build the mechanic so that they try to do the Bayesian comparison and find that they cannot, while still having confident, fine-grained judgments about which theory the old evidence favours. If you think prior amnesia is a confusion or a red herring, say so plainly and propose something better rather than accommodating it.

I also want a distinction I have been using put under pressure. Call evidence *stale* when the agent knew the fact and already knew it bore on the hypothesis class before the theory was articulated; call it *dormant* when the agent knew the fact all along but only saw its bearing on the theory once the theory was stated. My claim is that the intuition of confirmation is strongest and most insistent in the dormant case, and that this is exactly the case where the Bayesian has least to say, because what really moves the agent is the newly noticed entailment rather than the fact itself. Stage both cases in that order — stale first, dormant second — and then tell me whether the distinction is doing real work or whether it collapses under pressure; if it collapses, tell me what distinction to put in its place.

Deliverable: rules, a materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline, the payoff scheme in a form that fits on one handout, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto specific parts of the argumentative structure — including, for each of the standard escapes (treating the agent as if she did not know E; treating her as learning that the theory entails E; reassigning priors or going counterfactual), which moment is meant to make it attractive and what it costs. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I expect at least one student to refuse to bet on propositions about entailment, and I expect the market to be dominated by two loud students — how to handle each, and be frank about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and which I will simply have to state in the debrief.

Finally: mark which of your design choices are load-bearing, and say what would change your mind about them. In particular, tell me what would convince you that a scoring-rule-and-market setup is the wrong vehicle here at all — that it makes the problem look like a fact about willingness to stake rather than a fact about confirmation — and where you think your own design is most likely not to produce the experience I am asking for. If the prior-amnesia mechanic or the stale/dormant split is the weakest link, say that instead of building around it.
