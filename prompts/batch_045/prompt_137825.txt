I am working through a recent popular book on rational choice, and I would like to know how much of its treatment of the "Death in Damascus" case I should trust. The passage in question runs roughly as follows:

"Suppose you are in Damascus, and Death calls on you to say that he will be back at noon tomorrow. You have two options: stay where you are, or take the road to Aleppo tonight. Death is an excellent predictor of what people will do — suppose he is right nineteen times out of twenty, and that his accuracy does not depend on which way you jump. Your setting out for Aleppo is therefore strong evidence that he will be waiting in Aleppo, and your staying put is equally strong evidence that he will come to Damascus. Evidential decision theory asks you to perform the act you would be most glad to learn you had performed, and by that test the two acts are equally bad news; once you subtract the discomforts of a night on the road, evidential decision theory tells you to stay at home and wait to die. Causal decision theory reasons differently. Death has already settled on his destination, and nothing you do tonight can change it: your action has no causal influence on where he will be. So the probability that you die in Aleppo is simply the prior probability that Death wrote down 'Aleppo' — one half — and the journey is clearly worth taking. The case is Newcomb's problem in new packaging: in each, a predictor's reliability manufactures a correlation that has nothing to do with causation, and causal decision theory sees through it while evidential decision theory is taken in. This is why the profession has by and large moved to causal decision theory, and why evidential decision theory now survives only as a historical curiosity. There is one further lesson, oddly overlooked by the literature: if you have a coin, use it. No predictor, however reliable, can anticipate a fair toss, so by letting a coin decide your destination you halve your chance of dying. The rational traveller in Damascus goes where the coin sends him."

Two things in this struck me as doing more work than the book admits: the claim that the probability of dying in Aleppo is "simply" one half, and the offhand stipulation that Death's accuracy does not depend on what you do. Neither is argued for. A friend who works in this area also tells me that the case is, if anything, harder on causal decision theory than on evidential decision theory — something about its recommendation not being stable, so that whatever you settle on, the theory then tells you to do the opposite — but I cannot tell whether that is a genuine problem or a specialist's technicality, and the book never mentions it.

Please go through the passage and tell me, more or less sentence by sentence, what someone working in decision theory would accept, what they would regard as an acceptable simplification for a popular audience, and what is wrong or misleading in a way that matters. I would be especially grateful for treatment of the following:

(i) Is it fair to present evidential decision theory's verdict as "stay at home and wait to die", or is the real disagreement between the theories narrower — or differently located — than that phrasing suggests?

(ii) What would the "one half" have to rest on? Given that Death is a nineteen-in-twenty predictor of what you will do, is that prior available to you, or is it constrained in a way the book ignores?

(iii) Does the stipulation that Death's accuracy is act-independent carry any of the weight? If he were, say, better at predicting people who stay than people who flee, would the two theories' verdicts still come apart, and in which direction?

(iv) Does the coin-flip moral at the end follow from anything true in the passage? And if it does follow, does it support the theory the book is advertising, or is it neutral between the theories?

(v) What exactly is the instability my friend mentions — what fails to be stable, for which theory, and under what assumptions? Does it bear on which theory handles the case better, or is it a separate problem altogether?

(vi) Is the comparison with Newcomb's problem apt, and does anything remain of the closing claim that the case settles the choice between the theories?
