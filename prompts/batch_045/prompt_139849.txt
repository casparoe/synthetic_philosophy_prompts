Here is the question exactly as it appeared on the paper:

Question 3 (25 marks). Jackson's Mary knows all the physical facts about human colour vision before she leaves the black-and-white room, and she learns something when she sees red for the first time. (a) Set out the knowledge argument as tightly as you can, making explicit any premise that is not stated in those two sentences. (b) Explain how the ability hypothesis responds to it, and explain how that response differs from the phenomenal concept strategy. (c) Now consider Prosthetic Mary. Before her release, neurosurgeons reconfigure her visual system so that she can imagine red on demand, pick red things out reliably in any scene, and recognise and re-identify red on later occasions — every ability on Lewis's list — while by her own report, and by every behavioural and neural measure we have, she undergoes no conscious experience of red and nothing whatever changes for her subjectively. And here is the script, transcribed verbatim apart from a spelling correction; it came in at 640 words.

Script 47, Question 3.

(a) The argument needs a suppressed premise. Explicitly: (1) Before her release Mary knows every physical fact about human colour vision. (2) On release she learns something she did not know before — what it is like to see red. Jackson's inference goes through only if learning is always a matter of coming to know a fact, that is, only if all knowledge is propositional. So the missing premise is (3): if someone learns something, then there is some fact that they have come to know. From (1), (2) and (3) it follows that (4) there is a fact about what it is like to see red which is not a physical fact, and physicalism is false. In his earlier work Jackson went further and inferred that qualia are causally inert, though he later abandoned the argument altogether; the argument itself does not depend on that further claim, since it is an argument about the extent of Mary's knowledge and not about the causal powers of experiences.

(b) The ability hypothesis — Lewis and Nemirow — denies that Mary gains any new knowledge at all. Knowing what an experience is like is not knowing a fact but having a capacity: to imagine the experience, to recognise it when it occurs, to remember it. Since none of that is propositional, premise (3) fails, and the argument never gets off the ground. The phenomenal concept strategy is the same reply under another description. It also says that what Mary acquires is not a new fact but a new concept, and a concept just is a cluster of abilities — the ability to deploy a recognitional-demonstrative mode of presentation of the very property she already knew about under a physical description. Loar's and Papineau's versions differ only in how they tell the story about what the new concept refers to; the mechanism, and the reason the argument fails, are exactly as in Lewis and Nemirow. Mary acquires abilities rather than knowledge, and so there is no additional fact for the physicalist to find room for. Both replies are therefore purely epistemic: they explain why it seems to Mary that she has learned something without conceding any non-physical fact.

(c) Prosthetic Mary has all of the abilities. So if knowing what red is like just is having those abilities, then Prosthetic Mary knows what it is like to see red while she is still in the room, before she has ever seen anything red — which is absurd. The ability hypothesis is therefore refuted: the case shows that the abilities are neither necessary nor sufficient for the knowledge, and once Lewis has built the knowledge out of abilities, the case pulls them apart. The phenomenal concept strategy, by contrast, is untouched by Prosthetic Mary, because it does not analyse knowing-what-it-is-like into abilities at all; it says that a genuinely new concept is acquired, and concepts are not abilities. The physicalist should accordingly abandon the ability hypothesis and rely on the phenomenal concept strategy. That is the right result anyway, since the phenomenal concept strategy is the stronger reply: unlike the ability hypothesis it also does something about the explanatory gap — the sense that something is left unexplained even once all the physical and functional facts are in — which is a different datum from the one the knowledge argument trades on, and one the ability hypothesis was never designed to handle.
