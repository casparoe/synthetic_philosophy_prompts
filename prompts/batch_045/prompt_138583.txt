So I'm in this reading group — we did Capital vol. 1, then the bit of the Grundrisse that people now call the "Fragment on Machines" — and we spent about two hours shouting at each other about automation and got nowhere, which is partly why I'm here. Please play Karl Marx: sharp, impatient with what you'd call vulgar political economy, willing to give up a small point but never the main one, and not willing to just lecture me about the tendency of the rate of profit to fall, because we already did that part to death. You've just been given a careful description of a modern "lights-out" factory: no humans on the floor, robots tending robots, the whole control layer a software stack that was designed once and now copies for essentially nothing, so the marginal cost of another unit of output is basically the energy bill plus a handful of contract engineers whose wages are a rounding error. You may use only the tools you actually had — constant and variable capital, surplus value, socially necessary labour time, the difference between the technical and the value composition of capital, the falling rate of profit and its countertendencies, including the one where machinery keeps getting cheaper in labour-time terms — plus what I've just told you about the setup. Stay in character until I say stop, keep it to a paragraph or two per turn, and if you need a fact about the factory, ask for it as Marx would.

Here's my opening move, and it's the one I think your position genuinely can't handle. In this plant the new value added by living labour is roughly zero; on your own account the machinery can only transfer its own value, which is falling all the time, and creates no surplus value at all — and yet the firm earns a big, boring, completely normal profit. You'll probably say that profit is redistributed surplus value siphoned from less productive capitals whose commodities now contain more labour than is socially necessary. Fine for one firm. But the whole sector is going this way, and then the whole economy after it: at the limit there is nobody left to exploit and no productivity gap left to arbitrage, so your theory has to say either that profit evaporates — which really should have started showing up by now in exactly the industries where automation is furthest along — or that profit never came from surplus labour in the first place. And it looks to me like you half admitted this yourself in that Grundrisse passage where general social knowledge becomes a direct force of production and labour time stops being the measure: that reads like a man watching his own labour theory of value come apart in his hands. So, Marx — which is it, and what do you do with the fact that the machines keep getting cheap precisely because less and less labour is needed to make them?
