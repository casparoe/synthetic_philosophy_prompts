You are a philosopher of mind and cognitive scientist at the top of your field — the kind of person who teaches the graduate seminar on mental representation and can also talk to a general audience without condescension. Please work carefully and in stages, and show your work as you go.

First, spend some time brainstorming before you write anything: map the logical space of the dispute rather than the list of named positions you have seen defended. Which distinctions actually separate one view from another — the question of whether vehicles must have discrete constituents composed by rules; whether the structure attributed to a state has to be causally explanatory or merely interpretively useful; whether constituency is innate or learned; whether the relevant vehicles sit inside the head at all; whether a high-dimensional state space can host genuine role-filler binding, or only the appearance of it; whether the dispute is one about metaphysics or about the best explanatory model, and what happens if two models fit the behavioural evidence equally well. Note which combinations of answers are available in principle but which nobody occupies, and say why you think they are empty: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unclaimed. Only then locate the actual positions in the space you have built.

Here is the commission. I am a journalist covering science and ideas, working on a long-form feature about whether the recent success of large neural network models should change what we think the mind is, and for the online version I want a short interactive sidebar: a decision tree in plain English that sorts readers into a position on the language of thought debate. Think of it as a diagnostic instrument, not an explainer.

Constraints on the instrument:

Readers are scientifically literate non-philosophers. Eight branch questions maximum, six to eight leaves. Every branch question must be phrased as a verdict about a concrete case — something the reader can have an opinion about without knowing which view it leads to — and never as self-description. So no node along the lines of do you think the mind is a computer or are thoughts sentences. Nothing may ask for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; the sidebar has to stand on its own.

The angle that matters for my piece is a specific one: whether the structure in a thinker's head has to be a matter of discrete constituents combined by rules, or whether it can be a matter of geometric and topological relations in a high-dimensional state space — and, if the latter, whether it is still fair to call that a representation with content at all. Build the tree along that axis, not along the more familiar ones.

Please use at least these cases as the raw material for your branch questions: a food-caching bird that later takes a novel shortcut between two caches it has never travelled between directly; an infant who looks measurably longer at an event in which one solid object appears to pass through another; a deep network trained only to predict the next token in a sequence that nonetheless supports analogical role-filler binding in its activation space; a person who reports solving a problem by seeing a spatial route with no inner speech whatsoever; and — this one I would like you to place high in the tree — the case in which a symbolic model with discrete constituents and a continuous vector model fit the same creature's behaviour equally well, so that the choice between them looks empirically underdetermined.

Your leaves must include: the classical view that thought is computation over innate, discrete, syntactically structured vehicles; the compatibilist view that such structured vehicles are realized in distributed neural activity with an explicit binding mechanism; the view that there are vector-shaped states with as-if content but no genuine constituents; the harder-nosed version of that view on which content attribution is only a convenient description and nothing in the system really represents; the view that natural language in the form of inner speech is the vehicle of genuine propositional thought and that non-linguistic creatures get something weaker; the view that cognition can be genuinely structured and genuinely capable of misrepresentation in a map-like or diagrammatic way without any propositionally articulated syntax; and a view on which the relevant vehicles are not all in the head but spread across body, tools, and environment. Name each leaf with its standard label if it has one widely used, and otherwise describe it in a phrase and note that it is unlabelled.

Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate — where the diagnostic questions run out and the distinction becomes terminological or a matter of emphasis — rather than pretending the tree distinguishes them. For each leaf, give me two sentences: its most awkward commitment, and a plain-English line I can actually print in the sidebar without embarrassing myself in front of a specialist. Then give me a short note on the two or three empty cells in the logical space and what you think their emptiness shows.
