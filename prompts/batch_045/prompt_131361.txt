I've been reading about long-run predictive failures — Malthus, the population-bomb literature, peak oil — and I keep catching myself wanting to say of some failed forecasts that they were right but early, and of others that they were simply refuted. I'd like to know whether there is a principled line here, or whether right but early is just what people say when they don't want to give up a theory.

Here are my verdicts, case by case.

1. Malthus, in the 1798 Essay: population tends to grow geometrically, subsistence at best arithmetically, so that any gain in per-capita output is eaten up and the check reasserts itself through misery, vice, or moral restraint. Over the two centuries after he wrote, real wages in Europe rose enormously and population growth slowed on its own. I want to say: not refuted, dormant. The mechanism is still there; what defeated it was the Green Revolution and, underneath it, industrially fixed nitrogen, plus the demographic transition — and both look like borrowed time, since fertilizer runs on finite inputs and the transition has itself slowed.

2. The famine predictions of the late 1960s and 1970s — in their starkest form, hundreds of millions starving to death within a decade or two — which didn't happen, together with the ten-year commodity bet Ehrlich lost to Julian Simon. I want to say: refuted as stated. And I want to say that even though I still grant Malthus dormant status in case 1. I know these two cases look structurally identical; the only difference I can point to is that Ehrlich attached dates.

3. Peak oil. Hubbert's curve is usually said to have called the US peak within a year or two of 1970; the same sort of exercise applied to global supply in the 2000s forecast a peak that shale pushed off by decades. I say: the US case confirmed, the global one early rather than wrong — but only because a shale boom looks like a finite, one-off reprieve. I am noticeably less confident here than in case 1.

4. The older scarcity panic about natural nitrates — guano and Chilean saltpetre — before nitrogen fixation. I say: dissolved rather than postponed. There is no moment at which that worry comes back, because the constraint was on a particular feedstock, not on the nutrient.

5. Moore's law. I say: not a candidate either way. It held for decades, but it functioned as an industry roadmap that engineers worked to meet, so its success isn't evidence about any mechanism. I'm not fully sure, though, that a self-fulfilling prediction should be disqualified from being right.

6. Julian Simon's claim that human ingenuity is the ultimate resource, so scarcity never wins in the long run. I say: not right-but-early but unfalsifiable — no observation could count against it, and if my principle licenses it, I have built an immunity machine.

7. Early quantitative projections of the rate of CO2-driven warming, made before satellite records, which are generally said to have tracked observed warming reasonably well. I say: confirmed. This is the case that makes me trust the category at all — though what was confirmed was a rate, not a date for catastrophe.

The pair I can't reconcile: I treat the Green Revolution and nitrogen fixation as a genuine excuse for Malthus (1) but Simon's ingenuity as no excuse at all (6), and both say that technology bought us time. Whatever distinguishes them will probably cost me a verdict somewhere else.

My tentative principle — call it the dormancy test. A failed long-run forecast counts as right but early rather than refuted only if: (a) the mechanism it named is still operating and measurable now; (b) the factor that defeated it is itself finite or self-limiting, with a checkable expiry; (c) the theory could have named that factor, and its temporariness, out of its own resources before the failure was observed, rather than recruiting it afterwards; and (d) the theory says the reprieve is borrowed — that deferred pressure builds rather than vanishing. I take (c) to be the anti-immunity clause and (b) the anti-vagueness clause. Two things I can't settle: whether clause (c) could ever be satisfied by a theory like Malthus's, which says nothing about nitrogen at all; and whether a prediction with no date attached can be early in the first place, which is what separates 1 from 2 in my gut.

Please test this against my seven cases, repair it where it fails, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop. Then give its verdict on two cases I haven't settled: the fusion-power forecast that has been about thirty years away since the 1950s, and the 1970s talk of a coming ice age.

Please keep your answer under 850 words, in this shape: a table with one row per case (1 through 7), my verdict, and whether your principle saves or overturns it; then the repaired principle as numbered clauses, each followed by a note on which case forces it; then your verdicts on fusion and the cooling scare; then one sentence on whether right but early is a legitimate status or just a way of never losing an argument.
