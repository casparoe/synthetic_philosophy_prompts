I'm TAing a grad seminar on theories of consciousness this term, and for the last review session I want one handout that actually sorts people instead of handing them a menu of labels. Me and a friend spent an hour after class last week arguing about the higher-order stuff and we kept just trading names at each other — that's just the thought-theory, no that's the inner-sense version, no that's dispositional — and it dawned on us that neither of us could say what the difference was supposed to amount to in an actual case. So: build me a decision tree / taxonomy of the higher-order family and its near neighbours, constructed from the logical space rather than from a list of names. That means: lay out the branching questions first and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the real positions in that space, then tell me which boxes are empty and why (incoherent? just unattractive? nobody has bothered?) and which pairs of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate — flag those rather than inventing a fake distinction to split them.

Constraints on the nodes. Every branch node has to be a question someone can answer without already knowing which theory it leads to, and it should hang off a concrete case rather than a slogan; no node may be "do you think a higher-order state is required?" in fancy dress. The cases I want used as the diagnostics, at minimum: (1) a crossed-metacognition patient — intact first-order visual state, but her higher-order state accurately tracks a different visual state, so she sincerely and confidently reports green while her first-order state is red; (2) an empty higher-order state — she is represented as being in a state she isn't in, with no first-order candidate there at all, and the question is whether she now undergoes something or nothing; (3) a retrospective case — she isn't aware of being in pain at the time but forms an accurate higher-order state about it a second later; (4) an alien co-processor — an implanted device that reads her first-order states and writes accurate higher-order states into the system, possibly with a lag; (5) a stipulated dissociation in which stimulation moves her confidence / metacognitive sensitivity while leaving first-order discrimination unchanged; (6) animals and pre-linguistic infants. I also want at least one node on the regress (does the higher-order state itself need to be conscious, and if so how does anything stop) and at least one on order-relativity: the worry that at the subpersonal level one and the same state can count as first-order under one description and higher-order under another, so that "higher-order" may be description-relative and explain nothing.

Format: around eight branching questions, ten to fourteen leaves, sized so the flowchart plus legend fits on two sides of A4 — keep each node to one line. Each leaf gets a short label, the commitments that put you there, and one sentence on the most awkward thing that leaf is stuck with. Attach a person's name to a leaf only where you're confident, and mark names as optional: the handout has to be usable from the commitments alone, because I don't want any leaf resting on who wrote what. Please make sure the leaves or explicit near-misses include an actualist thought-theory, a dispositional variant, an inner-sense or quasi-perceptual variant, self-representational / same-order views, global or whole-system higher-order variants, a tracking version where the higher-order state needn't represent its target as such, first-order and workspace-style rivals, and the deflationary nothing-here-to-explain option. Finally, mark at each node whether the branch is decided by a conceptual argument or by something we'd have to go and test — I want students to see which of their answers are empirical IOUs.
