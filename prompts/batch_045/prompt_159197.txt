So afterwards I started making up variants of the case, partly to annoy the group, and one of them has been bugging me. Say the ship has its planks swapped out gradually, one a year, and the originals are kept in a warehouse and eventually reassembled — that's the standard set-up, plus the second-ship twist somebody (Hobbes? I might be misremembering) added later. But now imagine a third scenario: each plank, as it comes off, is immediately chipped up, chemically pulped, and hot-pressed with a bit of resin into a brand-new plank of the same dimensions, which is then fitted straight back into the hull. So at every moment the ship contains essentially all of its original matter, and essentially none of its original structure — no original fibres, grain, joints, nothing. Call that Ship C. To me as a chemist it feels like the interesting one, because it splits apart the two things people normally lean on: sameness of stuff versus sameness of arrangement. (I also want to know whether it makes any difference if the replacement is done all in one weekend rather than over thirty years, or if that's a red herring.)

What I'd like from you is a ranking rather than an essay.
