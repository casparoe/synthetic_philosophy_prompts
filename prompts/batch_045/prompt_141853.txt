I'm finishing the preface to an edited volume I put together — twenty-eight chapters, several thousand substantive claims — and I keep catching myself in the same awkward posture in unrelated parts of my life, and I'd like to know whether one principle actually governs it or whether I'm just being inconsistent in a way I've learned to live with. Start with the two cases everyone names. A lottery: one winner out of eight hundred thousand, drawing tonight. I won't say to a friend "my ticket will lose," it feels like a lie or at least a cheat, and yet I've already made plans for tomorrow that assume it loses, and I don't feel the slightest compunction about that. The volume: if a colleague emails to ask whether chapter 9's dating of a manuscript is right, I'll answer yes without hedging; I won't say "every claim in this book is true"; I'll write in the preface that errors no doubt remain and I consider that honest rather than evasive, though I don't think it's strictly required of me; and I'm perfectly willing to write, in the same preface, that I read and checked every chapter carefully, even though that sentence looks like it ranges over the whole set in just the way the "every claim is true" sentence does and I reject that one. Then the cases from the rest of my week. I sit on our research-integrity committee and on a lab safety review, where someone signs off item by item — four hundred items, each with something like a one-in-a-thousand chance of being missed — and I'm comfortable with each individual sign-off, and comfortable telling people to go ahead and work in the lab, but I think the summary line "this lab is safe" needs something more than the four hundred sign-offs, some systems-level check, before anyone signs it. My brother, who inspects bridges, says the paperwork is an exercise in make-believe and that he's only ever certifying item by item; I think he's wrong about the aggregate and right about the items, and I can't say why both. But then flip one variable: if the four hundred readings all come off a single instrument that might be miscalibrated, or if all twenty-eight chapters rest on one shared method that might be flawed, my confidence in each individual claim drops sharply even though nothing about any single item has changed, and I'd want to withhold the individual "yes" that I gave so easily ten minutes earlier.

The pair I genuinely cannot reconcile is this: I withhold "my ticket will lose" at a probability of about 0.9999987, and I happily sign off on an inspection item at 0.999, and the only difference I can name is that a false safety claim can kill someone while a false lottery claim costs nothing. If stakes are doing that work, I don't see why the stakes of a scholarly volume — readers do rely on the whole thing, and a bad book propagates errors — don't similarly poison my chapter-by-chapter yeses. There's another pair I'm suspicious of: our campus seismologist says "no significant quake today" each morning, about one-in-a-thousand per day, and I think every one of those assertions is proper, including the one that turned out false, and I wouldn't blame her for it after the fact; but if she collected all three hundred sixty-five predictions into an almanac published in January and asserted them all at once, I'd withhold, and since the probabilities are identical I suspect I'm tracking something other than probability — whether one audience will aggregate them, whether the evidence is fresh when the claim goes out, whether the speaker is answerable for the set — and I can't tell which. And one more: last month our committee voted to accept a sixty-page report as accurate while every one of us believed some sentence in it was wrong; voting yes felt fine, saying "every sentence is true" would not have, and that gap feels like the lottery gap but I don't think for the same reason, since here I think a standard like "accurate enough for its purpose" is legitimately in play. My own stab at a principle, which I'd like you to test and repair rather than admire: a member of a set of claims is assertable only if it clears some probability threshold and is what I've been calling "isolated," meaning that its credibility wouldn't be touched by learning that other members of the set are false, and the aggregate claim is assertable only if the speaker has checked the set as a set, specifically for shared causes of failure. That handles my chapters and my brother's items, but I can't spell out what isolation consists in, I don't think it explains the almanac, and I don't know whether it makes the lottery case come out right for the right reason or by fiat.

So: is there one principle that gets these, or nearly? Give it to me clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up — I'd rather sacrifice a verdict than keep a baroque principle, but I want to be told which one is cheapest to lose and why. Then run it on two cases I haven't settled. The first is the one I'm actually facing next week: I'm on a dissertation committee, I believe every substantive claim in the dissertation, I'm going to sign the report saying the defense passed and the work is sound, and I'll tell the candidate in person that there's certainly a wrong footnote or a misdated source somewhere in it — is that just the preface again, or does it change because what I'm certifying is a person and a body of work rather than a set of propositions? The second is one you construct yourself: give me a case where your principle yields a verdict you think I'll find uncomfortable, since that's the fastest way for me to see where it bites. Commit to a single account and defend it, with the alternatives mentioned only as they bear on your reasons; don't hand me a menu and leave the choice to me. Mark in the prose where your confidence in a clause drops off, and where you'd concede a verdict is a coin flip. And write it as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables — the way you'd write to a colleague in an email.
