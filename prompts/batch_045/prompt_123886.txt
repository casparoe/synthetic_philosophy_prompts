I convene a small reading group in philosophy of biology — mostly graduate students and two sceptical systematists — and two of its members have spent the better part of a year arguing past each other about the species problem. One is an unrepentant pluralist: she thinks there are several equally legitimate species concepts, that the choice among them is indexed to the theoretical question being asked, and that the hope for a single concept covering all of life was never more than a methodological prejudice inherited from vertebrate zoology. The other is a monist of the general-lineage stripe: he holds that there is exactly one defensible species concept — a species is a segment of a metapopulation lineage — and that interbreeding, monophyly, ecological distinctness and diagnosability are merely fallible operational criteria for detecting that one thing, not rival definitions of rival things. They have now agreed, rather grudgingly, to write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to produce a first draft for them to react to. Please write it.

I want the substance to be genuinely in the weeds rather than a restatement of the standard textbook list. A few things I would like handled explicitly. First, I have found it useful to distinguish two grades of pluralism, and I would like the statement to keep them apart: question-relative pluralism, on which the correct concept varies with the research question asked of the same organisms, and taxon-relative pluralism, on which different parts of the tree of life simply demand different treatments. Second, both sides should say where they stand on the concept-versus-criterion move — the monist's claim that one general concept plus many imperfect detection criteria absorbs everything the pluralist wants — with the pluralist's reply given in wording she would herself sign and not in a parody of it. Third, please address the status of the species *rank*: both should say whether species taxa can be perfectly real while the category of species is conventional in the way that "mountain" or "river" is conventional, and what follows either way.

For structure: begin with a numbered section of agreed claims — I would expect eight to twelve of them, each substantive enough that someone could in principle deny it, covering at least the taxa-versus-category distinction, the failure of every single operational criterion across all of life, what would be lost if species talk were abandoned, and what the disagreement is *not* about. Then give a table of residual disagreements, with columns for the crux, the pluralist's own formulation of her position on it, the monist's own formulation of his, a case or piece of evidence both accept as bearing on that crux, and each side's prediction about that case, written down in advance. At least one crux should concern asexual and microbially promiscuous organisms where gene transfer is not tied to reproduction; at least one should concern two diverging lineages that continue to exchange genes; and one should turn on a hypothetical finding that a continent-scale genomic survey reveals no gap anywhere in the distribution of pairwise divergence values, only a continuum. Finish with a short section saying which parts of their dispute you take to be merely verbal and why, and what each side would count as evidence against itself. No citations, references or reading lists, please — we have the literature to hand. Please write the whole thing in British English.
