So I run a monthly discussion thing at a brewery — maybe twelve regulars, mostly teachers and nurses, two software people, one guy who's read a lot of Sapolsky. No assigned reading, we just talk for about ninety minutes. Next month they voted on "does what you believe change what you see?" and I said sure, then realized I have no idea how to actually run that so it isn't just everyone trading anecdotes for an hour and a half.

Don't explain the cognitive penetration debate to me — I took a couple of philosophy classes years back so I know the jargon and the general shape of it. What I need is a plan for the session.

Specificly:

- What has to be in place before the question even sounds like a real question to them? My worry is half the room starts from "of course it does, everything is top-down" and the other half from "no, seeing is just your eyes", and neither of those is the actual disagreement. So what are the first twenty minutes?

- One case should carry the whole thing. I want it to be one where the thing really looks different, not one where you just infer differently or look at a different part of it, and ideally something I can demo live at a table with a phone screen. It also can't be an illusion half the room has already seen on Instagram. What should it be, and what does it need to have and need to NOT have? I was playing with something I'm calling the swap test: show the same image twice with two different stories about what it is, then reveal the stories were switched and ask whether the looks flipped. Is that the right centerpiece or is it doing the wrong work?

- The misconceptions in the order they'll come up, and where in the sequence to head each one off. I can predict two: someone will say "that's just confirmation bias" as if that closes the subject, and the software guys will definately say the brain is a prediction machine so of course everything is penetration and there's nothing left to argue about. There's a third one I can't quite put my finger on, something like "if belief can change what you see then you can never trust your own eyes" — is that one coming, and is it a misconception or is it basically right?

- One of the teachers is going to ask how I'd even tell, at a brewery table, whether something looks different to someone versus whether they just came to a different conclusion about it. I don't have a good answer at that scale and I think the whole session turns on it.

- One question at the end that tells me whether they got it rather than just had fun.

- And what do I openly refuse to get into? I'm happy to say out loud "that's above what we can settle here," I just don't know where the line goes.

Constraint: no name-dropping of philosophers or studies. Last time I said Fodor out loud the room emptied. If a distinction only has a jargon name, give me a way to say it in plain words.
