I write novels — mostly near-future medical stuff, the kind where somebody invents a syndrome so a plot can happen — and for the new one I've made up a condition I'm calling dissociative access syndrome. The patients (my neurologist calls them "conductors") pass every test you'd give for whether a visual state is globally available: they name the shape, hold it in mind, bet money on it, use it to plan a reach. But they flatly deny seeing anything, they never volunteer the stimulus, and here's the bit I invented and want stress-tested: they keep fine-grained sensorimotor uptake. Grip aperture tracks the object's size, pupils and microsaccades respond to it, they learn its statistics over days — but none of it ever gets to the point where they could cite it as a reason for anything they do or say. In the vocabulary I've been making up: sensorimotor access without editorial access.

Next week I'm interviewing a philosopher of mind for a little thing I do where I interrogate the people I've been robbing. She's a global workspace theorist of the strong kind: phenomenal consciousness just is global availability — what a state's being broadcast feels like from the inside — and Block's access/phenomenal distinction marks a real difference in our concepts but not a gap that anything further has to fill. Her go-to move when you press her on why broadcasting should feel like anything at all is to say: you're treating "feels like" as a property of the state instead of as the subject's relation to her own broadcast state. She is extremely good at that move and it gets her out of everything. It's occured to me that she can also always say my conductors are just broadcasting weakly or partially, so I need to be ready for that too.
