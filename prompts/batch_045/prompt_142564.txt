For some years now, since retiring from a chair in experimental biophysics, I have been auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of mind, and I find myself increasingly interested in how much of John von Neumann's thinking about the nervous system survives contact with what we can now actually build. I have read the short posthumous book prepared from his Silliman lectures and published by Yale in 1958, and I have read the standard secondary summaries. The latter are of little use to me: they converge on the claim that he in some sense anticipated artificial neural networks, which strikes me as both roughly true and entirely beside the point. So I should like to ask for something more specific than a general verdict.

Conceive the following encounter. He is shown three results, in a form he has reason to trust, and I ask you to grant all three for the sake of the question regardless of how you would qualify them yourself.

F1. A machine that is digital through and through, trained end to end by gradient descent on a single objective — predicting the next element of a sequence — now performs at or beyond trained-human level on a wide range of tasks it was never trained for, including the production of correct proofs and working programs. No one specified to it what a proof is; nothing like a rule system was installed.

F2. The arithmetic precision at which such a machine operates can be driven down to a handful of bits per number with very little loss of capability. The precision consideration he used in arguing that the nervous system must be partly analog has, as a matter of engineering fact, constrained nothing.

F3. We now possess complete wiring diagrams, at synaptic resolution, for the entire nervous systems of several small animals, and for the largest of them a digital simulation that reproduces a good deal of the animal's behaviour. From that simulation no one has yet extracted a compact description of what the animal is computing; the hypothetical vocabulary of the nervous system, if there is one, has not been read off. The wiring diagram is complete and the algorithm is still missing.

On that basis, I want his position on four questions, in each case with the argument that gets him there rather than only the conclusion.

1. Does F2 force him to withdraw the analog claim, and if so, does the thesis that the brain is a computing machine survive the withdrawal with any content left in it, or does it collapse into the claim that everything physical computes? I am interested specifically in whether he would regard that collapse as a refutation or as a clarification.

2. F3 is, I take it, close to the situation he predicted: an organization that resists description in the language of mathematics as we have it. Would he take it as confirmation, or as a reproach to his own demand for a generative theory — that is, would he accept simulation without comprehension as a theory of the nervous system, or would he say that the connectome stands to a theory of the brain as a table of transistor states stands to a program, a level of description too low to be interesting? And would his own automata-theoretic machinery lead him to expect a threshold of complexity below which no nervous system can be read at all?

3. Given that the brain's organization was arrived at by evolution and then partly by learning, rather than specified by a designer, what would he say about the relation between the thing built and the specification of the thing built — and, more pointedly, about whether the hard problem of consciousness is for him a genuine obstacle, a placeholder for a theory we do not yet have, or a malformed question? Here I should be particularly interested in whether you think the analogy with his treatment of the interpretation question in quantum mechanics is a fair one, or whether I am importing it illegitimately.

4. Finally, what would he tell a funding body in 2026 to do: build larger trained systems, push connectomics and biophysical simulation, or something neither of those? And which one of his actual arguments do you think the intervening seventy years have refuted outright?

Two further requests about the character of your answer. First, please separate clearly, claim by claim, (i) what is reasonably settled about what he in fact held, (ii) what is genuinely contested among people who work on him — the reading of his remarks on the language of the nervous system, whether he is best described as a computationalist about mind, whether his attitude to physical theory was instrumentalist — and (iii) what is your own extrapolation beyond anything he committed himself to. Do not present a contested reading as the consensus; if a claim is contested, say so and give the alternatives. Attach a rough confidence, as a number, to each of the four predictions, and say which of them you would abandon first and what would most move it. Second, where you attribute a specific claim to him, mark whether it is something one would expect to find stated in the 1958 text, something that follows from his work on automata, games, or quantum mechanics, or your own conjecture — and paraphrase rather than quote; I have the book to hand and would rather have the idea than a page reference. Please do not give me a bibliography or a reading list: what I am after is the reasoning, and I can find the literature myself.
