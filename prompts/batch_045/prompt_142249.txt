I teach a graduate seminar on explanation in the special sciences, and for some years I have been circling a phenomenon I cannot find a name for. I would be grateful for help in deciding whether it is one phenomenon, two, or none.

Set the scene as I do in the seminar. There are three grades of description under which one can predict a system. One predicts from its physical constitution and the laws. One predicts from what it was designed, built, or selected to do. One predicts by treating it as a creature with beliefs and desires that acts to get what it wants given what it believes. I take it as familiar that the third grade is the cheapest and the most committal on the fewest details, and that a pattern it licenses counts as real when it compresses the available data without residue; I do not need those points rehearsed, and I am not asking for a bibliography. What interests me is a specific way third-grade predictions fail, and the fact that the failure seems to have no analogue at the other two grades.

Three cases, all of which I have used in class.

First. Two people approach each other on a narrow pavement. Each predicts the other's step, each predicts that the other is predicting, and the mutual sidestep dithers until something breaks the symmetry: a glance, a lowered shoulder, a convention about which side to pass on. The third-grade prediction is unstable here in a way that no additional information about muscles, optic nerves, or upbringing would settle.

Second. A chess program is described as wanting to win and as believing that castling early is safe. Against an opponent that does not model it, the ascription predicts beautifully and improves as you learn more about the program. Against a near-copy of itself, the ascription comes apart, and moving to a second order — it believes that I believe that it believes — sometimes restores the prediction and sometimes makes it strictly worse.

Third. A desk at a trading firm ascribes to a rival's algorithm the goal of detecting and front-running large orders. On historical data against passive order flow the ascription is excellent. In the month after the rival begins modelling our own algorithm it is worthless, and its failure is not noise: it fails in a structured direction, and the bad predictions cluster.

What I want identified. In each case the breakdown is not traceable to ignorance of the target's constitution or its design, and supplying such information does not repair it; it is traceable to the target's standing in a relation of mutual modelling with something, and it persists even when the target has no idea that I exist. But the phenomenon has a second face I cannot place. Populations in which everyone uses third-grade descriptions of everyone else often settle into patterns that are extremely stable and are stable *because* everyone is using them: a queue, a bidding convention, a price, a norm of deference. I cannot tell whether that stability is the same phenomenon with the sign reversed or a different one that merely looks similar.

Things I have tried and set aside. Self-fulfilling prophecy: in my cases the pattern is more often destroyed than produced by being known about. The thesis that social-scientific regularities are destabilised when policy relies on them: that is a claim about populations and equilibria and about the world changing in response to a rule, whereas mine is about an ascription losing its grip on a single target. Underdetermination of interpretation by evidence: my cases are not ones where the evidence runs out, since the predictions are determinately good or determinately bad.

So my questions, in the order I would want them answered.

1. Is there an established concept for the distinctive fragility of third-grade prediction under mutual modelling, and if not, is the best move to coin one? Either way I want a definition with boundary cases, not a label.

2. Is the fragility I have described one thing or several? In particular: is the instability that appears in the pavement case and the self-play case the same as the instability that appears when the rival algorithm updates, or is one a matter of non-convergent regress and the other a matter of the target changing?

3. Does the marker really fall where I have put it? I have been assuming this is specific to the third grade, since the function of a heart does not depend on who is observing it. But the function of a system built to track another tracker does seem to depend on that, which suggests the real divider is not the grade of description but whether the content ascribed is about another model. If so my whole framing is wrong and I would like to be told so, with the corrected cut.

4. Does any of this bear on whether the pattern is real? The predictability of these systems evidently depends on the epistemic and social embedding of the predictor in a way that the lower grades do not, and I can see three possible readings — that the pattern is therefore less than real, that reality for patterns was always relative to a stance and this is just a vivid case, and that the dependence is itself a real feature of the system that the lower grades cannot express. I want to know which is closest, and what would count as evidence against each.

5. Finally, the second face. What kind of case would decide whether convention-sustained stability is the same phenomenon as the instability, and is there any formal result — a fixed-point theorem, a convergence result, an impossibility result — that settles whether the second-order regress in cases like the second one bottoms out? I would rather be told that no such result exists than be given a gesture at one.
