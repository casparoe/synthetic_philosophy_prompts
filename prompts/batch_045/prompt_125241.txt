I'm writing a short internal doc at work on scalable oversight, and before we commit to a protocol I want the specification rather than the survey. The situation we actually care about: we have models producing answers nobody on the team can check directly — a patch to a distributed-systems codebase, a summary of a literature none of us has read, a triage call on a messy case — and we want to (a) get a verdict on those tasks that we can trust and (b) feed whatever the protocol produces back in as training signal for the next round. Concretely, the family of protocols I have in mind is the debate-style one: two disputants argue for competing answers in front of a judge that is weaker than either of them, sometimes much weaker, and in our case is itself a model, and the judge picks a winner. So: what would such a protocol have to deliver to count as adequate for that purpose? I want the full requirements list — the cases it has to get right, the failure modes it has to be closed against, the distinctions it can't collapse (losing an argument versus being wrong; the judge being unpersuaded versus the judge being unconvinced for a good reason; the honest answer being defensible versus being cheaply defensible) — with the concrete case or consideration behind each requirement, and a mark on which are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of one camp.

My rough first pass is below, and I'd rather you cut, merge, and add to it than ratify it. (1) For any specific false answer a disputant could advance, the honest answer must be at least as easy to defend — where easy has to be cashed out in burden-of-proof terms rather than as in-principle-defensibility, because a vivid lie is usually cheaper to state than to refute, and the honest answer to a messy question is often a long qualified story. (2) The winner must not be determined by skill at exploiting this judge's particular quirks — verbosity, confident tone, flattery, last-mover advantage, agreement with the judge's own leanings — so verdicts should be stable under swapping in a different but equally weak judge, under changes of presentation order, and under which side gets assigned which answer. (3) The recursion has to bottom out: disagreements must decompose into sub-claims the judge can actually check, and when they don't the protocol should return something like calibrated uncertainty or an explicit list of what is and isn't settled, rather than a confident verdict. (4) The incentives have to survive disputants who are themselves uncertain which answer is true, who would both privately prefer the same outcome, and who can model the judge better than the judge models itself. (5) Whatever the protocol outputs has to be usable as a training target without the disputants getting better at persuasion faster than they get better at truth. (6) It has to cope with questions where there is no decidable fact of the matter — genuinely mixed literatures, interpretive or normative calls — without systematically rewarding whichever side is more polemical. (7) It must not presuppose what it is meant to produce, i.e. it shouldn't need already-truthful superhuman disputants in order to extract truth from them.

Two things I especially want. First, say which of these (or of your own additions) are genuine adequacy conditions and which are disguised preferences of one research camp: I suspect, for instance, that no trusted model anywhere in the loop is purism rather than a requirement, and that the honest argument should be checkable with only a small amount of extra computation may be a constraint imported from interactive-proof theory that a real deployment need not honor. Second, flag any pair you think cannot be jointly satisfied — my own worry is that a judge hardened enough to resist manipulation becomes a judge that cannot be informed by argument, since both run through the same channel, and that (1) and (6) come apart on exactly the tasks we care about. If the list as a whole turns out to be unsatisfiable, that is the most useful thing you could tell me. Close by naming the single requirement that does the most work in ruling out the protocols people actually propose, and then — only a few lines, since I'll do the full scorecard myself — how the plain two-disputant setup, a single-critic setup where one model audits an answer and flags problems, and any variant you'd want to add do against the list.
