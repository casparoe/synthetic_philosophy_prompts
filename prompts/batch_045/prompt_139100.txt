I'm giving a 20-minute version of this at our ethics reading group next month and I'd much rather find out now that the middle bit is broken than find out from the floor. Below is the skeleton of the paper rather than a draft, so sorry if it's terse. Two ground rules: I don't want reading suggestions (I know the literature exists, I want to know whether the argument works), and please don't spend words telling me to define my terms or flag the ambiguities in "exist". Assume the boring charity and go for the joints.

The question I'm meant to be answering is whether the simulation argument should dampen longtermist fanaticism — the view that a tiny probability of nudging an astronomically large future dominates every near-term consideration. The usual exchange is shallow: one side says "if we're simulated the future isn't real so nothing matters", the other says "the sim is real for its inhabitants, so carry on". I think both are looking in the wrong place, and that the interesting conclusion is neither "yes it dampens" nor "no it doesn't", but that it changes the *object* of the fanaticism in a way the fanatic should find embarrassing.

Step 1 — call it the runtime discount. Longtermist numbers inherit their scale from physics: the future is big because there's a lot of usable energy for a very long time, so the number of minds that could exist is astronomical. But conditional on us being in a simulation of a period like ours, the size of the future isn't fixed by physics at all; it's fixed by how long whoever is running the thing has a reason to keep it running. And the reasons for running a simulation of, say, the early twenty-first century look like they have a much thinner tail than stellar physics does: research questions, teaching, entertainment, curiosity about what we'd do. Most such projects have natural stopping points. So conditional on simulation, the expected remaining runtime is not "billions of years"; it's drawn from something with a short typical value and maybe a long right tail. That doesn't set the future's value to zero. It just plausibly knocks several orders of magnitude off the expected number of future minds, which is exactly the quantity the fanatic needs to be huge. Notice this is not skepticism about the reality of the sim, and it survives even if the inhabitants are morally on a par with us.

Step 2 — where I think I used to be wrong. My first instinct was that the same reasoning also inflates the value of the present, so the ratio between near-term and far-term stakes is untouched and fanaticism survives. If simulations are cheap, then this very moment may be instantiated many times over, and the bednets I buy are duplicated along with everything else, so whatever multiplied the future multiplied the alternative too. I now think this is right and that the duplication worry is genuinely a wash: any multiplicative factor that applies to the value of what we do to the future applies equally to the value of what we do now, and fanaticism was always a claim about a ratio, not an absolute. So the dampening has to come entirely from step 1. But I'm not fully confident, and the thing that nags me is whether the duplication is really *counterfactually* symmetric — whether the copies of the present differ from my own token action in a way that matters when I'm aggregating, or whether I should be doing something with self-location instead.

Step 3 — the part I actually care about. Once you grant that the operator's purposes set the size and shape of the future, the largest single term in the expected value of the future is not anything we control through policy. It's what the operator wants. A fanatic who is simulation-conditional should therefore reallocate: instead of pandemic preparedness or AI alignment, the dominant intervention is whatever makes continued running more likely, and we have no idea what that is. Worse, I think one candidate pops out. If runs get terminated for being unproductive, and productivity tracks something like interestingness or unpredictability, then the simulation-conditional expected-value calculation may favour a *dramatic* future over a safe and prosperous one — variance over welfare — which flatly contradicts the risk-reduction the longtermist wanted. I half think this washes out too: under a wide, symmetric prior over operator utility functions, the "be more interesting" hedge has no more expected value than "be more boring", and I'd need some specific asymmetry in the prior to get the recommendation off the ground. I can't see what the asymmetry would have to be, and I suspect that if I could, it would be doing all the work.

The punchline I want to end on: simulation-conditional longtermism starts to look like the thing it was a secular replacement for. The object of the fanatic's concern becomes the inscrutable intentions of a hidden superior power, the practical upshot is a ritual we perform for an audience we can't see, and the content of the ritual is guesswork about what that audience finds compelling. Which makes me think the real moral isn't about the simulation hypothesis at all: it's that any framework whose recommendations swing that wildly on an untestable hypothesis is telling us something about the framework.

What I'd like from you, in under 600 words total, keyed to the steps:

(a) Is the step 2 wash argument actually right, or is there an asymmetry in duplication I'm missing? Say precisely where it breaks if it breaks.
(b) I want the strongest version of the worry that step 3 generalises into something that eats itself: if untestable hypotheses with big stakes get to reallocate our efforts, then God, aliens, and a hundred other things do the same, and no view survives. Is there a principled line between operator risk and the cases I'd want to dismiss, or does my argument collapse into plain Pascal's-mugging scepticism about expected value? If it does, tell me which premise to drop.
(c) Is the "it becomes a religion" ending a real philosophical point or just rhetoric that will play well in a seminar room and mean nothing?

Be blunt, and definitly don't bother being encouraging. Just this once I'd rather have the objection than the bibliography.
