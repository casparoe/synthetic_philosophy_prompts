I am a third-year undergraduate, and I sit as one of two voting student members on my university's Senate Committee on Institutional Values. The committee exists to produce joint recommendations on questions where the disagreement is not about how much of a single good to supply but about which considerations are reasons at all: divestment and the endowment, speech and events policy, memorialisation and naming, partnerships with outside organisations, the terms on which religious and secular student groups share university space. We get perhaps a dozen of these a year. We meet fortnightly for ninety minutes. The other members are two faculty, the university chaplain, the chief investment officer, a member of HR, and the Senate clerk; none of them has studied philosophy. Anything we produce goes to the Senate and, if it touches money, onward to a Board subcommittee, so it has to survive review by people who were not in the room, and it has to state its reasons in plain language inside a page.

This term I am also taking a seminar on value pluralism and public reason, and I have been trying to turn what I am reading into something my committee could actually run. The framing I find most attractive is the gains-from-trade one: where parties weight issues differently and no party can simply win, a package can leave each side better off than the realistic no-deal outcome, on its own ranking, without anyone converting to anyone else's values. The literature puts obvious pressure on that framing, and I want the pressure built into the procedure rather than left outside it: the pluralist point that the losses here are not commensurable on any single scale; the integrity objection, that a person can be asked to do what they would rather not do but not to become a different kind of person; Margalit's category of rotten compromises, and his worry about agents compromising on behalf of collectives they do not own; the contrast between a modus vivendi and something closer to an overlapping consensus; and the dirty-hands problem, that some residue survives a justified agreement and does not wash off.

I have drafted six candidate tests, and I am fairly sure some of them are unworkable or duplicate each other. Please turn them into a procedure, cutting, merging, and rewriting as you see fit, and telling me plainly where a step is a rule of thumb rather than something the theory supports.

1. Gains over the feasible set: each party must prefer the package, on its own ranking, to the no-deal outcome and to every other package actually on the table. My worry: parties rank packages while hating particular clauses, so I do not know whether the test should apply at package level, clause level, or both; and the no-deal baseline is itself contested, since each side threatens a different one.
2. Non-affirmation: no party may be required to assert, sign, or enact a proposition it takes to be false. Silence, forbearance, and action may be required; assertion may not. My worry: silence can function as endorsement when a party has a standing duty to speak, and some parties will refuse forbearance too.
3. A residue ledger: for each clause, record who bears residual moral cost, and stop if one side pays in integrity while the other pays only in money, convenience, or reputation. My worry: everyone will inflate their residue, and cross-currency trades may be the only trades available.
4. The absent-party test: identify who bears the residue without holding a seat, and stop if the residue falls wholly on such parties. My worry: it is easy to weaponise, since every faction will claim to speak for someone not present.
5. Lock-in and sunset: prefer reversible forms to irreversible ones, and flag clauses that create precedent, path-dependence, or new institutional facts. My worry: some of the goods at stake, such as an apology or a named scholarship, are irreversible by their nature.
6. Dissent preservation: signatories may publicly record that they signed under protest without voiding their signature. My worry: this seems to collide with the point of a joint statement, since public dissent destroys much of what the other side was buying.

Two further problems I want handled explicitly. The first is agenda-setting: committee preferences can cycle across packages in the way majority preferences cycle among options, so the feasible set is not well defined, and whoever sets the order of votes partly picks the winner. The second is what I think of as no-cost waiting: if one party can expect to get everything simply by refusing and outlasting the others, no gains-from-trade argument binds it, and I am not sure whether a procedure should say anything about that or simply report it.

What I want back, in three parts. First, the procedure itself: gates in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each gate, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the question to a named person or to a vote because it is a question of values rather than facts. It has to be runnable in about twenty minutes at the end of a meeting, out loud, by the clerk, with the committee answering; it has to produce a short written record the Senate can audit; and it should say how it resists strategic behaviour, especially inflated claims of residue. Second, three worked applications, with the ledger actually shown:

a. Endowment. A divestment campaign wants withdrawal and condemnation; the chief investment officer says the committee has a fiduciary duty and no view on moral questions. On the table: withdrawal from a narrow, defined slice of holdings, a published transparency report, funded scholarships for students from regions affected by extraction, and no condemnation language anywhere in the document.
b. Naming. The only deal on offer is that the university changes the name on a building in exchange for the campaigners withdrawing their public allegation that the university knowingly profited, and joining a statement recording that the university acted in good faith throughout. I want the name changed and I think this is the best available outcome; my suspicion is that the procedure will tell me no.
c. Speech and events. Secular-liberal members want an unqualified expression policy; religious and some identity-based groups want a dignity carve-out and a funded support programme for groups that get targeted. On the table: content-neutral time, place, and manner rules, a commitment that no invited speaker will be disinvited, and money for the support programme. My guess is that this one is genuinely unsettled by any procedure, and I would rather you marked exactly where judgment takes over than forced an answer.

Third, and briefly, what the procedure cannot do: cases it should not be applied to at all, questions it will silently beg, and the point at which running it becomes a way of laundering a decision someone else has already made.
