I spent forty years in experimental physics, much of it on error budgets for satellite remote sensing, and since retiring I have been auditing a graduate seminar in philosophy of the social sciences. I also sit on a research-council panel that awards a small number of grants by majority vote among reviewers who have all read the same dossier: the same application, the same three referee reports, the same budget annex. So I read the following passage with an eye to whether it should change how we run ourselves.

It comes from a recent popular book on collective decision-making that our reading group has been working through:

"Condorcet's jury theorem is the mathematical heart of the wisdom of crowds. Suppose a group must decide a question that has one right answer — say, whether a defendant is guilty. Assume that each member is more likely than not to be right, and that they vote independently, which in practice just means that nobody sees anyone else's vote before casting their own. Then the majority is more reliable than any single member, and every enlargement of the group raises the chance that the majority is right, which approaches certainty as the group grows without limit. That is why numbers defeat expertise: a large enough crowd of even marginally competent people will outperform the finest expert you could appoint, and adding members is therefore generally better than improving them. Condorcet also discovered the gloomier twin of this result — his famous paradox, in which majority voting over three or more options cycles endlessly and yields no winner at all — so the same analysis that vindicates majority rule also shows that it can break down completely."

Several things in that strike me as possibly off, but I cannot locate the faults precisely, and I would be grateful for a sentence-by-sentence audit: what someone who actually works on this material would accept, what is a tolerable popular simplification, what is a real misrepresentation, and whether the practical moral the passage draws survives the correction.

Three particular worries. First, my instinct from measurement is that the independence clause is doing all the work: averaging ten readings off the same badly calibrated instrument does not shrink your uncertainty, and a secret ballot does not seem to me to deliver what the theorem needs if the voters are conditioning on a shared evidence base. I have tried to make this precise: let the shared dossier be misleading with probability ε, and conditional on its being misleading let each panellist be wrong with probability q > ½, while conditional on its being sound each is right with probability p > ½. It looks to me as though the majority's error probability then tends to ε rather than to zero, so that ε is a hard ceiling on what enlarging the panel can buy. Is that argument sound as stated, and is the ceiling real? If so, would splitting the panel into sub-panels that read different subsets of the dossier help, given that each sub-panel then sees less evidence and its members are presumably less reliable?

Second, I am sceptical of "numbers defeat expertise". As I understand it the theorem compares the majority with a single member, and I do not see that it settles anything about enlargement versus professionalisation, especially when competence is unevenly distributed. Does it?

Third, I suspect the passage conflates two separate Condorcet results, and I also wonder whether the theorem applies to us at all. Our panel does not decide a binary question with a determinate truth; it selects which projects to fund, and it deliberates before voting, which plausibly raises each of us above chance while correlating our errors. Does the absence of a pre-existing fact of the matter disqualify the theorem outright, or does it still have force where the judgement has a forecasting element? And is the net effect of deliberation knowable in principle, or does it reduce to empirical questions about our particular panel?

Please mark your confidence as you go: distinguish what follows from the mathematics, what depends on a contested modelling choice or a contested reading of what the theorem is for, and what is merely plausible. And if my framing is muddled — if, for instance, the ceiling argument above is confused, or if the question "should we enlarge or professionalise this panel?" is not one the theorem can adjudicate — please say so plainly rather than answering politely around it; I would rather be corrected than accommodated.
