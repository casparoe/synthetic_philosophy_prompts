I'm writing a piece for a philosophy-literate film blog on Arrival, and I want it read through the future-contingents debate — Aristotle's sea battle and what has come since — rather than through the usual free-will-versus-determinism frame, which I think asks this film the wrong question.

Three things, handled precisely. First, separate the fatalisms: logical fatalism (if it is true now that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, the battle cannot fail to occur), foreknowledge fatalism, and causal determinism. Which, if any, is Louise's predicament actually an instance of, and does the Ockhamist reply — that a past truth about what was going to happen is a soft fact, dependent on the future rather than a hard fact already settled — dispose of it? Second: in branching-time terms, where many futures are possible and one is actual, Louise's future-memories seem to need a designated actual branch, the so-called thin red line, in order to be knowledge at all rather than apparent memories of what never happens; but once that branch is in place, her deliberation looks like a charade. Call this the answer-key problem: if a future version of yourself hands you the answer key, is that relevantly different from anyone else handing it to you? Argue one side. Third: does the film's physics — the aliens' atemporal experience, and the variational-principle framing Chiang gives the story in "Story of Your Life" — do any real argumentative work, or is it atmosphere that trades on a confusion?

Keep it honest in both directions: say where the film resists the reading, above all in the closing sequence, where Louise consents to the life she has already witnessed, and in her partner's reaction, which looks like a moral complaint about foreknowledge that has nothing to do with modality. Close by saying what a film can show here that the dialectic alone does not. Lead with your verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating the question.
