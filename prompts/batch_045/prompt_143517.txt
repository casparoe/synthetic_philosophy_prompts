=== THE QUESTION (20 points) ===

T1 and T2 are two cosmological theories. On the astronomical evidence alone they are exactly as likely as each other: priors 1:1. T1 says the world will contain 10^24 observers in total. T2 says it will contain 10^12.

(a) [5 pts] State the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA), and say in one sentence how they differ from each other. For SSA, say what reference class you're using.

(b) [5 pts] Apply both assumptions to this pair of theories — this is the presumptuous philosopher case. What posterior should you end up with, and in what ratio? Show the numbers.

(c) [7 pts] Now the Late Bloom variant. Same two theories, same priors, but you also know that you yourself are an early observer: you live before the year 2100. And the theories disagree about how their observers are spread out in time. T1 puts 10^12 observers before 2100 and the rest (10^24 − 10^12) after. T2 puts all 10^12 of its observers before 2100, because on T2 the world dies out shortly afterward. Apply both assumptions again to this version. Show the numbers again.

(d) [3 pts] The standard complaint about SIA is that it's presumptuous: it lets an armchair argument about how many observers there are overrule astronomy on what is, in part (b), a trillion-to-one bet. Does Late Bloom make that complaint stronger or weaker, and why? Two or three sentences.

=== STUDENT ANSWER (#22) ===

(a) SSA says I should reason as if I were a random sample drawn from the set of observers in my reference class. I'll take the reference class to be all observers capable of having thoughts like this one, whenever they happen to live. SIA says I should treat my own existence as evidence: the more observers a hypothesis contains, the more likely it is that there's a me, so P(H | I exist) ∝ P(H) × N(H), where N(H) is how many observers H says there are. The difference between them is that SSA takes it as given that I exist and only asks which observer I turned out to be, while SIA treats the bare fact that I exist at all as a reason to expect lots of observers.

(b) Under SSA nothing moves. Both theories say the world contains observers, and my being one of them isn't evidence for either one, so the posterior just sits where the priors were: even money, 1:1. Under SIA, T1 offers 10^24 chances for there to be a me and T2 offers only 10^12, so the odds on T1 are 10^24 / 10^12 = 10^12 to 1. That's the presumptuous philosopher: apparently we don't need to build the better telescope, because we already know the answer from the fact that we're here.

(c) Under SIA the story doesn't change. What does the work is the total number of observers, and T1 has 10^24 of them against T2's 10^12, so SIA favors T1 by 10^12 to 1 here as well. Finding out that I'm early doesn't touch that, since under SIA the weighting runs over the total population, and being early is just a fact about which observer I happen to be.

Under SSA I sample from the observers in my reference class. If T1 is true there are 10^24 of them and only 10^12 are early, so the chance that a random draw lands me before 2100 is 10^12 / 10^24 = 10^-12. If T2 is true, all 10^12 of its observers are early, so that chance is 1. So SSA favors T2 by 10^12 to 1. This is really just the doomsday argument in another form — being early is bad news about how many observers there will ever be. So the two assumptions point in opposite directions here, each by a factor of a trillion.

(d) I think it makes the objection worse. SIA is presumptuous in (b), and (c) shows it stays presumptuous no matter what ordinary evidence you feed it: even once you learn you're one of the early observers, SIA goes right on betting a trillion to one on the big world. SSA never does this. Because SSA treats your existence as a given, it leaves the priors where they were and lets the astronomers decide. That's the whole appeal of SSA, and it's why the presumptuous philosopher is an objection to SIA specifically.
