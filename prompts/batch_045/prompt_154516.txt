I'm in a graduate seminar on the historiography of the French Revolution, working through the three families of explanation we have been given — the social or class-struggle reading, the ideological and political-culture reading, and the contingency-and-war-emergency reading. Last week a visiting speaker used a line to close down an argument, and I want to know what, if anything, that line was worth: *revolutions devour their own children*. It circulates in that form, usually with the name of some guillotined revolutionary attached, though nobody in the room could say whether he ever actually said it.

The work it was made to do was this. A classmate argued that the Terror's turning on former revolutionaries — the Girondins, then the Hébertists, then Danton and Desmoulins — is hard to explain as a response to military emergency or as the ascendancy of a class interest, since the victims were the people who had made the revolution and who shared its enemies. Her conclusion was that the Terror is best explained by the internal logic of revolutionary ideology, which generates an unlimited demand for purity and so consumes whoever stands nearest. The visitor's reply was that no special ideological story is needed: this is simply what revolutions do.

I want the line taken apart rather than either accepted or dismissed. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims it could be making, and give a verdict on each: platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive but probably false, or confused. The distinctions I cannot sort out myself include at least these:

- a frequency claim (a large share of revolutions purge prominent early participants) versus a mechanism claim (the coalition that overturns a regime must be broad and heterogeneous, and any successor state must shed the ends of that coalition) versus a lawlike necessity claim;
- a functional variant of the mechanism claim: the people who brought a regime down have the specific skills, networks and militias for bringing a regime down, which makes them the principal threat to whatever replaces it — so the devouring is state consolidation rather than revolutionary logic;
- who counts as the children: the founding generation, the revolution's most radical products, the new kind of person the revolution created, or the revolution's own ideals and promises;
- whether a devourer is required — the revolution, a leadership faction, an impersonal process of competitive signalling in which revolutionary virtue is unverifiable and denunciation is cheap — or whether the outcome is emergent with no agent;
- whether the target cases are founders killed by a later generation, peer factions eliminating one another, or both, and whether devouring extends to imprisonment, exile and mere political obliteration, since that choice moves the numbers;
- the denominator problem: what the sample of revolutions is, and whether the cases that get remembered, taught and cited are selected for having been bloody in exactly this way.

Then the part I actually care about. For each reading, say whether it would carry the visitor's inference — from the revolution killed its own to no ideological explanation is needed — or whether it instead supports my classmate, or neither, because the same mechanism is equally available to the emergency and the class readings and so cannot discriminate between them.

Please commit. Tell me which reading you think is the most defensible one, which reading people who reach for this line are normally running, and whether the line, on its best reading, does any real work in the argument it was used to settle. Give your reasons, and mark explicitly where you are extrapolating past what the evidence supports rather than distributing your confidence evenly across the options.
