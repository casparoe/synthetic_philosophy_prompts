I host a podcast on the conceptual foundations of machine learning, and I'm scripting an episode in advance from a draft manuscript that my guest, Dr. Ines Kovač, circulated ahead of the recording. She's a philosopher of science who now works in mechanistic interpretability. Treat the summary below as the complete and authoritative statement of her position: don't supplement it from anything you associate with interpretability as actually practiced, and don't attribute any of it to real researchers.

Her view — she calls it mosaic realism — is a realism about neural representations built to survive the fact that our methods disagree with one another. She grants outright that the individual feature directions we recover are not among a network's real representational contents: not the columns of a sparse autoencoder's dictionary, not the directions found by a linear probe, not the components from PCA. Change the method, or the width of the dictionary, or the sparsity budget, and you get a different and mutually incompatible list of features. What is real, she argues, is what she calls the mosaic: the coarse pattern of similarity, clustering and composition among features — which features lie near which, which subdivide which, which combine to yield which. Her official criterion is that two dictionaries describe the same mosaic just in case they are α-equivalent, where α-equivalence holds when every feature of each dictionary can be approximated, on-distribution, by a nonnegative combination of some small number of features of the other. She leans hard on an analogy with temperature: thermometers built on different physical principles and calibrated differently disagree about numbers while tracking one quantity, and no one concludes that thermometers invent temperature. And she has a single stock reply to skeptical questions — "That's a measurement problem, not a metaphysics problem" — together with a standing distinction between what the network contains (the mosaic, which we discover) and what we use (dictionary-relative features, which are partly our own construction).

What I want from you is nine or ten questions I can actually put to her, none of which invite her simply to restate the view, each paired with a follow-up written against the answer you expect her to give. For each, say in a line what that expected answer is, then give the follow-up that presses on it. No questions about how she got into the field, about advice for junior researchers, or about the future of AI.

The pressures I want covered:

1. The deflection. Three questions, in order, that make "that's a measurement problem, not a metaphysics problem" progressively harder to reach for. My own sense of where the leverage is: in the temperature case there is an independent theory of the quantity saying what it does apart from thermometers; independent instruments converge numerically to high precision; and temperature has causal powers of its own. So ask what the corresponding independent theory of features is supposed to be, what a convergence statistic would have to beat for her to count convergence as evidence (a null baseline — dictionaries fit to two different models, or to the same model's activations with the feature structure destroyed), and what becomes of the analogy if a feature direction can be moved around — say by editing later-layer weights — while the network's behaviour is unchanged.

2. α-equivalence itself. The relation is defined on-distribution, so the mosaic looks relative to a data distribution; "some small number" is a free parameter, which reintroduces the granularity problem it was meant to dissolve; and it isn't obvious the relation is transitive, in which case there may be no single equivalence class for the mosaic to be. Press her on whether she can accept ontic vagueness about where one mosaic ends and another begins, and on what she'd say if transitivity failed.

3. Granularity and feature splitting. Wider dictionaries split what narrower ones treat as a single feature. I expect her to answer that the splitting hierarchy is itself part of the mosaic. If so: what fixes the grain, and is that hierarchy a structure in the network or a structure in the training data that any well-fit model would inherit?

4. Basis freedom. Ask how much of a network is covered by the point that you can apply an invertible linear map to the residual stream and undo it in the next layer's weights without changing behaviour, and what, if anything, picks out a privileged basis. I expect her to say sparsity does. Then: is the sparsest decomposition unique, or identified only up to permutation and scaling? Is sparsity a fact about the network, or a prior we bring because sparse things are the ones we can read? And if the directions can be rotated while behaviour stays fixed, what is the measurement a measurement of?

5. Unused and untrained structure. Activation space at initialization is not structureless, and probes can find directions carrying information the network never uses. I expect her to distinguish structure merely present from structure the network exploits. Then: how would we test for exploitation rather than mere presence, given that intervening on a direction the network never uses can still change its behaviour?

6. Content and misrepresentation. If representations are real, they can misrepresent, so something must fix what a feature is about: its upstream causes, or its downstream effects. I expect her to want both, or to lean downstream. Either way, press her with a model that systematically asserts falsehoods, where the two come apart: is she committed to a feature's content shifting when later layers are edited and the feature direction is left alone, or to a network's representing things it is disposed to deny?

7. What would settle it. Ask what finding would make her abandon realism rather than retreat to a coarser mosaic — my candidate is a result showing that the mosaic is fully predictable from statistics of the training data alone, with no reference to the network — and then ask, separately, what observable consequences the dispute has at all, given that she and an instrumentalist who treats features as useful fictions seem to agree on every intervention-level prediction. If the disagreement makes no difference to what we should expect from steering, to whether features transfer across models, or to whether a model's stated reasons can be trusted, ask her what is at stake in the book.

Finally: for each question, tell me how confident you are that the answer you've anticipated is the one she would actually give, and single out the question or follow-up you are least confident about. Where it matters, flag any technical assumption in my setup that you suspect I've got wrong.
