I'm part of a small working group that meets on Tuesday evenings alongside a graduate seminar on decision theory, and we keep circling the same disagreement. The background assumption we share is that deliberation should often not aim at the good in its own terms: a procedure that represents the good directly and then optimizes for it tends to distort the motives and relationships it depends on, and it hands the agent an invitation to stop changing the world and start editing its own target instead. So every candidate we take seriously is indirect — a procedure that aims at something else and hopes to arrive at right action anyway. What we cannot agree on is which form of indirection is least likely to fail, and in which direction it will fail.

I have written our candidates down as six procedures: (i) proxy maximization, where the agent optimizes a fixed, hand-coded proxy and never revisits what the proxy stands for; (ii) ideal-advisor emulation, where the agent predicts what a far better-informed, less frightened, more self-aware version of the relevant deliberator would recommend in this situation and does that, while deliberately never forming a view about what makes the recommendation correct; (iii) reflective-equilibrium bookkeeping, where the agent maintains a set of principles together with case judgments, revises either side for coherence, treats recalcitrant judgments and disagreement with peers as evidence, and acts on whatever set is currently in equilibrium; (iv) exemplar imitation, where the agent asks what a specified kind of person would characteristically do and does that, with no outcome evaluation anywhere in the loop; (v) reasonable-rejection filtering, where from the feasible options the agent acts only on a rule that no one could reasonably reject, with reasonable fixed procedurally — each complainant gets a veto subject to a cost rule — rather than by aggregating anyone's good; and (vi) a parliament with a settled aggregation rule, where several sub-deliberators each pursue a distinct partial normative view and are combined by a rule fixed in advance, the agent itself never adjudicating which view is correct. Two members of the group think the ordering is obvious and they disagree with each other about what it is.

What I would like from you is this: tier these six by how promising they are as ways of getting right action without ever representing the right-making features, and give each a score out of 100 together with a single sentence on why it lands where it does. I would also like two complications folded in. The first is what I have been calling endorsement drift: because the target is defined by what some idealized deliberator would endorse, and that deliberator's dispositions are themselves shaped by the procedure's own earlier outputs, repeated application can ratchet toward a fixed point that nobody would have chosen from outside, even though every individual step was locally endorsed. So please give the ranking twice — once for an agent that makes a single decision and stops, and once for an agent that acts over a long stretch and is permitted to revise its own procedure — and say explicitly which procedures move and why. The second is the opposite failure, which I have been calling option-blindness: a procedure that keeps its hands entirely clean of substantive value may have no way to register that every available option is bad, or to authorize a genuinely new one, and I want to know which of the six pays for its safety in that coin and how much it costs them. Finally, tell me which adjacent pair in your long-run ranking you would be least willing to bet on, and what single further observation about the agent would settle it.

Please answer as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, tables, or bold text — name each procedure in running text with its score given inline, the way you would explain all of this in an email to a colleague who has not seen my list.
