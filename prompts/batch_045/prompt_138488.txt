I am writing a chapter on the epistemology of historicity — at what point a tradition stops being legend — and I need to know what my supervisor will say about a case of my own before I build a section around it. She works with a three-place scale (history; not history but with a recoverable historical kernel; legend with nothing behind it), and her rulings so far are these:

- The Trojan War as the epics tell it — gods on the field, the wooden horse, the ten years: kernel only. A real city, destroyed in the right period, lies at the mound now called Hisarlik; the war as narrated is not history.
- The battle fought at Kadesh in Syria in the thirteenth century BCE: history — even though our accounts are the pharaoh's own temple inscriptions, boastful, numerically impossible and full of divine favour, and the enemy's side of it survives mainly in a peace treaty concluded some years later.
- The Exodus: legend, nothing behind it — no contemporary Egyptian record of the flight, no trace of a large population in the Sinai over decades. She adds that the late-thirteenth-century Egyptian victory stela naming Israel as a people in Canaan establishes the people, not the story.
- Arthur: legend — first named some three centuries after the fighting, in a compilation of around 828, while a British writer closer to those events describes the same war against the Saxons, names Ambrosius Aurelianus, and never mentions Arthur. Ambrosius she counts as historical.
- The Norse voyages to Vinland: history — the sagas were written down two centuries or so after the voyages, but a Norse site has been excavated at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland and dated to around the year 1000, with the kind of buildings the sagas describe.

My case: a steppe tradition, first written down in a monastic chronicle roughly six centuries after the events, about the migration of a people and the burning of a town I will call Arshat. The chronicle says the migration was commanded by a sky-fire that stood still over Arshat for three nights, and that is the reason the tradition itself gives for why the people left. At the site the chronicle's own itinerary identifies as Arshat there is a destruction layer radiocarbon-dated to within a generation of the date its king-list implies; above it lies a horizon of foreign-style pottery spread over several hundred miles; ancient DNA shows population turnover at about that date. No contemporary inscription anywhere names either the town or the migrants, and the king-list survives only inside the same late chronicle.

What will she say about the Arshat migration? More useful to me than the verdict: what rule is she running, which other rules fit her five rulings equally well but come apart here, and which further case — real or constructed — would best tell them apart? I would also like to know whether her middle verdict is doing genuine work, or is only a polite way of declining to answer.
