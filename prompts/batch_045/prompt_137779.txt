I chair the clinical guidelines committee at my hospital, a role I took up in January and was not prepared for. In March a large pragmatic trial reported against a practice our unit has recommended for twenty years. Nine months on we have still not changed the guidance, and not because anyone has found a flaw in the trial. A majority of the committee keeps reasoning, more or less out loud: what the trial appears to show cannot be true, since we know the practice works, so the trial must be misleading somewhere. I have come to believe this is not only stubbornness or vested interest but a structural feature of how a well-supported belief meets contrary evidence, and I want the committee to see the structure. What I need is a plan for a single forty-five-minute session with them in the new year: fourteen people, mostly senior clinicians plus two lay members, one whiteboard, no slides, and no usable way of saying the word dogmatism without the room closing.

The argument as I currently have it is this. If I am justified in believing H, and the new evidence E is incompatible with H, then my justification for H is already a reason to think E is misleading. If E is misleading, it is not a reason to doubt H. So a principle that sounds like plain rational stability, the principle that you should not abandon a well-supported belief on the strength of evidence you have reason to distrust, appears to license never revising anything at all. My own guess is that the damage is done by the slide from H entails that E is misleading to H explains why E came out looking the way it does, and that the legitimate cases are the ones where the explanation of the apparent counter-evidence is a further hypothesis one could go and test on its own. But I cannot get that distinction to land when I try to say it in the room.

So I would like the plan rather than the explanation. What has to be established before the argument can even be stated, and in what order, and what depends on what in a way I cannot reorder. Should I run it with full belief and outright rejection first, or go straight to degrees of confidence: these people live in confidence intervals and p-values, and the binary version may strike them as a philosopher's toy. Which single example should carry the whole thing, and what must it have and what must it lack: I tried a medical example and they simply re-litigated the medical example, so it cannot be one about which they hold a view, and I have been considering a historical case in which an apparent anomaly was explained by an auxiliary hypothesis that was then independently confirmed by pointing an instrument at a predicted location, though I suspect that example either does all the work or smuggles in precisely the thing at issue.

Then the misconceptions, and the point in the sequence at which to head off each. I expect at least four. Some will conclude that no belief is ever rationally revisable and some will conclude the opposite, that one is entitled to discount whatever one likes, and I would rather neither of those be the takeaway. Several will say that this is just confirmation bias with the serial numbers filed off, and I need to know where that objection is actually right and where it misses the target, since I would rather concede the overlap early than have it raised as a debunking at the end. One member reads a little philosophy and will have heard dogmatism used for a thesis about whether perceptual experience can justify belief without independent reason to trust the senses, so he will think the word means something other than what I mean and I need to know whether to disarm that at the start or let it surface. And at least one will say that a Bayesian update settles the whole matter, which I suspect is both true and inert: true because conditioning does rank the cases correctly, inert because it does not by itself say why the discounting move feels compelling.

The part I expect to be hardest is the twenty years of experience objection. It is fair, and I do not want to defeat it, because a well-supported belief really is entitled to a higher bar than a hunch; the disease seems to be the bar rising without limit rather than rising at all. Where in the sequence does that point belong, and what has to be in place for it to be heard as a concession rather than as a capitulation?

Finally: what should I openly refuse to explain in forty-five minutes and say that I am refusing, and what question can I put to them in the last five minutes whose answers would show me they have hold of the distinction rather than of a slogan?
