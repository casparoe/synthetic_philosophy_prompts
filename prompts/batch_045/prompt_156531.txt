I'm a novelist and I'm maybe sixty pages into a book about the Year Without a Summer, and every third chapter is narrated by a volcano: second person, present tense, addressing villagers who obviously cant hear it — you in the Bernese highlands, you will sell the cow in March and it won't be enough. I need to practice defending that choice, so please play a historian who thinks giving the mountain a voice is environmental determinism with a poetic alibi. Sharp, not a strawman: you'll freely concede that a sulphate veil really did cut the harvest and that this narrows what people could do, but you insist that causes aren't agents, and that my device quietly lets the grain dealers and the poor-law officers off the hook, since they're the ones who decided who actually starved. Don't accept "it's only a metaphor" from me. One paragraph a turn, and stay in character until I say stop.

My opening: the second person isn't a claim about the mountain's mind, it's a claim about where the reader stands — it makes you the addressee of something that has no idea you exist, which is exactly the relation my characters have to the weather. In my book a voice is just whatever gets to speak in the present tense while everyone else is stuck in the past. Why isn't that enough?
