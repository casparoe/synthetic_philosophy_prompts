Our reading group just worked through the death material in the Zhuangzi — him found drumming on a basin after his wife dies, the roadside conversation with the skull, the butterfly dream, the transformation-of-things stuff — and we kept falling back on the same picture: dying is just a change of state in one continuous stuff. Your qi gets lent out to hold a human shape for a while, then it gets handed back and reshaped into something else, like ice going to water or a caterpillar becoming a butterfly. Nothing is destroyed, so there is nothing to be upset about. Two people then pushed it further: one said grief at someone's death is basically a category mistake, the other said this gives us a real argument that death isn't bad for the person who dies, not just a mood worth cultivating.

Can you audit the analogy? I want the correspondence table — what plays the role of the stuff, of the shape, of the thing that persists, and what is supposed to map onto my death as opposed to death in general — then where it breaks, and which breaks matter for the job it was doing. The thing that's bugging me is this, which i've started calling the wrong-beneficiary problem: the continuity the analogy secures is continuity of the stuff, and the stuff isn't a subject. It can't lose anything, be comforted, or be the thing i am when i worry about dying, and it was already around before i existed and will be around after. So does the analogy only deliver comfort to whatever is left over — in which case it can maybe carry equanimity but not bad-for-me and definitely not grief-is-a-mistake? Or is there a version where the continuity is the right kind? Is there a better analogy that does the work — say one where what persists is a role or an office rather than a lump of material — and does that one smuggle in something the original was avoiding? Be honest if the disanalogies are mostly nitpicks and the conclusion survives anyway.
