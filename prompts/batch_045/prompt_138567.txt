I want a Kuhnian case analysis of the reception of the protein-only hypothesis for the transmissible spongiform encephalopathies — scrapie, kuru, Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, BSE — running from the mid-1960s to the late 1990s. I know the outline of Kuhn and the outline of the science; what I want is the disciplined mapping in both directions: what Kuhn's machinery says the participants were doing, which features of the episode it gets right, and what the episode does to the machinery. Please do not give me a summary of Kuhn, and do not supply references or a bibliography.

Two ground rules. First, treat the chronology below as stipulated rather than retrieved; if some date is off by a year, nothing in your analysis should depend on it.

- Scrapie, a fatal neurodegenerative disease of sheep, and kuru, a fatal neurodegenerative disease endemic among the Fore in Papua New Guinea (spread through funerary consumption of relatives, disproportionately affecting women and children, who ate the brain), were shown to be transmissible by inoculation of brain tissue into chimpanzees in the mid-1960s. Because they were transmissible and because incubation ran to years — in kuru, sometimes more than fifty — the agent was filed under the heading slow virus. Carleton Gajdusek shared the 1976 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the transmissibility work; by 1977 he was writing of unconventional viruses as a distinct class.
- In the same period the radiobiologist Tikvah Alper found that the scrapie agent was not inactivated by ionizing radiation or by ultraviolet light at doses and wavelengths that destroy nucleic acids, and inferred a target far smaller than any viral genome. The results were received with astonishment, because replication without nucleic acid appeared to contravene the central dogma. Alper herself did not accept a protein-only answer until the end of her life.
- In 1982 Stanley Prusiner published Novel proteinaceous infectious particles cause scrapie, coined the term prion, and defended the hypothesis that the agent is a misfolded form of a normal host protein that induces its own conformation in other molecules. The proposal was widely regarded as heretical. The 1997 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Prusiner for his discovery of Prions - a new biological principle of infection.
- Britain then suffered an epizootic of BSE in cattle, attributed to meat-and-bone meal in feed: more than 184,000 cattle were diagnosed between 1986 and 2015, with the peak of new cases in 1993, and some four million cattle were slaughtered in the eradication programme. In 1996 the National CJD Surveillance Unit in Edinburgh identified a new variant of CJD, affecting unusually young patients, and linked it to BSE. Total recorded vCJD cases worldwide remain in the low hundreds.

Second, do not use incommensurability as one undifferentiated notion. Separate three grades and tell me which obtain, where, and between which parties:

1. Referential — shared terms pick out different things across the divide (or fail to pick anything out in the other side's ontology).
2. Taxonomic — the similarity and difference structure of the domain differs, so that cases get sorted into kinds differently and there is no kind-by-kind mapping: which disease-tokens are instances of one disease, whether sporadic, familial and infectious forms are variants of a single kind or distinct kinds, and so on.
3. Normative or evidential — there is no shared standard for what would settle the matter: what counts as an acceptable mechanism, how much indirect evidence is required before a well-confirmed generalization may be given up, whether failure to detect nucleic acid is evidence of its absence.

Please work through the following in order.

1. Fix the unit of analysis. Kuhn's story is told of a community, and here the size of the community decides the verdict. What is the smallest community for which this episode counts as a revolution, and the largest for which it does not? Does the answer change if the unit is molecular biology as a whole, the slow-virus subfield, or the veterinary and public-health apparatus?

2. State the paradigm as a short list of commitments actually at stake — not biology-in-general, but the specific propositions that made the protein-only hypothesis unthinkable, and distinguish the hard commitments from the merely habitual ones.

3. Decide whether the scrapie and kuru results were, in Kuhn's sense, puzzles being solved within a paradigm or anomalies accumulating against it. In particular: was the introduction of the categories slow virus and unconventional virus a paradigm-preserving accommodation — normal science manufacturing a sub-kind to absorb the recalcitrant case — or the first move of a rival paradigm? Then ask when crisis arrived, and be honest if the honest answer is that the anomaly alone never produced one and that what produced a crisis was the cattle epizootic and the human cases: that is, an event outside the research programme rather than a theoretical failure inside it. If so, say what that does to Kuhn's crisis mechanism.

4. Apply the three grades, naming the specific terms and specific contrasts. Was the dispute over the reference of virus, replication, or infectious agent; over the taxonomy of diseases and strains; or over the standards of evidence? My suspicion is that most of the real work is being done by grade 3 and very little by grades 1 and 2, and that the episode looks incommensurable only if one conflates the three. Argue me out of that if it is wrong, and note where a residue of shared vocabulary — titres, incubation periods, inactivation curves, lesion profiles — makes the two sides more commensurable than the word incommensurability suggests.

5. Say where the framework is silent, and do not force it to speak. Name at least two features of this episode that Kuhn's apparatus has no resources to address and that plausibly mattered to the outcome: the role of an agricultural practice and a public-health emergency, for instance, or the fact that the resistance was partly sustained by a structurally available alternative — a viral genome somehow shielded from the inactivation treatments — rather than by attachment to a paradigm.

6. Turn the case back on the theory, on five counts. (a) Accommodation versus crisis: if normal science always has the resource of inventing an intermediate category, what distinguishes a framework that is accommodating an anomaly from one that is collapsing under it? (b) Shared evidence: both sides accepted the same inactivation data, so does the meaning-variance thesis explain any disagreement here, or is the disagreement over the bearing of uncontentious evidence — a kind of disagreement Kuhn's account handles badly? (c) Scale: if the episode is a revolution for the subfield and a routine extension for the discipline, does Kuhn's notion of a paradigm have a determinate scale, and what happens to the theory if it does not? (d) Loss: was there any Kuhn loss, something the earlier framework could state or explain that the later one could not? My candidate is the heritable diversity of strains together with the species barrier, which were awkward for a conformational account for a long time; confirm it, replace it, or deny that there was any loss. (e) Unfalsifiability: the Kuhnian vocabulary can narrate resistance as normal science and conversion as revolution, which makes it hard to fail. So specify in advance what in the record would have made you conclude that this was not a Kuhnian paradigm shift, and then say whether that condition was close to being met.

7. Finally, give a verdict in one paragraph: was this a revolution, a local revision of a taxonomy, or a case that Kuhn's vocabulary misdescribes? Take a position, and note in a sentence what a defender of the theory would say to keep it.
