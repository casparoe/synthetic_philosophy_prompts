Evening. Bit of an odd one, and I'd rather it were taken seriously than politely.

I'm 71 and retired — forty years as a physical chemist, mostly diffraction work, which is a long way from this — and for the last couple of years I've been going along to a philosophy group at the local U3A. My question is about numbers rather than concepts, but it feels like there's something conceptual underneath it.

There's a man in my rambling club, Pete, who spent thirty-odd years as a medical statistician. He's the sort who will put a number on anything. I've found I can usually predict what he'll say but not why, so I've started writing his answers down afterwards. These are from memory, but I'm confident about the shape of them:

1. A neighbour, 66, prostate cancer, Gleason 3+4. The registry has thousands of men in that bracket with a ten-year survival around 80%, but there's a genomic score that splits the bracket, and for his particular score there are only about seventy men and things look worse. Pete went with the 80%. Seventy cases, he said, is a rounding error.

2. Same neighbour's brother, also cancer, but the question was whether a particular targeted drug would work. Pete ignored the hospital's tumour category entirely and put him with everyone carrying a certain mutation, across several different cancers. That grouping was a decent size. His point was that the label the hospital files you under isnt the thing doing the work.

3. Someone asked about the chance of a second stroke in people with a hole between the heart's upper chambers. Pete refused to give a number at all, despite there being large trials. He said the trials lump together several different kinds of stroke, and the average over a mixture like that describes nobody.

4. My own hip, last year. The surgeon said these last fifteen years in 95% of cases. Pete grunted and said that was everyone, including 85-year-olds, and for someone my age and weight it was nearer 88%. So there he narrowed — and he narrowed on age, which isn't really a cause of anything, it just travels with the things that are.

5. A genuinely one-off thing: whether the village's last pub would reopen within a year. He did give a number, about one in three, and when I pushed him he said it came from pubs that shut after losing their kitchen. That's a class of maybe forty pubs in the whole country, which sits badly with number 1 — unless the rule changes when there's nothing better on offer.

Now the case I actually care about. A scan for something else turned up a 4.5 cm swelling on my aorta below the kidneys. The vascular team quote roughly 1% a year rupture risk at that size if we just watch it, and they generally operate at 5.5 cm. Mine, though, is the little sac-shaped sort rather than the usual spindle-shaped one, and the handful of small series that report the sac-shaped ones seperately reckon they're nastier — two or three times the rate — but on tens of cases, not hundreds. Meanwhile a group runs a computer model of the wall stress from the CT scan, and by their model my particular geometry comes out at about a third of the average risk for a 4.5 cm aneurysm.

So what number does Pete give me? I don't just want the number, I want the rule. As far as I can tell there are two or three rules that fit all five of his earlier answers equally well and they come apart on mine: one says go broad until the counts are stable, one says group by what's actually causing it and let the mechanism overrule the headcount, one says use everything you know and blend. Worse, the two narrow answers here point in opposite directions — the sac-shaped series pushes the risk up, the wall-stress model pushes it down — so even "go narrow" doesn't settle it unless you know which mechanism he'd trust.

Tell me which rules fit his record, which one you'd put money on, whether there's any real chance he just refuses the way he did with the stroke question, and what I should ask him next — about my aorta or about something else entirely — that would actually tell the rules apart. And don't tell me to ask the vascular team, I've already asked them and I got the 1%. It's my aorta so I would quite like the right number, but I'm more interested in why he'll say what he says.
