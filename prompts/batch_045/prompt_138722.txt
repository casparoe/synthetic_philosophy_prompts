I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the causal status of consciousness this term, and a colleague who works on mental causation let me see a draft of a survey chapter she's writing on the causality objections. There's a passage near the end on epiphenomenalism that I may hand out for Thursday's session, because it's vivid and it says in three sentences what would take me twenty minutes to say well. But I've read it four times now and I have a nagging feeling that it is confident in a way that hides two or three slides that a defender of the view would object to — while also possibly being right in its overall verdict for reasons it doesn't state. I'd rather not hand graduate students something that they'll be unable to see through and that I can't repair under questioning. The passage runs:

"Epiphenomenalism holds that conscious experience is produced by physical processes in the brain but has no causal influence back on them — consciousness on this view is the steam whistle on the locomotive, along for the ride and doing no work on the engine. The difficulty, pressed repeatedly in the literature, is that the view cannot be consistently held by anyone who believes it. If my experience of pain has no effects on the physical world, then it cannot have brought about my judgment that I am in pain, or the sentence in which I say so. But that judgment is about my experience, and on any causal account of mental content the subject matter of a judgment is whatever brought it about; on the epiphenomenalist's own story what brought it about were neural events rather than the experience itself. So the epiphenomenalist is committed to saying that we have no knowledge of our own conscious lives, that our pain reports no more say anything about pain than the whistle's noise says anything about the boiler, and, worst of all, that nobody is ever in a position to believe epiphenomenalism truly, since the belief itself would have to be about consciousness while no conscious state could be among its causes. This is not one more consideration to be weighed against the view; it is a demonstration that the view takes back with one hand what it offers with the other, and it is generally regarded as the single strongest argument against it."

My own hunches, which I don't trust very far: first, that the move from causal inertness to "we have no knowledge" quietly assumes that knowledge of a state requires being causally acted on by that state, which is a substantive and contested thesis rather than something the epiphenomenalist grants; second, that "cannot be consistently held" runs together two very different verdicts — that the view is self-contradictory and that a believer in it could never be justified in believing it — which I take to be separable; third, that presenting the puzzle as being about reports and utterances rather than about the content and etiology of judgment targets something slightly easier than the real thing.

What I'd like from you is an audit I can act on before Thursday. Take the passage claim by claim and tell me what an epiphenomenalist would accept as a fair statement of her position, what is a reasonable compression for a survey chapter, and what she would reject as a distortion — and, importantly, whether her strongest conclusion (that the view is self-stultifying and that this is the best argument against it) survives once the questionable slides are repaired, or whether it depends on them. Two further things. I've been playing with a distinction of my own in the seminar notes between the causal explanation of a judgment's *occurrence* and the determination of its *content*, hoping to concede the first to the objector while preserving the second through something like a constitutive rather than causal dependence of the content on the experience — a "content-inheritance without causation," as I've been calling it. I can't tell whether that is a real distinction doing real work or just a label for a move the epiphenomenalist was always going to make, and whether it lands me somewhere unstable. And I'd like to know whether this paradox is best understood as an instance of a general template shared with the self-refutation charges brought against eliminativism and global content skepticism — which would tell me something about what I should say when a sharp student points out that if the argument works it seems to work against views nobody thinks are refuted. Please flag how confident you are in each of the judgments you make, and single out the one point where you think you are most likely to be wrong; and spare me a bibliography — I can find the literature myself, and it's the structure of the argument I need to get right.
