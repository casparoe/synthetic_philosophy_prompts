I write a small, free newsletter for about forty friends — mostly engineers, a couple of lawyers, one retired chemistry teacher — in which I take an argument that looks airtight and try to find the exact place it leaks. The one I'm stuck on right now is the definition of ordinary dispositional words like fragile, soluble, flammable, and conductive. The tempting definition says that to be fragile just is to be such that you would break if you were struck, that to be soluble just is to be such that you would dissolve if you were immersed, and so on. I've convinced myself this style of definition has at least two distinct failure modes, and I want to exhibit both of them at once, in a single episode involving a single object, because showing them separately makes each one look like a mere curiosity.

The two failure modes I have in mind are these. First, the test itself can remove the very property being tested: the thing is fragile, but the event of being struck is itself what makes it cease to be fragile, so nothing breaks, even though it really was fragile right up to the moment of impact. Second, the thing can remain fully fragile through the whole episode while some other feature of the situation intercepts the result, so again nothing breaks — but this time the fragility is still there afterward, intact and unexercised. I'd like you to construct one realistic scenario — a household, workshop, office, or hospital object; nothing futuristic, no nanobots, no magic materials, nothing a reader couldn't buy — fitting in a single paragraph, in which one and the same episode does both of these things to two different dispositions of one and the same object. Further constraints: both dispositions must be genuine, not merely apparent (the object must really have them, not just look as though it does, and it must not acquire one of them only when the episode starts); a bystander watching the episode, and even inspecting the object afterward, should not be able to tell from what happened which disposition was destroyed and which was merely blocked; and — this is the constraint that matters most to me — the two failures must not be two unrelated examples bolted together. The same single mechanism, or the same single event, should be responsible for both, so that the object's second disposition is somehow tied to the fate of the first (for instance, the thing that strips one disposition also sets up whatever blocks the other, or one disposition is a disposition concerning the other). If the literature has established labels for these two failure modes, tell me what they are so I can recognize them if I meet them again, but don't let the labels do any of the work.

After the scenario, I need you to show that it meets the specifications rather than asserting that it does. For each of the two dispositions, lay out what the disposition is, what its stimulus is, what its manifestation would be, and what the if-it-were-struck-it-would-break style of definition predicts; then explain why the prediction fails in each case, and why it fails in a different way in each case. Then tell me which single feature of the scenario is doing the work — the thing I couldn't drop without the case falling apart — and show me what the scenario collapses into if I remove that feature (I expect it degenerates into two ordinary, separately uninteresting examples, and I want to see that). Finally, and most usefully for my purposes: how would someone who likes the simple definition push back? I assume they'll try something like restricting the definition to normal or ideal conditions, or saying the object wasn't fragile in those circumstances, or relativizing dispositions to background conditions — give me the strongest version of that reply, and then tell me what the reply costs them, and which of my two failure modes is the harder one for them to absorb and why. Please write the whole answer in American English, keep the prose readable for smart non-philosophers, and prefer plain description over technical vocabulary wherever you can.
