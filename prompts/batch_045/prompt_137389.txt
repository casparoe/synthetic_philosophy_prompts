Our moral epistemology reading group has been chewing on the moral testimony debate for about two years and literally nobody has moved, which makes me suspect we're not disagreeing about what we think we're disagreeing about. Curious what you'd make of it.

Here's the shape of it. One camp says there's something off about pure moral deference: if I believe some practice is wrong only because someone I trust told me so, with no grip on why, then at best I've picked up a true belief. I don't understand it, and a moral view you don't understand isn't really yours — it won't guide you when the trusted person isn't around, it won't survive the next person telling you the opposite, and it's arguably not the kind of thing you shoud be forming on someone else's authority anyway. The other camp says this is wildly overblown. We defer morally all the time and it plainly works: kids pick up that hitting people is wrong from adults, I can learn that some workplace custom is exploitative from a friend who understands the context better than I do, and if moral knowledge really required first-hand grasp of the reasons then almost nobody would know almost anything moral, which is absurd. They also point out that there don't really seem to be moral experts, so half the "expertise" framing is a red herring.

Every move has a countermove and we all know the countermoves by hart at this point. So please diagnose rather than adjudicate — I've read the main papers, no need to walk me through them.

My half-baked hypothesis is that the label covers at least three questions that keep sliding into each other: an epistemic one (can testimony transmit justification or knowledge here at all?), an agential one (is it permissible, or decent, to let someone else's judgment settle what you think and do?), and a constitutive one about what a moral judgment even is — a belief about a mind-independent fact, versus something more like a stance or commitment, which maybe you just can't inherit from another person's say-so. If that's right, the pessimists' arguments might be good against one and irrelevant to the others.

But I also can't shake a second, weirder suspicion: that the "data" here is really one very specific kind of case — a testifier who hands over a verdict and no reasons, and a recipient with no independent view who isn't going to get one — and that we're treating intuitions about that case as evidence about all testimony. I tried a variant where the testifier walks you through the reasoning conversationally instead of just asserting the verdict, and our pessimists softened a lot, which makes me wonder how much of the standoff is about the format of the exchange rather than about morality. Maybe what's bothering people is closer to: you can't outsource the activity of thinking, and some ways of telling are ways of getting you to do it yourself.

So: substantive, verbal, or several questions under one name? Are the sides weighting different cases? Is a hidden empirical or metanormative commitment doing the work — about whether moral expertise exists, or about what moral judgments are? And given whatever you diagnose, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, some empirical work on how the intuition varies, or giving up the question?
