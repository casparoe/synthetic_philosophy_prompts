I teach a large intro philosophy of mind course -- meets twice a week, 80 minutes a session, around 24 students, mostly first-years who haven't read anything yet. I want one session on the Mary case that isn't just me sketching a black-and-white room on the board and asking "so, does she learn something new or not?" They glaze over, and the two or three who do push back always push back in the same ways and we never get past a stalemate. Last year I tried a guess-the-soda thing on a whim and it fell flat, mostly because everyone already knew what all the sodas taste like, so nothing was ever at stake.

So: design me an actual activity -- a game, a staged choice, something they do -- where they run into whatever it is that makes the case feel compelling, and run into it before I name it. I'd like the word "qualia" to stay unsaid until the debrief. My own vague idea was to use tastes or smells since they're cheap and I can blindfold people: give each small group a one-page "complete physical specification" of some food item (ingredients, the chemistry, a made-up but plausible story about receptors and brain responses), let them study it, have them commit to a bet on whether they can pick that item out of a lineup of near-neighbors, and then do the reveal. But I'd rather have your version than mine, and if the betting mechanic is wrong, change it. Whatever you design needs: supermarket-cheap materials, minute-by-minute timing for the full 80 minutes, a payoff scheme that stings a little without anyone spending money (points toward something trivial, or public standing), and rules that don't break when someone in the room has definitely eaten the thing before.

Two things I especially want built in. First, a way to pull apart "I gained an ability -- to recognize it, imagine it, remember it" from "I gained a fact"; the skills version of the reply is the one my students find most persuasive and I don't want to just assert that it's inadequate, I want them to feel why it might be. Second, a second case near the end: someone who definitely had the experience years ago, has totally forgotten what it was like, and also has the full spec sheet. Does the reveal teach that person anything? I have a feeling that variant does work the standard one doesn't, but I genuinely can't tell whether it's just the same intuition in a costume -- if it's actually confused or collapses back into the first case, say so plainly instead of building me something polite. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto specific moves: the skills-not-facts reply, the "she already knew everything, so of course she could have predicted it" reply, and the "this only shows something about how we conceptualize experience" reply. It should also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- allergies and sensory issues, the student who's eaten everything, the one who points out that my one-page spec sheet obviously isn't complete physical knowledge and therefore the whole exercise begs the question, and how I handle that last one without deflating the room. And be honest about which feature of the argument the activity just can't reproduce and which I'll have to supply by talking at them afterwards: my real worry is that any activity like this only ever demonstrates *our* ignorance rather than Mary's total knowledge, and if that's fatal to the whole idea I'd rather hear it than get a design that's too polite to admit it. Also tell me how confident you are that this actually lands with first-years, and where the design leans on a contested reading of the case. No citations, no reading list, no literature names -- I just need the moves and the mechanics. Please write it in American English.
