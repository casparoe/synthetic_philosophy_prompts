I work in predictive modeling and uncertainty quantification. I'd like you to do a translation job for me, and then tell me what broke in the translation.

The source material is the philosophical dispute over the interpretation of probability: the family of views on which probability is long-run relative frequency within a class of repeatable trials; the view on which it is degree of belief constrained by coherence, with exchangeability doing much of the argumentative work; and the propensity family of views, on which probability is a physical disposition or tendency of a single chance setup, of which stable long-run frequencies are symptoms rather than the whole content. I know these positions at the level of names, slogans, and standard objections; I have not read the primary literature, so please don't assume familiarity with the dialectic, and please don't build anything on quotations or on who said what first.

The target vocabulary is my own working one, in which I'm fluent and which I'd like the translation carried out in as far as it will go: sampling distributions; confidence sets and their coverage under repeated sampling from an assumed model; exchangeability and the de Finetti-style representation of an exchangeable sequence as a mixture of independent and identically distributed ones; priors, posteriors, hierarchical models with partial pooling, and posterior predictive distributions; proper scoring rules and empirical calibration; and the machine-learning habit of splitting uncertainty into the part that more data would shrink (epistemic) and the part it would not (aleatoric).

To sharpen the comparison, here is a homemade three-way distinction I use when thinking about this. When a probability attaches to a single case, I distinguish (a) the repeatability claim: this case belongs to some class of trials whose limiting relative frequency is p; (b) the dispositional claim: this case has a physical tendency of strength p to come out this way; (c) the credal claim: some agent's, or some model's, predictive distribution puts p on it. In everything I actually do, (a) and (c) show up as distinguishable, and (b) looks like either a restatement of (a) or an extra piece of structure I never use. I want to know whether that impression is a fact about probability or a fact about the limits of my job. The case that made me wonder is that I put numbers on one-off events — the probability that a particular levee overtops at least once in the next thirty years, say. There is one levee and the thirty years happen once. My colleagues treat the output as a forecast to be scored; the client hears a fact about the levee.

What I want, in this order:

1. Restate each of the three interpretations, together with the strongest argument for it and the most serious objection to it, in the vocabulary above. Where a claim has a clean counterpart, give the counterpart. Where it has a counterpart only after you have bent it, give the counterpart and mark exactly where the bending happened — the bending interests me more than the mapping does. If, once translated, two of the three collapse into the same position in my vocabulary, say so and say whether that collapse is my vocabulary's fault.

2. The reference class problem. Does it have a real analogue in my vocabulary, or does my toolkit dissolve it rather than solve it? My suspicion is that it reappears as the choice of a conditioning set, or of which sequence of cases I am willing to treat as exchangeable with this one — a choice my practice settles by out-of-sample predictive performance rather than by argument. If that's roughly right, say whether the dissolution is genuine or whether I have quietly changed the subject.

3. The principle linking chance to credence: that a rational agent's credence in an outcome, conditional on that outcome's being p, should itself be p. The nearest thing I can locate in my own vocabulary is the sort of result which says that a forecaster whose probabilities all come from a single joint predictive distribution is, under her own distribution, calibrated in the limit. Is that the same claim, a special case of it, or a weaker cousin? If something is lost in the substitution, say precisely what, and whether the loss matters for anything I would ever be asked to do.

4. A residue list: at least two items from the philosophical debate that have no expression at all in the vocabulary I gave you. For each, say whether you take it to be a defect of my vocabulary — something my framework cannot say and should be embarrassed about — or a sign that the philosophical claim was less contentful than it looked, and say what would count as evidence for the first verdict rather than the second.

5. A diagnostic. Describe a task or an observation I could actually be handed, in the vicinity of my own work rather than a philosopher's hypothetical, that would force my vocabulary to grow something propensity-like. If you think no such task exists, say why my framework is guaranteed to get by without one.

One constraint: I am not asking for reading recommendations, and citations would be useless to me; I want the arguments, reconstructed in my terms. Keep the translation honest — if a step in some philosophical argument only goes through once you have imported an assumption that my vocabulary does not already contain, flag that rather than importing it silently and calling the result a translation.
