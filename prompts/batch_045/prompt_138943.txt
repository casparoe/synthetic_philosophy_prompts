I'm defending a strong line in a seminar paper and want to know how exposed it is. Good's theorem: for an EU maximiser who updates by Bayes' rule, a free signal can't lower your ex-ante expected utility, since the unconditional best action is still on the menu in every branch — you can always ignore what you learn. I want to push this normatively: the "right not to know" literature, and people who pay to avoid a genetic test result, are, I claim, either (i) in a case where the info changes their options, their payoffs, or other people's behaviour, so the theorem never applied, or (ii) leaning on preferences that aren't really preferences over outcomes — they care about the news itself, or about suspense, or they know they'll mis-update (say, underweight the base rate) and are self-paternalising. Either way there's no interesting residue: either the theorem holds or the agent isn't the kind of agent it's about, and that's their defect, not a limit of the theorem.

What would count against this? Three things. First, wether any of what I swept into (i) and (ii) actually has teeth: is there a case where the info is genuinely free, the menu genuinely unchanged, the agent updates correctly and maximises EU, and is still worse off for knowing? The one I keep chewing on is where knowing removes an option — a doctor who learns something is then professionally required to act on it, so the info shrinks her menu instead of expanding it. Is that a real failure of the ignore-it move, or am I just relabelling an assumption? Second, what would my own side concede: I asume I have to grant that the theorem is close to empty as a guide for real agents, who flunk the antecedents constantly — is that concession worse than it looks, does my normative claim collapse into "if you were already perfect you'd never regret knowing"? Third, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward and say which ones I should actually feel. I'm most moved by the empirical literature on information avoidance, so tell me if that's my real problem or whether I can write it off as just measuring irrational updating.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then argue for it — no preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
