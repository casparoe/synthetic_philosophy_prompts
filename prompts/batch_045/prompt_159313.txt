I teach an upper-level epistemology seminar (juniors/seniors, they've all had intro) and I'm writing this year's final, and I need one essay question on the regress problem that actually discriminates. Last year's papers were useless for grading purposes: nearly everyone turned in the same shape of essay — a paragraph setting up the Agrippan trilemma, a paragraph on foundationalism with some half-remembered stuff about basic beliefs and seeming states, a paragraph on coherentism where they say "mutual support" a lot, a paragraph on infinitism where the only real point is that it's odd to require an infinite chain a person can't possibly have, and then a conclusion that hedges between the three. I want a question where that essay lands in the B range at best, and where getting an A requires doing something with the positions rather than reciting them.

The angle I've been toying with in my own notes is a fourth option that none of the readings cover and that I don't have a recognised name for — call it terminism. Roughly: a belief is justified when the subject can meet the challenges that are live in her situation, and a chain of reasons may stop anywhere the questioning stops; the stopping point need not be self-justifying or infallible or about experience (as foundationalism wants), need not be supported by the rest of the believer's system (as coherentism wants), and the chain need not go on forever (as infinitism insists). It just has to have no unanswered challenge facing it. So stopping points can shift with context and interests, and in that sense they can be arbitrary. I'd like the question to force students to deal with three things in particular. First, before they classify terminism they have to say what makes the three traditional views three views — what the dividing question actually is: is it the shape of the chain, the nature of the terminus, the account of why regress isn't vicious, or something else? Because I think half the class secretly thinks the taxonomy is just about drawing diagrams. Second, they have to face the anything-goes objection: if stopping points can be arbitrary and vary with what anyone happens to care about, doesn't the view make justification too cheap, and what would have to be true of the practice of challenging and answering for it not to? Third, they have to say whether the distinction between propositional and doxastic justification does any work here — is the regress a regress of reasons that exist, or of reasons an agent can actually produce — and whether terminism's plausibility depends on which one you're talking about. And I'd like them to end by saying what the fate of terminism shows about the regress argument itself: whether the argument was only ever pressing given a picture of justification that the terminist is rejecting outright.

Concretely, what I want from you: an in-class question they'd answer in about 45 minutes (say 800 words), a model answer, and a scheme I can mark with. Please seperate the sections like this — "The question" (under 150 words), "Model answer" (700–800 words), "Marking scheme" (20 marks total, broken into four or five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, and a short note on what specifically seperates an A answer from a B from a C in terms of moves made, not just coverage), and "Typical errors" (one line per must-hit point on how students usually botch it). Two more things: I want the model answer to commit — it should pick one of the four options (or argue terminism collapses into one of the three, and say which and why) and defend it rather than surveying the options, and it should tell me plainly where the strongest objection to that verdict lands, since I'd rather have a model answer that's arguable than one that's evenly balanced. But keep it something a strong student could plausibly write under exam conditions: it should include at least one objection it stops to answer, not read like a polished journal article. No bibliography or citations anywhere, and don't rely on any source we haven't read; if a formulation matters, paraphrase it rather than quoting.
