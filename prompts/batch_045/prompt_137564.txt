Hey — I'm prepping a grad seminar on program equilibrium and I want somebody to take the unfashionable side of an argument I keep having with myself. Ground rules so we're on the same page: one-shot PD, each player submits a program, each program is handed the other's source as input and outputs C or D, and any program that hasn't halted within its step budget B is scored as playing D. No pre-play communication, no repetition, no third parties.

The bit I want to add is my own wrinkle, so you won't find it anywhere: call a program opaque at B if no opponent working with budget B can, within those B steps, establish what the program will do against an opponent that defects on it. Opacity is worth something — an opaque program can't be safely exploited, because a would-be exploiter can't tell whether defecting on it lands on mutual C-with-exploitation or on mutual D. But it's fragile: hand the opponent more steps and more programs stop being opaque, including programs that at a smaller budget were perfectly good cooperators.

So here's what I want you to argue: more reasoning power is not monotonically good in this game. Concretely, defend the claim that there's an intermediate budget B* at which both players do strictly better than they do at any substantially larger budget — cooperation lives in a window here, and it gets destroyed from above as well as from below. Spell the mechanism out rather than gesturing at it: give me a concrete pair of programs and walk through what happens at a low budget, at B*, and at a much larger budget, and say exactly which step of the reasoning breaks when the budget goes up. My hunch is that the break is in the "I cooperate only if I can't prove you'd lie down and take it" clause — the exploit-check some cooperators run before committing — but make the case in your own terms.

Then handle the objection that always gets raised in seminar: if a big budget is bad, why doesn't a high-budget player just hard-wire a smaller cap into its own source and be done with it? That's where I'll push back hardest.

Keep it under 600 words, and don't cite anything — I want the argument, not the literature. End with one sentence stating the exact thesis you'd defend, and then \answer{...} naming the single step of your argument you'd least like to have to defend in front of the room.
