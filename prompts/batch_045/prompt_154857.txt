hey, I'm taking a philosophy of technology seminar this term and I keep bumping into something I dont have a word for, hope this is ok to ask here.

Two cases that feel like the same shape. (1) A team building an autonomous driving feature does a serious internal red-team exercise, writes up a long list of ways the system could hurt someone, ships it anyway, and one of the listed failure modes actually happens. Now the line is that they *knew*, and the write-up basically becomes the case against them. (2) A hospital triage tool: the engineers flagged in a design doc that the model performs noticeably worse for a certain patient group, didn't fix it before rollout, and when someone from that group is harmed the flag is exactly what makes them blameworthy — while everyone else in the org, who never understood the model at all, is off the hook.

The thing I can't pin down: doing the epistemic work properly seems to *manufacture* blame for you without making anyone safer. The people who understood the risk get blamed for it; the people who didn't can appeal to how unpredictable these systems are. So the careful team ends up worse off than a team that never looked, and there's a pull toward willful ignorance that I don't see anyone naming.

I know it's near the responsibility-gap stuff we did a few weeks ago, but that was about harm with nobody to blame, and here the harm attracts plenty of blame — it just flows uphill to whoever knew the most, which feels like the opposite failure. It's also not just ordinary negligence ("you should have known" vs. "you did know and shipped anyway"), and it's not quite the pattern where a human operator soaks up blame for a machine's error either, since the person blamed here isn't a rubber stamp, they're the most competent person in the room.

Does this have a name, or am I squashing two or three different effects into one? I don't just want a label — I want the edges: whether it's really one phenomenon, whether it needs the organizational setting to get going or survives in single-person cases, and whether there's a good reply that foresight *should* attract blame and my "incentive toward ignorance" bit is a flaw in our liability practices rather than anything morally interesting. Please say plainly if I've described something confused — if the two cases aren't the same shape, or if the nobody-else-is-blameworthy part is doing all the work and I should just be asking about diffused responsibility. And give me a rough confidence level on each bit rather than one flat verdict.
