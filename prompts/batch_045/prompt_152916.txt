Background. Take the following as given, and do not spend your answer correcting historical or technical minutiae in it.

Truth is a coarse-grained property of propositions, but inquiry seems to call for something finer: an ordering of propositions by their closeness to the truth, so that some falsehoods count as nearer the truth than others, and some truths count as nearer than other truths. The logical problem of truthlikeness is to give an adequate account of that ordering and of its formal properties. Three broad lines of attack are standardly distinguished: the content approach, the consequence approach, and the likeness (or similarity) approach, which measures how close the possibilities a proposition allows are to the actual one using some distance or similarity structure.

Popper's comparative definition belongs to the consequence approach: one theory counts as at least as verisimilar as another when it has at least all the true consequences of the other and no more false ones. The famous collapse results of the mid-1970s, arrived at independently by two philosophers, sank this proposal. Because any false theory T entails T-or-P for every falsehood P, and T-or-P is itself false whenever T and P are both false, a false theory has infinitely many false consequences; the inclusion conditions then cannot be met, and the upshot is that no false theory is closer to the truth than any other false theory. The ordering collapses into plain truth, and it can no longer do the work that realism about science wanted from it. Subsequent work responded by adding structure — quantitative likeness or distance relations among possible states, so that near misses count for something rather than every false consequence counting equally — while retaining the thought that science aims at getting closer to the truth. Two further problems are usually distinguished alongside the logical one, and both presuppose that it has been solved: the epistemological problem, of how we could ever have reason to judge that one theory is closer to the truth than another, and the axiological problem, of what kind of cognitive value truthlikeness is and how it relates to truth and to the aims of inquiry.

The case. A flood-risk agency must choose between two catchment simulation models, M1 and M2, for setting defence heights. Both are known to be strictly false: each simplifies the physics and discretises space and time. The agency does not want to say merely that M1 predicts better on its test set. It wants to say that M1 is closer to the truth about this catchment than M2, because it takes closeness to the truth to be what licenses extrapolation to unobserved scenarios — a one-in-two-hundred-year event, or a changed land-use configuration.

To make this precise, the agency proposes what it calls query-relative truthlikeness (QTL). Fix a finite set Q of queries, each a target quantity paired with a tolerance: peak water level at a given gauge for a given event, within five centimetres; time-to-peak, within thirty minutes; total inundated area, within four per cent. The queries are chosen to be decision-relevant and measurable. For each query define the normalised error of a model as the ratio of its deviation from the truth to the tolerance, capped at one. Then:

1. Comparative (dominance) version: M is Q-closer to the truth than N when M's normalised error is no greater than N's on every query in Q, and strictly less on at least one.
2. Cardinal fallback: a weighted sum of normalised errors, with weights set by the stakes attached to each query.
3. Requirement R1: because Q is finite, the argument that inflates a false theory's falsity-content without bound has nowhere to go, so the collapse result is blocked.
4. Requirement R2 (refinement-stability): the ordering should not flip when a query is split into sub-queries or a tolerance is tightened, except where the increase in resolution is genuinely doing the work.
5. Requirement R3 (reparametrisation invariance): restating the same target system in different variables — stage, discharge, energy head, or a nonlinear transform thereof — should not change the ranking.
6. Requirement R4 (estimability): the ordering should be estimable from finite, noisy data, by some cross-validation-type estimator that converges to it as data accumulate, so that the ranking can also underwrite claims of progress when a model is recalibrated.
