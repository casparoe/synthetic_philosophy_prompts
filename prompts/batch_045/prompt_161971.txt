I'm taking an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar — we've just spent two weeks on epiphenomenalism and on the paradox of phenomenal judgment — and I spend one afternoon a week sitting in on the clinical ethics committee at the teaching hospital where I volunteer. The two have started to look like the same problem to me, and I want to know whether I'm seeing something real or just pattern-matching.

Here is the practice I keep coming back to. On every ward I've been on, pain is assessed by asking the patient to rate it on a 0–10 scale, and the number they give is treated as conclusive: it becomes the pain score in the chart, it triggers the analgesia protocol, and nobody asks the patient to justify it or checks it against anything else. The definition clinicians are taught is that pain is whatever the person experiencing it says it is, present whenever they say it is. But the same wards are full of qualifications to that: if the patient is ventilated, demented, or preverbal, staff switch to a behavioural scale scored by the nurse instead; if the patient reports 10 and the team suspects diversion, the number quietly stops being conclusive; and in the research protocols I've sat through, patient-reported pain is simultaneously the primary outcome and a soft, unblinded, subjective endpoint that reviewers would replace with a biomarker tomorrow if they had one. My committee is now discussing a draft policy built on exactly this discomfort. It imagines a bedside monitor — I know it doesn't exist, but humour me — that reads heart-rate variability, pupil dilation and facial action units and outputs what I'll call a concordance index between 0 and 1, and it proposes that for certain controlled drugs a self-report above 7 authorises a dose only when the concordance index is above 0.5. Where the two disagree, the draft says the nurse should record the patient's number in the chart but dose according to the index.

So here is what I want: an account of what theory of the relation between a phenomenal state and its subject's judgment about it this whole cluster of practices presupposes. Does treating the 0–10 as authoritative — as something the patient need not defend, and which outranks the nurse's own observations of the same body — actually require rejecting epiphenomenalism, or does it only require the much weaker claim that reports reliably track pains? Can an epiphenomenalist keep the practice by pointing out that the physical state realising the pain is also what causes the report, so the report is reliable without being caused by the pain — and if so, does the paradox of phenomenal judgment return at the level of authority rather than reliability, i.e. does the report then become a symptom on a par with pupil dilation rather than a judgment about an experience, and does the practice's asymmetry between report and behaviour survive that demotion? I'd like the components separated: which features are strictly presupposed, which merely sit comfortably with a theory of first-person authority, and which are underdetermined in the sense that several rival views — including ones the practice looks as though it rules out — would license them equally well. And finally, since the committee will actually vote: if epiphenomenalism were true, would that discordance clause — the patient's number in the chart, the index in the syringe — be the coherent policy, an incoherent one, or simply what the practice was already implicitly committed to in the behavioural-scale and suspected-diversion cases? Where exactly, if anywhere, does the existing practice contradict itself?
