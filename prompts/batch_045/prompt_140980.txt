I'm a second-year PhD student in economics putting together the framing chapter for a survey on decision-making under cognitive limits, and my advisor keeps telling me that the literature scores views against each other without ever saying what the scorecard is. So before I compare anything, I want the specification. Fix the purpose as follows: a theory of rationality for an agent with real resource limits — limited time, limited memory or working space, limited attention, limited representational capacity — that is meant to be genuinely normative, in the sense that it can tell such an agent what it would be reasonable to do and can still call some of what bounded agents do a mistake rather than merely a consequence of their limits. Not a descriptive psychology of what people actually do, and not unbounded Bayesian decision theory with a paragraph of hand-waving about costs bolted on. Given that purpose, write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of this kind has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and be explicit about why each one is a requirement rather than a taste.

Here are the candidates I'm carrying around, which you should feel free to reject, split, or replace, since part of what I want is a view on which of them are real desiderata and which are the prejudices of one research program dressed up as requirements. (1) The deliberation-cost regress: choosing how long to deliberate is itself a decision made under the same bound, so the theory has to say when to stop without presupposing an answer to the very question it is stuck on — my case is a fellowship committee with 400 applications, twenty minutes per careful read, six hours of scheduled meeting time, and the option of paying a screener to cut the pool. (2) Interruptibility: the agent may have less time than it believes and will not be warned when the time runs out, so it seems to me that any partial execution of the theory's recommended procedure should already yield a usable recommendation, and I want to know whether demanding that is a genuine requirement or an engineering preference. (3) Self-applicability: the recommendation must be reachable within the bound by the agent it is addressed to, and the agent must be able to tell, within the bound, that it has complied — otherwise the advice is empty. (4) Representational parsimony: the theory should not quietly presuppose that the agent has already written down a prior over all worlds or a utility function over an exponentially large outcome space. (5) The currency question: whether the costs of thinking have to be commensurable with the outcomes being weighed, and what happens if they are paid in effort or attention rather than in the coin of the decision. (6) Immunity to exploitation: should a bounded agent be required to be immune to money pumps or Dutch books, given that recognizing the pump seems to require exactly the reasoning it lacks? (7) Menu and framing sensitivity: whether it is admissible for a bounded agent's choice to depend on the order, granularity, or description of the options, or whether description-invariance is a requirement smuggled in from the unbounded theory. (8) Scaffold neutrality: whether deliberation offloaded onto a calculator, a colleague, a market price, or a written note counts as expanding the bound or as part of the act. (9) The convergence condition: should recommendations approach the unbounded Bayesian or expected-utility answer as the bound is relaxed without limit, or is that a prejudice, given cases where a cruder rule does better than a more inclusive one? (10) The bound's own specification: must the theory take the bound as given, must it say what the bound is a bound on, and must it handle an agent who is uncertain about its own limits and about when options will stop arriving? (11) Normative bite: the theory has to distinguish genuine error from mere limitation closely enough that it cannot end up excusing everything.

For each desideratum, give me the motivating case and mark whether it is non-negotiable given my stated purpose or optional. Then the part I care most about: tell me which subsets of the list are jointly unsatisfiable, with the argument rather than the assertion — I have a suspicion that the tractability requirements and the non-exploitability requirement cannot both be had, and another that interruptibility and any strong optimality-tracking condition conflict, but I want to see whether you agree and what the tension really turns on. Say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and point out anything important my list has missed. A short table at the end scoring Simon-style satisficing with aspiration levels, information-cost and rational-attention approaches, the fast-and-frugal heuristics and ecological-rationality program, and the bounded-optimality tradition in AI against the final list would be useful, but keep it brief: the criteria are the deliverable, and I can do the scoring myself if the specification is right.
