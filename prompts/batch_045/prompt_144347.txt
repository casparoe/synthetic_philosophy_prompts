I've been asked to run a two-hour evening session on the Principal Principle for our city rationality meetup, and I want a plan for the session rather than the session itself. Please don't write out the explanation or draft me slides; I can do that. What I need is the architecture: what has to be introduced in what order and why, what carries the idea, and where the predictable failures are.

The audience: fifteen to twenty-five people, mostly working programmers, two or three physicists, a handful of people whose entire philosophy of probability comes from Bayesian statistics and the sequences. Roughly half will arrive with the reflex that probabilities just are degrees of belief and that "objective chance" is either shorthand for a frequency or a leftover confusion. So the principle has to be made to bite before it can be stated, or it will sound like "your probability should equal the probability."

The carrying example. I have a candidate and I want you to either adopt it or replace it, with reasons either way. There is a sealed grey box in the town library, running on its own battery, with a screen and a lens. Every morning at nine the screen shows a number between zero and one; at noon the lens either flashes green or doesn't. The manufacturer is dead and the manual is one page: it says only that the displayed number is the chance that the lens will flash that day. The library has kept a public log for three years: on the 1,271 days the screen read 0.30, the lens flashed on 30.1 percent of them, and so on up the range. Nobody has opened the box, and I intend to refuse, on principle, to say whether its mechanism is deterministic.

Two variants the example has to be able to absorb. First, the Calibration Rival: a black-box forecasting model trained on the public log whose calibration over the last six months beats the displayed number. If the number is the chance and the model is better calibrated, what does the principle tell me to match? I want this staged so that it forces the gap between deferring to a chance and deferring to a well-calibrated forecaster, rather than letting the two collapse into the same thing. Second, the case where the information you would gain if the event happened would itself discredit the chance you deferred to — a second machine, deliberately boring in description, whose tomorrow's number depends on today's flash. I want that to land as a genuine tension and not as a puzzle-box technicality, and I especially want your advice on the moment I say "that information is inadmissible," because someone will immediately say that this is just reclassifying every counterexample as inadmissible, and I don't yet know whether to concede that, bite it, or reframe it earlier in the sequence so it doesn't look like a rescue.

Two constraints I'm fairly confident about and want you to check: no quantum mechanics anywhere in the example, and no coins or dice. I think I know why both matter — the first licenses "chance is a fundamental brute primitive, mystery, next question," and the second lets symmetry do all the work so the principle looks like a restatement of indifference — but tell me if I have it backwards or if the costs are different from what I think.

What I want mapped, concretely.

One: the dependency structure. What must be in place before the principle can be stated non-vacuously, and what depends on what. In particular, how do I get this audience to grant chance enough independence from opinion that the principle says something, without opening the metaphysical question of whether chances exist? And where does the conditional form go? My instinct is that the admissibility clause only becomes visible once they see that the principle constrains credence conditional on a chance proposition, and not unconditional credence, and I'm unsure whether that distinction belongs before the first statement of the principle or after it, as a repair.

Two: the misconception map, keyed to three people I expect to be in the room. The determinist physicist, who will say that under a block universe the chances are zero or one and the principle either tells him nothing or tells him to be certain. The machine-learning person, who will say that chance is just the output of the best-calibrated model and the principle is expert deference with extra formalism. And the decision-theory person, who will try to drag the entire second hour into Sleeping Beauty. For each: the strongest version of the point, the exact moment in the sequence to head it off, and how much of it I should concede rather than defeat.

Three: the check. One question at the end that distinguishes having got the idea from having memorized the formula. Tell me what a successful answer looks like and which near-miss answers I should be able to tell apart from the real thing.

Four: what to refuse to cover, and where in the session to say so out loud. My current list is the metaphysics of laws, the amended principles that quantify over conditional chances, the Sleeping Beauty literature, and whether chances exist at all. Tell me what else is on it.

Finally, a sequencing question I keep going back and forth on. I want the session to have one takeaway sentence, something like the thought that the principle's whole content lives in its admissibility clause, so that the real question is which information is permitted to move you away from the chance. Should I hand that sentence to the room at the start as a map, or withhold it and let it land at the end? Argue for one.
