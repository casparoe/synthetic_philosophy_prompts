You are a professional philosopher specialising in philosophy of medicine and bioethics. Please think carefully about the following problem: before settling on an answer, spend a moment brainstorming several candidate principles and the cases that tell against each of them, then present the one or two that survive. Write your answer in British English.

I have been trying to work out what I actually think about the therapy/enhancement distinction, and my verdicts on cases keep slipping out of the grip of the principle I was taught. The principle I was working with: an intervention is therapy when it restores or protects a capacity to within the range of species-typical functioning for a healthy person, and enhancement when it improves a capacity beyond what that individual's species-typical functioning would have given them; therapy is what we owe one another through publicly funded healthcare, while enhancement is a matter of private choice that the state may permissibly leave unfunded.

My verdicts, case by case:

1. A cochlear implant for a congenitally deaf two-year-old, with parental consent: therapy, should be publicly funded — though I am uneasy about saying a family is obliged to accept it.
2. Corrective lenses for short sight: therapy, and publicly funded. Obvious, even though the level being restored is set partly by what a literate society demands of eyes.
3. Growth hormone for a healthy child predicted to be extremely short, with no other pathology: enhancement, and I am uneasy about it; I would not fund it publicly. If the child were short because of a diagnosable hormone deficiency, I call it therapy and fund it — even though the treatment and the outcome are the same.
4. A prosthetic leg that lets an amputee walk and also run faster than any unmodified human: I want to call this therapy and fund it, even though it takes the person above species-typical functioning.
5. A safe drug that cuts a healthy adult's required sleep from eight hours to four with no other effects: enhancement, and I am untroubled by it — more untroubled than I am by the growth hormone case, which seems backwards, since the child cannot consent and the adult can.
6. Private tutoring for my own child: enhancement, positional, and I am entirely untroubled by it in a way I am not by the sleep drug, which also seems backwards.
7. A drug that blunts the emotional intensity with which a frightening memory is reconsolidated, given to a trauma survivor in the days after the event: therapy, fine, publicly funded.
8. The same drug given to someone six months into an ordinary bereavement, whose grief is severe but not diagnosable as a disorder: not therapy — I would withhold it — even though it would restore their functioning to normal sooner.
9. A vaccine: prevention, publicly funded, obviously fine. But now consider an immune booster that lifts immune responsiveness above anything found anywhere in the population, protecting against a novel pandemic. By my principle this is enhancement and therefore private business, yet that verdict feels wrong to me: it seems like exactly the sort of thing public health should fund.

Is there a principle in this neighbourhood that delivers all of these, or do some of my verdicts have to go? Please: state the best one or two candidates precisely, clause by clause, naming the case that motivates each clause; give their verdicts on cases I have not listed — in particular, on gene editing in embryos to raise general cognitive ability, and on cases where we could either modify the person or modify the environment instead (a ramp versus a mobility device, say, or acoustic treatment of a classroom versus attention medication); and tell me plainly which of my verdicts no single principle in this family can accommodate, and why. I would also like to know whether I am really asking one question or two that I have been running together — whether the line between therapy and enhancement, once drawn correctly, does any of the funding and permissibility work I have been expecting it to do.
