I got into an argument with a friend about anthropic reasoning last week and lost it, mostly because I couldn't say where the video essay he sent me went wrong. It's a pop-philosophy thing on the "presumptuous philosopher" and the two sampling assumptions, and I want to write a reply post without making a complete fool of myself. Can you audit it for me?

Here's roughly how he (and the video) pitched it:

"When you're trying to work out how many observers the universe contains, you have to pick a sampling assumption, and there are basically two. The self-sampling assumption says: reason as if you were drawn at random from the observers in your reference class. Your own existence therefore carries no evidential weight on its own — what does the work is the thought that you're a typical member of that class, which is where the doomsday argument comes from: if enormous numbers of humans come after us, finding yourself this absurdly early is a freak coincidence, so the end is probably near. The self-indication assumption says instead: reason as if you were drawn at random from all possible observers, so the bare fact that you exist is itself evidence for whichever hypothesis predicts more of them. That's the presumptuous philosopher case: theory A says the universe holds a trillion observers, theory B says a trillion trillion, and SIA says you should be a trillion times more confident in B before you've looked at a single peice of astronomical data. So the two assumptions really just disagree about whether your own existence is evidence for anything at all, and the presumptuous philosopher is meant to show that SIA is absurd, since cosmology obviously isn't the sort of thing you can settle from an armchair."

The bit I'm suspicious of is "all possible observers" — that sounds wether off or just over my head, I can't tell. I'm also not sure the "typical member" framing is how the doomsday argument actually works, or just a shortcut for a popular audience.

And the part I actually care about: someone in the comments proposed what they called a third option — count observer-moments rather than observers. On their version, a world with a trillion observers each conscious for a trillion seconds and a world with a trillion trillion observers each conscious for a single second come out exactly tied, instead of SIA handing the second one a trillion-to-one win. Does that dodge the presumptuous philosopher's conclusion, or is it secretly one of the two assumptions in a costume — and if it's a costume, which one is underneath, and why?

Don't bother with references or a reading list, I just need to know: which bits of the summary would someone working on this accept, which are subtly wrong, what a defender of either assumption would push back on, and whether the conclusions at the end survive once the mistakes are fixed.
