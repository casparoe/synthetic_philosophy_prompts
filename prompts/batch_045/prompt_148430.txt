I write about science and ideas for a living, and there's a sentence I keep hearing in interviews that I've started to distrust: ask two researchers to describe the same system and you get two accounts that genuinely collide, and then everyone assures me they're both right, it just depends on what you were asking, and the conversation moves on. My favourite specimen right now is from the virology beat — a virologist tells me a virus is alive, it has genes, it evolves, it does exactly the thing evolution does to things; a cell biologist tells me it obviously isn't alive, no metabolism, no membrane of its own, it's a machine assembled inside somebody else's cell; and both of them are, apparently, correct. I was talking too a philosopher friend the other week about whether that both correct means anything more than we stopped arguing, and she said what I actually want is Jain anekāntavāda, the many-sidedness doctrine — no single unqualified sentence captures reality, every claim is true only from a standpoint, reality outruns any one description — and then she said the thing I found genuinely annoying, which is that anekāntavāda is basically dialetheism with incense, because the third mode of the seven-fold scheme of conditioned predication says of one and the same thing that in some respect it is and in some respect it is not, and she, being one of those people who thinks some contradictions are true and that we should just switch to a logic where they don't blow everything up, reads that as a flat-out true contradiction. I'd like to watch that argument actually happen instead of having it summarized at me over drinks.

So: write me a dialogue, roughly 800 to 1100 words, between a Jain anekāntavādin who really holds the view (they should know nayavāda and syādvāda from the inside, not from a textbook blurb) and a dialetheist, stuck together in a dreary airport lounge after a conference panel on pluralism in the sciences — set it there, let it feel like two people who respect each other and have run out of patience. No strawmanning in either direction: the dialetheist should land real punches, and the Jain should have real answers rather than incense. Here's what I want them to fight over. Use my virus case as the worked example, and walk it through at least the first four modes of the seven-fold scheme — the is mode, the is-not mode, the is-and-is-not mode, and the inexpressible mode — with glosses, because I'm writing for general readers and I do not want a Sanskrit dump. The core question: is the third mode a genuine contradiction the Jain should just own, or only a contradiction in appearance because the two predications are indexed to different standpoints — and if it's the latter, what exactly stops anekāntavāda from collapsing into the trivial claim that it all depends, which is precisely the mush I started out suspicious of? I want the Jain to have to earn non-triviality out of the distinction between a genuine standpoint and a counterfeit one (I think the Sanskrit is nayābhāsa, but let them call it whatever you like), and to face the reflexivity problem head on: if no claim is true without qualification, is the doctrine itself only relatively true, or does it exempt itself, and either way why isn't that a self-inflicted wound? The dialetheist should get to press the points that actually hurt: that the inexpressible mode looks like her move anyway, that disambiguating everything away loses whatever made the original virology dispute feel like a real disagreement worth having, and that a view which never tells anyone what to do next is just a description of the mess. I've also been toying with a rescue of my own, borrowed from how physicists handle scale — effective theories at different resolutions, linked by an actual coarse-graining relation rather than just being different stories — and I want them to fight over whether requiring that genuine standpoints be related by a specifiable translation rule saves anekāntavāda or guts it.

Two more things. First, keep the three-way distinction sharp: whatever comes out of this, it shouldn't read as mere relativism, or as the lazy both-sides-ism I already know how to write myself, or as dialetheism with extra steps — the reader should be able to tell what would have to be the case for each. Second, don't give me a reading list, citations, or name-dropped sources; I have no way to check them from a hotel wifi and I'd have to cut them anyway, so work from the ideas alone and don't gesture at anything you'd need to look up. By the end I want to know what each of them would have to be shown to change their mind, what they concede, and — this is the bit I actually owe my editor — whether anything practical follows for a science writer or a journal editor staring at two labs with incompatible results, or whether many-sided truth just licenses everyone to keep talking past each other politely.
