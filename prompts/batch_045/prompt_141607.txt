I listen to a podcast about AI and work — the host is a genuinely well-read guy, does the left-econ thing, cites real numbers — and in a recent episode he gave what he called a two-minute version of Marx on automation. Roughly (I'm reconstructing from memory, so the wording isn't exact, but the claims are his):

"Marx basically thought machines were the good guys. Automation makes workers miserable only because capitalists own the machines; the same factories under workers' control would just shorten the working day and make everyone rich. His economics is what really matters though: value comes only from human labor, so when a firm replaces workers with machines it's sawing off the branch it's sitting on — surplus value is just unpaid labor time, and with no workers there's nothing left to appropriate. That's the famous tendency of the rate of profit to fall, and it's why Marx thought capitalism would break down on its own technology: the most automated industries would have the lowest profit rates, and eventually the whole system chokes. He works this out in the Fragment on Machines in the Grundrisse, which is about as close as the nineteenth century gets to predicting AI-driven mass unemployment."

Then his punchline: "So the joke is that every CEO racing to automate is speeding up the collapse Marx predicted."

A couple of things make me suspicious, but I don't know enough to say where he's wrong. One: the cheery framing doesn't match my memory of the machinery chapter in Capital, which I remember as pretty grim — longer hours, women and children, machines used as a weapon against strikes. Two: isn't the Grundrisse just his rough notebooks, not something he published or ever finished? Three, and this is the one I keep going back and forth on: I half-remember a result from the 1960s, I think by a Japanese economist, saying that if the real wage is held constant then any cost-cutting innovation actually raises the general rate of profit — i.e. the exact opposite of what this guy says Marx proved. Is that real or am I confabulating it? And if it's real, does Marx's law survive it, or is the law just dead?

So: go through his little speech and tell me what someone who actually works on Marx's value theory would accept, what they'd wince at as a simplification, and what's just wrong. I don't want a reading list — I want to know which claims break and why.

Also, here's the case I actually care about, because it's the one my brother-in-law keeps arguing about: he runs a plant that makes ceramic bearings, and they installed a line that runs essentially untended with two technicians where they used to have sixty machinists, while every competitor is still on conventional equipment. On Marx's own framework — not on intuition about supply and demand — what should we say about the value of what that plant produces, its profit rate compared to its competitors, and then what changes when the competitors catch up, and then when the whole economy has automated to the same degree? I want the answer to track the difference between those three steps, because I have a feeling the host is quietly getting the first step backwards by assuming "no direct labor" means "no profit." And then tell me whether his closing joke about CEOs hastening the collapse survives once you've fixed whatever needs fixing.
