After forty years as an analytical chemist in the pigments industry — most of that spent on colour measurement, calibration curves and inter-laboratory reproducibility studies — I retired three years ago and have since been sitting in on the philosophy of science reading group at the university, where I have got it into my head to give the Thursday colloquium paper in six weeks rather than the safe retrospective on colour science I was originally asked for. The thesis I want to defend is that Goodman's new riddle of induction is not a deeper and distinct difficulty layered on top of Hume's problem, but simply Hume's problem applied to our measuring procedures rather than to our observations, and that this becomes visible once you look at a case of the same formal shape that does not generate any paradox at all. The case is one I lived through: when my laboratory replaced its old scanning spectrophotometer with a diode-array instrument, we had for a while two colour vocabularies, and one could define a predicate, call it grapp, that applies to a panel just in case it is green when measured on the old machine and blue when measured on the new one; every panel in our archive was grapp, and grapp projected onto future panels just as well as green did, and yet nobody in the lab felt the smallest tremor of scepticism, because we could run the same set of reference panels through both instruments and build a conversion table. Grapp and green were cross-calibratable. Grue-with-respect-to-a-future-time is not: there is no shared sample across the transition, no conversion table, no way to check the predicate on both sides of the boundary before you have to stake everything on it. So my claim is that what makes green projectible is neither its entrenchment in English nor its carving at nature's joints, but the fact that the procedure for applying it is checkable across the positions over which we want to project — projectibility earned by the possibility of calibration, and the riddle deflated into the ordinary assumption that the future will resemble the past, applied one level up. I also want to draw the consequence I find bracing: a positional predicate whose positions are mutually calibratable raises no riddle, which suggests the interesting question is not which predicates are natural but which of our procedures can be brought into agreement with one another.

What I need from you is a Q&A preparation sheet, because the question period is twenty minutes and I am told the group does not suffer hand-waving, even courteous hand-waving. The room will be about twenty-five people: a formal epistemologist who does Bayesian confirmation theory and is likely to think the whole riddle is an artefact of talking about hypotheses in a language rather than about priors over a hypothesis space; a committed Humean who thinks the new riddle is the old riddle wearing a hat and will want to know what exactly I think I have added; a scientific realist who holds that projectibility tracks causal structure and natural kinds and will press me hard on whether I have made projectibility depend on us rather than on the world; a metaphysician working on laws and dispositions who will ask why the world could not simply contain a disposition that manifests as green before the relevant time and blue afterwards, so that grue is the real property and green the gerrymander; someone from the concepts-and-categorisation side who is sympathetic to Goodman's own entrenchment answer in a cognitive-science register and will say that I have quietly replaced entrenchment with our practices and inherited every problem that move has; and two or three graduate students who will go straight for the throat with some version of the mirror move, rebuilding my calibration criterion in a deviant vocabulary so that it selects grue instead of green, and who will not be placated by an appeal to what we in fact do. Please give me roughly eight objections, ordered by expected pain rather than by elegance — where expected pain means how likely the question is times how much damage it does if I fumble it — each labelled with the kind of person who will ask it and phrased the way that person would actually put it, with a plain-English gloss in parentheses whenever their idiom is one I would not naturally use. For each: what I should concede, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes standing up without notes, and one line on where that reply is soft. I would also like your judgement on which one or two of these I should concede pre-emptively during the talk itself rather than be seen to be backed into during questions, and I want you to name the objection I am hoping nobody asks — my own candidate is that grue is of course perfectly testable the moment the critical time passes, and that if my calibration story only says we cannot calibrate in advance, it has collapsed back into Hume and I have added nothing. Finally, two practical things: a short glossary of the terms I must not misuse on the night (entrenchment, projectible, positional predicate, confirmation function, natural kind, disposition and its manifestation, and any others that will come up), since I can derive a calibration curve but cannot follow a discussion conducted in confirmation-theoretic notation; and an explicit note on where my laboratory analogy breaks down faster than I think it does, because a chemist waving a colour-science anecdote at philosophers is exactly the sort of thing that will be taken apart politely and at length. Please keep everything at the level of argument rather than of citation: I cannot look anything up and I do not want to be sent to a literature I cannot read.
