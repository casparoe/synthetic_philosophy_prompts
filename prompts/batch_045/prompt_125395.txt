my friends keep using the "gender-equality paradox" as a conversation-ender — the finding as they tell it is that the more gender-egalitarian a country gets, the fewer women end up in STEM — and the analogy they always reach for is that it's basically a natural experiment, like a free market: strip away the external barriers and what's left is people's real preferences, the way consumer demand reveals what people actually want once prices and budgets are what they are. conclusion: the remaining gap is nature showing through, and theres nothing left here for justice to complain about.

I'm suspicious but can't say exactly where it breaks. One of them even offered a mechanism: generous welfare states make the female-typed career path cheap (long parental leave, easy part-time, big flexible public sector), so in exactly the countries we call most egalitarian the price of taking the male-typed path goes up — meaning you'd predict a wider gap there even if underlying preferences were identical.

Can you audit the market analogy properly? what maps onto what — the budget, the price, the preference, the "revealed" bit — then where the mapping breaks and which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion they want. does a revealed-preference framing even have the resources to tell apart "wanted it all along" from "adapted to a choice set that was never neutral" or "responding to a price she doesn't pay equally"? and is the conclusion riding on quietly equating egalitarian with neutral and chosen with natural? if the analogy cant carry it, is there a better one, or is the honest answer that justice is about the shape of the choice set rather than the outcome?
