I'm part of a small study group that is preparing background papers for a constitutional-reform commission, and I've been asked to produce one document that both factions in the group could actually sign: the sortitionists, who want a chamber selected by lot from the adult population for a single short non-renewable term, and the electoralists, who want to keep an elected chamber but repair it (term limits, stricter rules on post-office employment, publicly financed campaigns). They have now argued past each other for several sessions, and I don't want another document that just lists both sets of talking points. What I want is the text of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between them: the claims both can sign about what elections and lots each reliably deliver, the residual disagreements stated precisely enough and neutrally enough that each side would accept the wording of its own position, and then an explicit crux list with a pre-registered prediction from each side. Please do not adjudicate: I'm not asking which side is right, and the paper should not read as a verdict.

One thing I want the document to really test is an argument I've been circulating in the group under the name of the unpredictability dividend. The thought is that a lot-selected member is identified only at the moment of selection and cannot stand for a further term, so there is no ex ante market in her future votes: no one can buy a promise from a person who cannot yet be named and who has no electoral future to protect, whereas a term-limited elected official still needs party-list placement, campaign money, and a post-office career, so term limits may merely relocate the influence market rather than shrink it. Our electoralist replies that sortition removes the retrospective sanction without replacing it, and that amateurs with no mandate will defer to the permanent expert secretariat, so the capture is not eliminated but moved inside the building. I'd like that exchange turned into a properly formulated crux rather than two speeches — and if you think it is really two or three separate questions (about ex ante influence-buying, about retrospective sanction, about staff versus donor capture), split it and say how they come apart. I also suspect at least one of our disputes is partly verbal — I think we may be using accountability and capture in two different senses each — and I'd like you to identify which part is merely about words and why, and which part survives the re-description.

Please format the result as, first, a numbered section of agreed claims, each one substantive rather than platitudinous; second, a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the formulation the sortitionist would accept, the formulation the electoralist would accept, whether the disagreement is verbal or substantive, and the kind of consideration both would grant bears on it; and third, a short predictions ledger naming, for each substantive crux, one concrete institutional pilot or thought-experimental case both sides accept as diagnostic, together with what each side predicts in advance about how it will come out and what it would concede if it came out the other way. Where a consideration would have to be an empirical result about existing citizens' assemblies or actual lobbying rules that you can't state with confidence, prefer a designed hypothetical pilot instead. This is a working paper for the group, so no citations, bibliographies, or references to the literature are wanted — the arguments should stand on their own.
