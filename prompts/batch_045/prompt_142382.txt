Our reading group is half ML people, half philosophers, and we've spent the whole term arguing about Goodharts law, and I want to close the last session with a poem instead of another summary. Write me a sestina: six stanzas of six, end-words rotating the usual way, three-line envoi using all six. End-words something like measure, target, good, world, game, trust — swap in better ones if youve got them — and the thing I want the form to do is this: the words never change, but what they mean drifts, so that by the fifth stanza "measure" means whatever the proxy happens to track and "good" means whatever pushes the number up. Stable sign, sliding referent. That is the poem.

Don't do it as one undifferentiated blob though — walk it through the different ways this goes wrong: picking the top of a noisy ranking and watching it fall back; pushing far outside the range anybody ever checked the measure in; moving the number without moving the thing you cared about; someone noticing the metric and adapting to it. I also want a stanza where nobody is gaming anything and the measure still rots — the population shifts, or the prediction was self-defeating — because half the room says that isn't Goodhart at all but something closer to the Lucas critique, and I'd rather the poem stage that fight than settle it. And one stanza where the gaming is informative: the loophole-finder has told you something true about your metric.

Then a short paragraph, not an essay, on where you cheated: which reading of Goodhart you leaned on, which failure modes you collapsed into each other, and which parts are basically arithmetic versus which are genuinely contested among people who actually work on this.
