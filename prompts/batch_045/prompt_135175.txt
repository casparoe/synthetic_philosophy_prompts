Putting together where I've landed on the mental illness debate for a departmental talk and I want a coherence check before the Q&A eats me alive. Here's the set, roughly:

1. Szasz was basically right in the 1961 book: mental illness isn't a disease in any literal sense, it's a label we stick on conduct and experience we disapprove of or can't make sense of. Not more of a disease than being a jerk is.
2. I'm a thoroughgoing materialist about the mind. Every mental event is a brain event, no exceptions, nothing spooky left over.
3. The line I actually want to draw: X is a disease only if there's a specifiable abnormality at the level of the organ or tissue, the kind of thing a pathologist could in principle point to. So Alzheimers, Huntingtons, a tumour pushing on the frontal lobe — diseases. Grief, problem gambling, most of what gets diagnosed as depression or anxiety — not diseases, whatever else they are.
4. We should scrap both the insanity defence and involuntary commitment. The law treats everyone as a responsible agent, full stop.
5. Addiction isn't a disease, but I do think addicted people's agency is genuinely compromised and that they're less blameworthy than the folk view has it.
6. The state should fund therapy, counselling, crisis houses and housing-first programmes on the same footing as cancer treatment, and the main reason is simply that the people using them are suffering.
7. Guy in my running club — call him D. — got a left frontal tumour in his forties, started harassing people at work in a way totally out of character, got convicted, tumour came out, behaviour stopped dead. I think he shouldn't have been convicted. And I think that if he'd refused the surgery while being a danger to people, we should have been able to make him have it, the way you can quarantine someone with an infectious disease.
8. I'm fine with kids getting ADHD and autism diagnoses so they can get extra time in exams and other accomodations, and I think the diagnosis is doing real work there — more than just being a bureaucratic key to a door.
9. I've been on sertraline for six years and it clearly does something. I don't think that's evidence either way.

The thing my supervisor keeps picking at is 1 and 2 together, which I think is a misreading, but I've been wrong before.
