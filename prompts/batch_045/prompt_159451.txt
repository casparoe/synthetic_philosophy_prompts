I've more or less settled on anti-intellectualism about knowledge-how: knowing how to φ isn't a matter of knowing *that* some way w is a way to φ, not even under a "practical mode of presentation" or whatever else the intellectualist adds to make the proposition feel action-guiding. It's just having the relevant ability. Tell me what should move me off it.

The case I keep chewing on is invented, but it's doing most of the work for me: suppose skill uploads are possible. A novice gets an expert's motor package installed, plays the etude flawlessly on the first try, and can't articulate a single thing about how she's doing it. Now variant B: same upload, except the implant also carries a compressed instruction manual that she has no access to, never consults, and which plays no causal role in her playing. If she knows how in both variants, intellectualism is in trouble; if the presence of the manual seems to matter at all, then something propositional is doing work the ability story can't account for. I'd like to know how much weight my own reaction there should get.

So: give me defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including the ones a committed anti-intellectualist would herself concede as counting against the view rather than just what critics say. I'm thinking of things like the paralyzed-pianist style case (ability gone, knowledge-how plausibly retained), the fact that knowledge-how is gradable in a way knowing-that supposedly isn't, the way knowledge-how explains successful action, and the question of whether infants, animals, or a simple trained system can know how at all. And be straight with me about whether "ability" has been stretched — abilities to φ under ideal conditions, to recognize correct φ-ing, to imagine φ-ing — until the view can't actually lose any case, and whether that's a real problem or just what a good theory looks like once it's been stress-tested.
