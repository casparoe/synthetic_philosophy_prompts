For the past two years I have convened a monthly reading group, Explanation and Its Limits, in a back room of our public library: fourteen or so members, nearly all retired, including three ex-engineers of various kinds, a former GP, a retired accountant who can always be relied on to ask what a thing is *for*, and one man who reads ahead and will try to break whatever I put in front of him. I have ninety minutes, one long table, no screen and no whiteboard, and whatever I can carry in a box; I can make things with card stock, a craft knife and a glue gun, and I would rather not spend real money. Before I retired I was an experimental biologist — controlled-environment physiology, factorial designs, analysis of variance — and it is that training, more than anything I have read since, that has made the distinction we mishandled last month feel sharp to me.

The session that went badly was on the vocabulary of cranes and skyhooks, which I met years ago in a popular book and can no longer place, together with the companion complaint about greedy reductionism. Two failures. Half the room concluded that any appeal to a higher-level pattern — the leaf arrangement tracks the light, the animal does that in order to escape — is a skyhook unless and until it is cashed out at the bottom, which left them unable to say why the higher-level description had been useful in the first place. The other half concluded that asking for a mechanism at all is a philistine tic of the hard sciences, and became quite attached to explanations of the form the system is organised so as to. Nobody felt greedy reductionism as a vice; going all the way down struck them as simply more rigorous, the way more decimal places is more precise. What I want them to feel is that the test of an explanation is whether it does work, that work means predicting how the thing will behave under interventions nobody has tried yet, and that a description at the lowest available level can be *worse* than a coarser one precisely because it carries along detail that does no predictive work and so throws away the generality that made the pattern worth explaining.

Here is the shape of the idea I would like you to build a game around, and you should feel free to improve on it or replace it. In a factorial experiment one looks for the level at which a factor has a main effect while variation in the finer-grained factors beneath it contributes only negligible interaction; an explanation that is obliged to drag the fine detail along with it is one where that detail has effects of its own, and otherwise dragging it along is a cost, not a virtue. So: construct an activity in which participants must produce descriptions of some system, pay something for the information those descriptions require, and are then scored only on predictions about cases they have not seen — with the cases arranged so that (i) an over-specific bottom-level description fits the observed cases perfectly and fails the unseen ones, (ii) a mid-level mechanism description costs little and transfers, and (iii) a merely redescriptive or purpose-shaped story costs nothing and transfers not at all. I want a second round in which the load-bearing difference sits one level *below* the comfortable middle, so that the group is punished for having learned that coarser is always better; without that, the activity will merely have replaced one prejudice with its opposite. Give me the physical setup with a materials list I can actually assemble, the rules, the cost and payoff scheme using counters rather than money, any roles you think the group should be assigned, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes including roughly twenty-five at the end for talking.

Two further things. First, the debrief: map specific moments you expect to occur onto the specific errors they illustrate, and tell me what to say when the man who reads ahead points out — as I think he will — that the bad explanation is not a skyhook at all but simply a bad hypothesis, and that I have built a game about overfitting and dressed it up in philosophical vocabulary; I would like an answer to that which is honest rather than reassuring. Tell me also what will predictably go wrong on the first run and how to handle it. Second, and this matters to me most: be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce. I can see one problem myself — my props will be artefacts that I designed and assembled, so for them it is literally true that someone arranged them that way on purpose, which is the very answer I need to discredit, and I suspect the game will quietly reward design-talk in a way that no corresponding game about organisms or ecosystems would. Say how you would handle that, or whether you would let it stand and break it in the second round. I also suspect the scoring rule can only ever test prediction, never whether a description identifies the mechanism that actually produces the behaviour, and I would like you to say plainly if that is a limit the facilitator simply has to state at the end rather than something the game can show.

Please commit: where you have a choice — the scoring rule, the physical system, two rounds or three, whether to name the ideas at all — make it, and give me your reason, rather than laying out the alternatives and leaving me to choose; I would rather argue with one definite design than be handed a menu, and I would be grateful if you flagged the single choice you are least confident about. And please do not send me to further reading: the library closed its philosophy shelves some years ago and I have no institutional access, so anything that depends on a citation is of no use to me.
