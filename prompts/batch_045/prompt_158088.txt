I am helping to write a resolution-ready version of a claim for a long-horizon forecasting platform, and what I need from you is a number and the reasoning behind it — not a survey of the literature and not a reading list. The claim under consideration is this:

**P.** The rate at which human beings intentionally inflicted severe physical suffering on other human beings, weighted by intensity and duration and expressed per capita, was lower in the decade 2010–2019 than in the decade 1750–1759.

Take the empirical backdrop as stipulated: the well-recorded indicators of lethal interpersonal violence, in the parts of the world for which long series exist, fell substantially between the early modern period and the present, with the twentieth century containing large exceptions driven by industrialised warfare. I do not want that backdrop re-litigated. What I want to know is how much uncertainty about P survives once the backdrop is granted.

Give me your credence in P, decomposed. The terms I currently have in mind are the following; add any you think belong, and drop any you think collapse into another.

1. **Inference.** How much confidence the surviving evidentiary base — court and coroners' records, skeletal evidence, police statistics, victimisation surveys — supports even the well-measured part of the trend, given that recording practices, the definition of a reportable event, and the social visibility of different classes of victim all changed over the interval in question.

2. **Proxy scope.** Whether the quantities we measure well, homicide above all, track the quantities we measure badly: non-lethal assault, violence within households, sexual violence, violence against children, violence inside institutions.

3. **Displacement.** Whether a large share of the apparent decline consists not in violence failing to occur but in violence being replaced by standing, non-violent coercion — confinement, surveillance, policing, chemical restraint, architectural and legal design — so that the capacity to inflict suffering is retained while its exercise becomes unnecessary. Call the sum of realised violence and violence merely held in reserve the latent violence budget. The question is how far the headline decline should be discounted on the grounds that the budget did not shrink even though the drawdown did.

4. **Category substitution.** Whether harms that would earlier have counted as intentional and interpersonal now appear as unintentional or self-inflicted ones — traffic, industrial injury, addiction — and whether the intentional/unintentional boundary is tracking a real difference in what matters rather than doing argumentative work.

5. **Commensurability of the unit.** Whether severe physical suffering, weighted by intensity and duration, is comparable across persons and epochs at all, given that suffering is felt and reported through concepts that are themselves historically conditioned, and that treatment which was ordinary discipline in one century is battery in another.

6. **The denominator.** Whether per capita is the right normalisation, as against per person-year, per waking year, per lifetime, or the proportion of lives substantially touched — and whether the comparison inverts under some of these, given much larger populations and much longer lives.

7. **Tail versus mean.** Whether P is a claim about the average exposure or about the right tail, since a large fall in the mean is compatible with a roughly constant number of people whose lives are dominated by violence.

8. **Determinacy.** Whether P has a truth value at all, or is instead the sort of claim whose answer varies with reasonable operationalisation with no further fact of the matter, so that the honest response is a distribution over precisifications rather than a single number about the world.

For each term give a rough probability, or a rough size of effect where a probability is the wrong object, with one line of justification. Then combine them into a single number for P, state explicitly how you are combining them, and identify the term the final number is most sensitive to — the sub-question which, if settled either way, would move you furthest.

Two further requests. First: my own credence in P is about 0.6 and a colleague's is about 0.25, and we do not disagree about the empirical trend in homicide. Tell me where you think that gap most likely lives — which of the terms above, or which term you would add, best accounts for two people who agree on the data and disagree about the claim. Second: tell me what single piece of evidence or argument would move your number most, and by how much. I am not asking you for sources or citations; I am asking what such evidence would have to show, and I want it to be the sort of thing one could in principle have.
