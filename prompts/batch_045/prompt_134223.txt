You are a professional philosopher who works on decision theory and the philosophy of action, and I would like you to think carefully before you commit to anything: begin by sketching for yourself, privately, the candidate diagnoses of the impasse I describe below — a verbal dispute, a hidden empirical or formal question, two questions sharing one name, a clash of bedrock intuitions, an under-specified thought experiment — and test each against what the two sides actually claim, discarding the ones that do not survive that contact, and only then write your answer.

Here is the case, stated so that nothing has to be looked up. There are two boxes. Box A is transparent and contains a visible one thousand dollars. Box B is opaque and contains either a million dollars or nothing, and which it contains was already decided by a predictor, on the basis of a prediction of your choice: a million if the predictor predicted you would take only box B, nothing if it predicted you would take both. You may take only B or both boxes, and you do not know what was predicted. Two principles pull apart. The dominance argument says: whatever is in B is fixed by now, so in either state of the world taking both boxes leaves you a thousand dollars better off, and you should take both. The expected-utility argument says: since the prediction is very likely to match what you do, taking only B makes the million overwhelmingly probable, and you should take only B. Standard decision theory splits along the same line: the causal version evaluates acts by what would follow from performing them, intervening on the act and holding fixed what the act does not cause, and so two-boxes; the evidential version evaluates acts by how probable outcomes are conditional on them, and so one-boxes. I know this much, and I do not need it rehearsed at length; use your space on the diagnosis.

The question is why this has not moved in more than fifty years. The puzzle was devised by a physicist and handed to philosophers by Nozick in 1969, and a survey of professional philosophers taken in 2020 found a modest plurality for taking both boxes, roughly 39 percent against roughly 31 percent for taking only one, with the remainder undecided or opting out. In my own field — I spent close to forty years in experimental physics, mostly precision measurement, and have only recently begun sitting in on a philosophy reading group — a question that stays open for fifty years with the same arguments on both sides is a reliable sign of one of three things: the question is under-specified, there is no conceivable test that would settle it, or there are two questions under one name. Which is it here, and if more than one, which is doing most of the work?

My own move, which I would like you to take seriously and then criticise, is to bring the predictor down from infallible to fallible, because the limit case strikes me as doing rhetorical work it does not deserve. Suppose the predictor is right 70 percent of the time. If its accuracy is symmetric — the chance it predicts one-boxing given that you one-box equals the chance it predicts two-boxing given that you two-box, both 0.7 — then I make one-boxing worth an expected 700,000 dollars against 301,000 for two-boxing, and the two come level at an accuracy of 50.05 percent, a hair above chance. Please check that arithmetic and correct me if I have it wrong; I would rather be corrected than agreed with. Two consequences interest me, and I want you to press on both. The first is that noise does not dissolve the dispute at all: the dominance argument is conditional on what is already in box B and therefore survives at any accuracy whatsoever, so that relaxing infallibility leaves one principle saying two-box for every accuracy and the other saying one-box for every accuracy above a threshold near chance. The disagreement gets starker, not softer. The second is that the single number '70 percent accurate' does not determine anything that matters: what matters is the joint distribution over predictions and acts, and a predictor whose errors are asymmetric — one that over-predicts two-boxing, for instance — can be 70 percent accurate overall while having a conditional structure on which two-boxing is the better bet. So part of the stall may simply be that 'the predictor' is under-specified, and that each side has been tacitly filling in a different mechanism: a common cause in your prior state, a simulation that runs ahead of you, an infallible being whose foreknowledge seems to make your option 'get the million' unavailable.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the dispute is a proxy war over whether reasons for action track causal structure or information — whether the correct input to a decision is what would happen if I did this, holding fixed everything my act does not cause, or what is likely to be true given that I do this — and that 'rational' is doing double duty, meaning both sensible-here-and-now and what one would have been wise to arrange in advance to be the sort of agent who does. My physicist's instinct is that these are two questions asked at two different times and that conflating them is most of the puzzle. But I can see three ways to be wrong, and I want you to adjudicate among them: that this is only a cheaper way of saying what the algorithmic or functional versions of decision theory say when they relocate the unit of evaluation from the act-token to the decision procedure, in which case I have relabelled rather than diagnosed; that underneath the noise there is a genuine formal disagreement, about which quantity a theory of rational choice is even permitted to take as input, which my fallible version displays but does nothing to resolve; or that the real work is being done by the causal story about the predictor that neither side has specified, so that the debate is in effect a debate about which mechanism we are imagining while pretending to argue about principles.

One further thing I would like settled, because it bears on whether the impasse is empirical in disguise. The game could be run for real with a predictor of merely human accuracy — a statistical model trained on a person's past choices, say — and the one-boxers would very likely come away richer. My sense is that this would settle nothing, because the disagreement concerns what one ought to do rather than what tends to happen, and because whichever side ran the experiment would read the result as confirmation. Is that right, and what is the argument for it?

Given the diagnosis you settle on, tell me what progress in this debate would actually consist of: a distinction that separates the questions, a formal result that shows one side's input to be admissible or not, an empirical finding, an abandonding of the question in favour of a better one — or tell me that no progress should be expected, and why that verdict is not merely a counsel of despair. Say plainly where your diagnosis is silent and where you are guessing.
