So I've ended up on the committee at the hospital that decides how a fixed pot of donated money gets spent each quarter — my mum was on it for years, she got sick last winter, I took her seat and then never got off it — and we've got a vote in about three weeks that I can already tell is going to go the way the last six went, which is nowhere. The case: we can spend the money on one course of treatment that gives a four-year-old a shot at a basically normal life, or we can put the same money into a year of a clinic that gives roughly three hundred people with chronic breathlessness a small but real improvement each. The QALY arithmetic comes out more or less even, within the margins people are willing to squabble over. Half the committee says: totals are what matter, and if the totals are within noise then break the tie on something else, cost, whatever. The other half says: no, it matters that the benefit lands on three hundred separate people and the burden of skipping the clinic lands on three hundred people while the benefit of funding the kid lands on one, and there's something about taking each person seperately that adding up just loses. Nobody has moved in two years. And I've read enough of the philosophy to know the literature version of this fight — the complaint that utilitarianism doesnt take seriously the distinction between persons, Williams on integrity, Nagel on agent-relative versus agent-neutral reasons, Parfit pushing back on the person-affecting restriction, that old Taurek paper saying the numbers shouldn't count at all — has been running since the 1970s with the same shape and no resolution either, which makes me think the stall is structural rather than a failure of imagination.

Here's my hypothesis, and I want you to take it apart. Part one: nobody on my committee is a pure aggregator and nobody is a pure anti-aggregator — last quarter the spread option had clearly more total and even the splittists voted for it — so what we're actually fighting about is the exchange rate between per-person size and headcount, how much total you're allowed to give up in order to spread it around, and all the grand arguments about persons are stories we tell afterwards to dress up different settings of that one number. Part two: I noticed my own reaction shifts with a detail that shouldn't matter. When I imagine the three hundred patients never meeting and never comparing notes, my pull toward funding the kid goes up. When I imagine them all in the same waiting room, able to see that each of them got a crumb while one child could have been cured, it goes down. So maybe part of the intuition that persons are separate is really an intuition about complaint and comparison, not about persons at all.

So: what kind of stalemate is this? A verbal dispute, a bedrock moral disagreement neither side can argue for, a hidden empirical question about what our intuitions are tracking, or two different questions under one heading — is a committee, or a morality, supposed to make the world better, or supposed to be justifiable to each person seperately? Am I right that a single exchange-rate parameter is doing the work, and is the waiting-room version of the case diagnostic or just a confound? Ground it in what each side actually says rather than giving me a survey, and then tell me honestly whether anything — a distinction, some empirical work on what people's intuitions track, a formal result, a change of question — could actually move it, or whether I should accept that we're going to keep voting four-four. Please write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, like an email, and give me the diagnosis in the first couple of sentences instead of building up to it.
