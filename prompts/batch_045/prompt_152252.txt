I've been working through a semi-popular overview of Bayesian confirmation theory — one of those short, confident books that compress a whole literature into a chapter — and it gives an account of the problem of old evidence that I neither fully trust nor know how to fault. Before I build anything on it, I'd like an accuracy audit. I should say up front that I have no library or database access where I am, so please argue the points out rather than telling me whom to read: I can't chase references.

The passage is this (copied out, slightly compressed, but otherwise as it stands):

"Bayesian confirmation theory says that evidence E confirms hypothesis H just in case P(H|E) exceeds P(H): the evidence raises the probability of the hypothesis. It also says that learning E is always to be modeled as conditionalization on E. Put those together and you get the problem of old evidence. Conditionalizing on something you already fully believe cannot change your credences at all — if P(E) = 1, then P(H|E) = P(H) — so it cannot raise your confidence in H. Yet scientists constantly cite evidence that everyone had already accepted: the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion had been on the books for half a century before general relativity predicted it, and it is still counted among the theory's strongest supports. Bayesianism is thus forced to say that evidence which is already certain confirms nothing, which is absurd. The standard repair, proposed in the 1980s, is to conditionalize not on E but on the newly learned fact that H has E as a consequence. You keep your credence in E at 1, but you are not certain in advance that H entails E, so you assign the implication H ⊃ E a probability short of 1, and conditionalizing on that implication raises the probability of H. The repair works. Its moral is that the problem of old evidence is really just the problem of logical omniscience in miniature: Bayesian agents are assumed to know everything that follows from what they believe, and once you drop that assumption the paradox evaporates. A second moral follows for the partisan debate. The problem afflicts only subjective Bayesians, who model the actual opinions of an actual person. Objective Bayesians, who fix their priors by symmetry and maximum-entropy constraints rather than by consulting anyone's opinion, are immune, because whatever evidence there is has already been priced into the prior at exactly the value it ought to have."

What I want, claim by claim, is what a careful Bayesian would accept, what they would call an allowable simplification, and what they would reject — and then, separately, whether either of the two closing "morals" follows from the parts that survive. Begin with the first sentence: is the positive-relevance criterion, offered there as the definition of confirmation, one a defender would endorse, and does anything later in the argument actually depend on which measure of confirmation you choose?

Four things I am stuck on.

First, the repair. When I try to execute it on paper I get stuck at once. If E has probability 1 for me, and H ⊃ E is the material conditional ¬H ∨ E, then the probability calculus seems to force P(H ⊃ E) = 1 as well, since the conditional is entailed by E alone; in which case "learning" it changes nothing and the repair is empty. The only way I can see to get the repair off the ground is to let the agent be probabilistically incoherent — to assign E probability 1 while assigning the material conditional something less — but then the agent is not a probability function, and it is obscure to me what "conditionalizing" is even supposed to mean. So: is the passage skipping a step, or have I misdescribed the move? And if the move genuinely requires a non-standard updating rule for a logically non-omniscient agent, does that alter what sort of solution it is and what it can be credited with?

Second, the identification of the problem of old evidence with the problem of logical omniscience. That strikes me as too quick, and here is the case that makes me think so. I already know for certain that the pavement outside is wet — I looked. Someone now proposes a new hypothesis: it rained overnight. That hypothesis does not entail wet pavement (a street-cleaner or a burst main would do), but it makes wetness more likely, and I already knew that too, long before the hypothesis was proposed. (a) Does the wet pavement confirm the hypothesis that it rained? Intuitively yes, and P(rain | wet) > P(rain) looks like exactly the judgment confirmation theory exists to vindicate. (b) Is this an instance of the problem of old evidence? It seems to be, twice over: conditionalizing on wetness is a no-op, and I think the Bayes factor collapses as well, since P(wet) = 1 forces P(wet | X) = 1 for anything X to which I give positive probability, so the likelihood ratio P(wet | rain) / P(wet | ¬rain) equals 1. (c) But nothing in the case turns on logical omniscience. There is no unrecognized entailment for me to discover, and a repair that works by conditionalizing on H ⊃ E has no foothold, because rain does not entail wet pavement. So is the passage's first moral simply mistaken, or does my case stop counting as a problem of old evidence once the problem is correctly formulated? And if I have the collapse of the likelihood ratio right, is that a second and independent version of the difficulty rather than a restatement of the first?

Third, the claim about objective Bayesians. I cannot tell whether "already priced into the prior at exactly the value it ought to have" describes a solution or restates the problem. If the prior already reflects E completely, then there is apparently no work left for E to do as evidence, which is the difficulty rather than a way around it; and since maximum-entropy priors are standardly fixed by constraints supplied by the evidence, the objective Bayesian looks like someone guaranteed to be in the P(E) = 1 situation. Is there any version of objective Bayesianism for which the passage's claim is true, or is it backwards?

Fourth, my own attempt at a diagnosis, which I would like tested rather than encouraged. It seems to me that what is genuinely new when a hypothesis is first proposed is often not a proposition but a hypothesis space: before anyone thought of H my credences were spread over {H₁, …, Hₙ}, afterwards they range over {H₁, …, Hₙ, H}, and nothing in my old credence function determines how much of the enlarged space H is owed, or how the old evidence is to be redistributed across it. On this view the problem of old evidence is not about old evidence at all; it is the problem of the priors for new hypotheses in disguise, and it survives every repair that stays inside a fixed space. Three things to check. (i) Does it handle the wet-pavement case, where no entailment is discovered and no likelihood changes? (ii) Is it a real proposal, or a redescription that merely relocates the freedom — the freedom to fix a prior over an enlarged space — from where the problem was to somewhere less visible? (iii) Does it make confirmation objectionably historical, so that whether E confirms H for me can turn on when someone happened to think of H, or on which rivals happened to be on the table? I suspect (iii) is the deepest objection, and I would like it stated in its strongest form together with the best available reply.

One methodological question underneath all of this, which you may prefer to answer first. The whole tangle may be a conflation of two questions: the synchronic one, whether E is evidence for H relative to what I now believe, and the diachronic one, whether my coming to accept H can be modeled as a sequence of Bayesian updates. Bayesianism may have a good answer to the first and a gap in its answer to the second. If so, does that dissolve the problem of old evidence or merely relocate it — and is the dissolution available to someone who thinks the point of confirmation theory is to guide inquiry rather than to grade finished bodies of belief?
