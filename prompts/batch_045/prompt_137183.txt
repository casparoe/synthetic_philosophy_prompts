I'm a journalist — I write a column on science and ideas — and I'm stuck on something I definately should have thought through before pitching it.

The piece is about what happens when a founding document and the institution that grew out of it come apart: charters versus the things they charter. And the case I keep coming back to is Frederick Douglass on the US Constitution. Here are two chunks from an 1860 speech he gave in Britain (usually printed as "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?"):

Thus, for instance, the American Government and the American Constitution are spoken of in a manner which would naturally lead the hearer to believe that one is identical with the other; when the truth is, they are distinct in character as is a ship and a compass. The one may point right and the other steer wrong. A chart is one thing, the course of the vessel is another. The Constitution may be right, the Government is wrong. If the Government has been governed by mean, sordid, and wicked passions, it does not follow that the Constitution is mean, sordid, and wicked.

And later:

It should also be borne in mind that the intentions of those who framed the Constitution, be they good or bad, for slavery or against slavery, are so respected so far, and so far only, as we find those intentions plainly stated in the Constitution. It would be the wildest of absurdities, and lead to endless confusion and mischiefs, if, instead of looking to the written paper itself, for its meaning, it were attempted to make us search it out, in the secret motives, and dishonest intentions, of some of the men who took part in writing it. It was what they said that was adopted by the people, not what they were ashamed or afraid to say, and really omitted to say.

Things I can't work out on my own:

1. What is the ship/compass/chart bit actually *doing* argumentatively? Is it just a metaphor for "the text isn't to blame for the users," or is there a stronger claim hiding in it — something about what a charter is supposed to be *for*?

2. The second passage lays down a rule: framers' intentions count only insofar as they're plainly stated in the document. Is that a principled textualism, or is it a forensic move he's forced into by the fact that the Philadelphia convention met behind closed doors? And does the argument bottom out in a claim about *semantics* (what the words mean) or about *ratification* (who adopted what, and what they could be said to have consented to)? Those seem like very different arguments to me, and I can't tell which one he needs.

3. The bit I really want to get right: how does his own rule handle the clauses that pretty plainly gesture at slavery — the three-fifths clause, the one about persons held to service or labour being delivered up, the one barring Congress from ending the importation trade until some date in the 1800s? I want the strongest version of his answer under his own stated method, and then the strongest objection to it. Not "well, it's obviously motivated reasoning" — I've read that take and it feels lazy. Show me where the method does real work and where it gives out.

4. The analogy I want to use, and which I suspect quietly begs the question: Douglass is doing what a good engineer does when she seperates a system's written specification from the system actually deployed — the spec can be sound while the build is a disaster. But a spec only constrains anything if somebody can audit the build against it, and (here's my worry) his reading of the Constitution seems unfalsifiable until the text itself gets amended. I want to call this "charter drift" in the column. Is that a fair label, or does it smuggle in the conclusion that the document never constrained anything? Also: a compass is supposed to *steer* the ship, but his rule seems to make the compass's reading independent of the course the vessel actually takes. Is that a contradiction, or are those two claims living on different levels and I'm just confusing myself?

One more: once slavery is actually abolished and the text is amended, does his 1860 argument become trivially true, or does it get *replaced* by something else? I keep flip-flopping on this and I only have 900 words.
