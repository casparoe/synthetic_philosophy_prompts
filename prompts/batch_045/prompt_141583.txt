I'm a postdoc in a systems neuroscience lab, and something happened at group meeting a couple months back that I can't stop chewing on. We have two competing models of how a circuit in the prep I work on holds a persistent firing state — the recurrent-loop story and the intrinsic-current story. Both fit every dataset we can currently produce, and I mean every one, with comparably good fits. Someone said, more or less: they're empirically equivalent, so there's no fact of the matter about which mechanism is actually doing the work. I said of course there's a fact of the matter, we just can't get at it with the preparation we have, which is why we're building the one that could distinguish them. Nobody moved. So I went and read the philosophy to find out who was right, and as far as I can tell the grown-ups have been having exactly this argument for about forty years without moving either.

The shape of it as I've picked it up: one side produces cases where the evidence can't decide between incompatible theories — the cooked-up rivals, the contrived alternatives, the conventionalist stuff about geometry, and then the more recent historical move, look how often scientists had all the evidence in hand and still never conceived of the theory that turned out to be right. The other side says those cases are gerrymandered and ad hoc, that empirical equivalence isn't a stable relation anyway (it depends what auxiliary assumptions you're bundling in and when you ask), and that at bottom the whole thing is ordinary skepticism about induction in a lab coat, a problem for everything and therefore no special problem for science. Every move is on the table, everyone knows every move, nothing moves.

So diagnose it, don't referee it. Is this a substantive disagreement or a verbal one about what 'empirically equivalent' means? Are the two sides quietly indexing that phrase to different things — data we happen to have, data we could get with instruments that exist, data anyone could ever get — because in my lab that choice is doing all the work? Do they differ about who bears the burden of proof, or about which cases count as data? Is there a hidden empirical question about the history of science carrying more weight than anyone admits? Or are they answering two different questions under one heading: one about what we should believe now, one about whether we should keep looking?

My hypothesis, for what it's worth: the argument is stuck because it's being asked to do two jobs at once, and only one of them is actually supported by the interesting cases. Tell me if that's right or if I've misdescribed it. And given your diagnosis, what would progress even look like — a distinction, a formal result, some way of actually settling the historical question — or is this one where no progress should be expected, and why. Please don't give me a survey of positions; I've read enough of those and they're what made me think it's stuck.
