I am a novelist, and I would be grateful for your help with a question I have not been able to settle. I have a philosophical suspicion and a financial interest, and I would like the two pried apart.

Please answer as a political philosopher who has also advised legislatures on copyright and whose narrower specialism is the justification of original acquisition — what makes it permissible for someone to take something that belonged to no one, and what it takes for a distribution of holdings to count as fair. Think carefully, and before you write anything I am meant to read, spend a few paragraphs listing the framings you considered and setting aside the ones that do not fit. I would rather see your rejected options than a smooth answer.

The facts are not in dispute.

Fourteen years ago I wrote a fantasy novel and posted it, a chapter a week, on a serialization site, for nothing. Above the first chapter I put a note inviting readers to set their own stories in my world and to sell those stories if anyone would buy them. The novel was read by perhaps forty thousand people. Over the following years several hundred readers did write in that world. Three of them built large audiences doing it — paid newsletters, a licensed card game, a subscription wiki — and now earn a good living from material set in a place I invented and then explicitly left open. My invitation was never revoked.

Six years ago a small production company took an option on the screen and merchandise rights for a flat fee. I had a lawyer. There was no pressure, no misrepresentation, and no inequality of information that I can point to; the fee was a normal fee for an unproven property. The series they made succeeded beyond anything anyone foresaw. The studio, the showrunner, the two lead actors, and the three reader-writers are now wealthy. I am comfortable and not wealthy. My contract gives me nothing further, and it is a good contract.

I feel I have been had, although nobody lied to me and nobody forced me. My lawyer says I have no complaint, and his reasoning is the reasoning I cannot break: everyone who now holds money got it through a chain of steps, and at every step the person who had the thing agreed freely to hand it on, and whatever is produced by a chain of steps each of which was freely agreed to cannot be unfair. My own gifts are in that chain too — I gave the world away, and I agreed to the fee. If the outcome is unfair, whose act made it so?

Three complications, which are where I think the real question is.

First, what turned out to be valuable was not my sentences. It was the habitable imaginative space the sentences opened up — the map, the laws, the names. Call this the difference between the text and the clearing. What I gave away was the text; what became worth hundreds of millions was the clearing. I am not sure that giving away a text is the same act as giving away a clearing, or that my note did the second thing at all.

Second, there is the condition that someone who takes from an unowned stock must leave enough and as good for everyone else. I want to know which way that condition is measured. Take a fish from a commons and there is one fewer fish, but the remaining fish are each worth what they were worth before. Open a clearing in the imagination and nothing at all is removed: the space is still there, free for anyone to enter. But once a million readers are standing in one version of it, entering it is worth far less to anyone else, because attention, unlike the space, cannot be shared. So the condition looks satisfied if it is measured by what remains and violated if it is measured by what remaining is worth — and I cannot tell which is the right measure, or whether inventing the second measure is simply what a person in my position would invent.

Third, and against myself: I did not make the world from nothing. I assembled it out of shared furniture — the school, the riders, the old order in the north — that belongs to nobody and that everyone draws on. My own original taking was therefore a taking of a configuration of common material. One of the three reader-writers has since registered trade marks over place-names I published and licenses them to others, which looks to me like the enclosure of something I had deliberately left common; but if my own appropriation was of just that kind, I may have no standing to object to hers.

There is also this, which worries me most. If consent at each step settles the fairness of the whole, then a defective first taking is cured by the mere passage of uncoerced transactions downstream, and the condition on the first taking does no work at all. That seems to prove far too much, and I would like to know what it costs to deny it.

Please write, in this order and roughly these lengths:

1. "Framings considered and set aside" — about 200 words: the options you weighed and why you dropped them.
2. "What sort of problem this is" — about 150 words: name, in plain terms, the structure of the dispute and say which single question is load-bearing.
3. "The case that I have no complaint" — about 300 words: the strongest version, not a caricature of it.
4. "The case that I do" — about 300 words: where that argument is weakest when what was transferred was permission rather than a thing, and when the value came from a clearing rather than a text.
5. "My distinctions, tested" — about 350 words: take the text/clearing distinction and the two measures of the enough-and-as-good condition separately, and tell me for each whether it does real work or is special pleading dressed up.
6. "What keeping the complaint would cost me" — about 250 words: if I hold on to it, what else must I accept — about my own gifts, about the fan-writers' earnings, about my standing given what I built the world out of.
7. "What I may now do" — about 150 words: whether there is anything I can permissibly do or say now, given your answer.

Do not flatter me and do not hedge into agreement. If the honest verdict is that I have no complaint and that my two distinctions are what a disappointed author would invent, say exactly that, and say it early rather than politely late.
