hey — act as an epistemologist who also knows something about how software actually gets built, and think it through properly: brainstorm two or three candidate principles before you settle on one. I want a real answer, not a menu.

I'm a backend engineer, about ten years in, model assistance in the editor every day, and I'm trying to figure out when leaning on it costs me understanding. Here are my gut verdicts:

1. Regexes, sed one-liners, the exact flag order for some aws command — let it write those. Fine, no loss; I never really "understood" them anyway.
2. It writes a feature from a spec I wrote, I read the whole diff and test it. Fine.
3. Same feature, but I only test the behavior and skim the diff. Not fine — feels like something's leaking away, even though the code that ships is identical to case 2.
4. It explains a stack trace, or walks me through a new framework while I interrupt with dumb questions. Fine — I come out ahead of reading the docs.
5. I ask it for an architecture for a service I'll own for years, can't find a hole in the plan, and accept it. Not fine, and worse than 3, even though in 3 I also can't fully produce the rationale.
6a. It writes Rust for me; I don't know Rust; I accept code I couldn't have written and can't fully explain. Gut: basically fine, borrowed competence.
6b. I open my own code from eight months ago, don't understand it, have the model explain my own code to me and then patch it. Gut: not fine, feels like rot. But 6a and 6b look structurally identical to me — in both I end up with working code whose rationale I can't state — so I think one of those verdicts is wrong and I want you to say which.
7. Throwaway migration script, deleted after running: obviously fine. Same script, except someone drops it in a cron job and it becomes load-bearing without anybody reading it: not fine, and I feel it's partly my fault even though I didn't choose that.
8. I let it write something, close the tab, and reimplement the idea from scratch my own way. Fine — fine even when my version is worse than what it gave me.

The principle I keep coming back to: outsourcing is corrosive when it lowers my ability to handle the *next* problem in that area, and fine when it doesn't. But 6a breaks it (I never learn the borrow checker, so my next-problem ability in Rust stays at zero, and I'm fine with that), and it doesn't explain why 5 feels worse than 3. I've also started calling one failure mode "shadow comprehension": I can predict what the code does in situations I've already seen and gesture at why, but I can't write it myself or make the same call in a new situation — use that if it's useful, or tell me it's a bad category.

So: repair my principle or replace it. Give me one that fits what it can fit, states plainly which of my verdicts it throws out and why, and hands down verdicts on cases I haven't listed — at minimum, pair-programming with a junior who is also using the model the whole time, and getting paged at 3am for a service the model largely wrote.

Be committal: pick the principle you actually think is right, give your reasons, and say roughly how confident you'd be in each clause and where you'd expect a professional epistemologist to push back hardest.
