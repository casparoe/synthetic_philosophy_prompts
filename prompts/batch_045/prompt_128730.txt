I am writing a paper on slurs, and I keep finding that the literature advances one example at a time: a case is produced and offered as decisive for or against a whole framework. I want the specification before the verdicts.

Write the desiderata list: what any adequate theory of slurs has to deliver. Fix the purpose as two jobs at once — (a) a predictive job: for an arbitrary expression in an arbitrary language, say whether it is a slur, which group it targets, and roughly where it sits on a scale of offensiveness; and (b) a normative job: say what, if anything, is wrong with a particular utterance of it, when uttered by whom and to whom, and what an appropriate response would be. If no single theory can do both, say so, tell me which job to give up, and what a theory stripped of the other job should look like.

For each requirement give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference internal to one research programme. At a minimum I want requirements motivated by the following, which I have not seen handled together:

1. A language has an ordinary word for an ethnic group; philologists establish it began life as a slur, but no living speaker — including members of the group — knows this.
2. A speaker coins a group-directed derogatory expression on the spot, which hearers have never encountered but instantly read as hostile and as aimed at a group: a neutral group label with a pejorative affix, a mocking imitation of the group's accent, or a gesture.
3. A slur is borrowed into a second language where it functions as a neutral or affectionate in-group label; it is uttered by a member of the borrowing community and overheard by someone from the community of origin, and the two hearers diverge completely.
4. The slur is the only term in common use for the group; the official alternative is clinical, archaic, or offensive in some other way.
5. The slur is applied to someone who is not a member of the target group, or it encodes a taxonomy the relevant science rejects.
6. The same lexical item under negation, in the antecedent of a conditional, in a question, in reported speech, in quotation, and in a linguistics lecture about slurs — where offense seems to survive non-assertoric embedding but attenuates differently in each.
7. In-group members use it as a term of endearment while out-group members cannot, including in quotation; and reclamation sometimes occurs without anyone deciding anything.
8. Successor terms acquire the offense of the terms they replaced, and some slurs lose their group-specific content and become general-purpose insults.
9. Group-directed contempt conveyed by prosody, gesture, or pronoun misuse, with no distinctive lexical item.
10. Group-directed insults for voluntarily joined groups, for dominant groups, and for occupational or regional categories.

Say which distinctions the theory must respect rather than collapse: slur versus pejorative versus epithet versus profanity; targeting versus referring versus predicating; content versus force versus uptake versus harm versus wrongness; a norm against use versus a difference in meaning; type-level versus token-level facts; binary versus graded offense. Note any findings from sociolinguistics the theory may not contradict.

Then flag any subset of your own desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want — and name the single requirement that excludes the largest share of the current candidates. If it helps, close with a short table scoring the four standard families (derogation as truth-conditional content, as a conventional implicature or expressive dimension, as a pragmatic inference, or as the violation of a use-prohibition) against the list, and say what observation would settle the two disagreements that matter most. A table is fine; keep the argumentative weight on the criteria.
