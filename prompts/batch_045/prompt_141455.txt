Five of us in a metaphysics reading group — three endurantists, one perdurantist, one person who refuses to say — have spent a full semester on the persistence debate and have ended up exactly where we began, which is what I want you to think about rather than the debate's merits. The pattern is always the same. The perdurantist runs the argument from change: a persisting thing can't be literally bent and later literally straight if it is wholly present at both times, so it must be extended in time by having temporal parts, and "is bent" is really a matter of having a bent part. The endurantist replies that properties are instantiated relative to times, or that the relevant predicate is time-sensitive in a way no argument from mere change can rule out. Then the argument from coincidence: temporal parts let you count objects by counting spacetime regions and explain how the statue and the clay can differ. Then the argument from vagueness: temporal parts give sharp boundaries where ordinary sortals give none. Then the relativity-flavored argument that there is no privileged slicing of spacetime into "all of the object now," which the endurantists in the room meet either with presentism or with the claim that the objection just assumes the perdurantist's ontology of time. Every move has an equal and opposite move, and nobody has shifted an inch, including the person who refuses to say. My hypothesis is that this is not one substantive dispute that is merely hard, but at least three disputes stacked under one label: a dispute about the ideology of location, i.e. about whether "wholly located at a region" is a primitive notion with real content or something that has to be analysed, and if so in terms of parts; a dispute about which data a theory of persistence is answerable to — ordinary sortal judgments about what we can count and refer to, versus what fundamental physics says about what occupies spacetime; and, underneath both, a disagreement about whether metaphysics is meant to systematize ordinary thought or to take its cue from our best science, which is the kind of thing people in the room hold at different strengths without ever arguing for it.

Here is the probe I have been using, which I would like you to take seriously rather than set aside as a distraction. Call a property a diachronic intrinsic when it is not a relation to any other object and yet its instantiation at a time depends on what happens at other times: being a life that was on the whole a happy one, being a knife that has been resharpened exactly twice, being a career that peaked early. These matter, I think, because the perdurantist's standard move with shape does not obviously transfer — no instantaneous temporal part is a mostly-happy life, and I do not see what extrinsic relation to a part could do the work — while the endurantist's "instantiates-at-a-time" apparatus seems to swallow them too easily, at the cost of making "at a time" carry most of the theoretical weight. I also want your reaction to a third position I sketched and could not get anyone to take seriously: that persisting objects have no temporal parts and are not wholly present at times either, because occupying a time at all is a derivative, sortal-relative matter, so that the question "wholly or partly present?" never gets a grip. If that view is incoherent, or is secretly one of the other two, or is perfectly thinkable and just unfashionable, each of those verdicts seems to me to tell us something different about whether the original dispute has content.

So: give me a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict on who is right. We have read the literature — refer to positions and arguments by name if useful, but do not summarize the debate and please give no bibliography, references, or citations; I will add those myself. What I want is a supported account of what kind of stalemate this is — verbal, substantive, stacked, different-questions-under-one-name, or a sign of an ill-posed question — using what each side actually commits to, plus an honest answer about what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a proof of notational equivalence, a case where the theories genuinely diverge in what they count, a settlement of the methodological question, a shift to a better-posed question, or nothing at all. Make sure to separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what remains contested, flag where you are speculating rather than reporting, and do not present a contested position as the consensus view. Lead with a two-sentence statement of your diagnosis, and keep the whole response under 700 words.
