I am a first-year PhD student in machine learning, working in a lab that does interpretability research on Transformer language models: the kind of work where one looks for a direction in the residual stream (the vector that gets passed along from layer to layer) whose presence or strength tracks something about the input, such as whether a sentence is in French, whether a claim is true, or whether the model is about to copy a token it has seen before.

At lab meetings we keep circling around a question I care about and can't get settled: does a trained network really *represent* features of the world, or is representational talk just shorthand that helps us predict and steer a system that isn't about anything? One of the senior students in the lab has a way of closing these discussions. He says, in a tone that implies the matter is settled, that "a feature is just a direction in activation space," and when pushed he adds that there is no privileged basis anyway, so asking what the network "really" represents is like asking what a vector "really" is. The line is clearly doing argumentative work, but I cannot tell whether it is a platitude about linear algebra dressed up as a philosophy of mind, a substantive empirical hypothesis, or an argument against the entire question that I am entitled to refuse.

I would like a menu of the precise claims this sentence could be making, ordered roughly from the trivial to the substantive to the strong-and-probably-false. For each reading I want: what exactly it asserts; what would have to be true of the network's geometry and training for it to hold; whether it is a platitude, a live empirical hypothesis, or false or a pseudoproblem; and — most importantly — whether that reading would actually entitle him to the deflationary conclusion he draws from it. I am less interested in being told which reading is correct than in being able to see which one his argument needs.

Please engage in particular with the complications I think the slogan papers over, and tell me which of them are real and which are my own confusions: that a space of activation tuples does not come with a canonical vector-space structure, so it is not obvious that averaging two activations is even a meaningful operation; that "direction" is ambiguous between a one-dimensional subspace (a ray) and a linear functional in the dual space, i.e., between the direction a feature points along and the direction a detector is sensitive to; that talk of angles, orthogonality, interference, and projection requires an inner product that the space is not given; that basis-relativity is not the same as metric-relativity, and that the network's nonlinearities — softmax, elementwise activation functions, per-coordinate normalization — are defined in a fixed coordinate system, so an arbitrary rotation of the space compensated by the next layer's weights will not in general preserve the function; that a ray can encode presence but not intensity, and that intensity requires a choice of scale, orientation, and sign; and that a direction's being *decodable* from the activations seems like a different matter from its being *used* by downstream computation, which I have been calling readout-realism versus causal-realism.

For each entry in the menu, say what empirical finding would settle it, mark how confident you are in the verdict, and state what would change your mind or where your reasoning is most likely to break down. I do not want citations, a reading list, or a survey of who has argued what — I want distinctions I can press in the room. Please write the answer as a numbered menu I can bring to the next lab meeting, in American English.
