I'm a novelist, and the book I'm drafting at the moment has a long middle section set at a small, under-funded research institute that spends its money on questions nobody else will fund; one of its two standing programmes concerns the moral standing of terrestrial arthropods. Two of my characters — let's call them the scale-sensitivity researcher and the skeptic, though I hope their positions will be more interesting than their labels — have been circling each other for years, and I want to stage the scene in which, after a week of forced cohabitation at a workshop, they produce the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration. The scale person's case is the familiar one in outline: the number of insects alive at any moment, and the far larger number of insect lives begun and ended per year given how short those lives are, is so vast that even a probability of sentience small enough to be dismissible in a single case yields an expected moral stakes that dwarfs everything else on the ledger. The skeptic's case is that this is an artefact of multiplying a made-up number by an astronomically large one, that the probability here isn't a probability at all but a gesture, and that an argument whose conclusion is insensitive to every probability you could honestly assign — one centipede of a chance and a tenth of a chance give the same practical upshot — is not transmitting information from the evidence to the conclusion, only from the exponent. I'd like both of them to be genuinely good at this, and I'd like the document to contain a few moves I've heard them make in my head and want to see developed rather than the standard talking points: (i) the point that the probability and the headcount are evidentially coupled — the behavioural and neurological markers that would raise your confidence that an adult dipteran has valenced states are largely the same markers that would raise it for springtails, oribatid mites and soil nematodes, which outnumber insects by orders of magnitude, so a belief-revision that raises the probability cannot be plugged into the insect count alone without cheating; (ii) the related complaint that the taxonomic boundary of 'insect' is doing argumentative work it cannot justify, and that once the boundary is moved the scale argument either explodes past any possible intervention or quietly reverts to a general claim about small organisms that nobody knows how to act on; (iii) the asymmetry that insect welfare is unusual among large-scale causes in that the interventions are cheap, population-scale and available now, while the feedback on whether they helped is essentially unobtainable — you can act at a scale of trillions and never learn anything, which is a problem different in kind from ordinary cluelessness about side-effects; and (iv) a dispute about the unit of account, i.e. whether the thing being multiplied is organisms, lives-per-year, or something like valenced-state tokens, and whether switching units is legitimate bookkeeping or silent double-counting. Please don't have either of them lean on named studies, paper titles, authorities or citations — my characters can't check them and I don't want invented ones; where a number or an empirical claim matters, give an order of magnitude and mark it in brackets as something the scene would have to fact-check.

What I'd like from you is the document itself, and I'd like it to be good enough that a reader who works on this stuff would believe two people signed it. Concretely: a numbered section of claims both of them can sign, phrased so as to be neither vacuous nor tilted — including, if they can, something honest about what is agreed regarding the scale argument's arithmetic as opposed to its epistemology; then a table of the residual disagreements, each stated twice if necessary, once in the wording its adherent would accept and once in neutral language, with a note from you afterwards on which of those disagreements are genuine and which were only ever about words; then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them — and for each crux a concrete case, measurement, or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. For every crux I also want the falsifier: what each of them would count as evidence against their own view, and specifically which single consideration would most likely make them change their mind, stated in a way my characters could actually say out loud in a room rather than in the abstract language of a methods paper.

Finally, because I'm going to put this in a novel and I don't want it to be smoother than it deserves to be, please close with a short note to me rather than to them: a page at most, saying where the joint language is doing concealed work — which formulations are agreed only because they're empty, where the two of them have changed the subject without noticing, which of the cruxes you suspect no available evidence could settle, and where your own reconstruction is most likely to fail, either by making one side weaker than it really is or by borrowing a premise the skeptic would in fact refuse. I'd rather have the seams visible than have a document that reads as though the field had made more progress than it has.
