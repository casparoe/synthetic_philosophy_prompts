I've been going to a history-and-philosophy-of-science reading group down at the continuing-ed place since I retired — plant pathology for most of my working life, then a stretch doing climate-impacts modelling, so I come at this from the organism side rather than the archive side. We've spent the last few weeks arguing about germs, weather and volcanoes as "actors" in history, and it kept going in circles, because everyone was using agent, cause, driver and trigger for different things. So I want to build a decision tree I can put on a handout: start someone at "of course the blight mattered, everybody knows that" and walk them down to a named position. Sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like — no branch should be a question like "are you a determinist?", because half the room won't know the word and the other half will just pick their team.

Two cuts of my own that I'd like built into the tree, and tell me honestly if either one is a red herring. First, what I've been calling the substitution test: take a case where a pathogen or a cold decade genuinely changed what happened. Now swap in a different pathogen or a different anomaly with roughly the same demographic kick — same mortality, same timing, same region — but different natural history: different vector, different incubation period, hits children rather than adults, arrives in waves rather than all at once. If the historian's story survives the swap unchanged, then the organism was only standing in for a demographic shock and its identity does no work. If the story has to be rewritten — because the rhythm of the waves mattered, or because people could see who was getting sick and drew conclusions, or because the thing came back every summer — then the specific critter is doing explanatory work and can't be substituted out. Second, the two-key test: was the outcome overdetermined, in the sense that the germ on its own or the social arrangement on its own would each have been enough, or did it need both — a pathogen that only turns catastrophic given a particular pattern of land tenure, crowding, or stored grain? I suspect a lot of our shouting was people answering different ones of these.

Cases to build the branch questions out of, at least four of them: Tambora and the cold year that followed; the potato blight in Ireland in the 1840s; yellow fever and the French expedition to Saint-Domingue (if the numbers in the version I've heard are off, never mind, it's the shape of the case I want); smallpox in the Americas after 1492; and, if you want a modern one where the organism visibly responds to what we do, antibiotic resistance on a hospital ward. Swap one out for a better-known case if you think one of mine is factually shaky, but say so when you do.

Two structural things I can't settle. One: should germs, climate and volcanoes be an early branch of the tree, or a note attached to each leaf? My instinct is that a pathogen that evolves in response to our interventions is doing something a stratovolcano plainly isn't, and I'd like the tree to be able to say a bug qualifies and a mountain doesn't — but maybe that splits the tree into three trees, and I'd rather know that than have it fudged. Two: I want the leaves to include, in whatever words you'd use — the "agency needs a mind, so it's causes all the way down" position; a position where an organism genuinely qualifies because of how it's organised and tracks its environment, but an aerosol cloud doesn't; a symmetrical one where anything that makes a difference in the network counts; climate-or-geography-sets-the-course; the enabling-conditions or possibilist one; the one where the germ only finishes what policy and poverty had already started; and both versions of the blame question — that talking about germs as actors excuses the humans, and that it actually raises human responsibility because somebody built the conditions. Attach whatever names go with them, but if you're not confident anyone actually defends one of them, mark it unnamed or invented-for-the-tree rather than pinning a name on it that you're half guessing at.

Where the logical space has holes — combinations my questions allow that nobody seems to occupy — say so and give me one line on why: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really separate; if two of them only differ in emphasis or methodology, I'd rather have that admitted than have the branches pretend otherwise. No citations and no reading lists — my library access lapsed when I retired and I can't chase anything up, so don't build the taxonomy out of papers you're not sure exist.

Keep it to about eight branch questions and ten or twelve leaves. Alongside each leaf put a one-line confidence marker: how sure you are that somebody really holds the position as you've stated it, and whether my framing of it is doing it violence. Then finish by naming the single branch or leaf you're least confident about and what would have to be true for you to be wrong about it.
