For a graduate reading group on well-being, I need a decision tree that sorts people on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: what, if anything, do the pleasures of a cold beer on a hot day, of finishing a proof, of hearing that a friend is safe, of scratching an itch, and of getting lost in a piece of music have in common.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of theories. Give six to eight branching questions and at most ten leaves; phrase every node as a concrete claim or case a newcomer could answer without knowing which position it leads to — for example, whether a pleasure you now judge worth having can have failed to feel good to you while you were having it, whether a state you dislike being in and would stop if you could could nonetheless be a pleasure, whether two pleasures with no introspectively shared "tone" can still be ranked as greater and lesser, and whether whatever unifies the pleasures has to show up in experience at all. Only then locate the actual positions at the leaves — felt-quality or hedonic-tone views, attitudinal views, neurofunctional common-currency views, pluralism, eliminativism — naming a leaf only where a name is standard and marking the rest "unnamed."

Flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot actually separate, and for each unoccupied combination say briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. One leaf has to accommodate a view I have been defending and want placed honestly rather than flattered: that what the pleasures share is not a feeling and not an attitude but an attentional profile — pleasures draw attention back to themselves and resist being released, and hedonic comparison is cashed out in terms of how insistently and how durably attention is held. If that puts my view in an empty cell, say so and diagnose it.

Give the questions and the tree first, then at most a page of commentary on the flags and the empty cells, and end the whole answer with a single line of the form \answer{...} naming the leaf my view lands on, or UNOCCUPIED if you place it in an empty cell.
