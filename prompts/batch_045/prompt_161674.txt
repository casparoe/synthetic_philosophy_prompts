I am completing a PhD in political philosophy and, for the past two years, have served as the non-legal member of an intake panel at a national asylum authority, advising the caseworkers who triage claims before they reach a hearing. I would like the two questions at the centre of my field's literature — who counts as a refugee, and which state has to take them in — turned into a single decision procedure that a caseworker with a fortnight's training can run in under an hour: the questions in the order they must be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision upward because the remaining disagreement is one of values rather than facts. Roughly four hundred claims a year pass through us, every triage note has to be defensible in a written appeal, and a legal officer signs off.

On the first question, the procedure has to handle three recurrent hard cases: the claimant fleeing generalised criminal violence who can name nobody with a reason to target them; the claimant from a province the state has quietly abandoned after slow-onset environmental collapse, where the reason for leaving is the disappearance of protection rather than the presence of a persecutor; and the claimant whose proposed social group is defined only by the harm its members share. Say whether a well-founded fear requires an identifiable agent, or whether a failure of protection can substitute for one, and at which step the procedure should decline to answer at all.

On the second question, give me a priority ordering among the grounds on which a particular state can be picked out — presence or first arrival, causal contribution to the displacement, capacity to absorb, and the claimant's own ties of language, family, prior employment, or prior colonial administration — and say which are trumps and which are only tie-breakers.

Then run the whole procedure on three cases from last month: a farming family from a delta province whose land has been destroyed by saltwater intrusion, whose district administration withdrew two years ago, where no armed group holds the territory, and who walked into the adjacent state, which already hosts a refugee population equal to several per cent of its own citizens; a journalist with documented threats from a named official who passed through three safe countries and claimed in ours, where her sibling lives and where she once worked for six months, and whom our panel would prefer to return to the transit state; and an unaccompanied seventeen-year-old whose feared persecutor was the local commandant of a militia since disbanded, where the fear was well-founded when he fled and nobody can say whether the militia will re-form. Finally, tell me where the procedure cannot be run at all — missing country-of-origin evidence, credibility, anything else — and what it could never settle even if run perfectly, such as whether a state may close its borders to all comers or how large the total obligation is.
