I'm a policy intern at a housing advocacy organization and I'm also auditing a comparative public-law seminar, where we keep circling the same question from three angles: a German-flavored worry that the state must not be allowed to assemble a picture of a person out of scattered records, an American-flavored instinct that openness about records is acceptable so long as it is official secrecy that gets policed, and a Chinese-flavored willingness to make civic trust itself legible and scoreable. Rather than argue in the abstract I've been reading the published rulings of an independent data-governance commission in a mid-sized parliamentary democracy, and I've noticed that its track record can be read three or four different ways. Its precedents, as I have them: it refused a single national identity number that would have allowed health, tax and schooling records to be linked, even though every agency already lawfully held its own file, saying the wrong lay in the linkage rather than in any record alone; it required registers of company beneficial ownership to be public and searchable by anyone, including foreign journalists; it struck down mandatory real-name verification for posting on discussion forums but upheld it for anyone buying political advertising; it permitted live CCTV monitoring of a city centre but no retention beyond seventy-two hours absent suspicion and no facial recognition at all; it approved automated matching of tax and customs records to detect value-added-tax fraud, conditional on the matching rule being published and on every hit being reviewed by a named official before it could affect anyone; it blocked a private firm from selling reliability scores to landlords while approving a public register of unsatisfied court judgments, provided a person could see and contest their entry; it required senior civil servants to publish their assets each year and declined to extend the same duty to recipients of state benefits; and it refused a school wellbeing scheme that would have logged children's sleep, attendance and self-reported mood in order to flag neglect, even with parental consent.

The case now in front of the commission is a statutory housing conduct register. Before signing a lease, a landlord would have to run a prospective tenant against it, and receive only a green, amber or red result rather than the underlying entries; the tenant would be entitled to request their own file and to contest entries, but not to see the scoring rule. The register is fed by final eviction orders, by noise complaints upheld by a mediation board, and — incidentally, because the software simply ingests the equality body's case-management system — by the fact of having been a party to a housing discrimination complaint, including as the complainant. Its stated aim is to reduce homelessness caused by a small number of serial nuisance tenants.

I'd like you to do four things. First, reconstruct the candidate rules that fit this track record jointly: I can see at least a purpose-limitation or anti-fusion rule, a power-asymmetry rule on which disclosure duties track how much power a person exercises over others, and a proceduralist rule on which the real evil is arbitrariness, so that published rules, human review and a route to contest license almost any volume of data. I'd also like you to try out one further rule that I think the precedents do not rule out and that I find attractive: that what matters is not how much the state can read about you but the ratio between what the state can read about you and what you can read about the state, so that a measure increasing both sides' knowledge can be permissible while a purely one-directional one is not. Second, say which of these rules predicts what about the register, and where they come apart: I suspect the anti-fusion rule and the proceduralist rule diverge sharply here, and I'm unsure whether the fact that the register fuses official determinations rather than behavioural traces should matter to the anti-fusion rule. Third, give me your best guess at the actual verdict, with the reasons for preferring that reading of the precedents, and tell me honestly which precedent is the closest analog and how much weight it bears. Fourth, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, name the minimal variant of the register — or the single further case — that would best separate the surviving rules, and say what principle the commission would be committing itself to under each of the available decisions.
