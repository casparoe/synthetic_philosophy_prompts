I'm a software engineer — I build forecasting and backtesting systems for a living, no philosophy background beyond one Coursera course — and somehow that got me invited to give a guest talk next month at a university philosophy of science reading group. Topic is whether non-empirical arguments can confirm a theory, with the string landscape / multiverse as the test case.

Here's what I'm actually arguing. I want to seperate two questions people keep running together: whether non-empirical assessment is ever evidence at all, and how strong it is in this particular case. My claim is that we should treat it as a forecasting method and audit it the way I'd audit a model at work:

1. Write the criteria down in advance, pre-register them: "nobody has found a viable alternative after N years of looking", "the theory unexpectedly unifies stuff it wasn't built to unify", "arguments of this shape have paid off before."

2. Build a dataset of historical theories that were accepted mostly on those grounds before they had decisive evidence, with a symmetric inclusion rule — you have to include the ones that later died, not just general relativity and continental drift — and score the method's hit rate.

3. Correct for the fact that the cases everyone cites are survivors, and check the conclusion holds under a few different scoring rules, because otherwise the backtest itself is just fitted to the answer you wanted.

My punchline: the method survives step 1 — it isn't meaningless — but at step 2 the base rate is much worse than the multiverse literature implicitly assumes, so all of this buys the landscape is a modest credence bump, not confirmation. And the burden shifts: if you want more than that, you pre-register your criteria and you eat whatever score comes out.

The room: two philosophers of science, one Bayesian who thinks non-empirical arguments can perfectly well be evidence, one old-school falsificationist who thinks "non-empirical confirmation" is an oxymoron; a theorist who works on the landscape and the measure problem and is tired of being told she isn't doing science; an experimental particle physicist who thinks the whole field is unfalsifiable and says so loudly; a statistician who is the reason I was invited and who will care about leakage, base rates and whether my dataset is contaminated; and maybe ten grad students.
