I run a small reading group attached to a philosophy department, and last term we worked through Nietzsche's early essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life — the one that sorts the ways history can serve a living thing into three: a monumental mode that hunts the past for greatness to emulate and for proofs that the great was once possible, an antiquarian mode that reverently tends the inherited, the small and the local simply because it is one's own, and a critical mode that hauls parts of the past before a tribunal, condemns and destroys them, on the grounds that anything alive must be able to wound and overcome what made it. We spent most of the term on the surrounding machinery: the plastic power by which a person or a culture takes up what is alien and bygone and reshapes it into its own growth; the claim that acting and hoping require a bounded horizon, a capacity not to know, rather than total recall; the suggestion that too much historical sense becomes a kind of twilight in which nothing can any longer be begun. Everyone in the room agreed with all of that in the abstract, and then we fell apart over an invented case that I would now like you to write as a dialogue rather than analyse in prose.

The case: some sixty years ago a city installed the Record, a complete, immutable, publicly searchable register of everything said and done within its boundaries, kept continuously ever since. Nothing has been forgotten and nothing can be. Over the same sixty years the city has become strikingly inert: no new guild, school, newspaper or festival has been founded there in nineteen years, although the Record shows its citizens proposing, debating and refining plans for such things more industriously than any previous generation. The city is now voting on two rival remedial bills. The Thinning Bill, drafted by the jurist Livia Serrano, would institute a periodic critical expurgation: a tribunal, sitting each decade, empowered to condemn and permanently destroy designated stretches of the Record, on the Nietzschean ground that a culture must be able to break its past in order to live. The Counter-Record amendment, drafted by the archivist Anselm Okri, would leave the Record untouched but would have the city appoint, for each quarter, two chorographers who compose a monumental and antiquarian counter-record for public use — exemplars to emulate, a lineage to revere — while working in a sealed chamber from which the Record is withheld, and while openly avowing that what they compose is composed rather than found. Please write the dialogue between Serrano and Okri, set in the hours before the vote.

Make them equally capable interlocutors who actually answer one another rather than delivering speeches past each other, and let the argument get into the joints of the problem rather than staying at the level of slogans. In particular I would like the dispute to turn on questions like these: whether the life-serving work of monumental and antiquarian history is done by the images a community lives inside or by the beliefs it holds about the past, and hence whether an avowed fiction can do the same work an unrecognised one does; whether critical history can genuinely be exercised on a past that is wholly and permanently present, given that condemning a past seems to require occupying some standpoint outside it; whether Okri's chorographers, precisely because they are sealed off from the truth, restore the plastic power or merely manufacture the appearance of it, and whether their appointment by the city converts monumental history into official propaganda; whether Serrano's tribunal is anything other than vandalism dressed in a philosophical vocabulary, and whether destroying a record that everyone has already read and metabolised can restore the horizon it is meant to restore; and whether either bill is reachable from inside the culture it is meant to cure. If it serves the argument, let one of them press the possibility that the Nietzschean remedy was always the unhistorical rather than any particular policy about archives — but let the other get a real reply to that, and let the ending be earned rather than tidy. Around nine hundred words, in British English, in a register that is polished but spoken. Please do not pad the piece with verbatim quotations from Nietzsche or with scholarly apparatus; paraphrase whatever of his vocabulary the characters need, and keep the argument self-contained so that a reader need not have read the essay to follow it.
