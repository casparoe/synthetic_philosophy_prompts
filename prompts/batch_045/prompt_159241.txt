I am an addiction psychiatrist, and the vocabulary I actually think in is clinical and behavioral: cue-induced craving, extinction and reinstatement, depot buprenorphine and naltrexone implants, Ulysses contracts, and the planner–doer model of self-control from behavioral economics, in which a far-sighted planner binds a myopic doer using devices that work by physically changing what the doer can reach. I read no philosophy, and the jargon I have run into while looking into updateless decision theory — logical counterfactuals, the claim that I am the policy rather than the person — reads to me as oracular.

Two months ago my brother relapsed after nine years sober, and my mother said that the man who made promises to us nine years ago is not my brother. I do not know what to do with that claim, and I am told this theory is the most developed attempt to say what such a claim means and what follows from it.

Please translate updateless decision theory into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: what the policy is, what it is evaluated over, what updatelessness forbids, and why the later, better-informed self has no standing to reopen the question. Then give me the residue report — what translated cleanly, what had to be bent to fit, and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. Three things I especially want:

1. My commitment devices are causal: they work by making the bad option unreachable. My sense is that this theory's commitment is not causal at all — that it is a correlation between what I decide now and what something else has already done or already predicted, with no influence running in either direction. Is that right, and can it be stated in my vocabulary at all, or is acausal dependence simply not a well-formed idea there?
2. Is updatelessness anything more than my own field's point that sophisticated agents bind themselves in advance? Where exactly does the extra content sit — in evaluating whole policies rather than single choices, in what counts as an observation, in the claim that what correlates with the prediction is my decision algorithm rather than my decision?
3. A case I made up, to see where the translation breaks. A clinic admits patients to a scarce two-year program only if a model trained on their journals predicts they will complete it; the prediction is made and the offer posted before I know any of this. I then open one envelope containing two papers: my predicted probability of completing, and an unrelated genetic finding showing that for me the program's side effects will be far worse than the brochure says. My training says to update on both and decline. Does the theory treat the two papers differently, and if so, why?

Where the translation fails, tell me honestly whether that is a limit of clinical vocabulary or a sign that the theory is not saying anything definite at that point.
