I'm a third-year undergraduate, and a philosophy course I'm taking this term has made me suspicious of something I'm being paid to do, and I'd like advice on it.

I'm a research assistant on a trial of an eight-week self-management programme for people who have had chronic pain for years. The manual instructs facilitators to open every session by having each participant say out loud, and then write down, a confident expectation that their pain is going to improve — phrased not as a hope or a goal but as a prediction about what will in fact happen. From the papers I've been given, my sense is that expectancy does affect reported pain and function, but that the effects are modest and not consistently replicated, so the script asks for considerably more confidence than the evidence underwrites. Two participants have told me privately that saying it feels like being asked to lie to themselves. I have two sessions to run next week and I need to decide whether to read the line as written, soften it, or decline to read it at all.

When I raised this, the principal investigator gave two replies. First, that the statement isn't a forecast anyone is asked to defend with evidence; it is a component of the treatment, and if it helps, asking whether it was evidentially supported beforehand is asking the wrong question. Second, that anyone who balks can simply act as though they expect to improve without actually expecting it, which she says gets the effect without asking anyone to believe anything they can't back up.

Is she right? Does the fact that adopting an expectation may help bring about what it says bear on whether it is permissible to encourage it? Does her act-as-if variant actually avoid the objection, or does it just move it somewhere else? And does it matter that what I'm being asked to facilitate is other people's believing rather than my own?
