I help run a small real-money betting and investment club with four friends. Nothing professional: we put in our own savings, we meet once a month, and there is no training in economics or in philosophy among us. Over two years we have ended up with a set of house rules that we never really voted on, and now that I have written them down I cannot tell what they commit us to.

We always stake a fixed fraction of whatever the club holds at that moment, never a fixed sum, so our stakes automatically shrink after losses and grow after wins. The fraction comes from a rule I found online, on which a bigger edge and a shorter price mean a bigger fraction, and which I am told works out to maximising the expected logarithm of our money rather than the expected money itself. We deliberately stake about half of what that rule recommends, because none of us trusts our own estimates of the odds. We turn down a bet that is plainly good value in expected cash if taking it would risk a large share of the club in one go, while accepting exactly the same bet at a tenth of the stake without discussion. We judge ourselves by the year-on-year growth rate of the club's total, in the geometric sense, and never by the average of what we made per bet. Our charter says that what we are maximising is the growth rate of the fund in those futures in which the fund is still there to grow, a clause we adopted only because we could not agree what should count if a bad run wipes us out. And when someone asks what it is all for, we say we want to be the largest of the clubs that started the same year as ours when we hold our twenty-fifth reunion; one member says that is just another way of saying grow fastest, another says that if we are behind near the end it might mean taking bets we expect to lose money on.

What does a set of practices like this presuppose? I would like the implicit theory of rational choice reconstructed: what it says about choosing well over time that a plain expected-money calculation leaves out, and whether it presupposes that we are nervous about risk in the ordinary sense, or that additional money means less to us as we get richer, or whether a club that felt neither of those things would end up in the same place anyway. I would like to know what going broke has to be for the clause about futures in which the fund still exists, and for the refusal of large one-off bets, to make any sense: whether it is genuinely different in kind from merely being poor, and whether our bankroll properly includes the salaries we could add next year. I would like to know whether being largest is the same object as growing fastest, or a different one again. And I would like to know where the rules are philosophically underdetermined: which of them several quite different theories would equally permit, which commit us to something contested, and what we would have to change if we abandoned the growth-rate framing and simply took the bet with the best expected cash return at every meeting.

Please answer in British English, as exactly five numbered points, each point one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no concluding summary. I have no library and no way to check references, so please do not point me at books, papers or further reading; argue it out from the case itself.
