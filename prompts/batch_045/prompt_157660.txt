I've been spending the last few months with Schrödinger's non-technical writing — the epilogue to *What Is Life?* (1944), the essays gathered in *Mind and Matter* and *My View of the World* — read alongside the Upanishads, and I keep failing to say in prose what the doctrine actually claims. Every sentence I manage either softens into "we're all connected" or tips into quantum mysticism, neither of which is what he says. So I'd like a poem to do the work instead: a pantoum of six quatrains, and I'll spell out the rule so there's no guesswork — the second and fourth lines of each stanza reappear, word for word, as the first and third lines of the next, so that a line keeps its exact wording while its neighbours and its sense change; in the sixth and last stanza the lines that have travelled the furthest come back to close, and the poem ends on the line it began with. The point is that the form should be the argument: one line of experience turning up in situation after situation, unchanged and yet no longer the same, which is about as close as verse can get to the claim that the many are aspects of one thing and not a collection of similar things.

Three things a specialist ought to recognise in it. First, that the identity claim is numerical, not a resemblance: not that our streams of consciousness resemble one another, but that there is only one and that the plurality is an appearance. Second, the direction of explanation runs inward, not outward — he does not derive consciousness from the brain, and holds that the self turns up nowhere inside the scientific world-picture precisely because the self is that picture. Third, that this isn't a hypothesis or an inference from physics: nothing in the poem should suggest that entanglement or the measurement problem gets you there, because for him it comes from what is immediately given, and he himself insists it is not something that can be proved, only recognised. Two devices of my own, which you should feel free to use if they help: each quatrain is spoken in the first person but by a different apparent speaker, so that the reader realises late that "I" was never indexed to one biography; and at least two of the recurring lines should shift their meaning as they migrate, a line that first reads as one person's private complaint returning as a claim about the only subject there is. I've also been toying with holding the whole thing in the present tense, no "then," no "once," on the thought that on his account consciousness is only ever in the now and the aspects are not stages in a sequence.

It's to be read aloud — about ninety seconds — at the end of a public lecture I'm giving, so it has to work on the ear as well as on the page: plain words, no Sanskrit tags, no exoticised diction, no "thee" and "thou." The closing stanzas should reach the ethical consequence without preaching — that if there is only one subject, whatever is done to another is done to the only one there is — but I don't want that resolved into consolation, and I don't want the strangeness smoothed over; leave me with the discomfort I have reading him. A reader who knows these texts should be able to point to the line where the metaphor stops and the doctrine is actually asserted; a reader who knows nothing about them should still come away with a poem they'd want to hear again.
