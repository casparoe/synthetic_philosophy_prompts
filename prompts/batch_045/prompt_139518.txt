Hi — I write novels, and I need someone to audit a bundle of things I've talked myself into believing, because I've agreed to do a panel at a literary festival in the autumn with an actual philosopher on it and I'd rather find the soft spots now than on stage. So: you're a professional philosopher who specialises in philosophy of mind and has supervised theses on externalism and the metaphysics of mind — please think this through properly rather than firing off a quick verdict, and maybe start by brainstorming where the fault lines might be before you commit to anything. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations: I can't check references and don't want them, just the arguments.

Some context, because it's why I care. The book I'm finishing is about a retired cartographer, Marian, who had a stroke in her sixties and has almost no reliable memory for her own life or for appointments, and who runs everything out of what she calls the card room — maybe nine thousand index cards, in a shorthand she reads fluently but didn't invent, because something like half of them were written by her daughter over a decade, typed up from what Marian told her, corrected when Marian contradicted them, and re-filed as things changed. Marian consults the room constantly, never double-checks it, is lost without it, and would defend any card in it as what she thinks. About two-thirds of the way through, a landlord has the room cleared out while she's in hospital, and I want the reader to feel — and I want to be entitled to — that this is a harm done to her, closer to brain damage than to having her shed burgled. Here's roughly where I've landed philosophically, and I want to know whether it hangs together: (1) I accept the parity argument — if a process going on outside the skull does the same job, plays the same role in guiding action, as an internal one would, then it counts as part of the cognitive system; there's nothing special about what the vehicle is made of or where it sits. (2) But I don't want to say everything causally hooked up to a person is part of them, so I add the usual-looking restrictions: the resource has to be reliably there when needed, easy to get at, automatically trusted rather than checked, and something the person has taken up and endorsed in the past. That's what keeps the classic notebook case in and the internet in my pocket out. (3) I think cognition extends but consciousness doesn't — there's nothing it's like to be the card room, and the extended states are non-conscious ones. (4) I think the thesis is a real metaphysical claim about what constitutes a mental state, not just a handy redescription, and there's a fact of the matter — though I also think no experiment could settle it either way, since the two sides agree about all the causal goings-on and just describe them differently. (5) I'm a functionalist about what makes something a mental state, and I stop at the person: I'm happy to say an individual's mind reaches into her artefacts, but I balk at saying a team plus its shared database is one mind, or that there's a group mind sitting over the individuals. (6) I want the moral and legal upshot: destroying someone's external store can be a harm to her in something like the way damaging her brain is. And (7) also — and this is the bit my partner says contradicts everything else — I think heavy reliance on external scaffolding genuinely degrades us, that outsourcing memory makes people worse at remembering and shallower at understanding, and that this is perfectly compatible with the scaffolding being literally part of the machinery.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like it, ranked by severity rather than all flagged as equally bad, and for each real one say what would have to give and what that would cost me — which single revision would clean up the most, and which commitment is doing the least work. In particular: does my stated principle actually deliver the verdict I want about Marian, given that she didn't write half the cards and didn't devise the code, or am I helping myself to something in (1) and (2) that doesn't reach her case? Is the tension I'm most worried about — (3) against (6) — really a tension, given that we happily count damage to someone's non-conscious memory traces as a harm to them? And can I keep (5)'s refusal to go beyond the individual while holding (1)? Be specific about which numbered commitment has to move, and please don't just tell me the literature is unsettled — tell me what my own package commits me to.
