So here's the situation I've gotten myself into. I'm a philosophy professor — mostly ethics, I teach the comparative courses too — and last spring I let myself be talked onto the clinical ethics committee at the hospital affiliated with our university. You know the arrangement: they wanted "a humanities person" on the committee, I said yes before thinking about what I was agreeing to. We get maybe thirty consults a year, they come in from a nurse or a resident, and we have roughly forty-eight hours to put something in the chart. The committee rotates — an oncologist, a nurse manager, a social worker, a hospital lawyer, a community member, and me. Right now we run everything through the four-principles framework (autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice) that everyone was trained on in some half-day workshop, and honestly it's stopped being useful to us. It basically outputs "go ask the patient what she wants" in exactly the cases where that's the thing under dispute: families insisting the patient not be told, adult children disagreeing with each other about what their mother "would have wanted," patients whose whole sense of what they owe their family makes the language of individual rights sound like a foreign language. There's no vocabulary in it for any of that.

Meanwhile I've been reading the Confucian role ethics literature — the reading of the early Confucian texts on which persons are constituted by the roles they actually live rather than being antecedent choosers who then take on roles and relationships, and where what you cultivate is relational attentiveness, ritual propriety (li), something like filial responsibility (xiao), and a situated judgment (yi) that just isn't the sort of thing that can be specified in advance. It feels far closer to how these cases present themselves in the room. So the chief of staff has asked me to draft something the committee could actually use, and I agreed, which is where I'm stuck.

What I want from you is a decision procedure, not an essay about role ethics — I can write that myself. I mean the thing we'd run: the questions, in order, we'd ask ourselves on a consult; what counts as an adequate answer to each; where the procedure ends in a substantive recommendation and where it deliberately stops and hands the question back to a person because what remains is a value question, or a relationship question, or simply not ours to settle. It has to be runnable by non-philosophers in an afternoon, and it should be honest about which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Critically, there's an objection I keep circling and I want you to take it seriously rather than wave it away: isn't writing role ethics down as a checklist exactly the degeneration the view is supposed to be diagnosing? If yi is precisely what can't be cashed out ahead of time, then any procedure I hand the committee will have replaced the exercise of judgment with a rule, and I'll have reproduced — in Confucian vocabulary — the thing I was objecting to in the four principles. Tell me whether that kills the project or whether there's a form the procedure could take (less a flowchart, maybe, and more a structured set of questions that forces the right kind of attention) that isn't self-undermining. Either way, say which distinctions from the theory have to survive the translation for the result to still be Confucian role ethics rather than generic family-systems bioethics, and which we can drop. Then run it on three cases, please. First: a sixty-eight-year-old woman with newly diagnosed metastatic lung cancer; her adult son, who is her caregiver, formally asks us not to disclose the diagnosis, says she "wouldn't want to know," and says that in their family this is what a good son does — the treating team wants to tell her. Second: a patient with advanced dementia, no advance directive, no spouse; two estranged daughters arrive with sharply different accounts of what she would have wanted and of who should speak for her. Third: a case where your procedure gives an answer I definately won't like — pick one yourself and say why it's uncomfortable; I'd rather find out now than in a real consult. And please don't pad it with citations or suggested reading, I'll do my own reading — I want the procedure, the worked cases, and where it breaks.
