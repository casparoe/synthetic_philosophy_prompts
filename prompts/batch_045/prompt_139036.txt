Background: my thesis is on the normativity of evidence, and Wittgenstein is really not my area, I know just enough to be dangerous. There's a survey chapter in an edited volume on epistemic normativity that someone told me was the fastest way to get up to speed on "hinge epistemology," and it opens like this (I'm reconstructing it from memory so it's probably not word for word, but this is the gist):

"Wittgenstein's last notebooks, published after his death as On Certainty, are about certainty in the sense of the feeling you have when a doubt simply doesn't arise: you don't feel certain that you have hands, you just do. He calls the most general of these certainties 'hinge propositions' — 'here is a hand', 'the earth has existed for a long time', 'things don't go out of existence when nobody is looking' — after his own image of a door that turns on a hinge: they stay put, not because we have evidence for them, but because they are what we use evidence *for*. They are groundless, and so they cannot be known: knowledge requires grounds, and where there are no grounds there is no knowledge. Hinge epistemology takes this up as a thesis about epistemic structure: justification bottoms out in states that are arational, unsupported by anything, and not themselves open to rational assessment. On this picture the view is a version of externalist foundationalism: hinges are basic beliefs, known without inference, and the rest of our beliefs get justified by being inferred from them. The obvious objection is that the whole thing collapses into relativism — if the bottom level is groundless then any old set of hinges is as good as any other, and the view has no resources for criticising a flat-earther or an astrologer, which is a reductio of the entire approach. Most people now think the answer is that hinges are not beliefs at all: they are more like unreflective animal behaviour, instinctive ways of acting that we share, and once you see that, the demand for justification doesn't even get a grip, and the relativism worry dissolves."

Here's the part I actually care about, though. My supervisor doesn't work on this either but enjoys poking at it, and he has a worry he calls the parody problem. Take the flat-earther, or take a Victorian gentleman's "one simply doesn't discuss money with servants": in each case you have a commitment that functions inside someone's practice exactly like a hinge is supposed to — it isn't inferred from anything, it isn't up for dispute, it determines which doubts count as genuine and which questions are even askable, and a whole lot of other commitments take their shape from it. Structurally it's indistinguishable from "here is a hand." But one of these is supposed to be in good standing and the other is supposed to be mere dogma, and if all the view can appeal to is the structural role, he says, it can't tell you why. As far as I can tell there are two replies people reach for: either real hinges are the ones that are *indispensable*, in that you can't get any cognitive project off the ground at all without relying on them, so you're entitled to them without evidence, whereas the parodies are optional commitments you could inquiry perfectly well without; or you fall back on the brute fact that these are the things that stand fast *for us*, and say there's no deeper answer than that. He thinks the first reply quietly abandons the groundlessness claim and the second one just *is* the relativism objection, answered by restating it.
