before I dig any further into this I want the spec, not the theories. me and a friend have been going in circles for weeks about whether you actually *owe* a tip in a city where tipping is the convention and wages are set assuming it, whether you owe it if your only visiting for four days, and whether a guy who strolls across an empty street at 2am against the light is doing anything wrong at all. so: write me the desiderata that a theory of *conventional obligation* has to meet — a theory that tells you when an arbitrary-looking everyday rule (tip 20%, don't cut the line, don't cross on red) generates a real moral obligation rather than mere prudence, etiquette, or law — with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

fix the purpose first, since adequacy is relative to it: I want a theory good enough for two jobs. (1) it has to tell a short-term visitor, or someone who announces up front that they reject the practice, what they owe. (2) it has to tell a city, a platform, or the crowd at the bus stop whether they may enforce, reproach, or reallocate — not just whether it's true that the rule binds. a theory that only does job 1 is incomplete for me.

here's my candidate list of cases it has to get right, but add, drop, or reframe anything you think is a bad desideratum and say why:

- the no-difference case: compliance is near-universal, so your single act changes nothing — nobody is worse off, no coordination breaks. is there still a reason not to skip the tip or cross on red? a theory whose only tool is marginal consequences looks forced to say "no", and I don't think that's acceptable, but argue with me.
- the announced non-participant: someone who says loudly, in advance, "I'm not tipping" or "I'm not queueing." some conventions seem to dissolve under that announcement (you tear up your deli ticket and leave), others seem stubbornly not to (the tip still feels owed). the theory needs a principled story about which is which.
- the visitor and the dissenter as separate cases: the tourist from a no-tipping country vs. the local who thinks tipping is a racket. ignorance and dissent shouldn't automatically collapse into the same verdict — but it's also not obvious they come apart, and I want to know if separating them is a real desideratum or just a prejudice of people who like consent theories.
- conflicting conventions across jurisdictions: jaywalking is the local pedestrian convention even though it's illegal, and drivers there brake for it; a driver from a town where nobody crosses on red shows up and honks. who owes what to whom, and does the legal rule matter at all?
- the convention resting on an injustice: tipping as wage subsidy, queue position sold by a corrupt clerk. does the obligation survive when the practice itself shouldn't exist, and can a convention obligate you to comply *and* obligate you to work against it?
- near-threshold cases: queues hold at ~90% compliance and collapse into a scrum below it. an adequate theory needs something to say about acting just above or below the threshold, not just "it's a coordination equilibrium, comply."
- exit costs and lock-in: leaving a queue is cheap; exiting a tipping regime isn't, short of never eating out again. is "you could have walked away" even the right test, and for which conventions?
- the enforcement asymmetry: the wrongness of cutting and the permissibility of the crowd shaming the cutter have to be separable. some theories can't pry them apart and I want that flagged.
- the day-one case: a brand-new coffee shop's tip jar, with no one's reliance behind it yet, no expectations formed. does anything bind, and if not, when does it start to?

distinctions it must respect — and mark which ones are load-bearing and which are cosmetic:

- arbitrary-but-binding vs. binding-because-beneficial: it must not explain every convention's force by pointing at the good the practice does; the whole puzzle is that the content (20% rather than 15%, stand here rather than there) looks arbitrary.
- obligation to follow vs. obligation to defer to local practice vs. licence to enforce.
- what you owe strangers vs. what you owe people who have already relied on you — the waiter who has already served vs. the one who hasn't.
- first-personal vs. third-personal reasons: must the theory hand the agent a reason she could actually state and act on, or is an external account enough? I suspect this one is a camp preference dressed up as a requirement.

then the interesting part. which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp wants? which subset do you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable? my guess is that some trio of {no-difference case, announced non-participant dissolves *some* conventions, obligation survives an unjust practice} can't all be held at once, and if so I want you to say what has to give and sketch what the best-surviving-subset theory would look like. and which single requirement excludes the most otherwise-popular accounts — I'd guess the no-difference case kills straightforward reliance/expected-benefit views and the announced-non-participant kills consent and fair-play views, but tell me if I've got it backwards.

after that, a short scorecard (table is fine, two lines each) for five or six contenders: the coordination/equilibrium view, the reliance or expected-benefit view, the consent or fair-play "you took part" view, a joint-commitment or "we're in this together" view, the respect-or-attitude-expression view, and the straight debunking view that says all of this is etiquette with moralized window dressing. keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring — I can re-run the scoring myself, I just don't trust my own list of requirements yet.
