So I've ended up defending a fairly specific version of the higher-order theory of consciousness, and I want someone to try to break it before I build a paper on it. The version: a mental state is conscious iff it is being represented right now by a higher-order state of the right kind, where the right kind is not a belief or a judgement but something non-conceptual and perception-like (an inner-sense reading, minus any requirement that it be accurate), and I read the biconditional constitutively rather than causally — being conscious just is being so represented, so the theory isn't in the business of saying what switches consciousness on or what produces it. Then two further moves I'm much less sure about. First, I bite the bullet on targetless higher-order states: if the lower-order state isn't there, I deny that you get a conscious state whose character is radically misrepresented; you just get a misfire and nothing is conscious. Second, I let the higher-order state be realized in the very same psychological and neural vehicle as the first-order one, which is how I think I stop the regress and the homunculus worry before they even get going.

What I want is a map of how exposed this thing is. Not the greatest hits — I've read the standard objections and I can recite them. What would actually count against it, ranked from fatal down to merely awkward; what it predicts that a first-order or global-workspace or self-representational rival doesn't predict; and which of these a committed higher-order theorist would themself concede as counting against the view versus dismiss as intuition-mongering. I have a specific worry I want you to take seriously: my second move may just quietly turn me into a self-representationalist with extra steps, and if so I want to know what exactly I'd have to give up to avoid the collapse, and whether avoiding it costs me the things the higher-order story was supposed to buy me in the first place (the contrast with unconscious perception, the handling of blindsight, the tie to metacognition and reportability).

Also tell me which defeaters have arguably already arrived rather than being merely possible. On that: I keep coming back to REM sleep, where the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is metabolically dialed way down and metacognition, insight and reality-checking are famously lousy, and yet there is obviously experience happening. How much weight does that actually carry against a theory like mine, and what is my best reply? Same question for the partial-awareness cases, where some feature of a scene seems consciously present but unrepresented by anything higher-order.

Two things about me: I'm least moved by bare intuitions about what we'd say in invented cases, and most moved by (a) some case the view simply cannot describe at all, as opposed to describes counterintuitively, and (b) anything from the neuroscience of metacognition and sleep — so weight those accordingly and be explicit about where evidence runs out and it's all intuition after all. And be honest with me about whether higher-order state of the right kind has quietly become an empty placeholder that can absorb any counterexample handed to it, and if it has, whether that's genuinely a problem for a constitutive theory like mine or just what theories of this shape look like.

Please write in American English, keep it readable for someone who knows the area but isn't deep in it, and don't pad it with a literature survey — I want the exposure map, sorted.
