I ran into Strawson's Basic Argument in a reading group and more or less bought it. Then I tried to rewrite it in my own words and its grown a tail, and now I think it proves a lot more than Strawson claims it does — or at least more than I'm willing to accept. Numbered so you have something to aim at:

(1) I'm morally responsible for an action only if I'm truly the source of it — it comes from me, in a stronger sense than "it came out of my body."
(2) I'm truly the source of an action only if I'm the source of the character, values, and way of weighing reasons that produced it.
(3) I'm the source of my character only if I made myself the way I am: deliberate self-shaping, picking my influences, training my habits, whatever.
(4) Any act of self-shaping issues from the character I already have at the time I do it. So (3) just moves the problem back one step, forever.
(5) So nobody is ever morally responsible for anything, and it doesn't matter whether determinism is true. I can live with this, or I think I can.
(6) Here's the tail. (1)–(4) never used anything specifically moral. The requirement looks like a requirement about *sourcehood*, and sourcehood is what every other "this is hers" property rides on too. So swap in something else: if I'm not the source of my own character, then my being callous rather than generous is like my being tall rather than short — something that happened to me, that I didn't author, and so something that can't be assessed as better or worse in the way we assess what people do.
(7) So the argument doesn't just retire blame, punishment and resentment. It retires the judgment that one character, decision, or life is morally better than another. Nothing anyone does is morally better or worse than what they'd otherwise have done. There's only more harm or less harm.
(8) That I can't accept — not because its grim but because it seems to make the whole discussion, including the discussion where Strawson talks me into (5), pointless.

What pushes me toward thinking something in (1)–(5) rather than (6) is broken is a case I made up, the ouroboros agent. Suppose someone has been revising her own character deliberately since she was old enough to, and suppose every feature of her current character has at some point been revised by her own deliberate act — no trait is simply left over from before. Every revision issued from the character she had at the time she made it, so there's no first self-creating act and Strawson's regress bites, and by the letter of (2) and (3) she isn't a source of anything. But if even *she* isn't a source of her actions, I don't know what would have to be true of anyone. Which makes me think (1) or (2) isn't a demand about sourcehood at all, just a demand no possible agent could meet — and a condition nothing could satisfy can't be what responsibility consists in. Which is another way of saying it proves too much.

So: which step do you reject? If I have to choose I'd rather keep (1)–(5) and drop (6), but I want to know what keeping them costs me, and if you'd drop something on the other side instead, tell me what Strawson loses by it and what I'd have to put in its place. My worry about keeping (1)–(5) is that I then can't say what moral assessment of a person even is, if it isn't a desert claim.
