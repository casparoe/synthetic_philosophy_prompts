"Grice says conversation runs on a kind of unspoken contract. Everyone agrees to follow four rules: say what you believe and don't say what you lack evidence for (Quality), say neither more nor less than is needed (Quantity), stay on topic (Relation), and be clear and orderly (Manner). What a sentence means is fixed by the dictionary and the grammar. But when a speaker breaks one of the rules, the hearer notices something is off, assumes the speaker is still trying to communicate, and works backwards to figure out what he must have meant. That extra bit is the implicature — the implied meaning on top of the literal one. 'Some of the guests left' therefore implicates that not all of them did, and 'it's cold in here' said to a housemate implicates 'shut the window.' Grice's own definition of speaker meaning is just this: to mean something is to intend to get the hearer to believe something by getting him to recognize that intention. So the whole picture is: speaker meaning equals code meaning plus implicature. And implicatures are cancellable — you can always tack on 'but don't read anything into that' and the implicature vanishes — because they're inferences the hearer makes, not anything in the words themselves. He makes an exception for a few words like 'but' and 'therefore,' where the inference has got frozen into the word, which just shows that a conversational implicature can harden into a convention over time. The practical upshot, and the reason I teach it: implicature is basically the mechanics of plausible deniability. Since what you imply is cancellable and isn't part of the sentence's meaning, a speaker can always retreat to 'I never said that' and still be telling the truth."
