I have been working through the accuracy-first programme in formal epistemology — the project of deriving the norms of credence (probabilism, conditionalization, the chance–credence norms) from the single assumption that the fundamental epistemic good is closeness to the truth, via dominance or expected-accuracy arguments over inaccuracy measures. I follow the broad shape of those arguments. What I cannot see is how much of them rests on a structural feature of the measure chosen, which I have only managed to isolate by direct computation. I would like that feature explained, and I would like the intuitive picture as much as the algebra.

Set-up, so that we are using the same notation. Fix a finite partition of possibilities {w₁, …, wₙ}. A credence function c assigns a non-negative real number to each cell, and I do not assume at the outset that the values sum to one. The truth-value vector vᵢ has 1 in the i-th position and 0 elsewhere. Consider two candidate inaccuracy measures:

- Brier: I_B(c, wᵢ) = Σⱼ (vᵢ(j) − c(j))², i.e. squared Euclidean distance to the truth-value vector.
- Log: I_L(c, wᵢ) = −ln c(wᵢ), taken to be +∞ when c(wᵢ) = 0.

Here is what I get when I run the two-cell case and the expectation calculations myself; please correct any arithmetic that is wrong.

1. Take the incoherent c = (0.6, 0.6) over a two-cell partition. Brier: 0.52 at each world; the coherent p = (0.5, 0.5) scores 0.5 at each world, so p dominates c.
2. The same c is not log-dominated by any coherent p: beating −ln 0.6 at both worlds would require both coordinates to exceed 0.6, and a coherent pair sums to one. (The subadditive c = (0.4, 0.4), by contrast, is log-dominated by (0.5, 0.5). So the log score condemns stinginess toward the truth but seems blind to credence inflation.)
3. For any probability q, E_q[I_B(x)] = Σⱼ (xⱼ − qⱼ)² + (1 − Σⱼ qⱼ²), which is minimized uniquely at x = q over all of Rⁿ — so the Brier-based argument need not restrict the competing credence functions to the simplex at all.
4. E_q[I_L(x)] = −Σⱼ qⱼ ln xⱼ is not minimized at x = q once incoherent competitors are allowed. With q = (0.5, 0.5): x = (0.6, 0.6) scores −ln 0.6 ≈ 0.511 against q's own −ln 0.5 ≈ 0.693, and x = (t, t) drives the score to −∞ as t grows.

So the failure is not peculiar to the dominance version of the argument; the expected-accuracy version looks equally hostage to the choice of measure. My questions:

(a) What exactly is the property doing the work? My guess is that the Brier score is sensitive to the whole credence vector — in particular to the total credence distributed across it — whereas the log score is 'local', depending only on what you assign to the true cell. If so, what is the right general statement of the condition an inaccuracy measure must satisfy for the argument for probabilism to go through: strict propriety over the whole non-negative orthant rather than merely over the simplex? And is such a condition, which builds in that the target vectors are exactly the unit-mass truth-value vectors, anything more than probabilism restated as a constraint on the measure?

(b) Is my example an artifact or structural? I would guess the log score is, up to positive affine transformation, the only strictly proper local measure, which would make the failure structural rather than a quirk of one rule. Is that right, and what are the caveats?

(c) Where does this leave the familiar decomposition of the Brier score into a calibration and a refinement component? Is the Brier score's sensitivity to how credence is spread across the false cells just its refinement component showing up in the accuracy argument?

(d) The part that troubles me most. The Brier verdict about how inaccurate a credence function is depends on how finely the space is carved. With w₁ true and credence (0.8, 0.2, 0) over three cells the score is 0.08; with (0.8, 0.1, 0.1) it is 0.06; splitting the residual 0.2 evenly across n false cells gives 0.04 + 0.04/n, which tends to 0.04. The log score is completely insensitive to this, but it is sensitive to refinement of the true cell in the opposite direction: if the true cell is split and the 0.8 divided evenly, the log score worsens from about 0.223 to about 0.916, and the Brier score worsens too. So: relative to which partition is the argument for probabilism run? What are the options — the maximally fine partition into worlds, a canonical partition supplied independently, a requirement that verdicts be invariant under refinement? How does the choice interact with (a), and does the Brier score collapse into something effectively local in the limit of refinement, taking the argument with it?

(e) Finally, the intuitive core: what is the 'shape' of a measure that penalizes both misplacement and mismatched total magnitude, and what is the veritist's positive reason for thinking closeness to the truth should be measured that way rather than locally?
