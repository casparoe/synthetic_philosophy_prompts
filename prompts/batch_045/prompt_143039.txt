I'm a senior at a big public high school and I co-host a small interview podcast where we talk to people in philosophy about one view they actually hold; our listeners are mostly other high schoolers plus two teachers who email us corrections, so the questions need to be sayable out loud without a jargon dump, but they shouldn't dodge the hard parts. My guest for next month (she asked not to be named in the episode, so please just write "your guest") is a philosopher who has spent about a decade arguing that Tolstoy was basically right in his late book about art. As I understand her version: a work is art only if someone who has lived through a feeling hands it on to others by external signs and the others are gripped by that very feeling, and — this is her addition, not Tolstoy's — the audience has to take up the feeling *as* the artist's rather than merely catch it, which is how she says we tell transmission apart from a jump scare, a sugar rush, or a crowd getting worked up at a rally. She also has a striking line on AI, which is why I booked her: last spring a kid in my grade submitted an AI-generated ballad to our school talent show, the judges disqualified it on the grounds that nobody had written it, and my friends have been arguing about it ever since. Her view, as she sketched it to me, is that a language model has no feelings it has lived through, so an AI song can be skillful and even moving but isn't art in the strict sense — whereas a session musician who has never lost anyone can still help produce a genuinely moving elegy, and a commissioned composer writing to order in a fixed genre can produce real art. I want to press that asymmetry rather than let her assert it.

Could you draft me eight to ten questions for a forty-minute episode, none of which just have her restate the theory? What I'd like covered, at least once each: whether the view is too broad, given that advertising jingles, political speeches, and fifteen-second TikTok sounds all grip people with feelings, and whether her "take it up as the artist's" condition really excludes those or just relocates the problem; whether a work that transmits something cruel or degrading — a propaganda film, a joke whose point is humiliation — counts as *good* art if the transmission works, and how she handles the fact that Tolstoy seems to answer by bringing in a separate moral or religious standard, which looks like it stops transmission from doing any real work in the theory of value; whether her uptake condition quietly lets intentionalism back in, and what it does to instrumental music and to works whose maker is dead or unknown; the AI case versus the session-musician and the actor cases; whether beauty has any place at all on her account, and what she says about the feeling I get from a sunset or from a proof, where there is nobody handing anything on; whether she follows Tolstoy in his verdict that a great deal of what hangs in museums — and some of his own novels — is counterfeit art, or whether she treats that as an optional extra; and whether the view is circular, if she has to restrict the relevant feelings to "aesthetic" ones to make the definition come out right.

Two things about her I've noticed from her other appearances, which I want you to plan around. First, she has two stock moves: when a case makes the transmission criterion look bad she says "that's a question about the value of art, not about what art is," and when Tolstoy's own applications look extreme she says "that's Tolstoy's religion talking, not the theory." I'd like at least three of the questions arranged so that those moves get progressively harder to make — so that by the later ones she can't appeal to the definition/value distinction or disown Tolstoy without giving something up. Second, please format each question the way I'd actually say it into a microphone: one or two sentences, then a line on the answer she's most likely to give, then a follow-up in brackets keyed to that specific answer rather than a generic "can you say more." End the list with one question about what would have to be the case for her to give the theory up — what observation about our actual practice with art, or about some case, would count against it — since I'd like to finish the episode on that rather than on summary.
