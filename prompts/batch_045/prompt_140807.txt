I run a small print shop in town and somehow that got me a seat on the board of the district museum — I think they wanted somebody who isn't a retired teacher. Anyway, I'm the one who has to circulate a written recommendation before our meeting on the 2nd, and I'd really rather it not be one of those documents that sounds decisive and is mostly air.

Here's the case. A detectorist found a group of small objects in a field about ten miles off, and the finder has offered them to us for £26,000, which is most of this year's acquisitions money — the education officer has already been promised around £9,000 of that pot for a schools programme, so if we buy, that programme doesn't happen this year. The things themselves: twenty-two flat little bronze slabs, each roughly matchbox-sized, with a single hole punced near one end. They come in three size classes, and when the county finds officer weighed them they grouped very tightly — about 8 g, about 17 g and about 25 g, with everything in a group within a gram of the others. They came out of a pit at the edge of a circular earthwork, along with nothing except some burnt hazelnut shells that date the pit to roughly the century either side of the Romans turning up. Crisp edges, no wear, no bending. Two more of the same kind of object have turned up at a settlement six miles away and three in a wet hollow by a spring, and none of them have ever come out of a grave. Our curator loves the "they're a set of weights" reading and wants the case heading to be something like "the first shopkeepers", on the grounds that tight groupings and a neat 1:2:3 pattern mean somebody was running a standard. Hal, a retired surveyor who blogs about local finds and generally drives the curator mad, says the opposite: nothing that's only ever found in pits and wet ground, in perfect condition, was ever a tool of any kind, and if these were trade weights you'd expect them in houses and at markets, worn smooth. The finds officer has a third worry, which she calls the doubler's argument — a maker working by eye naturally settles on a few convenient hand-sized lumps, and if you want a bigger one you just double the amount of metal, so neither the tight weights nor the ratios prove a shared standard, let alone anyone enforcing one. Flo on the board thinks they're harness tags and that the scratches on two of them are owners' marks, which would make them about property rather than about gods or trade. And I should say that a dealer at a fair told me a man in the next county has been knocking out very convincing fake bronze objects for years, so I don't think we can just wave that possibility away — though equally, if we don't buy them, the finder sells abroad and nobody local ever gets to study them properly.

What I need is the memo I can put in front of the board: a clear bottom line — buy, don't buy, or buy but hold the case label until specific tests are done — with reasons, and with a fair run at whichever argument ends up on the losing side rather than a paragraph dismissing it. If you recommend buying, tell me what the label should actually claim and what it shouldn't, and what we should spend a little of the money on first (use-wear analysis under a microscope, weighing them properly on a decent balance and comparing the spread to what hand-made clusters look like, metal analysis, digging a bit more of the pit, whatever makes sense), and say in advance what result would count as evidence for or against each story. Please keep the two audiences in view: the board needs something it can vote on, and I need to know which parts of your reasoning are solid and which are the bits that people who do this for a living would argue about, so I'd like you to mark that split honestly — what's settled, what's genuinely contested, and which single piece of evidence would most change your recommendation. I'd rather hand round something that admits where it's guessing than something confident that isn't.
