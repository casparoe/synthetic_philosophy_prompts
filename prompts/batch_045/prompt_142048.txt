I sit on the Shared Research Facilities Committee at my university, and we've got a proper fight on our hands about how to allocate time on the cryo-EM. At the moment groups put in an application, we read forty-odd of them twice a year and rank them on 'scientific merit and need'; its miserable, the applications all read the same after a while, and I suspect half of what we decide is really about who wrote the best paragraph. One member has proposed we stop ranking altogether: give every group the same annual pot of 'facility credits', let them bid for slots, let whatever isn't spent lapse at the end of the year, and cut our job down to deciding how many slots exist and vetting for safety and ethics. A friend of mine who did his PhD in political theory heard about it and said, insufferably, that this is basically what some economist called Lange proposed ages ago and that Hayek had already shown it couldn't work — I gather the thought is that the people who know what the machine is worth to them aren't the people who can put that in a form, and that a price does something an application can't. I don't want to turn this into a seminar on Hayek, but I do need whatever is true in that argument, in our terms, because I have to recommend something by November.

Two local things make it messier than the abstract version. The registry has just brought in a rule that every allocation decision has to be explained in writing to whoever didn't get the slot, so 'someone bid more than you' either has to be an acceptable answer or we need a way of making it one — and I genuinely can't tell whether the credits are giving us reasons or just laundering the decision. Also, groups have already started swapping credits among themselves informally and we haven't decided whether to allow that or ban it, and we can't agree whether the credit price of a slot should be fixed for the year or nudged up and down until the slots all clear.

The bit of my friend's argument I half believe is this: the case for credits isn't that they'll produce the best allocation, because nobody can say in advance what the best allocation even is — that's rather the whole difficulty — but that they'd be a way of finding out things we currently just don't know, like that a group would have been perfectly happy with February instead. If that's the honest case then a pilot can't be judged on whether the outcome looked optimal in hindsight. Can you draft the one-page decision memo I'll circulate before the November meeting: a clear recommendation between the two options, set up as a two-term pilot with a review date. Please give it as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no sign-off, in roughly this order — (1) the recommendation, (2) the single main reason for it, (3) the strongest thing the other side would say and why it doesn't carry, (4) what we do about the written-reasons rule and about credit swapping, and (5) what we'd look at to decide whether to keep it.
