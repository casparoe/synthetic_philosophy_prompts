I'm applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need help turning a half-formed idea into a tightly scoped two-page proposal. The project sits at the intersection of philosophical logic and philosophy of mind, and it starts from the analogy Hofstadter develops in Gödel, Escher, Bach between self-referential formulas—especially a Gödel sentence that indirectly says of itself that it is not provable in its system—and self-aware minds, which he suggests arise when a system's self-representation becomes a strange loop or tangled hierarchy rather than a flat hierarchy of levels. I was discussing this with a friend who builds computational models of cognition, and we kept talking past each other: she treated the analogy as merely suggestive, while I kept wanting to know whether it could be turned into an argument. The literature as I loosely recall it has the same stalemate: critics say the leap from syntactic self-reference to consciousness is just a metaphor, while enthusiasts write as if a formal system's incompleteness already contains the seed of an I.

My half-formed idea is that the impasse comes from treating self-reference as a single property, and I want to split it into two stages. Call the first the coding stage: a system encodes a representation of itself, the way Gödel numbering lets a formula refer to its own provability or the way a system might store a self-model. Call the second the consumption stage: the system's own downstream processes actually read and use that self-representation in a feedback loop, so that the system's future behavior depends on its own self-description in a way that makes the hierarchy genuinely tangled and cannot be flattened into a straight input-output chain. I suspect that formal axiomatic systems and simple programs can instantiate the coding stage but not the consumption stage, whereas self-aware minds (and perhaps some future artifacts) instantiate both, and that the real philosophical question is whether the consumption stage can be made precise without collapsing into either mere causal feedback on the one hand or a mysterious emergent property on the other. This two-stage distinction is the angle I want the proposal to take, and I want to be explicit that this is a conceptual project that does not depend on building a working artificial system.

Please write the proposal document itself, addressed to a fellowship committee. It should open by stating the precise question the dissertation would answer—a question with a determinate answer, not a vague gesture at exploring the analogy—and then explain why the existing positions on the Gödelian analogy leave that question open. It should lay out the method: what I would actually do, month by month, over the fellowship year, to make progress on whether the consumption stage can be formalized and whether doing so would vindicate, refute, or merely reframe Hofstadter's analogy, and it should say why this coding-versus-consumption approach is more promising than the obvious alternatives, such as surveying metaphors of self-reference or trying to implement a toy strange loop. The proposal should also say what the possible conclusions are, which considerations would decide between them, and what would count as the project failing—for instance, if the consumption stage turns out to be trivial or irreducibly metaphorical. It should give an honest assessment of scope: what can realistically be finished in one year and what would have to be left for later. Make the case that the work is worth doing and can be done in the time available, but do not include a bibliography, a literature review by citation, or any footnotes; refer to positions and arguments only in general terms, and I will add the citations myself. The tone should be rigorous and clear about the stakes, while remaining candid about the risks.
