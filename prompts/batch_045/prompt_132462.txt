Eight months ago I was made lead of the three-person forecasting team at a regional flood-risk and water-resources agency. We produce seasonal outlooks that a board — two civil engineers, a hydrologist, and two elected members — signs off on, and that are then used to set reservoir release rules, to schedule levee works, and, in perhaps two years out of ten, to decide whether to evacuate. We refresh the models twice a year, and each refresh puts twenty to thirty candidate regularities in front of me, each of which somebody has to approve or reject in about twenty minutes.

Last spring one of ours failed publicly. A model that had backtested beautifully against thirty-one years of record collapsed in its first live season, and the post-mortem could not say what we should have done differently: the pattern had genuinely been in the data, nobody had falsified it, and our usual tests — out-of-sample fit, cross-validation on held-out years, a holdout on the most recent decade — all passed. A friend sent me to Nelson Goodman, and I think I now see why: we had built the forecast on a predicate that fitted the record exactly as well as the ordinary one and that no amount of past data could have distinguished from it, a grue-style predicate whose content is fixed partly by where our own record ends. What I cannot do is turn that into something my team can actually run.

What I need from you is a procedure, not an explanation: a written checklist my team can work through on any candidate regularity before it is allowed into a model. Give me the questions in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure terminates in a refusal to extrapolate, and the points at which it has to stop and hand the question to the board because what remains is a judgement about how much risk the agency will carry rather than a judgement about evidence. Mark the steps that are really judgement calls in procedural clothing, and have the analyst initial those.

Constraints it has to respect:

1. It must be written in the vocabulary we already use — features, deployment window, holdout period, sensor swaps, protocol revisions, interventions — and must not smuggle in philosophical terms my team would have to be taught before they could apply it.
2. It must not rest on the thought that a grue-style predicate is somehow less well supported by the data we already have. It is not; my team will notice immediately if the procedure implies otherwise, and I will lose them. The procedure has to isolate whatever it is, besides past fit, that separates the pattern we may project from the pattern we may not.
3. It must answer my deputy's standing objection. She says that appealing to how entrenched a predicate is — the ordinary colour predicate is projectible because predicates like it have been projected before — is institutional conservatism with a philosophical decoration, and that it would have told us to ignore every genuine regime shift in our record. Either answer her, or concede the point and say what replaces entrenchment in the checklist.
4. It has to handle time-indexed features in general. Calendar year, row index, years since certification, firmware version, collected after the 2011 station move: each lets a model encode a grue-style predicate without anyone ever writing one down, and I cannot simply ban them, because several carry real signal about instrument drift and land-use change. Where is the line, and how do I tell which side of it a given feature falls on?
5. It must say what to do when the information a step needs does not exist. We often cannot recover whether a sensor was recalibrated in 1994, or what the volunteer observer in 1978 actually recorded.
6. It must tell me where it will misfire on data like ours: short overlapping series, instruments patched together across decades, a catchment we are ourselves altering with a bypass channel, and a board that reads any refusal to extrapolate as cowardice.

Then apply it, step by step, to three candidates currently in my queue:

(a) Turbidity spikes at a gauging station have preceded every recorded algal bloom in the reservoir by eleven to fourteen days, across thirty-four years. The turbidity sensor was replaced in 2011; before that the series comes from a different instrument with a different sampling frequency.

(b) A gradient-boosted model that gets most of its skill from a single feature: the number of years since the measurement protocol was last revised. It increases monotonically, it correlates with nearly everything, and at deployment it will be one year larger than at any point in training.

(c) The lower catchment has never produced two consecutive winters above the one-in-twenty flow. Our asset-management plan, already submitted to the regulator, treats this as a hard constraint on the maintenance schedule, and the board has built a budget bid around it. I suspect an honest procedure will tell me this is a predicate whose content is fixed by the length of our record rather than by the behaviour of the river. If that is what your procedure says, say so plainly, and tell me what, if anything, I can put in front of the board in its place.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. I do not want a checklist that lets the board feel the problem has been handled, when the part that actually bites — that we are extrapolating into a catchment we are in the middle of changing — is the part no checklist settles.
