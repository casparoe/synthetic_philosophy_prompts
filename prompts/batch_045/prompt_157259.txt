I interview people for a small grad-student run philosophy-of-science podcast, and next week's guest is an invented-but-plausible philosopher, Dr. Mara Voss, who defends a manipulationist theory of causation (roughly: X causes Y just in case Y would change under a hypothetical intervention on X that leaves everything else alone except via X) and whose new book applies that account to structural and social causation — whether a zoning ordinance caused a city's segregation, whether a hiring algorithm caused a pay gap, claims like that. Draft me seven or eight questions that go past her restating the view, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to make: one on whether the "leaves everything else alone" clause is itself causal and so circular, or whether it's just a coherence constraint on a whole causal model; one on cases where no intervention, even a hypothetical one, looks surgical because the variable is entangled with everything else (fat-handed interventions, social ones or quantum ones); one on causation by social kinds that shift when people are classified under them, so that intervening on the kind changes what even falls under it; one on simultaneous overdetermination and trumping preemption, where a finer-grained variable rescues the verdict but seems to make causation depend on how we carve things up; one on whether her view really dispenses with Lewis-style counterfactuals or has just regimented them with extra machinery; one on double prevention and omissions; and one on whether the theory is metaphysics or only a methodology for designing experiments, and whether that leaves a gap with what we ordinarily mean by cause. Write it all as continuous prose — no headings, bullets, bold, numbering or tables — just questions and follow-ups seperated by line breaks, and finish with a short paragraph telling me how confident you are that these really are the standard pressure points on this view and which single question rests on the shakiest reading of her position.
