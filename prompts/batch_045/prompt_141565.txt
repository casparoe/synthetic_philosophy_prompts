I'm a graduate student in a history of medicine seminar, and we just spent a week on Thomas Szasz (1920–2012), primarily his 1961 book The Myth of Mental Illness and his later works such as Ceremonial Chemistry and Our Right to Drugs. I find myself confused about how to apply his framework to a set of controversies that emerged only after his death. The other night, some friends and I were debating a hypothetical that a classmate proposed: suppose that, in the near future, a DSM task force proposes a new psychiatric diagnosis called 'compulsive digital engagement disorder,' defined by distress and functional impairment related to the use of social media and AI companion apps. Proponents cite functional neuroimaging studies claiming to show a characteristic pattern of brain activation, and they argue that the condition qualifies as a medical disease because it involves a measurable neurological signature. Around the same time, a company receives regulatory approval to market a prescription-only, large-language-model-powered chatbot that delivers cognitive behavioral therapy for this condition; the chatbot is covered by insurance, and some policymakers further propose that individuals who meet severity criteria but decline the chatbot should be required to use it under a scheme of outpatient civil commitment. Based on Szasz's actual arguments—his insistence that, without demonstrated neurological disease, psychiatric diagnoses are better understood as problems in living rather than diseases, his critique of medical language as an instrument of social control, his principled opposition to involuntary psychiatric treatment, and his libertarian defense of the rights of consenting adults to make their own choices, including regarding psychoactive substances—how would he have been likely to respond to 'compulsive digital engagement disorder' and to the mandated chatbot intervention?

In particular, I'd like you to distinguish several things. First, would Szasz have accepted the neuroimaging evidence as establishing a genuine disease, or would he have treated the diagnosis as a moral or social judgment in medical disguise? Second, given his views in Ceremonial Chemistry and Our Right to Drugs, would he have seen the chatbot as a form of state- or insurance-subsidized coercion, a legitimate tool for consenting adults, or something else entirely? Third, where would he have drawn the line between the voluntary psychotherapy he always defended and the coercive practices he attacked—and would the fact that the intervention is 'digital' rather than pharmacological change anything in his analysis? Please answer as if you are writing a short guest lecture for my seminar: reason from the internal logic of Szasz's position rather than from later psychiatric consensus, avoid simply asserting what contemporary critics say, and be explicit about which element of the scenario he would have found most objectionable and which, if any, he might have surprised my friends by tolerating.
