I'm in the final year of secondary school and I run the school astronomy club. Each term we put up a single-page handout in the science corridor, and this term's is on the Fermi paradox. My difficulty is that every version of this I can find, and every version my club members draft on their own, is the same list of candidate solutions with the same one-line verdicts attached, ordered by nothing in particular. I want the handout to actually rank something, and I want the ranking to be earned rather than recalled. So I have set the problem up as follows.

Here are the eight candidate explanations we have collected, in my own wording and under my own labels:

1. Rare Earth. Complex life, and intelligence in particular, requires an unusual conjunction of circumstances; the Galaxy holds at most one or two technological species, and we are one of them.
2. Hard step behind us. One transition on the way to intelligence, whether abiogenesis or complex cells or something else of that kind, is so improbable that it has effectively happened once, and everything after it is comparatively easy.
3. Filter ahead of us. Technological species reliably destroy themselves, or are destroyed, soon after becoming detectable, so the Galaxy contains ruins and absences rather than neighbours.
4. They stay home. Interstellar travel is physically possible but never worth what it costs; long-lived civilizations grow inward rather than outward, and nobody fills the Galaxy.
5. They went quiet. Civilizations that last do not stay loud: they move to low power, to small scales, to machine or simulated existence, and there is nothing left for our instruments to see.
6. The zoo. They are here, or nearby, and the absence of evidence is the product of a deliberate policy of concealment whose violations we are in no position to detect.
7. We are early. Intelligence appears when galactic history makes it possible, those conditions have only recently been met, and we are among the first rather than among the last.
8. We are looking wrong. We search for narrow-band beacons in our own idiom, on our own timescales, for a few decades; a species that communicates differently, or that never builds beacons at all, would be invisible to every search we have run.

Now the stipulation. Suppose that in the early 2030s an atmospheric survey returns an unambiguous technosignature from a terrestrial planet seventy-one light-years away: a compound in its atmosphere with no abiotic source at the temperatures involved, in an atmosphere that also carries oxygen. Suppose the host star formed roughly 1.8 billion years before the Sun, so that any biosphere there had a head start of that order. Suppose that over the following thirty years nothing else turns up: deeper surveys of the nearest five thousand stars find no second case; all-sky infrared surveys rule out any process converting more than a small fraction of the Galaxy's starlight into waste heat; targeted listening at the one known planet detects no artificial signal; and nothing artificial is found anywhere in the Solar System. In short, one confirmed neighbour in a system with a head start measured in billions of years, and otherwise total silence and no visible engineering at any scale.

What I want is a ranking of the eight that this scenario actually does some work on. Please score each from 0 to 10 on each of two separate axes, and then give me one combined tier list.

Axis one, earned versus borrowed: to what extent would this explanation already have led us to expect roughly this pattern before the observations came in, rather than being adjusted afterwards to accommodate them? High scores go to explanations that were committed to something like this in advance; low scores to ones that only fit once you are allowed to hear the news first.

Axis two, cost of fitting: how much additional machinery does the explanation need in order to absorb the details, especially the head start of roughly a billion years with no visible consequences anywhere, and the emptiness at five thousand stars? High scores go to explanations that absorb the findings with little added apparatus. Note that an explanation which could absorb any finding whatsoever should score low here rather than high: if it cannot be embarrassed by any observation, that is a cost, not a virtue.

Then give me the combined result as tiers, top, middle and bottom, with one sentence of justification per entry, and a strict one-to-eight order only if you think a strict order is genuinely defensible. Please flag any entry where the two scores pull in opposite directions, and say how you resolved the conflict. Finally, tell me whether any of the eight stops being an answer to the question we started with once a neighbour is confirmed, and if so what question it is answering instead.

The constraints on your answer are the constraints on the handout. It is one page, so one sentence per entry. My club members will read the reasoning and argue with it, so it has to be followable by someone who has taken physics but not philosophy, and it has to be about this stipulated case rather than about which explanation is most plausible in the abstract.
