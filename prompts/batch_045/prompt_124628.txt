Here is my situation. I am a third-year PhD student drafting the background section of a chapter on higher-order theories, and I also run the reading group for our graduate seminar, for which I have to write a one-paragraph handout characterization of the view. While looking for something accessible to cite to my students, I came across the following passage in a well-reviewed trade-press popular-science book on consciousness (I will not name the author; they are a competent popularizer, not a crank, which is partly why I am unsure how much to trust it):

"Higher-order theories say that what makes a mental state conscious is that the mind has a further state directed at it — a thought, or something like an inner perception, to the effect that one is now in that state. Consciousness is therefore a form of self-monitoring: a mental state becomes conscious when the mind notices itself having it, in the way you notice your own thoughts when you stop and introspect. Since the noticing state has to represent a mental state as such, the theory is committed to saying that only creatures possessing the concept of a mental state can be conscious at all — no concepts, no consciousness — which is why the view entails that newborn infants and probably all non-human animals have no conscious experience whatsoever. The theory is also, despite appearances, a theory of access rather than of feeling: it tells you which states are available for report and for reasoning, not what makes them feel like anything, and so it quietly changes the subject from consciousness proper to reportability. Its commitments also do not stop with creatures like us: any system that carries information about its own internal states — a thermostat wired up to track its own sensor, say — qualifies as conscious on this view. And there is a deeper structural problem. The higher-order state is itself a mental state, so it too should need a further state to make it conscious, and so on without end; the theory is either viciously regressive, or it has to admit that some mental states are conscious all on their own, in which case it has conceded to its opponents exactly what they were arguing for."

My instincts, for what they are worth, are that the "introspect"/"notice" gloss is doing illegitimate work, that the thermostat sentence is a straw man, and that the claim about infants and animals is stated as an entailment when it is at best a contested commitment of some versions. But I cannot say precisely where the passage stops being a fair popularization and starts being a caricature, and I do not trust my instincts here.
