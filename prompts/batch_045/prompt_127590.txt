So I don't have any academic background — I do scheduling for a freight company — but me and a friend have been going back and forth on this for maybe a year now, mostly in voice notes, and we finally decided it would be more useful to write something together than to keep loosing the same argument. Can you draft it for us?

The argument is about AI takeoff speeds. He thinks there's going to be a sharp jump: at some point a system gets good enough to do real AI research, that makes the next one better, the loop closes, and the whole thing goes close to vertical — weeks or months, not decades. So there's basically no long period where the world is run by only mildly superhuman systems. I think that's a story about software and it mostly ignores how long it takes to turn knowing something into having done it. I keep calling it the two-clocks problem: there's the clock on how fast capability improves, and there's a second clock on how fast capability gets converted into actual power in the world — factories, robots, permits, supply chains, regulators, people actually being willing to hand things over. His answer is that a smart enough system just shortens the second clock too, by designing the robots and talking its way past the regulators. My answer is that shortening isn't the same as eliminating, and that a lot of the steps on the second clock are slow for reasons that have nothing to do with intelligence. And then we're stuck again.

So write the document we should have produced: a joint statement from an adversarial collaboration between a fast/sharp-takeoff person and a gradualist. I want:

- A numbered section of claims both of us can sign. Please don't let them be mush like AI will be important or the future is hard to predict — I want the kind of claim at least one of us would have argued with a year ago, the sort of thing that actually costs something to agree to.
- The real disagreements, stated precisely, and each one phrased so that whoever holds that view would read it and say yeah that's mine, rather than saying that's a cartoon of what I think.
- The cruxes: the sub-questions where settling one would genuinely move at least one of us. Several should come out of the two-clocks thing. Another one I want in there is how long a system could be much better than us at one narrow thing — say ML engineering — while being barely better at most other things, and whether that gap can persist or basically can't. I've started calling that the apprenticeship gap, maybe there's a proper name, don't worry about it. And if you think there's a crux we've both missed, put it in and say why it matters more than the ones we found.
- For each crux, one concrete near-future case or hypothetical observation that both of us would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance so neither of us can wriggle afterward. Make the predictions as checkable as you can — rough numbers or rough time windows. For example: what does each of us predict happens to the total research output of a big lab in the first two years after AI systems can do end-to-end ML engineering work at the level of a decent human engineer — 2x, 10x, 100x, and over what window?
- For each crux, what each side would count as evidence against their own view. I want the honest version, where both of us can actually be wrong, not the version where every outcome confirms whoever is saying it.
- And wherever the argument is really just about words — whether fast means weeks or decades, whether takeoff describes capabilities or power — say which part is verbal and what the real question underneath it is.

Format: numbered agreed claims first, then a table for the disagreements and cruxes with columns for his prediction and mine. Call us Sharp and Gradual. Somewhere around 900 to 1100 words. Please don't make it read like a survey article and don't cite or reference any papers or authors — the point is what the two of us could put our names to, not who said what first.
