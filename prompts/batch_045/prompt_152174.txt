hey — i'm in a 2nd year political philosophy seminar and we each have to run one of the student-led sessions. Mine is the Rawls week (original position, veil of ignorance, difference principle), 75 minutes, about 22 people, and I can't use real money — I can hand out participation credit and maybe a chocolate bar or two. Everyone will have already done the reading, so assume they can recite "inequalities are justified only if they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged" back at me.

What I want is an actual simulation, not a discussion. And the thing I want them to run into is the indexing problem: that "least advantaged" has to be measured in something, and that whoever comes out bottom by that measure need not be bottom in the way that actually matters. Right now my section reads the difference principle as basically "be generous to the poor" and I don't think they've felt why the choice of index is doing real work.

Here's the wrinkle I've been kicking around — tell me if it's a bad idea and what to do instead. Each person gets a "life" with TWO numbers on it: an income, and a second thing that matters but isn't income (I was thinking free hours per week, or a health/security score, or authority at work — you pick and justify it). Behind the veil they don't know which life is theirs, they only know the list of lives and that they'll get one at random. Then they have to agree on a rule that redistributes income — the catch being I only let them write a rule that mentions ONE of the two numbers. After the vote I reveal the second column and they discover that the person their rule did least for — the bottom of the income ranking — is not at the bottom overall, and may in fact be doing great on the other thing, while somebody middling by income is genuinely worst off once both count. Then they have to argue about whether their rule was unjust or merely unlucky, and whether any rule written in one currency could have been safe against both.

what I need:

- mechanics: how lives get assigned without me hand-making 22 bespoke cards (a deck of cards? dice? a printed grid?), whether people deliberate individually or in groups, how many rounds, and a minute-by-minute on a 75-minute clock
- a payoff scheme that makes maximin genuinely rational behind the veil rather than just "be nice", and where maximizing the average is a live temptation rather than a strawman — half the room will want to average and I want that to be a real option with a real cost, not something I stack the deck against
- the reveal: what goes on the board, in what order, so the "oh" actually lands
- a debrief mapping specific moments in the game onto (a) why the veil changes the reasoning at all, (b) the problem of what index to use for primary goods, (c) why the rule that comes out is maximin-ish rather than average-maximizing, and (d) what my two-currency setup actually shows versus what it only looks like it shows
- an honest list of what will go wrong the first time I run it. I'm guessing: someone refuses the fiction and argues about the real world, someone writes a rule that tries to name both currencies and I have to decide whether that's cheating or brilliant, and someone points out the whole thing is rigged because I picked the second numbers. Tell me how to handle each and which of those is actually a good objection I should encourage rather than deflect
- be frank about what the game cannot reproduce about Rawls's argument — the bit I'll have to say out loud in the last five minutes because the simulation can't do it — and flag anywhere your design leans on a contested reading or just takes liberties to stay playable

Don't give me three possible payoff schemes and tell me to choose. Pick one, give reasons, and say what it costs you. And end the whole thing with a last line beginning "Verdict:" that says, in one sentence, the single thing participants should walk out having felt that the reading could not have told them.
