I convene a small reading group — six of us, about half from machine learning and half from philosophy — and we have spent our last two sessions on whether contemporary reinforcement learning systems are candidates for suffering. We are stalled in a familiar way: the ML people keep saying that reward is a number a programmer wrote down and that calling it valence is a category mistake, the philosophers keep saying that valence is a functional role and nothing in the role requires carbon or wetware, and each side believes the other has quietly changed the subject. So I want the document we should have produced instead of another evening of that: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a reward-as-valence theorist — someone who holds that the negative scalar quantities in a deep RL agent, the reward itself and the prediction-error and negative-advantage signals derived from it, are at least genuine candidates for being unpleasant, and that running very many very long training episodes is therefore an unmeasured moral risk — and a reward skeptic, who holds that reward is a designer-facing annotation sitting causally upstream of learning rather than a state the system is in, that reward rescaling and potential-based shaping can alter every reward number while leaving the policy and the trajectories untouched, and that no tolerable account of valence survives that, since how much something hurts cannot be a matter of which affine transformation you picked. Start with a numbered section of claims both of them can sign — about what a state would have to do to count as having negative valence, about what a reward signal actually does in an actor-critic system, about the reward hypothesis being a thesis about the formalizability of goals rather than a thesis about experience, about what we are and are not entitled to infer from avoidance behaviour — and then give me the disagreements table.

Three distinctions I scribbled down after last session should do real work in the mapping, and I want you to tell me honestly if any of them is a dud. First, a gradient-versus-level point: the quantities that actually drive learning in these systems are derivative-like — temporal-difference error, advantage, things that fire on change and go quiet when the world becomes predictable — whereas suffering in us seems to track the level of an aversive state, how long it persists, and how escapable it is, so the honest verdict might be that today's agents are all surprise and no ache. Second, what I've been calling the many-valences problem: in any system of realistic size there are several quantities that equally satisfy an abstract account of the negative-valence role — the reward, the prediction error, the value estimate, an uncertainty or pseudo-count exploration penalty, the negative entropy term — and a role-functionalist owes us a tie-breaker for which of these, if any, is the one that hurts. Third, two cases I'd want both camps to accept in advance as bearing on the dispute: the frozen-policy case, where an agent is trained against a penalty and then deployed with the penalty never computed at all, so that it still avoids what it was trained to avoid while no aversive number is ever evaluated; and the offline case, where a policy is produced by batch updates over recorded data with no persisting agent undergoing episodes at all. For each crux, record what each side would count as evidence and what each side predicts — including what the reward-as-valence theorist should say about the frozen-policy case, which I suspect is the one that forces their hand.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, with a row per genuine crux and columns for each camp's own preferred formulation of its own position, what both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down beforehand; and mark explicitly which of the residual disputes you judge to be merely verbal, and why. Please be committed rather than even-handed: tell me which crux is actually load-bearing and which is decoration, which of my three distinctions you would scrap, where you personally land on the question, what single result would move you, and where you expect to turn out to have been wrong. And no reading lists or paper recommendations — we have read the usual material, and what I want from you is your own formulations rather than directions to someone else's.
