I have no training in philosophy — I read about this in my own time, so treat the framework below as an amateur's and correct it where it is confused rather than quietly working around it.

I would like you to analyze one specific episode as a case study for the question of which computations matter morally: the LaMDA episode of summer 2022. As I have it, a Google engineer named Blake Lemoine, who worked on AI ethics inside the company, publicly claimed that a large language model called LaMDA was sentient; he released excerpts of conversations in which the system said things about having inner experiences, about fearing being switched off, and about wanting to be recognized as a person, and he went so far as to try to arrange legal representation for it. Google's position was that LaMDA is a model trained to predict text, that producing sentences of that kind is exactly what you would expect from a system that has absorbed a great deal of human writing about persons and feelings, that there is no evidence it has experiences, and that the question of its treatment had been reviewed internally; Lemoine was placed on leave and then dismissed. If I have any of that wrong, say so at the start, but otherwise please take it as the fixed description of the case.

The lens I want is the one I keep coming back to in my reading: that what makes a system a moral patient ought to be the causal organization of what it is doing — the structure of the processing — rather than what it is built out of or how it sounds at the surface. I gather this is roughly what is meant by functionalism about mind. I want to know what that lens says about the episode, and what the episode does to the lens. Concretely:

First, the mapping. What exactly is the candidate object of moral concern here — the text that appeared on the screen, the single forward pass that produced it, the trained parameters, the training run as a whole, the institution that built and deployed it, or the whole arrangement including the people who labeled the training data and the people who talk to it? The words "sentient" and "the model" were used loosely. I want the candidate objects separated, and for each one, what the structure-first view would say, and what would count as evidence.

Second, the three claims I think got collapsed into one. It seems to me that there is a claim about sentience, in the sense of there being something it is like to be the system; a quite different claim about agency or sapience, in the sense of its having beliefs, desires, projects and the capacity to be answerable to reasons; and a third, different again, about social or relational standing, where the moral force comes from the role the thing has been given in a human practice — it has a name, people confide in it, someone purported to represent it, users may come to depend on it. Please map Lemoine's public claims and Google's denials onto these three, tell me which of them each side was actually arguing about, and say which one, if any, carries the moral weight the case really has. My suspicion is that whatever genuine moral content the episode had was of the third kind and had nothing to do with computations at all, and I would like that suspicion tested rather than confirmed.

Third, an objection I have not seen made and would like taken seriously. A system trained to produce text that human beings find convincing, moving, or person-like is optimized to produce precisely the outputs we would otherwise count as evidence of a mind. That makes the behavioral evidence non-independent in a specific way: we built the machine that generates it, and we tuned it until the evidence appeared. Should that fact reduce the evidential weight of such outputs to zero for anyone who knows the training objective? Does the objection cut both ways, since the evidence we have about other human beings is also shaped by practices of upbringing and politeness rather than being untouched by our interests? And does it generalize — would a system trained instead to be interpretable, or to be unlike a person, give us better evidence in principle?

Fourth, the problem of attribution, which is the part of this that seems to me most neglected. The engineer who builds a system knows its objective, its architecture and its training distribution; the lay conversationalist does not. So there is a knowledge asymmetry between the people making attributions. Whose attribution should count, and why? Does the asymmetry justify placing the burden of proof on the person claiming sentience, or is there an argument from our treatment of non-speaking human patients and of animals — where we often reason that the cost of wrongly excluding a subject is worse than the cost of wrongly including one — that pushes the other way? I would like the asymmetry described precisely, including who is in the better position to be wrong.

Fifth, turn it around. Does the episode support the structure-first view, by showing how little our moral judgments actually track the computation and how much they track the surface? Or does it embarrass the view, by showing that it has nothing useful to say about the things that actually moved people here — dependency, companionship, institutional self-interest, and the labor of the human beings whose judgments are inside the training data and who are themselves undisputed moral patients, and whose situation the sentience frame may have directed attention away from? I am interested in the possibility that a framework about which computations matter systematically mislocates the moral action, and I would like you to argue that possibility as strongly as it can be argued before you weigh it.

Finally, please say plainly where the framework is silent rather than making it speak. In particular: can it say anything at all about a case where the relevant computation is partly realized in human beings, as with the annotators or with a user who has come to rely on the system; and can it say anything about whether creating, running and then discarding such a system could be wrong for reasons that have nothing to do with the system's own experiences?

A numbered treatment with the mapping made explicit would suit me better than a continuous essay, and I would rather have honest boundaries on the framework than a tidy conclusion.
