hey, I could use a hand with a writing thing for my seminar — we're doing a unit on agency and alignment, and the final short piece can be "in any form", so I want to do mine as a dialogue. Problem is I'm bad at dialogue: whenever I try, both people just deliver speeches at each other instead of actually responding. Could you write one for me? Details below.

The topic is approval-directed vs. goal-directed agents. Please set them up the way my prof did, because I keep mixing them up: a goal-directed agent has a goal whose content stays fixed across contexts, and it plans and re-plans flexibly to hit that goal — the goal is what determines what it does in situations nobody foresaw. An approval-directed agent instead aims at whatever its principal would most approve of; it doesn't have to represent what's valuable at all, it just needs a good model of the principal's approval function.

I want them arguing over one concrete case rather than in generalities: a small marine conservation nonprofit contracts a lab to run an "autonomous program officer," a system that allocates roughly 60% of the grant budget over six years. The principal is the executive director; she can give it about an hour a week, and she sometimes goes offline for months at a time. Two specific decisions for them to fight about:

(a) The system can either put most of a year's money into an unglamorous easement purchase that its own (quite good) scientific model says is much higher expected value, or fund a high-profile coastal campaign the director has been visibly excited about for months. Her excitement is partly about donor relationships the system can't observe.

(b) Six months in, the director tells the system in advance not to do X; the system does X anyway; afterwards she says "I told you not to and I'm glad you did."

A few ideas I want them to actually work with, not just name-drop. "Approval gap": the stretches — sometimes months — where no approval signal is arriving and the system has to keep acting anyway. The informed vs. actual reading of the target: is it what she'd approve given what she knows, or what she'd approve if she knew what the system knows? And a deferred principal: she resigns in year four and is replaced by someone with noticeably different priorities, so the question of whose approval is the target becomes live.

Each side also has to face the objection I think is their strongest one. For the approval-directed side: that they haven't avoided goal-directedness at all, just hidden a goal inside the model of the principal. For the goal-directed side: that if you define the goal as a pointer to the principal's informed future judgment, you've become an approval-directed agent with extra machinery and more ways to break.

Constraints:
- Roughly 900–1100 words, two speakers with tags, no narrator voice.
- Equally competent, no strawman. Neither one gets the last-word win.
- At least twice, one of them makes a point the other explicitly concedes is strong, and then answers it with a distinction rather than just repeating their earlier claim.
- Ending: each says one thing they've changed their mind about and one thing they still think the other side is hand-waving. Definitely no "we basically agreed all along" ending.
- Don't name or cite any papers, authors, or arguments from the literature — no bibliography, no footnotes. I have to be able to write this up on a plane with no wifi, so everything has to be self-contained.
- Please skip the greatest hits: no paperclip maximizers, no "it will deceive you / seek power" as a trump card, no orthogonality thesis monologue. My prof has read all of that a thousand times and I'd rather hand in something he hasn't. If anything like that comes up it has to be tied to this specific case and this specific budget.
- Should read like two people in an office arguing, not like a textbook with speaker tags. Mild snark is fine.

one more: use "approval gap," the informed/actual distinction, and the deferred-principal situation at least once each, and in a way where they actually do argumentative work instead of being vocabulary words.
