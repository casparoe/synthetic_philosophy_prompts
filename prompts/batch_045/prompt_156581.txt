hey — I work on safety cases for autonomous systems at a regulator (mostly rail and maritime, some drone stuff), and I keep getting pulled into the same argument with a friend who's a control engineer. He thinks the whole "responsibility gap" thing is philosophers reinventing a problem that engineers and courts have been handling forever: when something goes wrong and nobody knew or could have known, you don't blame anyone, you fix the system, and if compensation is needed you hang it on the operator or the firm via strict liability. I think there's something left over. My credence that there's a genuinely distinct problem here is around 0.6; his is around 0.15. To make that a real bet: count the question as resolved "yes" if, looking back in 25 years, someone competent would say the debate produced at least one conceptual distinction or normative requirement that wasn't already available in the pre-existing machinery of excuses, strict liability, and organisational responsibility — and "no" if it turns out to have been those old tools applied to a shinier causal chain.

So give me your number, but mainly the decomposition, because I suspect our disagreement is hiding in one sub-question. Here's my attempt at a split — reorganise it however you like, and add terms I've missed: (1) whether "responsibility" in the relevant claim is mostly backward-looking blameworthiness/desert, mostly forward-looking answerability, or mostly liability for redress — and whether the gap only opens for some of these; (2) whether desert-type blameworthiness genuinely requires control over, or knowledge of, what you were doing, as a conceptual precondition rather than just a very strong empirical regularity; (3) whether the epistemic position of the deployer of a learning system is relevantly different from that of a large organisation where nobody could have traced the failure in advance — i.e. whether "many hands" plus ordinary ignorance is already the same problem in slower motion; (4) whether the residue can be discharged by second-order blame (blame for creating or tolerating a setup in which first-order blame has nowhere to land), and whether that definately reaches everyone who seems implicated — the engineer, the procurement officer, the deploying firm, the regulator — or whether it leaks somewhere; (5) whether a purely forward-looking institutional fix absorbs what's left.

Here's the bit I actually want you to engage with rather than pattern-match, since it's my own framing and not something you'll have seen: I've started splitting the worry into three sub-gaps — a blame gap (nobody deserves blame), a sanction gap (nobody may permissibly be punished or made to pay), and what I'm calling an answerability gap (there is nobody who can give an adequate account of why it happened, nobody who can answer "why did you do that?" in the first person at all). My sense is that the third one is the genuinely new one, that it survives even when there's a perfectly good strict-liability scheme sitting on top of it, and that it's what people are reaching for when they say someone must be responsible and then can't say what for. Give me a credence for each of the three being a real, distinct problem that doesn't dissolve on inspection, plus a credence on my claim that the third is where the interesting action is.

Numbers with one line of justification each, then the combination, then: which single term your headline number is most sensitive to; which term you're least confident about and what would move it most (a real case? a worked-out argument? a shift in how blame itself gets theorised?); and where you think my friend and I most likely actually disagree. Also please be explicit about what's settled or orthodox among people who work on this versus what's genuinely contested — don't hand me a live controversy dressed up as the consensus view, and flag the places where you're just reporting your own intuition with nothing much behind it.
