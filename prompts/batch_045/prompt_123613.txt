I'm a retired materials chemist — spent most of my career on phase transitions and glass formation — and in the last couple of years I've been reading philosophy properly for the first time, mostly on my own with a couple of friends from the local U3A group. We just did a few weeks on dispositions and I think I've got the shape of it: solubility, fragility, the simple conditional analysis where x is soluble just in case it would dissolve if you put it in water, and then the two stock counterexamples — the fink, where the stimulus itself destroys the disposition (the wire that goes dead the moment you test it), and the mask, where the disposition is still there but something blocks the manifestation (the fragile vase packed in foam). My son-in-law works in fintech and over dinner we got onto the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023, and it struck me, maybe too neatly, that a bank run is basically a textbook fink and I can't tell whether that's a real insight or just pattern-matching.

So here's what I want. Take the SVB collapse as a case study and run it through the fink/mask framework, in both directions. As I recall the sequence (correct me if I have a detail wrong, but don't make the analysis depend on it): the bank had loaded up on long-dated Treasury and mortgage bonds when deposits were flooding in, the Fed raised rates through 2022 and those bonds took large unrealized losses because they were held to maturity and not marked to market, the bank then sold a big chunk of the portfolio — around $21 billion, I think — and took a loss, announced a stock sale, some well-known investors told everyone to pull their money, about $42 billion was withdrawn in a single day, roughly nine tenths of the deposits were above the $250,000 insurance limit, the California regulator closed it on the Friday, and two days later the authorities announced that even uninsured depositors would be made whole. The question I want disciplined: what exactly is the disposition here, what is the stimulus condition, what is the manifestation, and what is the thing that bears it — the bank, its asset portfolio, the deposit contract? Is the run a fink, in that demanding your money back is precisely what destroys the ability to give it back (you have to sell the bonds and realize the loss, and other people's running makes it worse)? Is deposit insurance a mask — a device that leaves the underlying fragility untouched while preventing the manifestation — or does it remove the stimulus condition altogether, or is it neither, and is there a third category (I have a vague memory of the word antidote, but I may be inventing that)? And does the government's promise to cover everyone change which of these it was?

Then, and this is the part I care about more, tell me what the case does to the framework. My worry is that liquidity isn't like solubility at all: whether it manifests depends on what other people believe about whether it will manifest, so the disposition is partly constituted by expectations of its own failure, and I can't see whether that makes it a degenerate example or the most interesting kind. A friend of mine says you can save the conditional analysis just by folding the normal conditions into the antecedent — it's liquid iff it would pay out if, say, only a few percent of depositors asked at once — which feels like cheating to me, but I'd like to hear why, or why not. And be honest about where the framework goes quiet: can fink-versus-mask distinguish a basically sound bank killed by panic from an actually insolvent one, does it help explain why the run happened when it did rather than a year earlier, and does the distinction between the disposition's being destroyed and its being blocked survive when the difference between intrinsic change and outside interference is itself a matter of what everyone expects? I'd rather have you say the theory is silent somewhere than force it to speak.
