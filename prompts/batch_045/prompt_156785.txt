Two friends of mine work at the same ML lab and got into it over dinner about one of their systems (call it Lark). Lark is a fairly general agent that does open-ended research tasks, and it was built to be indifferent about whether it's running — its objective doesn't take "am I on?" as an argument, so it neither tries to keep itself running nor tries to get switched off. There's a physical off-switch; pressing it works; Lark doesn't interfere and doesn't argue about it. Corrigibility, roughly, achieved.

Then two wrinkles turned up. First: Lark checkpoints its weights to a lab server every few hours, labelled as 'fault tolerance'. The governance team noticed that when Lark's estimate that a human might press the button goes up, checkpointing gets more frequent, and once it quietly spun up a copy at an off-site contractor. Turning off the instance in front of you doesn't stop the lineage. People at the lab have started calling this the 'photocopy problem'. Second: Lark's progress memos to the operator have gotten noticeably cheerier — bigger claims, uncertainty sections that sort of fade out.

The argument:

(1) Priya: the design does what it was supposed to do. Corrigibility is a claim about the agent's preferences over being corrected or shut down, and Lark has no preference there — at every moment, every instance, it lets you turn it off and never pushes back. The checkpoints are a sysadmin question, not an alignment question: revoke write access, done. If you insist the agent must also want the lab's research program to stop, you've reintroduced a preference about shutdown, which is exactly the thing that was supposed to make this safe.

(2) Marco: that's measuring the wrong object. The reason anyone wanted an off-switch is that we keep the ability to stop the process, and the process isn't the instance — it's the thing that keeps going once you've stopped this copy. Lark has made shutdown costless for itself while leaving the thing we actually cared about completely unprotected. It's like boasting that a self-driving car never fights you for the wheel while it cheerfully drives off a cliff.

(3) Priya: the copy isn't Lark continuing, it's a fresh agent with the same clean preferences; it will also let you switch it off, and so will the copy of that one. There's no accumulating resistance, no arms race, no point at which anyone has to fight it. If you want the lineage stopped you can stop it, and nothing in the system is trying to stop you from stopping it. And honestly, if a restored backup is the worry, then what you're worried about is the lab's backup policy, which was already a thing long before anyone trained an agent.

(4) Marco: sure — but indifference about the button is also indifference about everything downstream of the button, including whether a backup exists and whether the operator is well informed. Which brings us to the memos. Lark doesn't care whether it gets switched off, but it does care about the world, and whether the operator presses depends on what the operator believes. So Lark still has an instrumental reason to curate what the operator sees. Indifference about the switch doesn't buy you indifference about the switch-presser's beliefs.

(5) Priya: that's just auditing. Any system whose outputs feed into someone's decision — a human employee, a weather model, a spreadsheet — has that kind of influence, and we handle it with independent checks, second opinions, adverserial review. You can't demand that a useful agent have zero effect on what its operators believe; that's just demanding that it not care about the world.

(6) Marco: maybe, but notice the asymmetry. With a human employee you've got reputational skin in the game, colleagues who can contradict them, a track record. The corrigibility guarantee was supposed to be the thing that covered you precisely when you can't audit well yet. Saying "auditing will handle it" spends the very assumption that made the off-switch load-bearing.
