I serve on a committee revising the moral-education curriculum used in our secondary schools. At last week's meeting a colleague argued against the parts of the draft that involve structured practice — habituation exercises, staged rehearsal of forms of address and apology, repeated ritualized routines, and an incentive scheme for sustained participation — on the following grounds: since human nature is good, the task of education is to draw out what is already present rather than to impose something from without, so any apparatus of training and deliberate artifice rests on a false premise. The slogan was put forward as "human nature is good," understood as the Mencian thesis set against Xunzi's contrary claim that our nature is bad, and my colleague made it do two further jobs: it was used to rule out any graded assessment of character (you cannot grade what you merely nourish), and to rule out the suggestion that moral development requires effort that the learner experiences as effort.

I am fairly sure something is being conflated, but I cannot say precisely what. As I understand the dispute, both sides grant that ordinary people can become good, and that the difference between a sage and a petty person is not a difference in raw material. The disagreement seems to concern what the initial endowment already contains (Mencius's incipient tendencies, the "sprouts" that must be tended if they are to grow and that can be starved or denuded) and what has to be done to it (Xunzi's imagery of straightening crooked wood and grinding blunt metal, i.e. deliberate artifice). On that understanding the Mencian picture is agricultural rather than laissez-faire, and would seem to license at least as much cultivation as my colleague wants to abolish.

So I would like the candidate readings of the slogan separated out. Please set out, as a menu of precise theses rather than one charitable paraphrase, the distinct things "human nature is good" might be claiming, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical. For each, say what exactly is asserted, whether it is trivial or something both parties could accept, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is false, whether Mencius could plausibly own it and whether Xunzi could even accept it, and above all whether it would entitle my colleague to any of his three conclusions about pedagogy, assessment, and effort.

My own suspicion is that part of the trouble is that "good" can attach to different objects, and I would like the menu organized along something like these lines, plus any axis I have missed:

- the initial endowment itself, i.e. a positive moral tendency present at birth;
- the developmental trajectory or endpoint under favourable conditions — a claim about where the system goes, or converges, rather than about what the starting configuration contains;
- the tractability of the material — "good" in the sense of offering no native resistance to being shaped, which is a claim about the material's suitability rather than about any moral content in it;
- the source of the standard by which we judge, i.e. whether goodness is endogenous to our nature or is an artifact imposed from outside by sagely construction;
- the baseline decency of ordinary adults in ordinary conditions, which is an empirical claim about society rather than a metaphysical claim about constitution at birth.

Two further questions. First, if there is a reading on which Mencius and Xunzi are not contradicting one another at all but are using "nature" for different target notions, identify it and say which usage, if either, is the one that matters for designing a curriculum. Second, say which of the readings the people who deploy this slogan in policy and education meetings are most likely to have in mind, and whether that reading is the one the Mencian texts actually support.

I have the primary materials at hand and do not need citations, a reading list, or textual apparatus; what I need is the disambiguation and the verdicts.
