Imagine a mid-sized US state government that's oddly convinced by Szasz: they'll accept, for the sake of argument, that "mental illness" isn't a disease but a label we stick on conduct and experience we disapprove of, and they've hired you to work out what actually follows. You've got one legislative session and political capital for maybe two or three areas of law — civil commitment, the insanity defense, forensic confinement, disability and insurance categories that require a diagnosis, mandated outpatient treatment, whatever you think belongs on the list.

Which two or three do you spend it on, in what order, and why? I want the reasoning more than the list: what you're ranking by (how many people it touches, how much of the rest of the system hangs off that one area, tractability, how bad it gets if you're wrong), where those criteria pull against each other, and which changes that people credit to Szasz don't really follow from the premise. Concretely: I keep hearing that his view is liberating for patients, but if you drop the disease framing and with it the insanity defense, it looks like what you mostly get is more convictions and longer sentences. Is that the real upshot, or a bad inference?

One more wrinke: say that in fifteen years we get a cheap, reliable biological test that cleanly separates several of the big diagnostic categories. Which parts of the agenda you just set would that actually change, and which parts were never really about the metaphysics to begin with?
