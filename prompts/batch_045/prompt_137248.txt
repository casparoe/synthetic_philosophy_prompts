My flatmate is doing a reading group on free will and he's built an argument I can't find my way out of, even though I'm sure the conclusion is nuts. Numbering is mine.

1) A piece of behaviour is an exercise of agency only if the mechanism producing it is sensitive, at the time, to whether the behaviour is succeeding — if it stoped achieving its point, the mechanism would adjust it there and then.
2) The sphex wasp case from all the textbooks: she drops the paralysed cricket at the threshold, goes in to inspect the burrow, comes back out, and if you've moved the cricket a few inches while she was inside she drags it back and then goes in to inspect all over again, and again. Her routine doesn't track whether it's working, it just restarts. That's sphexishness, Hofstadter's word for it, and it's supposed to be the opposite of agency.
3) So the wasp isn't exercising agency there. I'm happy with that one.
4) But (1) is a claim about mechanisms, not about species or about what they're made of, so nothing in the argument turns on being an insect.
5) Human expert performance runs the same way: while it runs, the mechanism is offline to whether it's succeeding — more offline than a novice's is, because making an expert attend to the individual steps is what wrecks the performance, not what repairs it.
6) So the better you are at something, the less agency you exercise in doing it, and the beginner consciously checking each move is the freest one.
7) And an ideally settled agent — a saint whose character is fixed, who never has to reconsider what to do — would be the most mechanical thing in the room.

I want to keep (3) and I really don't want (6) or (7), but (1), (4) and (5) each look fine to me on their own and I can't locate the slide between them. One more thing that might matter: I've read that the wasp does adapt after enough repetitions, and that re-inspecting a disturbed burrow may be adaptive rather than stupid — I can't tell whether that helps me or makes things worse. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost you elsewhere?
