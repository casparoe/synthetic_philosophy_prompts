I help run a mid-sized nonprofit that funds after-school tutoring programs in a handful of cities, and I'm currently the person drafting the one-page guidance our program directors get about which outcome numbers they're allowed to promise funders. So I keep running into Goodhart-flavoured failure in an annoyingly practical form: we commit to a number, the number gets worked on, and afterwards nobody can tell whether anything actually improved. I'm also in a desultory reading group on specification gaming in machine learning, and the two conversations don't feel like they're about the same thing even though everyone recites the same slogan about a measure ceasing to be good once it becomes a target. Before I commit to any framework, I want the spec: what would a theory of which measures survive being targeted have to get right? Give me the desiderata list, each with the case or consideration behind it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are really just what one camp wants a theory to deliver.

A few things I want you to take a stand on, because I suspect the shape of the list is the actual result. Does the theory have to cover "selection without a target" — a quantity nobody is optimizing against that still degrades because survival or publication selects on something correlated with it (think reported effect sizes in a literature, or the average outcome among programs that make it past a funding cut)? Or is an agent aiming at the measure part of the definition, so that case belongs to some other theory? Does it have to respect the distinction between a metric that merely evidences the thing we care about (test-score gain as evidence of learning) and one that partly constitutes it (share of enrolled students who actually completed the course, where completion is part of what the program is for), and explain why pushing on the second kind can be fine while pushing on the first corrupts? Should adequacy require it to predict the shape of the failure — e.g. that distortion bunches up right at a funding cliff rather than spreading smoothly — or is "it breaks somewhere" enough? Does it need to handle chains, where you bolt on a second metric or an audit to catch gaming of the first and the audit becomes the new target? And is the four-way taxonomy that gets passed around online (causal / adversarial / extremal / regress-to-the-mean-ish) carving at the joints, or is it a prejudice of one camp?

Two more constraints from my situation. I need this ex ante: I have to pick metrics before anything is deployed, so a theory that only diagnoses failures after the fact is useless to me — tell me whether that's a fair desideratum or an unfair demand on the subject matter. And whatever the list is, it must not collapse "the number moved because people responded to the incentive in a way that bypassed the target" into "the number moved because the target moved," since for us those look identical in the data. Please flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied (I have a hunch full generality and ex ante specificity are in tension, and that covering no-optimizer cases fights with any mechanistic story about agents), and say which single requirement knocks out the most popular framings — a couple of sentences each on the slogan version, the economics-of-incentives version, and the ML reward-hacking version is plenty; I care much more about the criteria than the scorecard.
