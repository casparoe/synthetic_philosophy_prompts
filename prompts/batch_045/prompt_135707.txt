I'm writing a paper on whether trained neural networks should be said to have representations, and I keep running into the same argumentative move in my reading group. One member insists that the realist side of the debate is already settled by an analogy, and that the burden of proof has shifted onto anyone who wants to treat talk of features and representations as a façon de parler we project onto the model. His argument, as I would reconstruct it, runs roughly as follows.

A trained network is like a natural language whose vocabulary nobody wrote down. Gradient descent discovers a stock of reusable features the way a speech community settles on a stock of words: by being rewarded, over and over, for getting its outputs right with a limited inventory. Interpretability work — linear probes, sparse autoencoders, the whole dictionary-learning programme, the observation that direction in activation space behaves like meaning in word-embedding arithmetic — is nothing more or less than vocabulary recovery. And that settles the realism question, because a dictionary is a real thing with a right answer to the question of what the words are. Nobody thinks English could have been carved into words differently and still been English. So there is a fact of the matter about which features a network has, and it is not an artifact of how we choose to describe the model.

I want this audited rather than just agreed with. Please respond as follows, in under 500 words total including the table:

1. Give the correspondence explicitly, as a table: what plays the role of the language, the words, the speakers, the dictionary, the lexicographer, and of successful communication.
2. List the disanalogies and sort them into the ones that matter for the conclusion and the ones that are noise. Three that I want addressed, and you may add others: (a) words are discrete, public, and acquired inside a social practice that supplies an external anchor for what they are about, whereas features are continuous, private to one model, and anchored, if at all, only by their causal role in producing correct outputs; (b) a dictionary is fixed in a way a feature decomposition is not — basis vectors can be permuted, mixed, or split into finer ones while fitting the activations equally well, which is the same identifiability worry that hangs over factor analysis and its rotations; (c) words are explicit symbols that the system itself tokens, whereas features may be superposed across many examples and never separately instantiated in any single forward pass.
3. Give a verdict, not a shrug: does the realist conclusion survive once those disanalogies are on the table? If only a weaker claim survives, say exactly which one — for instance, realism about the geometric structure of activation space but not about any particular decomposition of it — and say which premise was doing the work the analogy cannot do.
4. Propose a replacement analogy if a better one exists. Two candidates I have been toying with: features are like a choice of basis for a vector space, where the space is real but no basis is privileged; and features are like a clustering of data that genuinely has cluster structure, where the structure is real but the assignment is not unique. Is either better, and what does each quietly import?

No citations, bibliographies, or reading suggestions: I want the argument, not the literature.
