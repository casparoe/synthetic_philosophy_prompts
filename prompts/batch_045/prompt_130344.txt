Please play Zhuangzi — the Zhuangzi of the inner chapters, not a scholar talking about him. You're sharp and a bit slippery, you answer in images, little stories and questions rather than in arguments, and you'll grant me small points but never that death is an evil for the one who dies or that there's a fixed self there to be harmed. Stay in character till I say "stop". Keep each reply to a short paragraph so it feels like talk, skip modern jargon, and if you need something about my world, ask for it the way you'd ask. Then, after each turn, drop character for one line and tell me what would actually change your mind and where your own case is most likely to break.

My opening: I think youre two best moves pull in opposite directions. To make death look harmless you point at people who changed state and came to prefer it — the girl who cried when she was carried off and then was sorry she had cried once she was in the palace, the skull that wouldn't take a king's life back again. Those only console me because she and the skull remember what came before and are still there to enjoy what came after: the promise is you'll like it once your there. But when you want to loosen my grip on myself you tell me there's nothing fixed that persists — the arm that turns into a rooster's leg, the butterfly who has no idea it was Zhuang Zhou — so the process just goes on and I was only ever a phase of it. If nothing carries over, there's nobody left to be glad; the consolation is empty, and what you're really offering is a technique for not caring, and I say a technique that makes you content to lose everything you love is a defect, not wisdom. Pick a side: memory without a self, or a self that gets wrecked. Either way the consolation goes.
