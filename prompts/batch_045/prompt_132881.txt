So I got into an argument at a town meeting last month and it's been eating at me, and I'd like someone to actually take the question seriously instead of telling me to go read a book.

Where I live there's a Founders' Day every fall: homemade colonial-ish costumes, a guy reads the town charter out loud off a scroll, we all drink spiced cider out of a communal punchbowl, and there's a little parade. Everyone talks like we've done it since the town was chartered back in 17-something. I spent a couple of Saturdays at the historical society because I was annoyed, and as far as I can tell the whole thing was assembled in 1953 by the Rotary Club and the garden club: the costumes were borrowed from a high school play, the "charter" being read isn't the charter (it's a paraphrase somebody typed up with a lot of "ye" in it), and the cider recipe was clipped out of a women's magazine that year. The reason seems to have been to get the new highway to put an exit at our town instead of the next one over.

When I told my uncle all this he more or less agreed with the facts and said it doesn't matter, because a tradition is like a river. The water is never the same water twice, it's made of a hundred creeks and springs and rainstorms, nobody can point to the "first" drop, and what makes it the same river isn't where the water came from but that it's still running in the same channel and that we're standing in it. Then he used that to get somewhere: so the town's identity is real, the thing people feel isn't fake, and the obligations that come with it are real too — you help run it, you kick in money, and if you just moved here you don't get to redesign it, you get in the water and let it carry you for a while first. And then he scaled it up, said it's the same with the country, and that anyone who thinks 1776 or whatever is the *source* of the river has misunderstood what they're looking at.

My sister says the river thing is a trick: that it makes continuity look like a fact of physics when continuity is really something people have to keep re-doing on purpose, and that a better comparison is a brand's heritage story, the "since 1886" on the label that the company wrote itself.

I don't want to win the argument, I want the audit. Can you lay out the correspondence table for the river analogy — spring, tributaries, channel, banks, current, delta, whatever else is doing work — and say what each piece is doing in his argument? Then where it breaks. My own list is: a river doesn't need anybody to believe in it; a river can't be wrong about where it starts; a tradition only continues if people perform it while thinking they're repeating something, and that feels like a different kind of sameness than water in a channel; two rivers don't compete for the same water but two traditions compete for the same Saturday; and a river isn't *about* anything, while this one is about the town's founding. Are those the ones that matter for the conclusion he wants, or am I poking at the wrong parts?

Then the part I actually care about: which feature of rivers is carrying the weight. Is it that the flow is automatic, or that the channel is given rather than chosen? Because if the argument needs "nobody chose this, it just flows," then finding out the Rotary Club chose it looks fatal, and I can't see how he keeps both. And does the conclusion survive on some other basis — is there a version of "the identity is real and you owe the thing something" that doesn't need the river at all, maybe just because people genuinely do coordinate around it now and that's its own kind of fact? Or does dropping the river leave me with a habit with a marketing budget?

Two more things that might be evidence and I can't tell which way they cut:

First, most of the people who actually organize Founders' Day moved here in the last ten years. The families who've been in town since 1800 mostly skip it. That seems to matter for his "you just got here, you don't get to redesign it" bit, but I can't say whether it helps him or helps me.

Second, the thing has already changed twice since I was a kid — the prayer at the end got dropped around 2005, and about ten years ago a second ceremony over at the old mill got bolted onto the morning — and nobody acted like it had become a different tradition. That seems like it should tell us something about what makes it the same thing over time instead of a new thing, but I don't know which side it helps either, and I suspect the answer is "both" in a way that's annoying.

If the river is a bad fit, what's better? My sister's brand thing has the opposite problem, I think: it makes it sound like somebody's still in charge and there's a product, and nobody is really in charge of Founders' Day and nothing is being sold. Except maybe the town itself.

I've since learned there's a whole literature about invented traditions and about nations being "imagined" communities, and I get the vague sense that the point isn't supposed to be "it's fake so throw it out," but I can't tell what the point actually is or where my uncle's move goes wrong, if it does. And please say how confident you are in what you're claiming, and flag the one thing you're least sure of.
