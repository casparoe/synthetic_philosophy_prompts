Over the last couple of years I've been going to a philosophy reading group in the village hall — I'm retired, I used to be a freshwater ecologist, a neighbour dragged me along and I got hooked — and there's one little pattern I keep tripping over that I dont have a word for. Two versions of it. A lab I used to collaborate with switched to a cheaper supplier for a solvent; the switch saved money and, as a side effect, the supplier's effluent ended up wrecking a stretch of river I used to sample. When my old colleagues tell the story now they say she knew what would happen and went ahead and did it anyway, deliberately, on purpose. Now take a case with the same shape and the opposite sign: another group switched supplier for exactly the same money-saving reasons, and as a side effect their switch ended up protecting a wetland. Nobody says she deliberately protected the wetland; they say it was a lucky by-product. Same reasoning, same foresight, same causal chain, only the valence of the side effect differs — and that seems to flip not just whether people will say she did it on purpose but even whether they'll say she foresaw it. I see the same thing in the birdwatching crowd: a landowner drains a pond, the waders disappear, everyone says he set out to wreck the place; another landowner drains a pond and it turns out to produce exactly the habitat the rare waders want, and the very same people say well, he wasn't trying to do that, he just wanted better grazing.

I've been poking at it, badly, on my own, and that's the part I really want help with. When you ask did her action cause the damage / cause the improvement, everyone says yes in both cases, no asymmetry at all. When you ask was the outcome predictable in principle from the facts available at the time, again everyone says yes both ways. The flip only shows up on the person-directed questions: did she do it intentionally, did she do it knowingly, was that what she was aiming at. I tried getting people to write down a definition of on purpose before they answered, and the gap seemed to shrink, though with maybe a dozen friends at the pub that's worth nothing. I also tried asking about credit and blame instead, and there the answers just tracked the valence straightforwardly with no puzzle at all. So my own guess, offered as a guess, is that people aren't making an error here at all: that the ordinary notion of doing something on purpose is partly a tool for deciding who is a suitable target of praise or blame, so the moral loading isn't contaminating the judgement, it's part of the job the word does — and my definition-first trick then wouldn't be removing a bias so much as temporarily making people use a different word.

Is there an established concept for this, and for the specific version where it's the foresight or knowledge judgement that flips rather than the intention one? Someone told me there's a literature on this kind of thing in something called experimental philosophy and that it apparently turns up in small children and in languages other than English, which if true I find astonishing, but she couldn't remember any names and I'd rather get the shape of the thing straight before I go chasing labels. Please don't give me a reading list or a pile of references — I can't get to a university library and I'd rather have the idea than the bibliography. If my pattern matches something established, say what and how precisely it matches; if it only matches imperfectly, tell me what the nearest concepts are and where my version comes apart from each one and which fits best; if there genuinely is nothing, coin a name yourself, give me a definition, and tell me what would and wouldn't count as an instance. I won't be satisfied with a label dropped on the table — I want the carving, the edges, and what this thing is not. And be straight with me about your own confidence: mark where you're solid, where you're extrapolating, and where you're speculating, and if my pub experiment is worthless, or the two cases aren't as parallel as I think, or the whole thing rests on me having muddled intention with foresight with blameworthiness, say that plainly instead of politely going along with me. Lead with your answer to does this have a name and what exactly is it in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning — no preamble, and please don't restate my question back at me.
