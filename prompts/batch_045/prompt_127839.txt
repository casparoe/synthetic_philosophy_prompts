I'm in an STS program, writing a qualifying paper chapter on technological determinism, and I keep tripping over my own verdicts about which artifacts have politics. I basically agree with Winner's canonical cases in the do artifacts have politics essay — the parkway overpasses built too low for buses, so that people who'd have come by bus (disproportionately poor and Black New Yorkers) couldn't reach the beach; and the mechanical tomato harvester, bred alongside a hard-skinned tomato, which nobody designed to drive out small growers but which helped do it anyway; and his two-way distinction between arrangements that settle a community's affairs and technologies that require or strongly mesh with a certain kind of social order. My problem is my own cases. Gut verdicts, roughly in order of how sure I am:

- Hospital electronic records where you can't close a visit note until you pick a billing code from a dropdown: political, strongly, and it feels like the same kind of case as the overpasses even though no one was trying to keep anybody off a beach.
- A voting machine with no voter-verifiable paper trail: political, strongly.
- Speed bumps on a residential street: political. They do a cop's job and you can't ask them for a warning. Best defense I have: they also push the traffic and the noise onto the next street over, so the costs aren't shared by whoever gets the benefit.
- Roundabouts replacing four-way stops all over the suburb I grew up in: not political, just safer and faster. But I know the objection — a roundabout also does a cop's job, it forces you to yield with no room to negotiate. My only answer is that everyone who uses it pays the same cost. I don't love that answer.
- The tomato harvester: political, but a weaker and different flavor than the overpasses, because the politics fell out of the economics rather than being written into the design.
- The standardized shipping container: not political. And here's my real headache — I say yes to the harvester and no to the container even though both look like unintended, diffuse, downstream effects on who has a job and where. If I call the container political because it gutted dock labor and rewrote port cities, I've said everything is political and the word stops doing any work.
- Bolted-down lecture-hall seating all facing the front: political, weakly, and I'm suspicious of myself here because I probably only say that from being an academic who cares about pedagogy, which is a bad sign for my other verdicts.
- A phone with a glued-in battery: I want to say not political, but I can't say why that isn't political while the harvester is, since both are about who's allowed to repair and who has to buy again.

The case I go back and forth on: a mesh-networked messaging app that works with no cell towers, used by protesters to organize in one country and by a ministry to coordinate raids in another. Same artifact, opposite politics. Does that show artifacts are polyvalent and only assemblages of artifacts plus users have politics, or is it just a case where the politics is genuinely undetermined?

My tentative principle, which I think is too broad: an artifact has politics when it settles a distributive question — about power, access, or risk — in a way that isn't up for ongoing deliberation and is expensive to revise. That seems to hand me political for the container and the roundabout too, and I don't buy it.

So: give me a principle, or two or three rival candidates if no single one fits, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you haven't seen. For each, I want the clauses mapped to the verdicts that motivated them, an honest list of which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, predictions on at least three cases I haven't listed (include a facial-recognition turnstile at a stadium, an algorithm that scores student essays, and one of your own choosing), and a verdict on the mesh-network app with a 0–1 confidence. Also tell me what would change your mind — which of my verdicts being right, or which consideration, would make you swap principles — and where you think your own argument is most likely to break. Don't hedge everything into mush; if you have to overrule me somewhere, say where and why.

After the prose, give me a JSON object with exactly these fields: "principle" (one sentence, stated precisely); "clauses" (array of objects with keys "clause", "motivating_case", "work_it_does"); "case_verdicts" (array of objects with keys "case", "verdict" — one of "political", "not political", "political in a weaker sense" — and "why"); "overturned_verdicts" (array of objects with keys "case", "my_verdict", "your_verdict", "why_you_overrule_it"); "unlisted_cases" (array of objects with keys "case", "predicted_verdict"); "undecided_case" (object with keys "verdict", "confidence", "what_would_settle_it"); "what_would_change_my_mind" (array of strings); "weakest_step" (string).
