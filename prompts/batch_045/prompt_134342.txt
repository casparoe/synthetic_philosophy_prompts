I teach an upper-level undergraduate metaphysics seminar — 14 students, one 80-minute session — and I want a structured activity that makes the problem of the two similar spheres show up in what the students do, not in what I later tell them. The students have met only the informal slogan that there cannot be two things exactly alike in every respect; I do not want anyone to use the phrase identity of indiscernibles or to name the standard two-sphere counterexample until the debrief.

The base scenario is a universe containing exactly two iron spheres, exactly similar in every intrinsic respect (same diameter, composition, temperature, colour, and so on), at a fixed distance from one another, with no other material objects, embedded in a space with no privileged position or direction (homogeneous and isotropic), and governed by laws that are perfectly symmetric under exchanging the two spheres. Into this scenario I want you to inject and build the activity around a new element: a Silent Registrar. The Silent Registrar is stipulated to be external to that universe. Its only role is to keep a private ledger assigning the labels *A* and *B* to the two spheres. It has no causal interaction with the spheres, no observable effects, no location, no intrinsic qualitative character of its own, and cannot be detected from inside the universe; it is a bookkeeping device whose sole function is to mark a distinction of which sphere is which without adding any qualitative difference to them.

Design a runnable role-play or staged deliberation with forced choices and explicit constraints — not a lecture, not a comprehension worksheet, and not a formal proof. The rules, roles, and payoffs should be arranged so that the students run into the difficulty themselves before it is named. A minimal shape: divide the class into four small groups. In the first phase, give the groups a permitted vocabulary for describing the spheres: intrinsic properties of the spheres, their distance from one another, relations to each other, and any global geometrical or nomological features of the universe. Explicitly ban labels, proper names, demonstratives, and any appeal to the Registrar's ledger. Ask each group to produce a description, in the permitted vocabulary, that they claim is true of exactly one sphere and false of the other. Award a discernibility point only if every other group agrees that the description picks out exactly one sphere and does not secretly rely on banned terms.

In the second phase, have each group make a forced choice among three claims: (1) there are not really two spheres, because they are exactly alike in every respect; (2) there are two spheres, but they differ only numerically and not in any respect describable from inside the universe; (3) there are two spheres and they are discerned by some relation, by the global symmetry structure, or by the mere possibility of the Registrar's labels. In the third phase, introduce the Silent Registrar's ledger, allow a short revision round, and add one more twist: announce that the laws and the space admit a global symmetry transformation (for example, a rotation or translation of the whole universe, or a relabeling induced by the symmetry of the laws) that would exchange the two spheres while leaving every permitted description and every observable fact unchanged, and ask whether the spheres have swapped and whether the Registrar's labels track a real difference or merely presuppose one.

What participants should come away seeing: they should feel the pull of saying both that there are two spheres and that there is no qualitative difference between them, and they should locate the real dispute in which kinds of property or relation are allowed to do the discerning — intrinsic versus relational, qualitative versus non-qualitative, internal versus external. They should discover, in practice, that symmetric relational predicates such as being two meters from a sphere do not single out one sphere, and that the Registrar's labels seem to distinguish them only if one either helps oneself to the numerical difference the example was supposed to test or admits a primitive thisness (a non-qualitative property of being that very individual) that the activity cannot itself vindicate.

The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto parts of the philosophical problem: numerical versus extra-numerical (qualitative or relational) difference; why the two-sphere scenario is taken to threaten some formulations of the identity of indiscernibles; the contrast between qualitative, relational, and haecceitistic (primitive-thisness-involving) discernibility; and the question whether an external labeler supplies a genuine property or merely relocates the mystery. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run — for example, students smuggling in left and right or this one despite the ban, groups mistaking the symmetric distance relation for an asymmetric one, students treating the Silent Registrar as just another object inside the universe and thereby breaking the stipulation that it is external and inert, uneven participation, or a group finding a cheap trick such as a self-referential description — and explain how to handle each. Be frank about which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce and which the facilitator must simply supply: in particular, it cannot settle whether non-qualitative thisness is a legitimate property, or how the principle should be formalized with modality and quantifier domains, and it is not a substitute for drawing the formal distinctions on the board afterwards.
