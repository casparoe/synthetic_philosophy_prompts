I'm a fourth-year PhD student and I'm giving a 30-minute talk at our metaphysics reading group next month (mostly grad students, three faculty) defending a view about causation by omission that I've been kicking around for a couple years, and I'd rather walk in with real Q&A prep than with vibes. Quick sketch of the thesis so you have something to aim at: omissions are genuine causes, but they aren't a special kind of causal relatum and they don't need any norm, expectation, or duty clause to get into the causal story. What I call the default-value picture: in an interventionist causal model every variable has a distinguished default value, and "the gardener didn't water the plant" just is the claim that the watering variable sits at its default. Omissions get *selected* without profligacy because of what I call liveness: a variable earns a place in the model only if there's a mechanism actually coupled to the outcome that could in principle have delivered a non-default value. The Queen of England has no such coupling to your plant, so she isn't in the model and never was, and the "why not her?" problem dissolves without anyone having to say she had no obligation to water it. The folk data where people's causal judgments slide around with what the agent was supposed to do I want to explain as a model-construction effect: norm cues tell you which mechanisms and couplings to bother representing, they aren't constitutive of the causal relation itself. Call that explanatory priority, not reduction. I've already got replies ready for the "absences aren't events, and your causal relata are events" line and for the standard double-prevention cases, so don't spend much time there.

The audience is the thing I want help with, because I know exactly who'll be in the room. There's a Lewisian who thinks the but-for analysis is roughly right for events and that omissions are at best quasi-causal; two people who do causal modeling in the interventionist style and who will want to know whether "default value" is anything more than notation they already use and whether liveness is smuggled in rather than read off the model; a legal theorist who thinks the but-for test has to be supplemented with a voluntary-conduct or duty condition and who'll say my account can't explain why the law treats omissions differently from mere non-occurrence; someone from the experimental side who thinks the norm-sensitivity findings show norms are doing real causal-selecting work and that my "model construction" story is unfalsifiable; and one philosopher of physics who is going to press me on whether "the system did nothing" is even well-defined once you stop talking about a model and start talking about what's physically going on. Q&A is about ten minutes, so I need to triage.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defense of the view: the seven or eight objections I'll actually get from these people, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that person would actually phrase it rather than in my vocabulary, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the talk itself before anyone asks, which single reply has to land for the view to survive the hour, and — the part I care most about — the one objection I'm hoping nobody asks. Bonus points if you can spot the place where liveness is doing more work than I've admitted.
