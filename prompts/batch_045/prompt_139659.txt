I'm a backend engineer, about ten years in, mostly on distributed storage and the boring reliability side of things, and I got into an argument at lunch with a teammate that I can't let go of. He says my phone is literally part of my mind now, because I don't remember anyone's phone number, I navigate by reading a blue arrow instead of by knowing where I am, and if you take my phone away I'm genuinely less capable in a way that isn't just "inconvenient". I said that's just a tool with a really good API, and that calling it part of me is a metaphor that got out of hand. Neither of us could say what would settle it. He then told me to read Clark and Chalmers's 1998 paper "The Extended Mind" (the one with Otto, who has something like Alzheimer's and keeps all his facts in a notebook, versus Inga who just remembers things, and the parity principle that says if the external bit does the same job as the internal bit would, you shouldn't draw the line at the skull). I read a summary and it felt both obvious and completely slippery, which I'm guessing means I haven't actually understood what's being claimed.

So: translate the thesis into the vocabulary I actually think in — caches vs. authoritative stores, write-through vs. write-behind, single-writer vs. multi-writer, read-your-writes and staleness, TTL and eviction, replication and failover, latency budgets and graceful degradation, service ownership and trust boundaries, single points of failure, blast radius — and do the translation claim by claim rather than as one big vibe. What in their argument corresponds to Otto's notebook being treated as an authoritative local replica rather than a cache someone else can invalidate? Which of their conditions is doing the work that "single writer" or "read-your-writes" does in my world, and which of them has no analog at all?

Two cases I specifically want run through the translation, because I think they're where the machinery should either bite or break. First: what happens to the story when the external store has a second writer, or worse, an adversarial one — a map that reroutes me past a sponsor's car park, an autocomplete trained mostly on SEO sludge, a feed tuned to keep me scrolling. Otto trusts his notebook and nobody is editing it behind his back; my phone is being written to by people whose objectives are not mine. Can something still count as "automatically endorsed" if the endorsement is the result of someone else's optimization? Second: the version of the criticism that says tight causal coupling to a tool doesn't mean the tool is part of the machinery that constitutes the state at all — I'd like that restated in my terms too, and then I want to know whether the translation makes it look stronger or weaker than it did in the original vocabulary.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Say plainly what didn't survive: what had to be fudged to fit, and what can't be expressed in systems vocabulary at all. My guesses are that nothing in my vocabulary captures there being a subject the states belong to, or the way belief is a normative status (you can be wrong, or irrational, or blameworthy in a way a stale cache entry can't), or the difference between something being part of a mechanism and something being part of a person — but you tell me if I'm guessing wrong. And then the bit I can't get my teammate to engage with: is the leftover stuff pointing at a real gap in how I'm modeling the situation, or is it leftover because the thesis was never saying anything sharp enough to translate in the first place? I'd like a straight verdict on whether the translation makes the extended mind sound more true or more trivial, and whether the answer to my lunch argument is "you're both describing the same system at different abstraction layers" or whether one of us is actually making a mistake.
