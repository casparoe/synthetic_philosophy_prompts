I'm writing my final essay for a seminar on collective intentionality and joint action, and I'd like to use Nolan's Inception as my case study rather than the usual textbook examples (two people painting a house, or going for a walk together). We've covered three accounts I want to read it against: Searle's claim that we-intentions are primitive and can't be reduced to I-intentions plus beliefs about others; Bratman's shared cooperative activity, with its conditions of mutual responsiveness, commitment to the joint activity and commitment to mutual support, and its allowance that participants may act for their own further ends; and Gilbert's plural-subject view, on which a joint commitment generates obligations and can't be unilaterally rescinded.

Two things in the film I want help with. First, the Fischer job itself: Cobb assembles the team, designs the plan, and withholds both his real reason for taking it, getting home to his children, and the fact that his own subconscious keeps sabotaging the work. Is that still a shared intention, or does the hidden ulterior end plus the asymmetry of knowledge collapse it into one agent's plan carried out by contractors with matching sub-plans? Does it matter that no one could have pulled it off alone, or that they improvise together when Fischer's subconscious turns out to be militarised? Second, Mal: she treats the world she and Cobb built in limbo as a joint project he had no right to abandon on his own, and her resentment drives the plot. On Gilbert's account that resentment looks structurally in order, whereas the film invites us to read it as pathology. Which is right, and does the film's central device, an idea implanted in a subject who then experiences it as his own, say anything about what shared agency needs over and above convergence of behaviour?

I also want to know where the reading fails. I suspect the ending vindicates a fairly individualist picture of agency, and I'd rather concede that than force the plural-subject reading onto it. And in a sentence or two: does the film add anything to the debate, the felt difference between acting as part of a 'we' and merely acting alongside others perhaps, that the Bratman/Searle/Gilbert literature can't?
