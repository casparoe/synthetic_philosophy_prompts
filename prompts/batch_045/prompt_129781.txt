I need a Q&A prep sheet for a talk I'm giving in two weeks, and the room is going to be hostile in about four different directions at once.

Setup: a large urban school district is deciding whether to renew a K–3 reading intervention program, and someone on the board has been passing around the line "reading is about 70% heritable, so there's a limit to what schools can do." I've been asked to give twenty minutes (plus fifteen of questions) to the district's research advisory board on what heritability estimates do and don't tell you.

My thesis, in two parts:

(1) h² is a ratio of variances, computed in one population living in one range of environments. It doesn't apply to individual children (it does not mean 70% of any given kid's reading score came from genes), and it's blind to anything that moves everybody. A program given to every third grader shifts the mean and adds basically no variance, so it's invisible to h² in both directions — something can be 80% heritable and still move a lot under a uniform intervention. I've been calling this the uniformity blind spot.

(2) So I want a second number, which I'm calling the closable share: the fraction of an observed gap that the best intervention we could realistically run would remove. It's defined against a set of feasible interventions, not against a variance decomposition, so it isn't a function of h² in either direction. PKU is entirely genetic and almost entirely preventable; a gap can be entirely environmental and completely unclosable with the tools anyone actually has. And because h² goes up mechanically when you make environments more uniform, a rising heritability in a district that is improving is a sign the district is working, not a sign that schools don't matter. That inversion is the thing I most want to land. I'll mention in one sentence the pattern people always gesture at where heritability comes out higher in better-off samples, but I dont want to over-claim it.

The room: two school psychologists who want to know what to do on Monday; three quantitative education researchers who live in value-added models and will care about measurement invariance and whether the test measures the same thing in different groups; one behavioral geneticist who does twin modeling, came expecting to be strawmanned, and will object in her vocabulary (gene-environment correlation, whether my "closable share" is just environmentality with a nicer name, whether it's even identified); a teachers' union rep who thinks this whole conversation is how you talk yourself out of funding; a parent rep; and the district data director, whose real question is whether my slides will get quoted in a board meeting as "the expert says schools don't matter."

What I want: the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would say it out loud rather than in a journal. For each, a reply I can give in about ninety seconds, and one honest sentence on where the reply is soft or where I should just give the point away. Don't pad it with objections from a psych methods journal — these are practitioners plus one hostile geneticist. No citations or reading lists; I need sentences I can say in the room. Include the question I'm hopping nobody asks; I have a guess what it is, but if yours is worse, give me yours.
