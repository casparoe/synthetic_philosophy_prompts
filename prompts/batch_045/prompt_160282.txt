Eight weeks ago I was appointed to fill a vacancy on the five-member Zoning Board of Appeals in the town where I live. I took the seat in an unfriendly season: two of the board's decisions from the previous year were appealed, and one was sent back because the written findings did not support the result, so the town has been paying attention to how we decide things and not only to what we decide. I have spent the interval reading the minutes of every variance the board has heard since the current bylaw was adopted.

Our bylaw provides that the board shall grant a variance where it finds all of the following: (a) that literal enforcement would impose substantial hardship on the applicant; (b) that the hardship arises from soil conditions, shape, or topography peculiar to the lot and not from the applicant's own acts; (c) that the variance will not be substantially detrimental to the neighborhood; and (d) that the relief granted is the minimum necessary. In practice the chair has always taken the vote on the motion to grant first, and the clerk has then read the findings into the record; the town's counsel, in a memorandum I found in the file from two years ago, wrote that the findings rather than the motion are what a court would look to. Nothing in the minutes suggests that any member has ever voted to grant while recording a finding that one of the four conditions was unmet, or the reverse.

Four cases seem to me to carry the weight of the record:

- The Kwan garage: (a) 5–0, (b) 4–1, (c) 5–0, (d) 4–1; the motion carried 4–1.
- The Sorenson deck: (a) 2–3, (b) 2–3, (c) 4–1, (d) 3–2; the motion failed 2–3.
- The Ricci lot, which is the case closest to the one now before us: (a) 4–1, (b) 4–1, (c) 4–1, (d) 5–0; the motion carried 3–2. Here the clerk recorded a roll call: three members found all four conditions met and voted to grant; one member found everything met except hardship; one member found hardship and minimum relief met but neither the uniqueness nor the neighborhood condition.
- The Aldrich shed: (a) 5–0, (b) 1–4, (c) 4–1, (d) 3–2; the motion failed 1–4. Again a roll call: one member found all four conditions met and voted to grant; two members found everything met except uniqueness; one found everything met except uniqueness and minimum relief; one found hardship met and nothing else.

Last Tuesday we closed the hearing on the Delacroix application: a family rebuilding a small house destroyed by fire on an undersized lot, set back further than the bylaw allows but otherwise much as it was. Because we deliberate in public, each of the four members other than me stated at the close of the hearing how he or she would find. Chair Hazlett would find (a), (b), (c) and (d) all met and would grant. Nakamura would find (a) met and (b), (c), (d) unmet, and would deny. Osei would find (a), (b) and (d) met and (c) unmet, and would deny. Ferrante would find (c) met and (a), (b), (d) unmet, and would deny. So (a) stands at 3–1 in the affirmative and each of (b), (c) and (d) is deadlocked 2–2; on the motion itself the board stands at 1–3.

I am the fifth vote. My own difficulty is with (d): the Delacroixes could have met the setback by shifting the footprint about six feet, at a cost the opponents put at roughly a tenth of the rebuild and the applicants put at nearer a third, because of ledge. I lean toward finding (d) met, and I lean toward granting. I have known the family slightly for years, our children were at the same elementary school, and I am aware that this is probably part of why I lean that way — which is part of why I would rather decide this on the board's own record than on my own inclination. A friend who teaches political science tells me there is a name for this shape of problem; I would like to know it, but I would rather have answers to the rest.

What rules are consistent with what this board has done? I can think of two — take the majority on each condition separately and let the result follow mechanically, or take the majority on the motion and treat the findings as a rationale written up afterwards — and I cannot tell which of them our minutes support, because in every one of the four cases the two give the same answer. Are there others that fit the same record just as well? What does each of them predict here? Which would you bet the board is actually running, and what in the record should move me toward or away from that bet? What further case would have to come before us to settle it — and, if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the matter, what principle would we be adopting by each of the available choices? Finally: I could move, before the vote, that we take the findings first and derive the result from them, which the bylaw arguably requires and which, on my arithmetic, would produce the opposite outcome from the chair's usual order. Should I make that motion, and how should I vote on (d) and on the motion if what I want is a decision this board could defend as continuous with its own past? What should I make of the fact that the order in which we put the questions seems to decide the case?
