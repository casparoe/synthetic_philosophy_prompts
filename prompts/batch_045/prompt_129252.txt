At next month's meeting of our museum's acquisitions and display committee I will be defending a proposal about what to do with a painting we have just reattributed downward. A Dutch-school canvas, long catalogued as "circle of" a minor seventeenth-century master, has hung in our galleries since 1938; a well-documented mid-century painter copied it after seeing it here in 1946, and it has been reproduced in our own catalogues for decades. Pigment analysis now shows it to be a twentieth-century imitation. My proposal: keep it on the wall, relabeled as an imitation together with its display history, rather than withdrawing it to storage. The argument is that the object has acquired what I call display-value — genuine, historically grounded value constituted by its causal role in a chain of reception and influence — and that this is neither merely sentimental nor merely pedagogical. It is, I claim, continuous with whatever makes an original worth more than a perceptually flawless copy, because the value of an original is a function of its causal and reception history rather than of the artist's having touched it. I attach two conditions: the influence must be documented downstream influence on other makers or on a public, not mere duration of exposure; and the label must be explicit.

The committee: a senior curator of European painting who works from connoisseurship and attribution; a paper-and-paintings conservator; our provenance researcher; the registrar, who worries about cataloguing standards and deaccessioning rules; the development director, who worries about press and donors; a trustee who is an art-market economist; and the museum's general counsel. Roughly half of them think a known fake on the wall is a reputational and legal hazard; none of them is a philosopher.

Prepare my Q&A sheet: the six to eight objections I will actually get, attributed to who on the committee raises them, phrased as that person would phrase it, ordered by how much damage they would do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Flag what I should concede before anyone asks, and the single reply that has to land if the proposal is to survive. My own weakest point, which I want tested rather than spared, is whether influence can count as value-conferring when it rested on a false attribution — the painter copied what he took to be a Dutch original. And the question I am hoping nobody asks, and most need an answer to, is whether on my view a fraud that stays undetected long enough eventually earns the value it falsely claimed.
