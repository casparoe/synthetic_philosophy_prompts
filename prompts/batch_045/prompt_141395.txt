Something's been bugging me about how animal-welfare rules actually draw their lines. If you want to do research on a monkey you need a protocol, a committee, a written justification, and you owe the animal enrichment — company, puzzles, things to work out — but you can do anything you like to a fruit fly and nobody even files a form. And the rules visibly don't know what to do about octopuses (the EU pulled them into the protected category a few years ago) or about crows and parrots, which get toys and social housing in zoos but which lab rules mostly treat like any other bird. Meanwhile the same system insists on enrichment for animals it fully intends to kill, and lets us raise and slaughter pigs without any of it.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of mind behind this pattern, using Dennett's four-rung ladder as the vocabulary: Darwinian creatures (hard-wired), Skinnerian ones (shaped by reinforcement), Popperian ones (can try out candidate actions in their heads and die of a bad guess instead of a bad outcome), Gregorian ones (get their guesses from others, via culture and language). Specifically:

- Which rung, if any, does the practice strictly presuppose — is the real cutoff the Popperian one, the Gregorian one, or neither? And which rungs does it merely fit nicely with?
- "Can learn" can't be the line, since you can condition a fruit fly. So what is the line? My worry is that the ladder is about design and the committees test performance: a Popperian animal raised in a barren cage will look Skinnerian on every behavioural test anyone runs on it. Does the practice end up protecting whichever rung an animal happens to be displaying in the enviornment we gave it, and what would it have to change to stop doing that?
- Enrichment seems to only make sense for a creature that can be bored. Is boredom a genuine additional harm, and is it essentially a Popperian phenomenon — the suffering of a predictive model running ahead of a world that isn't delivering? If so, can a Gregorian creature be harmed by *not having a culture* — say a parrot raised alone, missing a tradition it never had and so can't miss? Coherent welfare claim, or a category mistake?
- Where is the gradient just "vertebrate, probably sentient" wearing a theory of mind's clothes? Point at two or three places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined (several different theories of mind would license the same rule) and one place where they'd actually diverge.
- If the Gregorian rung does carry weight, what concretely has to change: single housing, killing enriched animals, farmed animals, or something else?

Keep clear on what the practice strictly requires versus what's only a good fit, and don't just restate the ladder back at me.
