For a piece I am writing on how institutions quietly accumulate something very like case law while insisting that every decision is sui generis, I have been following the restitution and shared-stewardship committee of one of the large encyclopedic museums; I have anonymised the institution and altered the details of the objects themselves, because its deliberations are confidential. Here is the track record I have reconstructed. (1) A corpus of court regalia and ancestral altar pieces carried off during the sacking of a West African capital by a European punitive expedition — the Benin kind of case — was returned outright, title and all, to the successor state's national heritage authority, subject to written guarantees of conservation, permanent public display, and no onward sale. (2) Architectural sculpture removed from a standing temple-monument with the permission of the authorities then in power — the Parthenon kind of case — was not returned: the committee found the removal lawful at the time and not theft, and offered a long-term renewable loan instead. (3) Ancestral remains taken from a burial ground by a colonial-era surgeon were returned directly to the descendant community, bypassing the state, and the committee accepted that community's stipulation that they be reburied without study or display; it has since indicated in public statements that human remains run on a separate track from objects. (4) A claim by a religious confraternity for liturgical pieces bought at a Brussels sale in the mid-1990s, with a valid export licence from the country of origin, was rejected: a documented post-independence commercial transaction, no coercion, nothing wrongful. (5) A sacred staff handed over as a gift during a treaty negotiation conducted under military pressure was returned to a community trust chartered by the successor state, on the reasoning that the giver's consent had not been free.

The case now before them is the one I cannot call. Two ivory armlets and a brass commemorative head were bought by the museum's own curator at an Antwerp auction in the early 1900s, in the immediate aftermath of a colonial military campaign against the river kingdom the objects come from; the campaign's records show that the royal compound was entered and burned, and the consignor at the auction was a retired officer who served in it, but nothing ties these particular pieces to that campaign rather than to some earlier circulation. What the museum's files do contain is a letter from the curator to the dealer, written a fortnight before the sale, saying in substance that he would rather not be told how the pieces reached the market and advising that nothing about their origin be put in writing. The claim is a joint one, and the jointness is the difficulty: the successor state's heritage authority wants the objects for a new national museum under the standard public-access guarantees; the descendant royal house, which is on bad terms with that authority, argues that the pieces are not artifacts at all but the regalia of an office, that a museum cannot own them, that they must be veiled and not photographed for part of each year, and that it should keep them in its own shrine-house, which has good climate control but very limited public access; and a third, weaker claim comes from a regional museum in the province of origin that already cares for a related corpus. One further complication is that the museum's governing statute permits permanent disposal only in defined circumstances, so a decision to return may have to be dressed up as something else, as the committee has done at least once before.

What I want from you is not a verdict on who is morally right, but a reconstruction of the decision the committee should reach if it wants to be consistent with itself, and a prediction of the one it probably will reach. Concretely: what two or three rules fit all five precedents about equally well — some variant of a trace-this-object-to-a-discrete-wrong standard, some variant of a colonial-context standard that puts the burden on the museum to show a genuinely free transfer, perhaps a categorical rule about sacred or inalienable things — and where exactly do those rules come apart on this case? Which earlier decision is the closer analog: the access-and-conservation conditions attached in (1), or the deference to a receiving community's own norms in (3), or the invalidity-of-consent reasoning in (5)? What does each candidate rule predict about title, about which of the three claimants should receive, and about whether conditions such as veiling or a photography ban can be accepted at all? What is your single best guess at the outcome and the reasons for preferring it? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so plainly and tell me what principle the committee would be adopting with each of the live options. And finally, since I get to interview two of its members next month: what one hypothetical further claim, and what one question put directly to them, would best expose which rule they are actually running?
