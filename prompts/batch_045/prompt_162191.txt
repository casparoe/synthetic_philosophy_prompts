A colleague and I have been arguing about whether virtual objects are real, and I cannot break his argument. It runs like this: a virtual sword in a persistent online game is nothing over and above a bit pattern; a bit pattern is an abstract object, and an abstract object is the same entity wherever it occurs, in the way that the number seven is. So when two players each hold a Dragonbane Blade, they do not hold two copies of one item — they hold numerically one and the same item, and the server merely grants two users access to it. From this he concludes that deleting one player's blade eo ipso deletes the other's, and that virtual theft is a category mistake: you cannot appropriate something that is not numerically distinct from what its owner already has. Our server was recently rolled back to a pre-exploit snapshot and then forked, so the blades duplicated in the exploit now appear to exist twice over, and he says that only restates the puzzle rather than answering it. Can you pinpoint where the argument fails: what fact about a running system makes two qualitatively identical blades two objects rather than one, and how much of the case for treating virtual theft as genuine theft actually rests on that fact rather than on something else entirely?
