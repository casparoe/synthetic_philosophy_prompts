I'm a senior in high school, and since this summer I've been stuck on an argument my friend — a first-year biology major — keeps making about the units of selection. I've written it out as carefully as I can. (1) Selection occurs only when entities vary in heritable ways that make them differ in how much they contribute to later generations, so anything that is to count as a unit of selection must (a) come in variants, (b) have offspring, and (c) transmit its variant to those offspring with enough fidelity for the differences to accumulate over generations. (2) Gene lineages satisfy all three: a gene is copied, its copies are copies of it, and the copying can be nearly perfect across very many generations. (3) Organisms and groups do not. An organism's offspring are not copies of it — a sexually produced offspring is one recombination out of astronomically many — and a fissioning group's daughter groups do not inherit its composition with the fidelity that cumulative selection needs. So neither organisms nor groups can be the things selection sorts. (4) Therefore genes are the units of selection; organisms and groups are vehicles, things genes build whose properties matter only insofar as they shift gene frequencies. (5) So no trait is an adaptation for the organism or for the group; there are only gene-level effects, and saying that the heart's function is to pump blood for the organism is shorthand that cashes out entirely in gene-level bookkeeping. (6) And the argument runs one level down too: a body is a population of cells, cells vary and reproduce at different rates and pass variants to daughter cells, and cell lineages are what literally get copied — so the organism is not a unit of selection either, and the germ line's sequestration, immune systems, and hearts are cell-lineage (ultimately gene-level) effects with organism-shaped side effects. (7) So there are no organism-level and no group-level adaptations at all, and the appearance of organisms as designed wholes is an artifact of selection among gene lineages.

I can't accept (7), and neither can he — he fully intends to keep saying that hearts are for pumping blood and that some social insects are sterile for the colony's sake — but neither of us can say where his argument stops being sound. I tried two cases on him. Case one: a colonial species whose colonies reproduce by splitting into daughter colonies that carry a biased sample of the parent's genotypes; colonies with more of trait T split more often, even though within any single colony bearers of T leave fewer descendants than non-bearers. He says that's fine, the group-level pattern is just the sum of gene-level effects, and that the two descriptions are competing bookkeeping for one and the same change in frequency — like describing a cloud by its particles or by its center of mass — so calling genes the unit is a choice of description, not a discovery. Case two: an organism with no early-sequestered germ line, so that a mutation arising in a branch that later flowers can end up in seeds. He bites the bullet there and says such a thing simply isn't a unit of selection, it's a coalition of competing cell lineages, and that what makes animals units is that we do sequester a germ line early. Both answers feel to me like they might be the same answer, but I can't tell whether that answer rescues his argument, quietly turns (7) into a conditional claim about the conditions under which a higher level can be a unit at all, or refutes it.
