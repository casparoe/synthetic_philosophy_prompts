**The Author's Double Effect**

**1. The scene that started this.** Halvard is a signalman at a junction. A runaway train is heading for five workmen on the main line. He can throw a lever that sends it onto a siding where one man, Reuben, is working. He throws it. Reuben dies, the five live. This is the bog-standard switch case, and everyone who reads chapter 3 argues about whether Halvard is a murderer.

Then, in chapter 19, the same sixty seconds gets narrated again — this time by Reuben's sister, standing on the platform. In her version the lever gets one subordinate clause: *the lever went over and Reuben was gone*. Same physics, same body count, same agent, same intentions. But readers of chapter 19 mostly don't argue about murder. They feel terrible and then they turn the page. Their verdict has moved, and the only thing that moved is the telling.

**2.** The doctrine of double effect says, roughly: there's a difference between bringing about something bad *on purpose*, as what you're aiming at or as your means, and bringing it about as a foreseen by-product of something good you're doing. The second can be permissible where the first is not, given proportionality and so on. The reason people deploy it on trolley cases is that it seems to line up with the split intuition: turning the lever feels tolerable, shoving a stranger off a footbridge onto the tracks does not.

**3. Here's what I think is interesting.** The lever/footbridge difference is supposed to be a difference in the *causal shape* of the act: in one case the death is a side effect, in the other it's the instrument. But my two chapters suggest the verdicts track something else. Two things, really.

(a) *Aspectual privilege.* Whoever narrates gets to pick the description under which the act is evaluated. "I turned a lever" and "I killed a man" are the same event. Agents do this in real life too, and they're usually believed.

(b) *Retroactive re-description.* A later chapter can retroactively make an earlier act count as intended. If chapter 20 reveals that Halvard would have thrown the lever even if the five had been safe — say because Reuben owed him money, or because he'd been waiting for a chance — then readers of chapter 3 go back and say, no, he was a murderer all along. But nothing about the causal shape of chapter 3 changed. Only the description the audience finally ratifies changed.

**4.** So the proposal: the line that's doing the work isn't the intended/foreseen line at all. It's what I'm calling *aspectual authority* — who has the standing to fix the description of an act, and whose description the audience will ratify. Notice that aspectual authority is not distributed morally. It tracks social standing, eloquence, who gets to speak first, whose version gets the last word. The signalman has a lot of it; the sister has less; the narrator has all of it, and that's the part that makes me uneasy about my own job.

**5. A test I want to propose**, mostly for my own use but I think it generalizes: call it the first-person disclosure test. A foreseen harm counts as *intended* if the agent could not, without self-deception, narrate the action in the first person, present tense, to the person who will be harmed or to that person's family, using the verb that says what physically happens. Halvard can say "I am throwing this lever." Can he say "I am dropping Reuben onto the rails in front of the train"? If he has to lie, or swap in a softer verb, or go passive — *the lever goes over* — then it's intended. I like this because it makes *lying* the tell rather than metaphysics, and lying is the thing novelists actually know about.

**6.** Where I think it goes next, not written yet: offstage harm. Readers forgive characters for harms the narration never shows at all. That suggests first-person narration might be a moral technology for laundering intention, which is a depressing thing to discover about the form I work in.
