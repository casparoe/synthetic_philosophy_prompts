I'm a journalist working on a feature about how moral distinctions get built into hardware and protocol. The piece started with a few days I spent in an intensive care unit watching a withdrawal-of-treatment protocol being run, and then with the adjacent protocols used for organ donation after the circulatory determination of death, where the withdrawal is staged in a particular order and the declaration of death is timed, and where everyone involved is scrupulous that nothing anyone does should be describable as bringing the death about. What I keep returning to is how much institutional weight is placed on the idea that there is a categorical difference between bringing about a death and merely allowing one, in circumstances where which of those two descriptions applies seems to turn on facts about defaults that nobody would otherwise treat as morally loaded.

What I want from you is as strong an argument as you can construct for a specific claim — stronger than the argument I've been able to make to myself, which keeps collapsing into hand-waving. The claim is this: in what I've started calling default-determined deaths — cases in which whether an agent kills or merely lets die is fixed not by her intentions, not by the directness or size of her causal contribution, and not by anything about what the patient may demand of her, but by an earlier and morally indifferent fact about how a mechanism, a protocol, or a causal baseline happens to be arranged — the killing/letting-die distinction carries no moral weight of its own, and a policy that treats the two as different in kind in those cases is enforcing an artifact of arrangement rather than tracking a moral difference. Please build the argument around three manoeuvres that seem to me to launder a killing into a letting-die while holding the agent's aim and the patient's prospects fixed: the dependency manoeuvre, in which the clinician first brings it about — with a paralytic, or simply by the intubation itself — that the patient cannot survive without support, and then merely declines to provide it, so that the very death which would have been a killing had it been produced directly is now classified as the underlying condition taking its course; the engineering manoeuvre, in which two clinically indistinguishable ventilators — one that runs continuously unless switched off and one that stops unless re-armed at intervals — mean that a purchasing decision made months earlier for reasons of cost determines whether the clinician who wants this patient dead must act or merely refrain; and the timing manoeuvre, in which one waits, for no reason connected to the patient's interests, until the underlying pathology has advanced far enough to be described as the cause, at which point the identical intervention changes category. If you think any of these misdescribes the case, or is a distraction, say so and substitute the strongest version you can.

I need the argument to survive the best defences of the distinction rather than the weakest: the rights-based asymmetry, on which a patient's claim that you not introduce a lethal threat has no equally stringent counterpart in a claim that you keep him alive; the causal or counterfactual account, on which the difference lies in whether the death depends on what you did or on a threatening process you merely decline to arrest; and the baseline view, on which the distinction tracks whether you worsen someone's prospects or merely fail to improve them. For each, tell me what it predicts about the three manoeuvres and why that prediction should be rejected. Then do three more things: identify the single premise of your own argument that you think is most likely to be false, and say what would follow for the claim if it is; tell me honestly whether what you've given me is really just the familiar equivalence thesis about acts and omissions in new vocabulary, or whether the laundering cases add something that the standard pair-of-uncles-style cases do not; and finish with one sentence stating the claim as I could put it to a general reader, plus a short note on which step a hospital ethics committee would balk at first and why. Please argue from the cases themselves rather than from the literature — I can't verify references from where I'm working, and I'd rather the reasoning be checkable on its own terms.
