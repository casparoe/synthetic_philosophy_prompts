I run a sort of irregular movie night with a couple of people from my backend/infra team — one film a month, then we argue about it over beers — and last week we watched Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove and I've been chewing on it since, because I can't tell whether it's a real argument against nuclear deterrence or just a comedy about a system with no rollback. For context: I have zero philosophy training, I spend my days on distributed systems, and the war room scenes hit me like a postmortem doc — a single paranoid node (Ripper) with unilateral authority, planes that only accept messages through a discriminator keyed to one man's doodle pad, a recall path that fails, and then the punchline that the other side has built an automatic, undeactivatable, unannouced cobalt doomsday machine that is going to fire no matter what anyone in the war room now does. I'd like a reading of the film through deterrence theory — the paradox that a threat only deters if you'd credibly carry it out, but carrying it out would be insane, so you end up wanting the capacity to be irrational, or wanting the outcome to be partly out of your hands. Where does the movie dramatize that paradox properly, and where does it just get laughs out of a failure mode that a better-designed system wouldn't have?

Two things I've been sketching that I'd like you to use or knock down. First, what I'm calling the announcement/verification trap: an automatic retaliation device is only a threat if the adversary knows about it, but announcing it isn't enough either, because you could always announce one you don't have — so real deterrence seems to need the adversary to *verify* it, which pushes you toward demonstrating or leaking in ways that are themselves dangerous. The film has Strangelove make roughly the first half of that point and the ambassador answer with the Party Congress reveal, but does it get at the second half, or does it just stop at the joke? Second, what I'll call the coercion gap: a perfect commitment device deters a first strike but can't *compel* anyone to do anything — once it's running there's nobody left to bargain with, no way to trade your way out, no off switch on either side. Does the movie show that? I keep thinking about the scene where the president is on the phone helping the Soviets shoot down his own planes, and about the mine-shaft plan where the escape hatch immediately turns into a new race the generals want to win. Does the film end up saying that deterrence logic is a self-replicating pattern rather than a policy anyone chose?
