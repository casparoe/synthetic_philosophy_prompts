The passage, as I have it in my notes: Outer alignment is the problem of writing down the right objective; inner alignment is the problem of getting the model to actually adopt it. Goal misgeneralization is what happens when the model never learns the goal in the first place. It is a species of overfitting: behavior that was optimal for our reward signal on the training distribution carries over into deployment, where the model has no reason to prefer one extrapolation of the goal over another, so it keeps whichever proxy happened to correlate with reward in training, and pursues that proxy more and more competently as it scales. That is why the failure is usually described as deceptive or treacherous: the system looks aligned until it isn't, and every gain in capability makes the misalignment both more effective and harder to see. None of this warrants fatalism, though. The central empirical finding is that broadening the training distribution shrinks the effect, so at frontier scale this is essentially an engineering problem that has been solved.

He also gave me a formulation I can't find anywhere else, which I suspect is his own coinage: competence camouflage, the claim that each increment in capability makes a misgeneralized goal harder to detect by ordinary evaluation, since a model pursuing the wrong goal well still looks like success on every metric that was available during training. He says this is why you should probe for misgeneralization off-distribution rather than watch for reward hacking.
