I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in philosophy or Buddhist studies. I read on my own — mostly English translations of early Buddhist texts, and, for reasons that began separately, a good deal of the effective-altruism literature — and the two reading habits keep colliding over one sentence.

The sentence is "Buddhism is about intentions, not consequences." As I have seen it explained by people who know the languages, it descends from a line in the Pali canon where the Buddha, asked what karma is, answers with a word usually rendered "intention" but said to be nearer to "volition" or "the mind's setting itself toward something" than to the loose modern sense of "meaning well." I mention that only because the gap between those two English senses may be doing a lot of the work.

I met it last in an argument about whether a Buddhist ought to care whether her actions do any good. My interlocutor's use was this: a Buddhist's reasons for acting stop at the quality of the mind she acts from; whether the action feeds anyone, cures anyone, or alters anything in the world is, in his words, consequentialist contamination, and auditing outcomes is something only a utilitarian would insist on. He deployed the sentence to dismiss, wholesale, the practice of comparing two candidate actions by their expected effects.

What I want is the menu hiding inside the sentence. The distinctions I have been turning over, without being able to tell which are load-bearing, are roughly these: a claim about what fixes an action's karmic quality, versus a claim about the criterion of right action, versus a claim about praiseworthiness or merit as opposed to rightness; a claim about what is within an agent's control; a claim about which consequences are the relevant ones — effects on the world, versus effects on the agent's own mind, dispositions and progress toward liberation; a claim riding on the fact that the standard term is often translated "skillful," which sounds like a notion measured by success; and a claim drawn from the no-self teaching, to the effect that if there is no one who owns the outcome, outcomes cannot matter. I also cannot tell whether any of these sits comfortably with the precepts being stated as prohibitions on bringing things about — on killing, on taking what is not given — rather than as prohibitions on states of mind.

To pin the readings down, three cases. (A) You spend a day at a food bank so overstaffed that your presence changes nothing measurable; you go from unfeigned goodwill and your mind is patient and generous throughout. (B) You take on a tedious administrative job that keeps a free clinic open, which reliably treats dozens of people a month; you do it resentfully, mainly because you were shamed into it. (C) You try to help, through no malice, and botch it, causing harm you did not foresee.

Could you lay the readings out as a list — a table would suit me best — with one row each: the precise thesis stated in a sentence; whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false or confused; whether it would actually deliver my interlocutor his conclusion; and what it implies about A, B and C, separately for "which act has more merit" and "which act is the better one to do," since I suspect those two come apart. Finally, which reading you take people to mean when they repeat the sentence, and whether that is the reading their argument needs.
