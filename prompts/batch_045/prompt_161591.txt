I've been chewing on the no-trade theorem (Milgrom and Stokey, 1982) and I think I've backed myself into something silly but I cant see the way out.

(1) The epistemic case for markets — Hayek's thing about prices pooling knowledge nobody possesses in full — says private information has to end up in something public. The only public output of a market is the price, and prices only move when somebody actually trades.
(2) So an informationally valuable market needs nonzero volume: no trades, no aggregation.
(3) No-trade theorem: with common priors, common knowledge of rationality, and common knowledge of who is willing to trade on what terms, nobody can profit from private information, because my willingness to trade gives away what I know and once you have it you no longer want to trade with me.
(4) Nothing in that argument is really about money. Take any institution where people hold private information, act on it in public, and are known to be competent and to share a background framework: journals, peer review, expert panels, prediction markets. Call them epistemic markets. Same reasoning runs: publishing, recommending, or forecasting gives away what you know, so by the time anyone reads it, it is already discounted and carries no news.
(5) So an ideal epistemic market — fully rational participants, shared framework, transparent procedures — transmits nothing, and by (1) and (2) aggregates nothing.
(6) What does make it work is noise: authors publishing for tenure, pundits asserting from career motives, referees with idiosyncratic tastes. And here is the part that worries me: the better the institution gets at screening noise out — stricter review, better calibrated forecasters, publication bias cleaned up — the less information flows. Perfect inquirers would be useless to learn from.
(7) But that is nuts. Deferring to good experts is rational, and journals that filter harder are more informative, not less.

I want to keep (3), since it is a theorem, and I want to keep (7). So something has to break in (1), (2), (4), (5) or (6). Which step goes, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost the rest? I especially can't tell whether the right move is to say aggregation doesn't need volume at all (the clearing price tells you everything even if nobody trades), or to say (4) smuggles something in by treating a shared framework as a common prior, or to say (6) conflates having non-epistemic motives with being irrational.
