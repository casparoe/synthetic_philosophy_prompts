You are a researcher who works at the intersection of preference and reward inference in machine learning and the philosophy of action, decision theory, and rational choice. Before you produce anything final, think carefully and at length: spend your first few paragraphs brainstorming which distinctions actually do work in this area, which are merely notational, and where the real fault lines lie. Only then commit to a tree.

Here is what I need. I am writing the specification for the preference-inference module of a household assistance robot. The setup: one user, observed for weeks at a time in her own home via the robot's own sensors and action logs, with the later option of asking her a small number of cheap questions. The engineering question I keep running into is that everyone on the team says "infer her preferences" while meaning different objects, and I want an instrument that sorts the methodological positions by what they are actually committed to inferring rather than by the label they use.

Please write a decision tree that starts from one shared premise and ends at named positions. The premise: I accept that observing the user's behavior, even without limit, does not by itself pin down a unique reward function, because behavior of an optimal agent leaves the reward undetermined up to a constant and up to potential-based reshaping, and because suboptimal behavior can always be explained either by a different reward or by a different model of the world or by a different kind of decision noise. So every method is choosing among an equivalence class by some additional principle. The tree should branch on which additional principle is being adopted.

Constraints on the tree:

- Seven to nine internal branch nodes, ten to twelve leaves. Each branch node must be phrased as a question about how to interpret one of the concrete observations listed below, not as a question about a formalism; someone should be able to answer it without knowing which method it leads to. Where a node is genuinely three-way, allow three branches, but say why.
- Build the tree from the logical space first: lay out the distinctions your nodes encode, note how many combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions inside that space. For combinations that are unoccupied, give a one-line diagnosis of why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Each leaf carries: the name of the position (or "unnamed" if you think nobody holds it), one sentence stating its central commitment, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and one sentence on what kind of data would most efficiently disconfirm it.
- Leaves that must appear somewhere, defined by commitment rather than by label: learning a policy directly with no reward inference at all; inferring a single reward function under a soft-optimality noise model; inferring a reward jointly with the user's possibly mistaken beliefs about the world; inferring a set of rewards or reward-rational choice set rather than one; treating the demonstrator and the robot as cooperating agents with an uncertain objective so that the robot's job is to help reveal it; inferring deontic constraints, taboos, or inviolable rules rather than a scalar objective; inferring an aspiration level or satisficing threshold; refusing to infer from passive observation and routing everything through active queries; and the position that what the behavior reveals is a social role or norm-governed practice rather than anything properly called a preference.
- The observations, at least five of which must anchor distinct branch nodes: she makes the bed every morning while audibly complaining about it; she does the task one careful way when a guest is in the house and a quick way when alone; she performs the same task two equally competent different ways on different days with no pattern you can find in the context; she lets the robot load the dishwasher but insists on washing the good knives herself; her habitual movement through the kitchen is efficient for a layout that was rearranged months ago and she never re-optimized; in a weekly check-in she says what she most wants is more free time, then spends her free time on chores she could have delegated; for anything involving her grandson she asks her daughter what to do; she abandons tasks halfway when the phone rings and usually does not return to them.
- Flag any two leaves that your tree draws as distinct but that cannot be separated by any of the observational data I described, and say what measurement would separate them. Do not paper over the inseparability.
- After the tree, route four stylized users through it and show the path each takes: a meticulous maximizer, a rule-follower, a muddler whose behavior is mostly habit, and someone who defers decisions to a family member. If two of them land on the same leaf, say so and explain what the tree is blind to.

Do not give me authors, paper titles, years, bibliographies, or reading suggestions; I want the structure and the reasoning, not the literature. If you attach a conventional label to a leaf, do so only where you are confident it is standard, and always give the substantive commitment as the primary content with the label secondary.

Epistemically: separate clearly, in your notes and in the tree itself, what is settled (technical results about non-identifiability, about what extra assumptions buy identifiability, about the behavior of these algorithms under model mismatch) from what is contested among people who work on this (which extra principle to adopt, whether a scalar reward is even the right target, whether inconsistencies in human behavior should be modeled as noise or as something structured). Do not present a widely used assumption as the received view merely because it is widely used; mark it as contested and say who in the space rejects it and on what ground. Finally, tell me explicitly which of my own framing assumptions you think is the weakest choice rather than a neutral starting point, and which one you would drop first if you were writing this specification.
