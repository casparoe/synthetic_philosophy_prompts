My old housemate did a philosophy MA and now runs a small blog, and he posted a short explainer on contextualism and pragmatic encroachment that ends by plugging a view of his own. I can't tell which parts are standard and which he's made up, but the second half smells off to me while the first half sounds right. Here's the paragraph:

Contextualism and pragmatic encroachment are really two versions of one idea. Both say that whether you know something isn't settled by your evidence alone. Contextualists think the extra ingredient is conversational: when someone raises a skeptical scenario, the standards for knows go up, so the same sentence picks out a stronger relation. Pragmatic encroachers think the extra ingredient is practical: when a lot rides on being right you simply need more evidence in order to know, and when nothing rides on it, weaker evidence is enough. That means both views are exposed to the same objection — knowledge becomes sensitive to facts outside the subject's evidence, so a person can stop knowing something without gaining or losing any evidence and without the truth changing, and that flickering is absurd. Really all this shows is that knowledge isn't a state of the person at all but a status we hand out for our own purposes, and that's the strongest argument for the plain invariantist view that knowledge is just a matter of having enough evidence, full stop.

He then says the way out is to keep things invariant about knowledge itself but let stakes move a different threshold — the one for what counts as reasonable reliance on what you know. He calls this reliance encroachment rather than knowledge encroachment, and claims it dodges the flickering complaint, since your knowledge never changes, only what it's sensible to bet on.
