I keep reaching for the strategy-stealing argument as a general template for thinking about initiative. Not just for games in the narrow sense, but whenever someone asks me whether it is better to move first: in a negotiation, in a product launch, in deciding whether to file first in a dispute. Before I lean on it any further I want a map of its exposure. The position I hold has three parts.

(1) The formal core. In any finite two-player game of perfect information that is symmetric between the players and option-monotone, the first player can guarantee at least a draw; and if the game cannot end in a draw, the first player has a winning strategy. By option-monotone I mean: giving a player an additional mark of her own colour on an otherwise empty cell can never lower her game-theoretic value, because she is always free to ignore it. Hex is the paradigm case, and the usual no-draw argument for Hex supplies the second half of the result.

(2) A claim about scope. Option-monotonicity is not an exotic condition. It holds wherever a player's marks are permanent and cannot interact destructively with her own other marks, and it fails only in games with a misère or self-blocking structure, or in games with a forced-move structure of the zugzwang type, where having an extra option can be a liability. So there is a large and natural class of competitive situations in which initiative is provably never a liability, and where the interesting question is only how much it is worth.

(3) A claim about practical import. The non-constructivity of the argument is not a serious practical objection. Knowing that a winning strategy exists already settles the deliberative questions that matter: do not accept a draw, do not pay for the privilege of moving second, and spend the available search budget on lines consistent with a first-player win rather than on lines the result has already excluded.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know separately which of them a defender of the position should herself concede as counting against it, as opposed to which she is entitled to dismiss as resting on a misreading of what was claimed. Five candidate defeaters, in the order I currently weight them:

First, the counterexample family. In the misère variant of Hex, where the player who completes her own connection loses, the surplus mark is a liability and the argument gives nothing. Is that a real defeater to (2), or does the defender simply reply that the theorem was never stated for non-monotone games, and if so, how much has the scope claim actually been narrowed by that reply?

Second, surplus fragility. Here is a notion I have been using: let the surplus fragility of a game be one minus the proportion of legal opening moves from which the guaranteed result is still available. Note that the standard argument does not merely fail to name a winning first move; it proceeds by supposing the second player has a winning strategy and deriving a contradiction, so the strategy it exhibits for the first player is defined only under a supposition that is false. There is therefore no lower bound whatever on the proportion of winning first moves. If that proportion is very small, then a bounded agent with a finite search budget may have a higher probability of winning by moving second, in which case (3) is not just incomplete but possibly inverted — and, if the opponent is the stronger player, moving first may mean making the first irrevocable commitment at the widest node of the tree. Does surplus fragility defeat (3), or does it only restate the non-constructivity point in sharper form, and if the latter, is the sharper form any more damaging than the original?

Third, more than two players. The argument appears to have no n-player analogue: there is no single opponent's strategy to steal, and in multiplayer settings moving first can make one the target of the rest of the table. How much of the position's practical reach depends on the two-player, zero-sum or near-zero-sum setting, and should (2) be read as silently restricted?

Fourth, the boundary cases of the formal core. Finiteness is doing real work: in a game with infinitely long plays, having spare moves available can be a burden rather than an asset, and the determinacy on which the argument's conclusion rests is itself a substantive assumption. Is that a live failure mode or merely a logically possible one?

Fifth, the vacuity worry. Is option-monotonicity an independently checkable condition, or is it in practice attributed to a game because the first player appears to do well there? If the latter, I want to know whether (2) has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or a reasonable division of labour between a theorem and its application.

Two further things. If you think a case can be constructed in which option-monotonicity and symmetry both hold and yet initiative is genuinely a liability for the agent who possesses it, construct it; that is the single thing that would move me most. And if you think no such case can be constructed, say what rules it out. Finally, tell me which of my three claims carries the most risk on its own, since I suspect the exposure is unevenly distributed and that (1) is nearly airtight while the risk is concentrated elsewhere.
