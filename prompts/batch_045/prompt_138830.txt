I work at a small research-funding foundation, and we are arguing about whether to stop assigning each early-stage proposal to a single reviewer and instead route it to a three-person panel that meets for twenty minutes and decides by majority vote. Both sides cite the Condorcet jury theorem, and I have come to think the citation is doing less work than everyone assumes, so I want a number with its derivation rather than a survey of what has been said about the theorem.

The proposition I want your credence on is this: for our actual decisions — binary fund/don't-fund calls on early-stage proposals, made by reviewers drawn from the same few subfields — replacing the single reviewer with a deliberating three-person majority would raise the long-run rate of correct decisions by at least five percentage points. Take 'correct' to be settled by a rubric we write down now and apply three years later: the funded project produced the result it proposed, where 'produced' excludes outcomes that would have arrived anyway without our money. Take the question to be resolved by the obvious experiment: for two years, randomly assign a subset of proposals to be assessed both by a single reviewer and, separately and independently, by a three-person panel, then compare the two hit rates against that rubric. Give me (i) your probability that the panel arm beats the single-reviewer arm by five points or more, (ii) your probability on the bare comparative with the margin ignored, and (iii) your probability that the proposition has a determinate truth value at all, given how much weight 'produced' and 'would have arrived anyway' are carrying. Hold cost out of the picture: assume the extra reviewer-hours are free.

I want the decomposition rather than a bare number, and I will show you my own attempt so you can tell me where it is broken. I modelled each decision as having, with probability λ, a 'shared-signal' character in which one common cue — the topic's current fashion, the framing of the call for proposals, a shared methodological prior — drives all three reviewers to the same verdict and is right with probability q; with probability 1−λ the three judgments are effectively independent and each is right with probability p. On that model the panel is right with probability λq + (1−λ)·M₃(p), where M₃ is majority-of-three accuracy under independence, and the single reviewer is right with probability λq + (1−λ)p, so the panel's entire advantage comes to (1−λ)(M₃(p) − p): never negative provided p exceeds one half, and entirely independent of q. The same model also predicts an error floor in the neighbourhood of λ(1−q) that no further enlargement of the panel removes. I do not trust this result, and I suspect the reason is that the stylization makes the shared component neutral between the two arms and holds p fixed across them. So: tell me what the model excludes that could make the panel actively worse than the single reviewer — I have in mind herding and information cascades, deliberation lowering effective p by substituting deference for private judgment, the shared cue being anti-informative in the sense that q < p, and the interaction of correlated error with unequal competence — then assign a number and a one-line justification to each of your sub-questions, show roughly how they combine into the three probabilities I asked for, and say how much of your final number rests on each of those exclusions. Our programme director puts 0.85 on the five-point claim and one of our board members puts 0.25; I am at 0.5. Tell me which sub-question their disagreement most likely lives in, which single measurement would move your own number the most, and what, if anything, would push it below one half.
