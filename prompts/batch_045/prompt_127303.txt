Hi — I'm doing a PhD on the politics of atrocity designation, and I'm also the part-time characterisation analyst at a tiny documentation outfit: four staff, no lawyer on payroll, and we put out maybe two public characterisation notes a month, three hundred words max, that journalists then quote and that two rival survivor associations either circulate or publicly disown. The board signs each one off within 48 hours of us flagging an incident. The decision that eats us alive every time is whether the note uses the word genocide — Lemkin's word, and the one that does all the political work — rather than crimes against humanity, ethnic cleansing, or just a description of the acts.

What I need off you is an actual procedure, not an essay: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each given we have 48 hours and no counsel, where the procedure halts with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values question dressed as a factual one. Two things it has to hold apart: whether the legal/moral test is met (intent to destroy a protected group as such, in whole or in part — including Lemkin's broader sense of destroying a group by dismantling its way of life, which the Convention drafters mostly dropped) and whether we should say the word in a note, given that naming has effects on funding, on our field staff, and on who gets to count as the victim. It should also say which distinctions survive being simplified and which don't, and where it will misfire.

Two in-house heuristics we've been using: a swap test (could someone escape the violence by ceasing to be a member of the group? if yes, they're targeted as a group, not for what they did) and campaign-shape intent (inferring the specific intent from the organisation of the campaign when nobody wrote it down). Tell me whether to keep, fix or bin them.

Then run it on three: (1) a campaign against a pastoralist community — men killed, wells poisoned, herds destroyed, survivors pushed into towns where they can't keep livestock, no one says the word destroy; (2) the killing of a few hundred religious elders and the levelling of shrines in one district, out of a group of a million, where those are the people who hold the ritual knowledge; (3) a case against the community I'm from, where a leaked manual and one minister's speech get us over your evidentiary bar — and where our in-country researchers, who are from that community, are the ones who'd be arrested. I want it in British English, and I want you to be straight about the case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like.
