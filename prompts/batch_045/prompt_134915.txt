I was an analytical chemist before I retired — process spectroscopy mostly, thirty-odd years of coaxing honest numbers out of badly behaved instruments — and since last autumn I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the university in town, which I'm enjoying more than I expected. Last week the topic was higher-order theories of consciousness and I didn't get much of a word in, so I've been picking at my notes since.

A friend who did some philosophy years ago sent me this when I asked him what the view actually amounts to:

"Higher-order theories say that what makes a mental state conscious is that there's a second, higher-order state about it — you're aware of being in it. So consciousness isn't an intrinsic property of the first-order state, something like its intensity; it's a relation between two states, and it's the higher part of the mind doing the noticing that does the work. That's meant to explain the difference between being in pain and merely having damaged tissue: the pain only becomes conscious when the higher-order state is there too. The theory is usually credited to an American philosopher called Rosenthal, and it's a 'thought' theory because the higher-order state has to be a thought, which is why it predicts that creatures without the relevant concepts — babies, and most animals — aren't conscious, or at least aren't conscious in the way we are. It also explains blindsight: the patient has no first-order visual state to be aware of, so there's nothing for the higher-order state to target, and they genuinely aren't conscious of seeing anything. The main rival is the global broadcasting view, but that's arguably the same claim in different words, since broadcasting just is a kind of higher-order monitoring. And notice that once you've said what makes a state conscious you don't need the hard problem any more — that's the attraction of the whole approach."

I don't want to go back to the group and repeat something silly, so: how fair is this? I'd be grateful if you'd go through it more or less sentence by sentence — what a defender of the higher-order view would happily sign on to, what they'd wince at, and what their critics would call a distortion. And for the parts that are wrong, does the friend's final flourish still follow once they're corrected, or does it collapse?

Two things of my own, which may or may not be to the point. The picture I formed while reading, no doubt because of the instruments, is this: a detector can sit there producing a perfectly good signal and nothing counts as a measurement until the signal is read out and logged, and if the read-out channel is miscalibrated you either get a number for something that isn't there or no number for something that is. That's how I was picturing the higher-order state — as a read-out, not as part of the measurement itself. But then I got stuck on whether the theory is committed to saying that a faulty read-out could log a first-order state the subject isn't actually in, and would then have to call that a conscious experience anyway; and also on whether adding a second state really leaves the first state unchanged, which is what the "it's just a relation between states" bit seems to need. Is the analogy doing me any favours or does it break down somewhere important?

Also: one person in the group kept saying the whole thing had been more or less confirmed by experiments on metacognition and confidence ratings, and that sounded to me like a different claim dressed up in similar words. Am I being unfair, or is that a genuine slide? My instinct is definately that the animals-and-babies bit can't be something the theory is happy to predict, but I've noticed in my short acquaintance with the subject that what a theory is stuck with and what its opponents say it's stuck with are often two different things, so tell me which one that is.
