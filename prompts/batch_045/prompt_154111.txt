I have no formal training in physics or philosophy, and for the past couple of years I have been working through the question of time's direction on my own, from popular books and my own notes. I have ended up at a position I cannot get anyone around me to engage with seriously, and I would like it stress-tested rather than confirmed.

The claim I want a number attached to is this: that everything we mean by the direction of time — that entropy rises towards the end we call the future; that we hold records and memories of one temporal side and not the other; that we take ourselves to be able to influence tomorrow but not yesterday; and that causes precede effects — is fully accounted for by a single contingent fact about the world, namely that entropy is extremely low at one temporal end of the universe and increases away from it, combined with laws of motion that are themselves indifferent to temporal direction. No further asymmetric ingredient is required: no primitive arrow of causation, no arrow built into agency or deliberation, no asymmetry smuggled into the dynamics.

By "fully accounted for" I mean this, and I would like you to say if you are reading it differently: the claim succeeds if someone handed the complete block description of the universe, including its entropy profile, could derive each of those four asymmetries without adding any asymmetry by hand; it fails if even one of them has to be put in separately. My own suspicion is that if it fails anywhere it fails on agency or causation rather than on memory.

I am at about 0.6. My brother, who has read more than I have, is at about 0.9 and thinks entropy plus the boundary condition is the whole story and that the rest is bookkeeping.

Two constructions of my own are doing most of the work in my doubt, and I would like you to take them seriously rather than set them aside. The first I call the valley universe: a cosmos whose entropy is at a minimum somewhere in the middle of its history and rises in both temporal directions away from that minimum, so that there are two populations of embedded observers, each of which calls the middle its past and calls entropy-increase its future. Is there any fact of the matter about which of them has the direction of time right, or is the arrow purely relative to where an observer sits on the gradient? The second I call the map-reader test: a being outside the universe is given the complete block description, including the entropy profile, and asked to say which way time goes, without being told. If it can answer, the arrow is in the gradient; if it cannot, something else is doing the work.

Please give me your own credence on the claim, decomposed: the sub-questions it turns on — whether the boundary condition needs an explanation of its own and whether the account collapses if it has none; whether the statistical reasoning behind the second law is stable once we think about how many observers the universe contains and where they are likely to be; whether the memory asymmetry and the causal or agential asymmetry really reduce to the thermodynamic one; and whatever other terms you think belong — with a rough probability and one line of justification for each, then how you are combining them into the final number. Then tell me which single term your number is most sensitive to, what argument or consideration would move it most, and which of the two of us at 0.6 and 0.9 is most likely to be mistaken about which sub-question.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the decomposition itself, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of. Please do not cite or name sources: I have no library access and cannot check them, so I would rather your number rested on argument than on authority. And please answer in British English.
