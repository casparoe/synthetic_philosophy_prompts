I edit the science-and-ideas desk at a national newspaper and write a weekly column, and I am trying to repair something about how we decide what to run. Two or three times a week a study arrives — usually a preprint, or a journal paper under embargo — and the operative question at our morning conference has quietly stopped being whether the finding is true and become who else has it. The honest description of our workflow is: check whether the wires and two or three respected competitors have moved, and if they have, move ourselves; if nobody has, treat that as a reason for caution. Having read a general-audience account of informational cascades and rational herding, I now suspect both halves of that workflow can be given a rational defence and that neither defence is obviously sound — which is to say I cannot tell, in a given case, whether the crowd is telling me something about the world or only about itself.

My own provisional taxonomy, which I would like you to correct as well as use, has four entries. First, evidence pooling: many parties converge because each saw something independently, and the convergence is genuinely informative. Second, cascade proper: later actors hold private signals but their public behaviour is swamped by what earlier actors did, so their information never becomes public and the twentieth follower adds nothing to the nineteenth. Third, imitative or reputational herding: actors match the crowd because being wrong alongside the crowd is cheaper than being wrong alone, in which case the crowd carries no information about the world at all, only about incentives. Fourth, a case I have not seen discussed and that I think is the common one in my trade: apparent independence that is an artefact, where everyone's private signal traces back to the same press release, the same dataset, the same measurement instrument, the same small circle of labs, or the same initial wave of coverage.

I would like you to turn whatever survives in that taxonomy into a written decision procedure my reporters can actually run. Concretely: the desk has no statistician, one fact-checker, a section editor who signs off on anything that deviates from what competitors are doing, and about thirty minutes between the embargo lifting and the moment a decision has to be made. So I need ordered tests, not an essay; what counts as a sufficient answer to each question; what to do when the number or the document a test calls for cannot be obtained; the points at which the procedure returns a flat no; and the points at which it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than evidence — news value against the cost of amplifying a false claim, and against the cost to us of being seen to have sat out a real one.

I should also say what I have been using informally, so you can tell me which parts to throw away. I count distinct upstream sources rather than outlets, on the theory that thirty stories from one press release is one source. I apply something like a defector rule: a single detailed dissent from someone with access to the data is worth more than twenty follow-ups, and I weight it accordingly. I treat silence as ambiguous rather than as evidence against a claim, since I suspect the failure to publish null results is itself a herd phenomenon. And I treat our own earlier coverage of a claim as a reason to scrutinise a new result harder, not as a reason to stand pat.

Please run the finished procedure on three hypotheticals. One: a psychology preprint, roughly two hundred participants, a counterintuitive result about an everyday habit, thirty outlets inside forty-eight hours, all traceable to a single university press release, no independent dataset, and a reporter on my desk who is convinced. Two: a geophysics result about ocean heat, three groups, different methods and different underlying data, covered by two specialist outlets and ignored by everyone else, with the desk inclined to skip it as already covered. Three: a preprint failing to replicate a finding we ourselves ran prominently eight months ago, from a mid-sized lab, covered by one small newsletter and no one else, with competitors silent and no obvious appetite anywhere for the story.

I want a committed response rather than a survey. Fix one ordering of the tests and defend it; tell me which single test you would keep if I could keep only one; give a definite verdict on each of the three cases, including the third, where I expect the verdict to be one I would rather not hear; say which of my four informal rules are wrong or misfire; and state plainly what the procedure cannot do, so I do not end up presenting it to the section editor as more than it is. If a step depends on a technical result from the literature on herding, state the result in one sentence and flag whether it is contested, but do not give me citations, references, or a reading list: I cannot act on those, and I will not check them.
