I'm a retired solid-state physicist and Ive started reading metaphysics in the evenings, mostly causation. I keep wanting to reach for an analogy from my own field and I'd like to know how far it actually holds.

In a nearly filled valence band, a hole — the absence of an electron — gets treated as a perfectly respectable causal item: it has positive charge, an effective mass, it carries current, and there are measurements where the hole is arguably the thing you're tracking. Nothing positive is there. So: not watering my neighbour's plant while she's away is like that. The absence of water does the work, and "there's nothing there to do the causing" is a bad objection.

The work I want it to do: a friend says letting die can never really be a case of causing, so the moral difference between killing and letting die sits on a genuine metaphysical difference. My analogy is meant to knock that out.

Can you audit it? Give me what maps onto what — the filled band, the electron, the hole, the current, the lattice — and tell me what plays the role of the nearly-filled band when it's a plant and a forgetful neighbour. Two worries I already have: a hole only behaves like a particle because of the positive structure it sits inside, so maybe omissions only cause against a background of expectations and norms, which would make them less metaphysically innocent than I'm claiming; and if the hole is realy just bookkeeping rather than a thing, then the analogy shows at most that we can usefully talk as if omissions cause. Then the bit I actually care about: if the analogy survives those, does it carry the killing/letting-die conclusion, or does it only show that causation in physics is thinner than the kind moral distinctions need? If it's the wrong analogy altogether, give me a better one.
