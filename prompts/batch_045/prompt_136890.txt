Hi — I could use a hand with something that's half a practical problem and half a conceptual one. I'm a philosophy professor (I teach a bit of formal epistemology and decision theory, though this isn't really my area) and I'm on a hiring committee with one colleague, Dana.

The setup: we're deciding whether to make an offer. Both of us read the same file — CV, writing sample, three letters, teaching evals. Then we split the reference calls. I spoke to two of her letter writers; Dana spoke to the third plus a senior person in the subfield who isn't a letter writer but knows her work. After that we wrote our numbers down seperately: I put the chance she'd get tenure here at about 75%, Dana put it at 40%. Worth saying that before either of us opened the file, we'd have given roughly the same number for a candidate with this kind of dossier — same department, same placement record over the last ten years, no reason to think we differ much on the base rates.

Then we did that thing where you show each other your numbers and revise. I went to 68, she went to 51. Next round: 63 and 57. Next: 61 and 60. Dana now says we should just split the difference at around 60 and send that to the dean, who wants one number from the committee by Friday.

My discomfort is this: neither of us changed our mind because of anything new in the last three rounds. Each time we were just reacting to the fact that the other person had a different number. And I have a nagging feeling that "meeting in the middle" is the wrong way to combine our views — or at least that it's a procedure with no principled defense — but I can't say why, and Dana says I only dislike it because it lands closer to her number than to mine. She may be right about the motive, but that doesn't settle whether it's correct.

Three things I'm genuinely unsure about:

1. Some of my colleagues over in economics have a result that goes roughly: two people who started out agreeing about the base rates, who are honest with each other and reasonably good at reasoning from evidence, shouldn't be able to look at each other's estimates and stay apart. If something like that is right, does it mean one of us was being irrational at the outset — that 75 and 40 couldn't both have been sensible readings of what we'd each seen? Or does it mean our case just doesn't fit the assumptions, and our opening gap was fine?

2. The bit I keep coming back to is that I'm not sure our evidence was cleanly separable into "yours" and "mine." Dana and I sat in on the same job talk, and I suspect she'd have come away more impressed by that talk if the candidate were strong than I would have, while I'd have come away less impressed than she would if the candidate were weak. So we weren't only pooling private signals; we were also reading shared material through different lenses. Does that make a difference to whether we should have ended up agreeing?

3. And then the practical question: is splitting the difference actually the wrong move, and if so what would be the defensible alternative? Dana did fewer of the reference calls but she's the one who really knows the subfield; I did more of the legwork but I might just be noisier. Is there any principled reason to weight one of us more heavily, or to just defer to whoever has more information — or is averaging defensible after all, for some reason I'm not seeing?

Anyway — thoughts welcome. I have to send the dean something by Friday and I'd rather it not be a number I can't justify.
