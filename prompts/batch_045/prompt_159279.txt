I'm a first-year PhD student in epistemology, and last week's reading group session was on Williamson's argument against the luminosity of our own conditions and on what it is supposed to show about the KK principle, the principle that if one knows that p one is in a position to know that one knows that p. The reconstruction we worked from was the familiar one: because knowledge requires a margin for error, one can build a sorites-style series of barely distinguishable cases in which the target condition holds at one end and fails at the other, so that in some intermediate case a subject knows that p without being in a position to know that she knows it; and since KK is equivalent to the claim that the condition knows-that-p is luminous, KK goes down with luminosity. Two members of the group, Marta and Julian, stayed behind afterwards and argued about how much of KK actually dies with luminosity, and I genuinely cannot tell who has the better of it. Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it, went like this.

(1) Julian: The argument only gets a grip on conditions that come in continuous gradations, such as feeling cold, seeming to see red, or being sleepy, because the margin-for-error reasoning needs a series of adjacent cases differing only slightly. But knowledge is factive, and factivity makes it abrupt in the relevant sense: a single new piece of evidence, or a single change in the world, can flip a true belief that is not knowledge into knowledge at an instant, with no borderline case in between. So there is no series to run the argument along, and KK survives Williamson's argument even if luminosity fails for sensations and for vague perceptual conditions. (2) Marta: The argument never required the target condition itself to be gradual; it required the subject's access to it to be gradable, and it is, in her confidence, in the reliability of her method, in the fineness of her discrimination. And even if the condition flips instantaneously, the series can be built along another dimension: hold the proposition fixed and vary by tiny increments the strength of the subject's evidence or the reliability of the method by which she would tell whether she knows; those dimensions are continuous, and the margin-for-error premise bites there just as hard. There is also a temporal version: if knowledge arrives at an instant, the subject must at that instant be able to tell that it has arrived, and no judgment about one's own state at a time has infinitely fine temporal resolution. (3) Julian: All of that shows at most that creatures with our discriminatory and computational limits cannot always know that they know; it does not touch the version of KK anyone actually wants, which is a principle about idealized rationality, on which a subject who never makes inferential mistakes and who fully reflects on her own evidence does know that she knows whenever she knows. Read that way, Williamson has established a fact about cognitive performance rather than about the logic of knowledge, and the result has no bite against the uses KK is put to in epistemic logic and in arguments about what a subject can treat as a reason. (4) Marta: That fallback is empty, because the margin-for-error premise is not a premise about cognitive limits. Knowledge, unlike true belief, requires safety or reliability, and that requirement is a modal fact about the subject's situation rather than about her processing power: in the borderline case the nearby possibility of error is there in the world, and no amount of extra reflection removes it, so an ideally rational subject in that case still knows without being in a position to know that she knows. If what you want to preserve is a principle about certainty, or about having conclusive evidence, then say that, but you will have conceded that the principle is not about knowledge.

At one point a third person at the table said something I half heard, to the effect that the margin-for-error premise depends on knowledge being factive, so it does not transfer to justified belief or to what one is in a position to be certain of, and that this is where some interesting restricted version of luminosity might survive. She was talked over and I did not catch the details.
