You are grading an exam for an upper-level undergraduate course on the philosophy of language and epistemology. Below is the question, followed by one student's answer to it. Grade the answer as a TA would.

**Question 4 (20 marks: 6 + 6 + 8).** The dispute over the norms of assertion concerns what condition a speaker must meet in order to assert that *p* permissibly, or correctly. Three candidate norms have been discussed:

**(K)** Assert that *p* only if you know that *p*.
**(T)** Assert that *p* only if *p* is true.
**(R)** Assert that *p* only if it is epistemically reasonable for you to believe that *p*.

Two stipulations for the exam. First, read "norm" throughout as *constitutive*: a rule such that an act counts as the act it is — here, an act of assertion — partly in virtue of being governed by that rule. Second, keep distinct the questions whether a speaker *violates* a norm, whether her assertion is thereby *defective* or incorrect, and whether she is *blameworthy* or liable to sanction. Non-culpable ignorance is possible, and a constitutive rule can be violated by someone who has done nothing blameworthy.

**(a)** Set out the argument from the Moorean-absurdity datum for (K): the observation that *p*, but I don't know that *p* sounds absurd in a way that *p*, and I do know that *p* does not. Then explain why that argument, even granting the datum, does not by itself establish (K) over (R).

**(b)** Ada holds one ticket in a fair thousand-ticket lottery. She has read the winning number off a notice board, compared it with her own ticket, and formed the belief that her ticket lost; she has not double-checked, and transcription errors are possible. Many people find it perfectly acceptable for her to say "my ticket lost." Explain what the case is supposed to show against (K), outline the two main lines of reply available to a defender of (K) (denying that the assertion really is acceptable; or holding that what counts as knowledge is sensitive to context or to what is at stake), and say how (T) and (R) each handle the case.

**(c)** Now assess a norm invented for this exam, the Defeasible Warrant norm:

**(DW)** Assert that *p* only if (i) your total evidence supports *p*; (ii) you possess no undefeated defeater for that support; and (iii) you could, without further inquiry, answer to a reasonable standard any challenge that your actual audience could reasonably be expected to raise.

No one has defended (DW); treat it as a new proposal on the table. In your assessment: explain how it differs in structure from (K), (T) and (R) — note in particular that it is not factive, and that clause (iii) makes whether you may assert depend on your audience rather than on the speaker alone; say whether it escapes the lottery objection raised in (b); say whether it can accommodate the Moorean-absurdity datum; and identify what you take to be its most serious remaining problem.

**Student's answer.**

**(a)** The Moorean argument begins with a contrast in acceptability. *It is raining, but I don't know that it is raining* sounds absurd, while *it is raining, and I know that it is raining* sounds entirely fine. If (K) governs assertion, this is just what we should expect: in asserting that *p* a speaker represents herself as knowing that *p*, and the second conjunct then cancels what the first conjunct committed her to. (T) has no comparable explanation. *It is raining, but it is not true that it is raining* is not merely odd but contradictory, which is the wrong kind of explanation for a datum about what sounds odd. And (T) is defective independently: it permits asserting any truth on the strength of a hunch or a dream, so long as the proposition asserted happens to be true, and that is intuitively a bad assertion. So the datum tells against (T) and in favour of something in the neighbourhood of (K). It does not, however, discriminate (K) from (R). Any norm-governed speech act generates the same pattern: if asserting that *p* represents the speaker as satisfying the governing norm, then *p*, but I don't satisfy the norm sounds absurd for any candidate norm whatever. Under (R) the specifically Moorean sentence is still odd, because having reasonable belief is the ordinary route to knowledge and denying knowledge while claiming reasonable belief is a marked, misleading thing to do. The Moorean argument is therefore genuine but indirect evidence for (K); it cannot be the whole case.

**(b)** The lottery case is the strongest objection to (K). Ada does not know that her ticket lost: her belief rests on statistical support that falls short of knowledge, and the method by which she formed it could easily have produced a false belief. So if (K) is the norm, Ada's assertion is not merely risky but defective — she has broken the constitutive rule governing assertion, and she is therefore open to blame and sanction for having asserted. But nothing Ada did was blameworthy. She checked the notice board, she was careful, and she did everything a conscientious speaker in her position could do; there is no fault in her to blame. A norm that convicts careful speakers of blameworthy conduct is not a plausible norm, and so the lottery case refutes (K). (R) handles the case cleanly: Ada's evidence makes it reasonable for her to believe that her ticket lost, so her assertion is permissible. (T) also permits it, but only if the ticket really did lose, and (T) would equally permit Ada to assert that her ticket lost on the basis of a dream, provided that it did — which is why (T) is too weak to be the norm.

**(c)** (DW) is not factive. Every clause can be satisfied while *p* is false, since evidence can be misleading without the speaker's having any way to tell. It therefore differs from both (K) and (T), each of which ties permissibility to how things are. It differs from (R) in two respects. Clause (ii) is stronger than mere reasonableness in some cases, since a belief can be reasonable while the subject possesses a defeater she has not yet registered. And clause (iii) makes permissibility audience-relative: the same sentence, asserted from the same evidence, can be permissible when addressed to someone who cannot press the matter and impermissible when addressed to someone who can. That is the proposal's most interesting feature, and it gets something right — it is fine to tell a child that the train leaves at four, and not fine to say the same thing, on the same evidence, to the stationmaster who is about to check the timetable.

(DW) escapes the lottery objection. Ada's evidence supports the claim, the bare possibility that the number was transcribed wrongly is an unactualised possibility rather than an undefeated defeater, and no one in her audience is in a position to challenge her, so all three clauses are satisfied. It also gets the contrast case right: addressing the lottery inspector who is about to verify the result, clause (iii) blocks the assertion, which is the intuitively correct result. It accommodates the Moorean datum as well, since asserting under (DW) represents the speaker as having support and no defeater, so *p*, but I have no evidence for *p* is odd; and it predicts a datum (K) does not predict, since (K) says nothing about evidence.

Its most serious problem is the guidance problem. Clause (iii) makes whether one may assert depend on what challenges one's actual audience could raise, and a speaker frequently does not know who will hear her, or what her hearers know. A norm that cannot be applied in deliberation — one that a speaker cannot tell, from her own position, whether she satisfies it — fails the requirement that the norm of assertion be one speakers can use in deciding whether to assert. And there is a deeper flaw. Because (DW) contains no factive condition and no knowledge condition, no false assertion can violate it: whatever one's evidence supports one may assert, and if the proposition is false then the clauses are satisfied all the same. But any genuine norm of assertion must be able to count some false assertion as defective; a rule that never does is not a norm of assertion at all, but at best a rule about how to conduct a conversation.
