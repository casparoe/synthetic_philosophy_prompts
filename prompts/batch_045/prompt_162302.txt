So anyway, bit of a favour: I'm putting the final together for my final-year seminar on conditionals and probability, and I want one hard question on Lewis-style triviality that isn't just "reproduce the proof". Half of them will ahve the derivation memorised and I want the question to seperate people who actually understand where the argument's weight sits from people who've learned the labels.

Background you can assume they've got: the Equation (for a language closed under the boolean ops and under a binary conditional connective →: P(X→Y) = P(Y|X) for all X, Y with P(X) > 0), Bayesian conditionalization, Stalnaker/Lewis selection-function semantics, Adams's Thesis and the Ramsey test, basic probability.

The version of the triviality proof they saw (six steps): take A, C with all four of P(A∧C), P(A∧¬C), P(¬A∧C), P(¬A∧¬C) positive. (1) P(A→C) = P(C|A), by the Equation understood the usual way — as a constraint on any probability function, hence on conditionalizations like P_C and P_¬C, since those are probability functions too. (2) Total probability: P(A→C) = P(A→C|C)·P(C) + P(A→C|¬C)·P(¬C). (3) P(A→C|C) = P_C(A→C) = P_C(C|A) = P(C|A∧C) = 1, where the middle identity is the Equation applied to P_C. (4) Symmetrically P(A→C|¬C) = P_¬C(C|A) = P(C|A∧¬C) = 0. (5) So P(A→C) = P(C). (6) So P(C|A) = P(C) for any A, C that are logically independent in the relevant way — every contingent proposition independent of every proposition it isn't logically tied to, which no real credence function can be like.

Here's what I want the question built around. A student last year floated an escape — call it the Anchored Thesis. She says: the Equation is a norm about one agent's actual credence function, and it says nothing at all about the credence functions she would have after learning something. P_C and P_¬C aren't her actual credence function, so steps 3 and 4 are illegitimate. She wants to keep conditionalization, and she wants to keep the Ramsey/Adams role — that credence in A→C is what should guide her credence in C if she learns A.

So the question has to make students do four things, roughly in this order of difficulty:

- Say precisely which premise of the six-step proof anchoring denies, and whether the proof is thereby blocked (it is, for that version).
- See that it's blocked only on the most radical version of the view: if the Equation is a standing norm — the sort of thing that constrains your actual credences whenever you have them — then conditionalization brings triviality straight back diachronically, because you can ask what your later self's credence in A→C has to be on each cell of the partition, then apply total probability. I don't want them to be able to wiggle out with "but I'm certain I'll never learn C".
- And the bit I actually care about: whether triviality can be got back from the actual, un-conditionalized P alone, if the language is also closed under conditionals in consequent position and the connective satisfies Exportation (X→(Y→Z) equivalent to (X∧Y)→Z), the principle we did in the week on selection-function semantics. I'm fairly confident it can — apply the Equation to the pairs (C, A→C) and (¬C, A→C) and let Exportation turn each into something whose conditional probability is trivially 1 or 0 — but I want the question to make finding that the thing that marks out a top answer, not hand it to them.
- Finally, say what's left of the position if the anchoring proponent also gives up Exportation or gives up embedding conditionals in consequent position: what view has she ended up holding, and what does it actually cost her. Don't let them get away with "it costs her the Ramsey test", because it doesn't — be specific about what is and isn't threatened, e.g. whether she can still claim to be giving a semantics for "if", whether the Equation can constrain anything about an embedded conditional, and whether a norm that holds for your credences now while you cheerfully predict your own future self will violate it is a norm rather than a coincidence of your current situation.
