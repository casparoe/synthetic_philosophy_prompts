I have been chewing on an argument about the thermodynamic arrow of time that I think proves far too much, and I cannot locate the step I object to.

(1) Every piece of evidence we have about the past is a present record: a memory, a photograph, an ice core, a laboratory notebook, the redshift of a distant galaxy.
(2) A present physical state counts as a record of a past event only if it is reliably correlated with that event — only if the correlation is not a coincidence and not a chance fluctuation.
(3) Statistical mechanics explains why present records are reliably correlated with past events: given that the universe began in an extremely low-entropy macrocondition (the past hypothesis), with a uniform probability distribution over the microstates realizing it, the dynamics make accurate, mutually agreeing records overwhelmingly probable.
(4) So every consideration we could cite in favor of the past hypothesis is a consideration whose status as evidence is explained by, and only by, the past hypothesis: without it, the overwhelmingly most probable history compatible with our present state is one in which our records are random fluctuations and the past had higher entropy than the present.
(5) Evidence that has the status of evidence only given H cannot supply independent nondemonstrative support for H.
(6) Therefore we have no reason to accept the past hypothesis, and so no reason to think entropy was lower in the past — no reason to think there is a thermodynamic arrow of time at all.
(7) And the argument generalizes: the reliability of a thermometer is explained by thermodynamics, the reliability of a telescope by optics, and so on; by (5), no instrument can support a theory that underwrites its own reliability.

I cannot accept (6), and (7) looks to me like a reductio of (5) rather than a discovery about science. I would rather keep (1)–(4) and give up (5) if I can. But whenever I try to say what is wrong with (4) or (5), I find myself simply insisting that records are trustworthy, which is the very thing in dispute. A further complication: someone in my seminar says (3) is too quick, because the same framework seems to make fluctuation observers dominate in a sufficiently large universe, which would undermine (3) as well — I do not know whether that is a problem for (3) or a separate one.

Which step fails, and why did it look sound? Then price the alternatives: what does each of (3), (4), and (5) cost me if I give it up instead, and in particular does giving up (5) commit me to having to take seriously theories that explain away their own evidence — Boltzmann-brain-style scenarios, or worse?
