I chair the Collections Stewardship Committee of a university museum, and I have to circulate a written opinion to the board before our meeting in two weeks, so I would be grateful for a draft I can work from.

The case is this. Since the 1970s we have held nineteen fragments of a marble votive figure — the "Thalassa Figure," Late Bronze Age, from the island of Thalassa in what is now the Republic of Meridia — that was broken in antiquity into thirty-one pieces. Seven further fragments are at the Nordhavn Museum of Antiquities, and five are with a private collector in Geneva. In 1994 all thirty-one were lent to us and physically reassembled for an exhibition; they join along original break surfaces, except that two junctions were bridged in plaster by a 1961 restoration, and one piece, the left forearm, was carved in 1958 by the same restorer to complete the display and is now bonded to the shoulder fragment. Since the exhibition the pieces have gone back to their three separate locations, and our gallery shows instead a full-size 3D-printed reassembly labeled "The Thalassa Figure." In 2023 Meridia's Ministry of Culture filed a claim under the Meridian Cultural Property Restitution Act 1979, which provides for the return of objects of outstanding cultural significance but never defines what an object is; the Ministry claims our accession 1994.112 (the nineteen fragments) and asks us to certify that the claim extends to all constituent parts of the Figure, wherever held. The procedural hook is that our own 2016 curatorial review rated each fragment, taken singly, as below the significance threshold, while saying that the fragments together would constitute the most important surviving example of Meridian monumental sculpture. So whether we are dealing with one object, or with thirty-one, or with thirty plus a modern addition, decides whether the claim succeeds. Our 1998 Stewardship Manual does not settle the principle: it says that an accession unit corresponds to what curatorial practice counts as a single object, and that fragments may share an accession number only where they were joined by continuous original material at the time of acquisition. Our registrar reads that as requiring contact of original stone; the Ministry's representative says the Figure is one object no matter where its pieces happen to be sitting; our counsel has told the board that a reviewing body will ask only whether our reading is reasonable and consistently applied, which means the reasoning in our opinion is what will actually be scrutinized.

A friend who teaches metaphysics tells me this is a version of what they call the special composition question — the question of when some things add up to a further single thing — and that philosophers divide among views on which any things whatsoever compose something, views on which composition happens only in special cases such as living organisms, views on which nothing is ever made of parts, and views on which there is no fact of the matter at all and we are simply choosing a counting scheme. I have read enough to know the menu; what I cannot do is convert it into a decision, and one trustee has already said in open session that because there is no fact of the matter here we should just pick whichever convention favors returning less and stop pretending the answer was discovered.

So what I need is a committee opinion, in American English, of roughly 800 to 900 words: open with a one-paragraph statement of your recommendation, then give the reasoning, then — before the practical section — a paragraph that states as strongly as you honestly can the best case for the side you are rejecting, including the trustee's no-fact-of-the-matter line, which should be engaged rather than dismissed and then shown why it still does not settle what we should do. Then set out a concrete course of action with a time frame: what we register, what we certify to the Ministry about the Geneva fragments we cannot ourselves return, what we do about the plaster-bridged junctions and the 1958 forearm, what we say to the Nordhavn Museum, and what weight, if any, our own gallery labeling and the printed reassembly should carry. Please do not simply survey the metaphysical positions or restate the Manual's wording; put them to work in reaching a decision. Close with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" that states your recommendation in no more than about twelve words.
