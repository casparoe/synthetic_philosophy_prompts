I am a high school student working on an independent project about the species problem, and before I write it up I would like someone to check whether the position I have arrived at actually holds together. Here is the set of things I currently believe, stated as carefully as I can:

(1) Species are real. Whether a given group of organisms is a species is a matter of how the world is, not of what is convenient for us to say. We discover species; we do not invent them.

(2) What makes a group of organisms a single species is that its members interbreed with one another and are reproductively isolated from other such groups. I take this to be the correct account of what a species is, not merely a useful rule of thumb for sorting.

(3) Sameness of species is transitive. If A is the same species as B, and B is the same species as C, then A is the same species as C. My reason is that if it were not, we could not count species or use them as units in classification, in conservation arguments, or in comparisons of biodiversity.

(4) Sameness of species across time is genealogical. A population living two million years ago and a population living now are the same species if one is descended from the other by an unbroken chain of generations — even though no member of the earlier population could ever have interbred with any member of the later one.

(5) Asexual organisms genuinely form species. Most bacteria never exchange genes by reproduction at all, and I do not think talk of bacterial species is merely a façon de parler.

(6) There is no intrinsic essence. There is no gene, no stretch of DNA, no set of traits, that is shared by all and only the members of a given species.

(7) In some cases there is no fact of the matter how many species there are. In the classic case of salamander populations spread around a large valley in California, each population interbreeds with its neighbours all the way around the ring, but the two populations at the point where the ring closes do not interbreed with each other. Likewise for a lineage that changes gradually over a very long time. Here, I think, where we draw the line is up to us.

(8) I do not find pluralism attractive. There is one correct account of what a species is, not several equally legitimate ones that we pick between depending on what we are studying.

(9) A case I invented to test myself: an unbroken chain of generations, in which every generation interbreeds with the one before it and the one after it, but which has gone on long enough that the last generation could not interbreed with the first. My verdict is that all of the organisms in the chain are the same species throughout, and also that the first and last generations are not the same species as each other. Both halves of that verdict feel right to me, and I cannot see how to reconcile them.

What I would like is an audit rather than a summary of the debate. Please go through this set and tell me which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, which only look as though they are, and — for the real tensions — rank them by severity, from the one that forces the most costly revision to the one I can patch most cheaply. For each real tension, say precisely what I would have to give up and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set; in particular, if a commitment has to go, tell me what work it was doing for the others. Please also flag any assumption I am relying on without having stated it. I have no access to a university library over the summer, so please argue from the position as I have stated it rather than pointing me to things to read.
