I'm on a small team that has to sign off on model deployments, and we keep going in circles about when we're allowed to reason about the average case and when we owe ourselves something closer to a worst-case argument. Nobody can say what flips the switch, so I've started collecting my gut verdicts on concrete setups, and I want to see what principle, if any, they add up to. Going by feel rather than by theory: (1) a chest X-ray triage model with a radiologist reviewing every output — average-case is fine, I'd ship on average numbers alone. (2) A coding agent with write access to the repo, no human reading every diff — average-case pass rates do nothing for me even at 99%, I want something worst-case-ish. (3) A model with root on a production box whose entire permitted action is running one fixed script with one fixed argument — average-case is fine, I sleep fine. (4) A plain chatbot, no tools, text in and text out, millions of users — I want worst-case-ish reasoning about manipulation and sycophancy, even though nothing it does in a single message is catastrophic. (5) Human sysadmins with root — average-case is fine: references, spot checks, done. My colleague Priya says that one is just special pleading for humans and I half agree with her. (6) A tutoring model we roll out to a hundred thousand instances at once — average numbers feel less reassuring than they would for a single instance, though I can't point to what changed about the per-instance risk. (7) A dumb heuristic spam filter that spammers are actively attacking — worst-case-ish, even though the stakes are about a dollar. (8) A model retrained weekly on user feedback, so the deployment distribution drifts along with the model — average-case numbers from last quarter feel like they don't even apply. Note that 3 has higher stakes than 2 and I'm calmer about it, and 4 has no tools at all and worries me more than 3, so whatever I'm tracking it isn't raw severity; and 5 versus 2 looks like the same setup with a more capable agent and the opposite verdict.

My own stab, which I don't fully trust: worst-case reasoning is owed when some process is actively searching for the failure modes, or when failures across copies are correlated so you don't get independent draws, and average-case plus monitoring is fine otherwise. I've been calling it the search-and-copy test, but it feels like two separate things stapled together, and it doesn't obviously handle 8, where the problem is that the average isn't stationary in the first place, or 6, where nothing is searching and the correlation is total and yet I only get mildly more worried rather than maximally.

So: is there one principle that covers all eight, or a small set with a clear division of labour, and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? State it precisely enough that I could paste it into a deployment checklist, and say which case motivates each clause. Then give me what it predicts for three setups I haven't made my mind up about: (a) an agent that writes and runs its own tests inside a sandbox, with a human approving merges; (b) an open-weight release, where anyone at all can fine-tune it however they like; (c) a frozen model, no feedback loop, five internal users, one narrow task. And what does it say about the one I keep flip-flopping on — whether the evals we use to make the ship decision themselves need a worst-case story, or whether it's fine that our measurement is only good on average? Last thing: be honest about whether your principle is really just expected-value reasoning with a fat tail plus uncertainty about which distribution is right, wearing a scarier costume. If it is, tell me what observation would actually distinguish the two, because I'd rather write the checklist in whichever language is doing the real work.
