I'm writing a five-thousand-word undergraduate term paper on the ethics of markets and I've reached an awkward point: I have confident, quite specific verdicts on individual cases, and I cannot find any formulation of the "commodification" or "repugnant markets" complaint that reproduces them without also generating verdicts I don't believe. So before I draft anything I want the pattern set out in front of me. I don't want a literature review and I don't want references — I'll supply those myself — I want the principle, and I want to know where my own verdicts have to give. Two half-theories I keep circling, which you should feel free to demolish. The first is a friend's, call it the unboughtness test: a market is objectionable when the value of the thing to the buyer is partly constituted by its not having been produced for money, so that buying it destroys what was bought. The second is my own, call it the desperation idea: what actually troubles me in the worst cases is that the seller trades because their alternatives are dreadful, so the transaction is a transfer from the badly situated to the well situated, and the right response is to improve the seller's options rather than to prohibit the trade. I can see the trouble with both. The first seems to condemn a lot that I approve of; the second seems to condemn dangerous, badly paid work that nobody, me included, wants to ban.

Here are my verdicts, gut only, on the cases I've been collecting. (1) A commercial broker matching wealthy recipients with poor living kidney donors at a negotiated price: wrong, and it should remain illegal. (2) Reimbursing a living donor's travel, childcare and lost wages, plus a fixed honorarium: fine, and I feel no furtiveness about it. (3) A single state purchaser offering one fixed, non-negotiable sum to anyone who donates a kidney after death, paid to their estate, with lifetime aftercare for living donors: I'm uneasy, but I lean towards acceptable. (4) Giving registered donors priority on the transplant waiting list if they later need an organ themselves: fine, positively attractive. (5) Paying people for plasma or for eggs: fine. (6) Paying healthy volunteers several thousand pounds to be deliberately infected with a malaria strain in a challenge trial: fine, even though it is paid bodily risk with no therapeutic benefit to them, and even though some of them do it because they need the money. (7) Charging forty pounds for a case of bottled water after a flood: wrong. (8) Surge pricing for taxis at one in the morning on New Year's Eve: fine. (9) Roses costing three times as much in the week before Valentine's Day: fine, not even a flicker. (10) A landlord putting up rents in a town where a flood has destroyed a third of the housing: wrong, and worse than the water. (11) Paying for fast-track airport security, a faster passport, a faster visa appointment: fine. (12) Paying to jump the queue for an NHS operation: not fine. (13) Paying for the same operation done privately, outside the NHS altogether: fine. (14) Paying a stranger to queue overnight for concert tickets: fine. (15) Paying someone twenty pounds to vote for a particular party: obviously wrong. (16) Paying people a flat ten pounds simply for turning out to vote, or entering them into a prize draw for doing so: fine, or fine enough. (17) Two MPs trading support, your bridge for my hospital: much less bad than (15), and I can't say why, since both are trades in political power. (18) Commercial surrogacy, where the payment is framed as compensation for time and physical burden but is contingent on handing over the baby and is large relative to her income: I go back and forth on this one every time I think about it. (19) Paying a hospital to put your name on a ward: fine. (20) Paying a child to write you a letter saying she missed you: not fine. (21) Paying an addict a few hundred pounds to be sterilised: not fine.

What I'd like from you: try to state one principle — or, if no single one will do, two or three candidates plus the cases that separate them — capturing as many of those verdicts as possible, and be explicit about which clause is doing the work on which case. Test the unboughtness test and the desperation idea against my list specifically rather than against examples of your own, and either repair them or say what should replace them. Then tell me what the account you favour implies about four cases I haven't settled: a state monopsony that pays living kidney donors a fixed price with no negotiation; a modest payment towards the funeral costs of someone who donated after death; a fertility clinic that pays egg donors differently depending on their exam results, so that the price varies with the donor's characteristics; and paying another parent to swap places with you on a school admissions waiting list. Finally, and this is the part I'll actually be defending, be plain with me about which of my verdicts no tidy principle can accommodate, which you would overturn, which you would keep at the cost of a disjunctive or two-part theory, and whether the fact that my verdicts resist a single principle is itself evidence that "commodification" was never one thing to begin with.
