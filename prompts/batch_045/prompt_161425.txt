I'm drafting a position paper on the politics of naming atrocities, and before I commit to it I want to know whether the six things I believe actually hold together. Here they are.

1. The wrong of genocide is distinct from, and worse than, the wrong of killing the same number of people: deliberately destroying a people is a graver wrong than killing the same headcount to win a war or to seize territory, because what is attacked is the group's continuing existence and not merely its members' lives.

2. I defend the Convention definition roughly as it stands: the closed list of protected groups is a defensible compromise rather than a mistake to be litigated away, the mental element should stay strict purpose to destroy the group as such, and I don't think Lemkin's own broader conception settles anything about what the law should say.

3. But I read the inference of that purpose generously. Perpetrators almost never leave a paper trail, so purpose should be read off the pattern of conduct — selection of victims by group membership, scale, the targeting of the group's institutions and its reproductive core — and I would infer purpose in a case where the pattern is the only direct evidence.

4. Non-lethal cultural destruction should count: language bans, child removal into an assimilating system, the erasure of archives and registries. A group can be destroyed with nobody killed, and I take the exclusion of this from the Convention to have been a concession to states with assimilationist policies rather than a principled line. My test case, which I invented so no one has written about it: over fifty years a state disperses a minority so that no district holds more than a small share of them, drops their language from schools and from civil registries, and fosters their children outside the community; nobody is killed or deported; the officials' stated aim, which I stipulate is sincere, is integration — they want the group to survive and prosper, just not to survive as the group it is — and two generations later it no longer reproduces itself as a group. I want to call this genocide.

5. The politics of naming is not incidental. When a government or an editor reaches for *tragedy*, *ethnic cleansing*, or *the events of 1915* where the criteria fit, that refusal is itself a wrong to victims and survivors, a continuation of the crime by other means, and I have argued that we should name even before any court has ruled.

6. I reject the ranking idiom and the treatment of the word as an honorific. I want genocide kept as a narrow descriptive-legal category, with the moral evaluation tracking the acts themselves — killing, forced transfer, child removal, imposed conditions — and nothing extra following morally from the label.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey: which pairs of these genuinely pull against each other, ordered by severity, and for each, what exactly would have to give and what that would cost me. Please also separate out any pair that only looks inconsistent and holds up on inspection, since I would rather know which of my worries are fake. Don't tell me which commitment to drop — trace what commits me to what, identify the cheapest repair, and I'll decide.
