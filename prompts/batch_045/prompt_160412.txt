Writing up a chapter on the anti-functionalist side of the hard problem and I built an argument against a friend that I now think blows up my own view, so help.

Set-up: Sophie's colour system is very slowly rotated through colour space over six weeks, while a compensater rewrites everything downstream so that her total functional organization — every discrimination, every verbal report, every introspective judgement she'd make if you asked her — stays exactly as at baseline. Call it the drift case. Here's my reconstruction of the Chase-and-Sanborn "quining qualia" line:

(1) Qualia, if there are any, are intrinsic: which quale an experience has is settled by the experience itself, not by its causes, its functional role, or its relations to anything else.
(2) So nothing in Sophie's functional organization at any point in the drift settles whether she is in the baseline quale or a rotated one — ex hypothesi she is functionally identical to baseline throughout.
(3) And nothing settles it for Sophie either: any report she gives, any introspective move she makes, is itself fixed by her functional organization, which hasn't changed.
(4) So nothing settles which quale she is in. No evidence, hers or anyone else's, discriminates the hypotheses.
(5) If nothing settles which quale she is in, there is no fact of the matter about what it's like to be Sophie.
(6) A property with no determinate instances isn't a property. So there are no qualia.

Here's why I dont trust it. Steps (2)–(6) never mention consciousness. Run the same form on which of two computationally identical neural realizers your cortex is implementing right now: you can't tell from the inside, your reports are fixed either way, so by (4)–(6) there'd be no fact of the matter about which circuit is firing — which is silly, someone with electrodes can tell. And the shape of it is basically Quinean indeterminacy, so if it works it seems to make belief content indeterminate too, which I really dont want.

But I also cant find the cut. (1) is the premise my chapter needs, and (3) looks airtight given how I set the case up, so the drift case seems to force eliminativism or functionalism on me, and I'm arguing against functionalism. I also want to keep that you and I might really be inverted relative to each other — a determinate difference nobody could detect from outside. Which step do you reject and what does rejecting it cost? Is there a version of intrinsicness that survives (6), or does surviving (6) cost me the interpersonal inversion case?
