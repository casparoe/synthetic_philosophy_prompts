I've been sitting on a view for a while and I want someone to try to break it. The view has two parts. (1) In actual groups the thing doing the work isn't common knowledge, it's mutual belief to some modest depth k — call it three to five — and k is set by the channel (does a public post get seen, can people signal that they saw it, does the platform echo acknowledgements back) rather than by anyone's rationality or priors. So the famous fragility results where one missing acknowledgement flips the outcome are artifacts of pretending the infinite tail matters, and the no-disagreement / no-trade results are claims about a limit real groups never approach. (2) Pushing toward actual common knowledge is often actively bad: the more a group succeeds at making everyone's beliefs public — polls, prediction markets, everyone posting their updates — the more it destroys the independence of private signals, and there's that result people cite where making a public signal more precise can lower welfare when people are also trying to coordinate. So the optimum is interior, "increase common knowledge" is the wrong target, and what you actually want is to tune k.

Concretely, imagine a platform feature I'll call a ratchet: a public thread plus a verified "N of M have read this" counter that refreshes once per round, where anyone can pay a small fixed cost to speed up the refresh. My claim is that this buys you essentially all the coordination you'd get out of full common knowledge, at a k that barely depends on group size, and that the tail past that k is doing nothing at all, so nobody should ever pay for it.

So: what would count against this? I want evidence and arguments that should actually move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to say which of them a committed holder of the view would concede counts against them and which they'd just bounce. Specifically: is "the tail doesn't matter" falsifiable, or have I insulated it — what observable difference is left between "k≈5 suffices" and "k≈5 suffices except when the stakes are huge or the downside is asymmetric"? What does the view predict that a full common-knowledge account wouldn't — about how coordination degrades as stakes scale, about when costly acknowledgements should genuinely be worth paying for, about whether you'd ever want to deliberately degrade a group's channel? Am I smuggling in an equilibrium-selection assumption that's quietly doing all the real work? And has the second half — the "too much common knowledge is bad" bit — been qualified into something that can't lose, and if so, is that a problem for a claim with that shape?
