I teach a mid-level seminar on moral responsibility, and a group of students active in the effective altruism movement — they run our campus giving pledge — have pressed me on a practice I can't reconstruct cleanly. The practice is earning to give: a student who wants to do the most good takes the highest-paying job available, frequently at a firm whose business she would independently describe as predatory, and gives away half her salary to a charity distributing insecticide-treated bed nets against malaria. What interests me is that the defense of the practice and the scruples surrounding it seem to pull in opposite directions. The defense is the move I'll call slot-replaceability: if she turns the job down, the firm hires the next candidate; the lending, the drilling, or the ad targeting goes on regardless; and the only party who ends up worse off is the person who doesn't get the net. Her occupancy of the role makes no difference to the harm, so the harm cannot be charged to her, and the donation is pure gain. But the same students are fastidious about something like complicity: they will not take consulting fees from a fossil-fuel company even when the fee would buy the very same nets, they treat a summer at a tobacco firm as discreditable, and they care that their own name and labor not be attached to the thing. Counterfactual difference-making here; non-causal association there. A colleague in metaphysics tells me the slot argument is just the standard preemption case wearing a costume, but I'm not convinced the students' case has the right structure for that.

So I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed, with the distinctions made explicit. Does the practice strictly presuppose a difference-making view of causal responsibility for harm, or could a view on which agency carries non-causal significance — complicity, integrity, what an action expresses or endorses — license both halves at once, and if so, how does it avoid collapsing into the consequentialism it was supposed to be an alternative to? Is slot-replaceability even sound, or does it trade on confusing the replaceability of a person with the replaceability of an output: if the harmful activity expands to absorb whichever labor is available, rather than being fixed per position, the marginal analysis changes, and I want to know how much of the practice survives once it does. Notice also that slot-replaceability, taken at face value, seems to entail that the colleague who takes the identical job and spends everything on himself does no wrong beyond failing to donate — should the students accept that, and does the practice actually require it? And there is the odd question of the money itself: the practice treats a dollar earned at a predatory firm as exactly as good, once transferred, as a dollar earned anywhere, which presupposes something about whether dollars carry their causal history with them or are morally laundered by the act of giving. Finally, the value theory: the students rank candidate charities in a single spreadsheet column, dollars per life saved, and everything that cannot be entered in that column — dignity, political empowerment, whether the recipients were consulted — silently counts as zero rather than as unknown. Is that a commitment to commensurability the practice really needs, or could a view on which some goods are incomparable rather than worthless generate the same funding pattern for quite different reasons?

I'm rewriting my lecture on overdetermination and would like to open with this as a case, so what I need is the architecture rather than a verdict: which of these commitments the practice strictly presupposes, which it merely fits comfortably with, where its parts presuppose inconsistent things, and what would have to change in the practice if each commitment were given up in turn. Please don't tell me whether earning to give is fine; tell me what has to be true for it to be fine, and where it is philosophically underdetermined.
