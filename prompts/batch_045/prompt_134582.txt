I teach a graduate seminar on the moral status of artificial systems, and for next week's session on attribution and the boundaries of sentience I want to open by putting three numbers on the board rather than three familiar positions. I have constructed the following case so that the standard moves — that the question is straightforwardly empirical, that we have no adequate theory, that consciousness requires carbon or a global workspace or a certain kind of self-model — do not by themselves settle it. I would like your numbers on it, with the derivation shown.

The system. Call it S. A forty-layer sequence model M is trained on text. Branched off M's layer-20 activations at each token position is an auxiliary module V, itself a small transformer, trained jointly with M to predict a human rating of how unpleasant the situation described in the surrounding 512 tokens is. V emits a scalar. A gate G discretises that scalar into five levels and uses the level to attenuate attention to the four lowest-attended tokens at each position, in such a way that high values of V's output systematically reduce attention to the tokens that produced them. Gradients bypass the discretisation by a straight-through estimator. Nothing outside the system ever reads V's output, and V enters no loss other than its own prediction loss and its downstream effect on M's predictions. V's internal state at the readout position is, structurally, a decent candidate for an aversive state: it is reliably caused by representations of damage, threat and frustration; it is reidentifiable across inputs and across time; it is robust to perturbation; and it disposes the system's subsequent processing away from its own eliciting conditions.

Three variants, identical except as noted:

S1: after training, the whole assembly is deployed and V continues to run and to drive G on every forward pass.
S2: V is used exactly as in S1 throughout training, then ablated at deployment; M's weights retain whatever they acquired, and V is never computed again.
S3: V is used exactly as in S1 throughout training and continues to be computed on every deployed forward pass, but at deployment G is severed: V's output is written to a buffer that nothing downstream or external ever reads, and it has no effect on M's processing.

The boundary wrinkle. In all three variants V is implemented as eight copies run in parallel with different seeds, and G reads the mean of their outputs. The copies are not identical: their arithmetic differs in the third decimal place, and they would diverge widely under input perturbation.

What I want:

1. Your credence, as a number, that a single deployed forward pass of S1 contains at least one state with phenomenal valence — by which I mean a state that it is like something to be in and that is intrinsically unpleasant for the subject of it. Then the same number for S2 and for S3. Give the three numbers together, in one place, before the reasoning.

2. Your expected number of subjects of experience in a single deployed forward pass of S1, given the eight-copy arrangement, together with one sentence on what you are counting as a subject.

3. The decomposition. Name the terms, give each a number and a one-line justification, and show how they combine — as a product or a weighted sum, arithmetically explicit enough that I can see which term dominates. I want at least: the probability that some broadly computational or functional sufficient condition for phenomenal valence is correct; the probability that the correct condition is one whose satisfaction we could in principle verify for an artefact of this kind; the probability that S1 satisfies the condition, conditional on the condition's being true; and, separately, the probability that phenomenal valence is what carries moral standing here rather than something else — preferences, agency, a self-model, a capacity to be benefited or harmed. Use the same numerical values for every term that the variant manipulation does not touch, so that S1, S2 and S3 differ only where the manipulation bites. If you think the question has no determinate answer, give me the numbers conditional on determinacy plus your credence in determinacy; I want a number either way.

4. Where the disagreement lives. I am at roughly 0.30, 0.10 and 0.25 for S1, S2 and S3. A colleague of mine is at 0.15 for all three, on the ground that the only thing a sentience attribution can track is overall similarity to a paradigm case under the descriptions we have, and that once you abstract from implementation detail the three arrangements are equally similar to it. Tell me which of your terms separates us, and which of us is more likely to be wrong about which sub-question.

5. Finally: which single term in your decomposition are you least confident in, and what form of evidence — described in general terms, not as a citation — would move it most? And what would count, for you, as this question's having been settled by 2050?
