I'm giving the commencement address at next month's graduation of the prison education program I've directed for eleven years, and I'd like you to write it.

The setting is a visiting room inside a maximum-security state prison. Forty graduates in prison blues; a hundred or so family members, many of them children; the warden; the commissioner of corrections; a dozen correctional officers, some there on duty and some because over the years they've come to respect the program; and two Norwegian corrections officials the commissioner has flown in. He wants to use the ceremony to announce that the department will convert one housing block into a Halden-style unit — real doors, real privacy, officers not carrying guns — and he would like my speech to showcase the department's rehabilitative turn. Roughly a third of the graduates are serving life without parole.

The difficulty I keep circling is one I've started calling the ratification problem. When a captive population's achievement is displayed by the authority that confines it, praise of the achievement is quietly converted into evidence that the confinement is humane, and the ceremony becomes a license the graduates themselves are made to sign. I need a way of honoring what they did that doesn't sign for them.

Concretely:

- Nothing false and nothing borrowed. Not that they are free now, not that they have been transformed, not that they have paid their debt, not that they are role models for anyone — for the third of the room who will die inside, each of those is either false or is really a claim about the institution rather than about them.
- No reentry rhetoric: no second chances, no talk of the day they get out, no recidivism statistics. I want an account of what the degree is for that stays true for someone who will never be released, and that therefore isn't rehabilitation in the department's sense, where rehabilitation just means a lower chance of coming back.
- The new block is genuinely better, and I don't want to hand the commissioner a reason to cancel it or the officers a reason to hear me calling them sadists — several have said privately that cells nicer than their own neighborhoods are an insult to everyone who never broke the law. But the speech can't function as the department's exhibit either.
- Mixed room: at least one line that lands for the officers, who will be the ones running the block, and one that lands for the graduates' children, and no academic vocabulary anywhere. The argument should nonetheless be philosophical: what punishment is owed — the sentence is the deprivation of liberty, not the deprivation of everything else that makes a life — and what a prison owes the people it holds.

Write a seven-to-eight-minute address that closes on something true rather than something rousing. Give me the speech itself first, with no preface and no commentary before it; after it, no more than 250 words on which lines were the concessions to the officers and to the families, and what each concession cost.
