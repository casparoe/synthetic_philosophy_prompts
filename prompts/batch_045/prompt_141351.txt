I am a final-year PhD student in philosophy working on the metaphysics of death, and I have been asked to run a teaching session for a hospital clinical ethics committee on how the determination of death works and where it comes apart. The clinicians on the committee are fluent in the two routes they actually use — neurological criteria and circulatory criteria — and they have all sat through summaries of the mid-century consensus statements. What I do not want is another such summary. What I want is a classification instrument that reveals those two routes as one contested corner of a much larger logical space, and that sorts people by what they are willing to say about cases rather than by which label they would apply to themselves.

Please build me a decision tree, and please build it from the logical space first and populate it with positions afterwards: begin with the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the familiar positions in that space, and tell me which combinations are unoccupied and why (incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored).

Requirements on the structure:

1. The entry point should be a commitment nearly every clinician already accepts — something like "a ventilated patient with total loss of clinical brain function, on adequate testing, is dead." From there, give me seven or eight branch nodes, each phrased as a single question that a working clinician or a first-year graduate student could answer from their own practice, without knowing any of the labels and without already knowing which leaf it leads to. No node may be a request to endorse a doctrine ("do you accept the higher-brain criterion?"); each must be a concrete verdict or a forced choice between concrete verdicts.

2. The nodes should do the real discriminating work. The distinctions I want separated include at least these: whether what dies is an organism or a person; whether death must be an event with a determinate time or may be a process; whether the relevant loss is irreversible (cannot be reversed) or merely permanent (will not be reversed, given a standing decision); whether what is lost is the exercise of a function or the capacity to recover it; whether the relevant part of the brain is the whole brain, the brainstem, or the cerebral hemispheres; whether a function sustained by a machine counts as the organism's own function; and — importantly, because the committee routinely conflates them — the difference between what death is, what criterion tracks it, and what test gives us adequate confidence that the criterion is met. If you think a distinction that matters is missing from this list, add it.

3. Leaves should carry descriptive names only (whole-brain, brainstem, higher-brain, circulatory-respiratory with its permanent and irreversible variants, the organism-as-integrated-whole view, the view on which the neurological criterion is a useful legal convention rather than a discovery about death, gradualism, and anything else that emerges). Do not attach author names and do not cite papers; I need labels the committee can use, not a reading list. Each leaf should also carry one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question you can honestly put on the tree, flag them as inseparable rather than inventing a node that merely restates one of them.

4. These cases must be used as the diagnostic material, and I would like you to say which node each one is doing its work at, and to tell me if any two of them turn out to be redundant: a ventilated patient meeting clinical brain-death criteria whose neuroendocrine function is partly preserved (no diabetes insipidus, thermoregulation and sodium balance maintained); a brain-dead pregnant patient maintained for weeks with a viable foetus; a patient after anoxic injury with extensive neocortical destruction who nevertheless breathes spontaneously, has sleep–wake cycles and brainstem reflexes; an infant born with hydranencephaly who breathes and reacts; a controlled donation after circulatory determination of death, where asystole follows the withdrawal of ventilation and CPR could in principle restore circulation during the customary hands-off interval but will not be given; a patient on veno-arterial ECMO with no cardiac output and no pulse whose cerebral circulation is well maintained; a patient in whom extracorporeal perfusion and cooling are begun within minutes of asystole, with cerebral structure preserved but no function; an isolated mammalian brain kept metabolically active by perfusion for some hours after the organism's death; and, as a control, a patient with a high cervical spinal cord injury who is permanently ventilator-dependent but fully conscious.

5. Finally, the instrument should note where four different practical purposes pull in different directions: organ procurement under the dead donor rule, withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, the legal moment of death for inheritance and criminal law, and what goes on the death certificate. I expect that some cases come out dead for one purpose and not for another, and I want to know which nodes shift when the purpose shifts.

Please write in British English, lay the tree out as indented nodes with a one-line rationale at each branch, and keep the whole thing to roughly 1,500 words.
