I've been reading about ethics on my own for a couple of years — no formal training — and I keep getting stuck on one objection to consequentialism: that we are, in practice, clueless about the downstream effects of what we do, so the theory can never actually tell us what to do. I want to write a short piece arguing that the objection is weaker than it looks, and for that I need one concrete case that I haven't been able to find anywhere.

What I want is a case where the cluelessness objection is at maximum strength and consequentialism still yields a definite answer about what to do — not a tie between the options, and not an answer smuggled in from outside consequentialism. Specifications:

1. The ignorance must not come from any of the usual suspects. No chaos or butterfly effects, no far-future or far-away consequences, no infinite or undefined expected values, no skeptical hypotheses about hidden sufferers or simulated worlds, no moral uncertainty (assume the theory of value is fixed — total welfare, say), and nobody in the story with special foresight. Keep it to one household over one weekend, ordinary stakes.

2. This is the part I care most about: the unknown effects must be genuinely act-dependent. It is no good having the same large unknown term show up in both options so that it cancels out of the comparison. That is the standard reply to this objection, and I want a case where that reply is unavailable.

3. The answer must not be reached by estimating the unknowns. Whatever reasoning delivers it has to work for someone whose evidence is silent about them, not merely someone who is bad at the arithmetic.

4. No cheap outs: the winning option must not simply be a smaller, safer or less ambitious version of the other; the two options' foreseeable near-term effects should be roughly comparable in value; and the verdict must survive repetition — if the agent faced this choice every weekend for a year, the answer should not collapse into "it doesn't matter, take either."

5. One paragraph, realistic, something a person could actually face.

After the scenario, please do this: show what each of three replies to the cluelessness objection would say about the case — (a) maximise expected value using your best guesses anyway; (b) set the unknown terms aside because they are common to the options, and compare only the differences you can foresee; (c) refuse to put probabilities on the unknowns and look for an option that is at least as good under every way of filling them in — and say which, if any, actually delivers a verdict here and why. Then name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, show what happens to the verdict when that feature is removed, and give the strongest attack available to someone who thinks the case only looks solvable. If you think my five constraints cannot be met together, say which one has to give and build the closest case that meets the other four.

Begin with the scenario itself — no preamble, no restating my question — and in everything that follows, put the bottom line of each part in its first sentence.
