So I write short stories for a dumb little Slack channel at work, and I want to do one about something my team actually ran into, except fictionalized enough that nobody gets annoyed. Last time I posted one, the stats guy on my team replied with a paragraph about how the numbers in it couldnt possibly be right, so Id rather the math hold up this time even if the prose is meh.

The wierd thing I want the story to be about: we have an internal forecasting tool where you type a question like "if the migration finishes before Fridays freeze, will p95 latency drop next week" and you get one number back. I got curious about whether that number is really the probability *of the if-then sentence*, and someone told me about a result by a philosopher named David Lewis (I think) saying it basically cant be: there's no ordinary proposition "if A then B" whose probability always matches the conditional probability P(B|A), and if you insist on it the whole thing collapses. I half understand it and I want the story to force me to understand it.

So: write me a story, 1000-1500 words, set at a company like mine, where a team hits exactly that wall. Heres the setup Im picturing, change it however you want if it serves the story:

- The tool shows a sentence and a number next to it. For "if the migration finishes before the freeze, p95 latency drops," it shows 80%. For "if the migration *doesnt* finish before the freeze, p95 latency drops," it shows 10%. For the plain "p95 latency drops next week," it shows 38%. (Those come from: 40% chance the migration lands in time; 80% chance latency drops if it does; 10% if it doesnt; 0.4 x 0.8 + 0.6 x 0.1 = 38%.)
- The number the tool shows is defined in the spec as P(consequent | antecedent), and the PM defends that: its the number you actually want for the decision, so of course it goes next to that sentence.
- The same system already lets users combine claims with and / or / not, and prices those combinations the normal way: a conjunction is never more likely than either conjunct, mutually exclusive things add up, negation takes you to 1 minus. That part has shipped and can't be quietly dropped.

What I want in the story: one scene where the contradiction actually surfaces, with the real numbers, worked through step by step enough that I can follow it and check it — not a character saying "this is like Lewis's result" and everyone nodding. My guess is the trouble comes from how the two if-then questions relate to the plain one, given that the migration either finishes or it doesnt, but if you think my numbers or my framing dont actually generate a contradiction, change them inside the story (keep the change visible) so that they do, and make the chain airtight.
