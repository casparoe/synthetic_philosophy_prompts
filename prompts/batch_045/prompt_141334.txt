I'm assembling a reader of original philosophical fiction for my upper-level seminar on consciousness, and I want to commission one story on the relation between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, and on how much of the phenomenon global workspace theory can plausibly be said to explain. I am not looking for a parable that illustrates a thesis from the outside. I want a story whose plot could not get moving at all unless the distinction were real, and in which the theory is load-bearing rather than decorative.

Let me fix the terms, so that we are using them the same way. Access consciousness is a matter of availability: a content is access-conscious when it is broadcast to a range of otherwise independent consumer systems and thereby made available for report, for reasoning, and for the rational control of action. Phenomenal consciousness is a matter of there being something it is like for the subject to be in the state. Global workspace theory, as I will use it here, identifies consciousness as such with the first of these: broadcast is the mechanism, it is all-or-none, and what is not broadcast is not conscious in any sense that does further work. The theory's familiar embarrassment is the asymmetry: it looks well suited to explain availability and badly suited to explain what it is like.

The world of the story. All of the following is stipulated by me for this assignment; none of it is in the actual literature, so do not go looking for it, and do not treat any of it as something a reader is expected to have heard of.

- Workspace broadcast can be measured cheaply at the bedside, and a hospital system has made the measurement legally operative. Call it the Harbor Protocol: below the broadcast threshold a patient does not count as conscious for the purposes of any procedure, so anesthesia may be withheld; above it, the patient's own reports become binding.
- Two dissociations have by then been documented, though nobody understands them. In the first, which the clinicians in your story call unlit access, patients retain full availability — normal ignition, normal report, normal guidance of action — while insisting, with what looks like genuine puzzlement and distress, that nothing looks or feels any way to them whatsoever. In the second, unwitnessed presence, patients whose workspace has been pharmacologically suppressed produce no report and no behavior at the time, but afterwards recover memories of vivid and sometimes agonizing experience from the suppressed interval.
- Rename any of the above if a better name occurs to you, but keep the structure of the stipulation.

What I want from the story.

- 1,200 to 1,500 words, third person limited, past tense, American English, with a title.
- One decision that somebody has to make and cannot defer: a procedure is scheduled for a patient whose index is below threshold, and the person deciding has to act. Give that person a stake that is not philosophical — a job, a patient, a grievance, an old debt to someone.
- Both dissociations dramatized in scenes rather than summarized by a narrator or explained by a character. No character may explain the access/phenomenal distinction in more than two sentences of dialogue. No seminar, no courtroom, no hearing, no deposition.
- The Harbor Protocol has to have a real rationale. It exists because trusting reports has gone badly before; somebody should defend it in a way that is not stupid, and the defense should cost the protagonist something.
- One passage in which the reader is given something the viewpoint character has no access to, and one in which the viewpoint character has something the reader is not given. I want the form of the story to do part of the work here.
- The story should close with the decision made and its correctness unsettled. Do not tell the reader whether anyone was phenomenally conscious. Do not resolve the matter with a late disclosure: no simulations, no recordings nobody remembered making, no reveal that the protagonist was the patient all along, and no use of the word qualia as though it settled something. No epigraphs, no footnotes, no afterword explaining what the story was about.
