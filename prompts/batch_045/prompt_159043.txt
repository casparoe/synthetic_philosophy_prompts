I'm in a science-policy seminar this term and we got stuck on the precautionary principle, so I tried to write the problem up as a clean argument afterwards and now I've argued myself into a corner. The case we were discussing was a proposal to release gene-drive mosquitos to suppress malaria, which still kills an enormous number of people every year, a lot of them small kids. The worry is that once you release them you can't really call them back, and no one can rule out some knock-on effect further up the food web. Here's the argument, which is mine rather than anyone's in particular:

(1) The core of the precautionary principle: when an activity carries a plausible risk of serious and irreversible harm, the fact that we are uncertain about how likely or how big that harm is should not count in favour of going ahead — we restrict, delay, or block instead.
(2) "Plausible" here has to be weak — weaker than "more likely than not" — because the whole selling point of the principle is that it applies precisely where the evidence is still thin. The strongest reading available is something like "not excluded by what we currently know."
(3) But for more or less any activity, some route to serious irreversible harm is not excluded by what we currently know: there is always some mechanism nobody has modelled yet. (My seminar has started calling this the saturation problem.)
(4) So the principle's trigger condition is satisfied by every option on the table, including doing nothing.
(5) And whatever you do in response — ban, permit, commission another study, ban the ban — is itself an activity with its own not-excluded catastrophic pathway. Banning the gene drive leaves the malaria going on; permitting it risks the ecosystem; waiting five years for more data risks both.
(6) So the principle directs you to restrict every option and also to restrict every restriction. That's either incoherent, or it amounts to "be maximally cautious in all directions at once," which you can't do when the options are exclusive.
(7) The usual escape hatch is asymmetry: the principle is only supposed to bite on proposed or novel activities, not on the status quo, so it never turns on itself like this. But "the status quo" is just a description of what we're already doing, and continuing to do it is as much an activity as starting something new is. Singling out novelty looks like status quo bias in a costume.
(8) So the precautionary principle either says nothing at all, or it says something indefensible.

I most want to keep (1) and (7) — (1) is basically the thing we were taught, and the point about the status quo not being morally special feels right to me. But (8) is obviously not something I believe, because I do think precautionary reasoning is doing real work in the gene-drive case. So something upstream has to go, and I cant tell what. My own guess is the slide in (3): that "not excluded by what we know" is far weaker than whatever "plausible" was doing in (1), so the argument quietly equivocates. But one person in my study group said the real trouble is (5), that it's a category mistake to run a principle about risky activities on the activity of regulating them, and someone else said the fix is to redefine precaution as preferring reversible over irreversible options, which I don't know survives (5) either.

Where exactly does this break, and what does each available repair cost me? If I drop or weaken (3), do I have any non-circular way left to say what counts as a plausible risk, without smuggling back in exactly the probability estimates the principle was supposed to let us do without? If the problem is really (5), what's the principled reason a principle aimed at risky activities doesn't apply to the risky activity of regulating them? And does the "prefer reversible options" version actually escape the regress, or does it just relocate it?
