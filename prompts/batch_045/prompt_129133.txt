I'm giving a toast at my younger brother Callum's wedding in three weeks, and I need help writing it. The occasion is ordinary enough — about a hundred and twenty people, a marquee, Callum's cover band playing later — but I've got two complications, one personal and one conceptual, and they pull in the same direction.

The personal one: six months ago I called off my own engagement, after nine years. I don't want the toast to be about that, and it mustn't be legible as being about that. But the reason I'm stuck is that I now know from the inside what it does to someone to be handed reasons to stay: the years, the lease, the plane tickets, the way everyone had already adjusted. Those were excellent reasons to go through with it and they had nothing to do with whether the thing was good. My ex-fiancé, Marcus, will be at the wedding; he still plays in the band with Callum. Nothing in the toast may read as addressed to him or as a retrospective.

The conceptual one: I've been chewing on the problem about reasons of the wrong kind — the familiar difficulty that you can have a reason to *have* an attitude, say because someone has offered you something for having it, without that being any reason at all why the attitude *fits* what it's about. Wedding toasts are almost entirely built out of that kind of consideration.

The load-bearing complications I've worked out for myself, which I want the toast to respect:

1. Wrong-tense reasons. A consideration can be a perfectly good reason to value a relationship that already exists — a shared history, a hard year survived, a rented flat — and still not be a reason for the commitment being made, because the commitment doesn't continue the relationship, it changes what the relationship is. A reason to go on being together can be the wrong-tense reason for promising to be. This is why I've also banned "always" and "never": they project backwards a stability that is the object of the promise rather than a record of it.

2. Borrowed-kind reasons. Some considerations are right-kind reasons for *someone else* to value the match but can't be taken up by the couple without turning love into something else. This one bites hard here: Callum met Amara six years ago when she was the night nurse on the ward where our mother was dying, and she came to the funeral. Our family's gratitude toward her is real and enormous, and it is exactly the sort of thing a toast reaches for, and it is exactly the wrong thing — it is our reason, not his, and spoken aloud it would set a debt down inside the marriage.

3. Uptake-safety. Whatever I say must be something the two of them could later say to each other in private without it curdling — nothing that turns a feature of their life into a scorecard item, nothing that becomes coarse once it is out loud.

So the positive instruction is this: build the toast out of noticing rather than assessing. Assessment is where wrong-kind, wrong-tense and borrowed-kind reasons get in. Noticing is uptake-safe.

Please do not use these words anywhere in the toast: lucky, soulmate, meant to be, perfect, deserve, should, ought, supposed to, everyone can see, always, never. Don't name any philosopher, theory, or problem. No explanatory asides to the room — the philosophers present (about a quarter of the guests, from Amara's first degree) should recognise the shape of the thing without being told, and everyone else should just hear a good toast. It should be warm, specific, about five minutes, 650 to 750 words. Leave me at most two clearly bracketed slots for memories only I could supply.

After the toast, give me a note of about 250 words: which sentence is doing the structural work, which conventional toast move you had to give up in order to hold the distinctions, and where you cheated. And if one of my three distinctions is actually confused rather than merely awkward, say so there rather than quietly working around it.
