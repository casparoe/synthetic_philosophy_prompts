I'm writing a novel and I've written myself into a corner on the arguing bits rather than the plot, so I'm hoping you can help.

It's set maybe forty years out, and a lot of it happens in and around a revision of the big diagnostic manual (fictional, but you know the kind). My main character is a psychiatrist on the revision committee, and I need her to have an actual view about which of the diagnoses in the book are real kinds and which are things the book itself manufactured — something she can say out loud in a committee scene without sounding like she's reciting a textbook.

I've been poking around the Hacking stuff on this: the looping idea, where you classify people, the classified people change in response, so the kind changes, so the classification changes, round and round; and the split between kinds that don't care whether anyone has sorted them out and kinds that only exist because we did. I get it in outline. I can't turn it into a rule.

So here's my actual gut, on the diagnoses I've made up for the book.

1. Adolescent Depersonalization Drift — dissociative episodes, derealization, "watching yourself from the ceiling." Essentially unheard of until about fifteen years before the book's present, then case counts climb almost in lockstep with a handful of viral videos in which kids describe exactly those symptoms in exactly that vocabulary. My gut: artifact, the label made it, vote no.

2. Operator's Residual Witness Reaction — intrusive visual flashbacks and hypervigilance in retired remote-aircraft crews. My gut: real. But this one also spread after a big public awareness campaign and a documentary, and I can't say why it isn't just case 1 with a different patient population. That bugs me more than any of the others.

3. Thibault's disease — a degenerative condition picked out by a protein marker in spinal fluid. My gut: obviously real, obviously not a looping kind, even though being diagnosed destroys these people's lives: insurance, work, marriage, they join a patient community and start narrating themselves completely differently. So "the classification changed everything" can't be my test, right?

4. A side-plot: she's diagnosing a Victorian diarist with a modern condition, mostly from the diary. The diarist is dead and can't loop. My gut: a legitimate description, but it feels like a cheaper kind of legitimacy, and I don't know why.

5. A cohort diagnosed in childhood who seize the label, use it as an identity, and end up behaving in ways the original description doesn't cover — the clinicians complain the kind has drifted out from under them. My gut: still legitimate, and here the looping feels like a feature.

6. The one I flip on weekly: a therapy app reads your messages and tells you you're suffering from cumulative relational depletion. You weren't miserable before. You are afterwards, badly, and you say being told was the best thing that ever happened to you.

My working rule was going to be: it's a real kind if the suffering was already there before the label arrived and the label just picks it out. But 6 breaks it (no prior suffering, and I still lean legit) and 1 arguably satisfies it (there was plenty of diffuse adolescent misery around first, and I still want to vote it down). So the rule is wrong and I dont know how to repair it.

What I want: a principle, or two rival ones if they come apart somewhere, tight enough to fit in a page of dialogue, that captures as many of my verdicts as it can. Say which clause exists to handle which case. Then tell me what it predicts about two bits of the draft I haven't decided on: (a) a system that hands people a diagnosis presymptomatically, before they've felt anything, on a risk score; (b) a diagnosis that exists mainly because an insurer needed a billing code, which does get people money and leaves them worse off in other ways. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts a decent principle would force me to drop — I'd rather cut a subplot than have her defend something dumb in chapter nine.

One last thing: whatever you give me has to do work in the scene, i.e. it should let her say why the committee should vote yes or no, not just whether a kind is real in some abstract sense. If the honest answer is that the epistemic question and the should-it-go-in-the-book question come apart, say so and give me both.
