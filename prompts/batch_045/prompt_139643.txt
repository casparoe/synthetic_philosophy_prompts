Here is what I want. I have been reading the old dispute about whether dreams are experiences: the side associated with Malcolm, which says that whatever a dream is, it is not something you undergo while asleep, and the reply associated with Dennett, which says that of course dreams are experiences and that the arguments to the contrary depend on a bad picture of what undergoing something has to involve. I am not interested, here, in who wins. I keep tripping over a structural feature of the argument that I cannot name, and that neither side, as far as I can tell, pauses to name either.

The feature is this. There is a question shaped exactly like an ordinary question of fact about what happened at a time — did you undergo this at that moment, or did the undergoing happen later than it seems, or did it happen at a different stage of processing, or did it never happen at all. The competing answers differ only in where in the causal sequence they locate the event of becoming conscious. And every source of evidence anyone can point to — your own memory the next morning, anything you say when woken, anything a scanner records, anything an observer outside your skull could see — comes out exactly the same on all of them. Both sides agree on that. Yet neither side concludes that the question is empty. Each insists there is a real difference between the hypotheses and that it matters, sometimes a great deal, because whether someone underwent something is often what makes an episode matter morally, or what separates a person from a machine.

Three cases, so the shape is clear.

One. I woke last week from a dream of walking down a long museum corridor looking for a numbered door. On waking I could report that the corridor was long, that I was searching for a particular door, that there was a smell of floor polish, and that the whole thing seemed to take several minutes. Now: was I undergoing that as it unfolded? Or was the narrative assembled during sleep and then, at the moment of waking, presented to me more or less all at once, with the felt duration being part of the presentation? Or was the narrative assembled and never presented to anyone, with my report produced by whatever machinery produces reports? I cannot tell you which. And it is not that I lack the evidence: anything I could say about how it seemed is itself part of what gets produced on all three stories.

Two. Nearly everyone I ask reports dreams that seemed already to be under way when they tuned in — a conversation joined mid-sentence, a journey that had apparently been going for hours. Here the question is not when the experience happened but whether there were stretches of the narrative that nothing corresponded to at all, and the same three-way structure reappears.

Three, from waking life, to show it is not really about sleep. Ask a friend whether, glancing at a cluttered desk, they consciously take in all the objects or only the one they are attending to. Whichever they say, there is a redescription of the same processing that puts the richness earlier and one that puts it later, and nothing they can say from the inside separates them.

What I think this is not. I tried tabooing the words experience and conscious and what it was like; the disagreement did not evaporate, so I do not think it is a merely verbal dispute. It is not ordinary underdetermination either: in the usual underdetermination case you can at least describe the evidence that would break the tie, whereas here I cannot construct the breaking evidence even in principle. It is not the general claim that there are no facts about consciousness, and it is not just scepticism about introspection. And I would be unsatisfied by the answer that the hypotheses differ only in where they put the Cartesian theater — true as far as it goes, it does not tell me whether the question is empty or substantive, or what the third hypothesis is doing in the list.

So: does this configuration have a name and a literature? If there is one, give it and locate it. If there is not, coin a term and define it. Either way, give me the carving rather than the label: what exactly the phenomenon is, what nearby concepts it could be confused with and how it differs from each, and where its edges fall. Two edges matter most to me. First, are questions about when an experience occurred and questions about whether one occurred at all the same kind of question — does my third hypothesis belong to the same pattern as the first two, or is it a different animal? Second, contrast the case where the analogous question has a checkable answer: whether I locked the door, or whether the memory of locking it was manufactured afterwards, is settled by going and looking. What is it about the mental case that blocks the check, and would a complete neural readout of when a narrative became available settle it, or would the dispute simply move? Finally, if the right thing to say is that there is no fact of the matter, tell me which kind of no-fact-of-the-matter that is — metaphysical, semantic, or merely epistemic — and what would show it. A name alone will not answer my question; the boundaries will.
