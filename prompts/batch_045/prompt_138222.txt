I have been putting together a graduate seminar on decision-making under normative uncertainty, and I have backed myself into an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept but whose steps I cannot fault. I would be grateful for a diagnosis from someone who has thought about this longer than I have.

(1) If you are uncertain which moral theory is correct, practical rationality requires you to maximize expected choice-worthiness: for each available option, take the choice-worthiness assigned to it by each theory you regard as epistemically possible, weight each by your credence in that theory, and sum.

(2) That sum is well defined only if the verdicts of the different theories are expressed on a single cardinal scale, so that a great wrong according to one theory can be traded off against a small wrong according to another.

(3) But the theories disagree not only about which acts are right but about what is assessed in the first place: some assess acts, some rules or policies, some motives or traits of character, some institutional arrangements. There is no unit of assessment common to them all, and hence no common scale on which their verdicts could be summed.

(4) So the sum in (1) is undefined, and (1) delivers no verdict.

(5) The usual repair is normalization: stipulate a scale for each theory, for instance by setting the distance between that theory's best and worst available option equal to one, or by equalizing the spread of each theory's verdicts across the option set.

(6) Normalization is sensitive to the option set. Adding an option you will certainly not take, or deleting one, changes the normalization and can invert the ranking of the options you were comparing; and normalization rules that seem equally reasonable disagree with one another in the same case.

(7) Hence what you are rationally required to do is determined in part by things that should be irrelevant: how the options are described and ordered, whether a dominated option happens to be listed, which normalization rule you adopt.

(8) A procedure whose output varies with presentation order and with options you will not choose is not a procedure of rational choice; it is a device for rationalizing whatever you were inclined to do.

(9) Therefore the expected-choice-worthiness approach to moral uncertainty is empty, and normative uncertainty never gives you a reason to act against the theory you find most credible.

What bothers me is that this proves considerably more than I set out to prove. The same reasoning would show that a legislature aggregating incommensurable conceptions of the good, or a regulator doing cost-benefit analysis, has no rational method either; and it would leave unexplained the mundane cases that made the topic worth teaching in the first place, where you are roughly halfway between a view on which some ordinary act is permissible and one on which it is seriously wrong, and abstaining plainly seems like the rational response. If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) and (6) than accept (9), but I cannot say what I am paying to keep them.

So: which step is doing the illegitimate work, and why did it look sound? If the argument is valid instead, tell me which premise to surrender, and what that surrender commits me to about hedging in the mundane case, about comparison of verdicts across theories, and about what it is for a moral theory to deliver a verdict about action at all.
