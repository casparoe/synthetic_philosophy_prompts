Last night I had a long, unresolved argument with a friend about whether it can be rational to make a sacrifice that makes no causal difference to anything. The trigger was mundane. I run a small bakery with thin margins and have decided to stop buying battery-cage eggs, even though my own custom is far too small to change any producer's output or any hen's life. My friend said this was self-expression dressed up as ethics, and that I should either admit I was paying for a feeling or buy the cheap eggs and give the difference to something that does make a difference.

The argument then turned on a move of hers that I had not encountered before. Grant, she said, that we do not know the world is small. Suppose the world contains, or might well contain, a great many agents facing structurally similar choices, spread across a very large or infinite space, or across causally isolated regions, or across merely possible situations of which ours is one. Now take an evidential rather than a causal view of rational choice. On that view my action is not merely a causally inert token: it is evidence about what other agents of the relevant kind do. If I am unsure which of the many similar agents I am, then conditional on my buying the cheap eggs I should expect a somewhat larger fraction of similar agents to buy them too, and conditional on my refusing, a somewhat smaller fraction. The expected difference in aggregate demand need not be zero even when my causal influence plainly is. It is the one-boxer's reasoning in Newcomb's problem generalised to cases where there is no predictor, no common cause set up in advance, and no causal mechanism whatever.

I said this seemed to prove too much, and that I did not know how to tell a real correlation from a gerrymandered one. She said the burden was on me to say where it breaks, and that dismissing it because it sounds strange is not an answer. Neither of us moved.

Please set out what you take to be the most important implications of this line of argument for how I should actually make decisions like this one. I am especially interested in the following:

First, what would I have to believe for the correlation to be real rather than merely stipulated? How much turns on the size or structure of the world, and how much on which class of agents I am supposed to count myself as a sample from? Does it matter whether the similarity that grounds the correlation is similarity of circumstances or similarity of decision procedure, and is there a principled way to settle the reference class rather than an arbitrary one?

Second, if it works, how large is the effect? If the class of correlated agents might be vast, does the argument make every small sacrifice astronomically significant, and would that be self-undermining: on that picture nothing is ever a drop in the bucket, and the category of causally pointless action loses all content? Or does the size of the effect track my confidence rather than the number of agents?

Third, that raises a sharper version: if I am genuinely uncertain whether the world is large, and I assign it only modest credence, is that enough for the evidential term to dominate ordinary causal considerations anyway, given how enormous the correlated class might be? Does the argument then swallow everything else I might do with my money?

Fourth, does the same reasoning reach sideways within a single life? Are my future selves, or my choices in other domains, correlated with my present choice in the relevant sense, so that sticking to a principle now gets independent evidential support from the fact that a person who does it now is likelier to do it later?

Please be concrete about what actually changes in practice for someone in my position, distinguish the load-bearing implications from the speculative ones, and say plainly where you think the argument fails, if it does.
