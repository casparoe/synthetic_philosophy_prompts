Two people on my team got into it at lunch today and I've been turning it over ever since, so here's the whole thing as best I can reconstruct it.

Context: our VP of Eng pushed through a change that pipes a lot more data into our telemetry setup so crash reports would finally be usable. The SDK she picked also, as a documented side effect, ships a chunk of users' browsing history to a third-party analytics vendor. She'd read that in the docs. At the all-hands someone asked whether she had intentionally started collecting browsing history, and she said no — that was a side effect, she was trying to fix crash reporting.

Move 1: Dev A says that's weaselly. She knew, she didn't care, she shipped it. For anything anyone actually cares about — blame, whether she should have done it, whether we should roll it back — "foreseen and she couldn't be bothered" is morally the same as "she aimed at it."

Move 2: Dev B says no, that's just false, and the distinction is load bearing. Aiming at something and foreseeing it are different mental states, and we track the difference for a reason; if you collapse them you can't even describe a tradeoff, and half of engineering is shipping things with known downsides.

Move 3: A says fine, but then he went and did a thing. He wrote up the identical scenario — same SDK, same docs, same "she knew, it wasn't her goal" — and fired it off to about 25 engineers in the company. Half got a version where the side effect was bad (the privacy thing); half got one where it was good (turns out the change also quietly fixed screen-reader support for free). Same mental states, only the valence differs. Bad version: almost everybody said yes, she did it intentionally. Good version: almost everybody said no. So A says: your distinction isn't doing the work here. The ordinary word "intentionally" is basically a blame dispenser — people aren't reporting a fact about her head, they're reacting to whether the outcome sucked. Which means the concept is contaminated by moral judgement and we should stop pretending it tracks anything real.

Move 4: B says that's one reading, but here's another: maybe "intentionally" was never about desire. Maybe it tracks whether the agent exercised control over something she could have avoided — we count a side effect as hers when she had the option to prevent it and didn't take it, and we only bother to go looking for that in the first place when the outcome matters. On that reading the asymmetry isn't contamination, it's the concept being about answerability rather than about what someone wanted.

Move 5: A says come on, that's circular. The concept is supposed to tell us who's answerable, and now you're saying it just means "whoever we already think is answerable." You've built the conclusion into the definition.

Move 6: B says OK, but he also isn't sure the poll shows what A thinks. In the bad version, people probably assume she secretly wanted the data — why else accept that cost — so the badness functions as evidence about her motives, not as a substitute for them. The vignette says she didn't want it, but people fill in a motive anyway. And for what it's worth, 25 engineers at lunch isn't a study.

Move 7: A says that's moving the goalposts, and anyway: if ordinary judgements swing this hard on valence and framing, then using "what we would say" as evidence is just bad survey research with extra steps — and that describes a whole style of doing philosophy.

I get both of there points and couldn't call it. Can you referee it properly: go through moves 1 through 7, say who has the better of it at each point and why, and tell me what both of them are missing (if anything) — about what "intentionally" is doing, about what a poll like that one could actually establish even if it were run properly, and about whether the good/bad asymmetry is a bug in our concept or a feature of it.
