I am a software engineer by trade, with no philosophy training, and I hold a militia commission in a signals battalion. Most of my working life is spent on one distinction that I think this debate completely lacks: the difference between what a system can sustain indefinitely and what it can bring online within days — standing capacity versus surge capacity. Next month our cantonal parliament votes on an initiative to replace conscription with a professional force, and I have been asked to give the eight-minute keynote at the annual dinner of the cantonal militia officers' society. The room will include career officers who know the mobilisation figures, spouses and teenage children who do not, my own daughter, who chose the longer civilian-service alternative rather than military service, and a city councillor from the peace group who accepted the invitation in good faith.

The argument I want to make — and this is the part I need help with — is that a militia is not a cheap army but a precommitment: a democracy binding its future self so that, in a crisis, paying an aggressor or submitting is not a live option for the electorate that will have to decide. Please write the keynote in English, in a plain register, about eight minutes long. It should: explain why the comparison to make is surge capacity against standing capacity rather than headline troop numbers; face the fact that this argument justifies coercing an eighteen-year-old partly out of distrust for the person he will become, and name that cost instead of dressing it up as tradition or civic virtue; concede where the argument underdetermines the institution, since if what matters is that the country cannot cheaply opt out of defending itself, more than one arrangement delivers that, and several people in the room will invoke Sweden and Finland in opposite directions on exactly this point; not sneer at conscientious objectors, at my daughter, or at the councillor; rest on no disputed history and no invented figures; and end without a cheap peroration.
