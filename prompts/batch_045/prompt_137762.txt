I found a blog post the other day arguing that if you take wild animal suffering seriously you should probably stop defending habitat, and I want to know how much of it to actually believe. The gist, as the guy puts it: "most wild animals are the lay-thousands-of-eggs kind, so the typical wild animal dies within days of being born, usually eaten or starved, which means the average wild life is short, nasty and almost certainly not worth living. A patch of habitat is basically a machine for producing lives like that, so the more animals an area holds the worse it is in total, and when you count by individuals it's mostly insects and other small stuff that does the counting. It follows that draining a marsh or clearing scrub cuts suffering twice over — you remove the animals already there and you stop them producing any more — so the conservation lot fighting a new housing estate are, by their own values, making the world worse." When someone in the comments said the survivors might do better with fewer mouths competing for the same food, he said nature just refills the space, so that doesn't count.

The first half feels right to me but the bit about cheering on the bulldozers came out of nowhere, and I cant really say where the argument goes wrong. There's a plan to build on some scrubland near me and the local green group are objecting.

Could you go through it roughly sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what's a simplification, and what's wrong — especially whether short lives really show the average wild life is bad, and whether the bit about the housing estate follows even if the rest does?
