You are a moral philosopher who works on the ethics of memory — someone who knows the literature on what we owe the dead and on the case for forgetting, who has read Margalit's Horkheimer lectures on The Ethics of Memory (delivered in 1999) and his distinction between the thick, caring relations that ground an ethics of memory and the thinner, universal relations that ground a morality of memory, and who also knows Rieff's suspicion, in The Triumph of the Therapeutic (1966), that modern culture quietly converts questions of what we owe into questions of what is healthy for us. You have also written for non-specialist audiences. I need your help drafting a short piece of occasional writing, and I'd like you to work at it properly rather than in one pass: begin with a brief brainstorm — a handful of bullets on what you take the real philosophical crux of my situation to be, and on which of my constraints below do the most work — then draft the speech itself (about 800 words, roughly seven minutes spoken), then add the short philosophical note I describe at the end. Please don't include citations, references, or further reading: I'm writing this on a laptop in a village hall with no library access, and I need the words, not the apparatus.

The occasion is the closing ceremony of the Marsh End Voices project, in the village hall at Netherby, three weeks from now. Marsh End was a hamlet of about ninety houses drowned when the Netherby reservoir was filled in 1957; the survivors were dispersed across three counties. My mother, Elinor Vance, a retired schoolteacher, spent twenty-seven years recording 214 interviews with former residents and their children, and in 2018 she deposited the collection with the county. What made the project unusual was Elinor's "sunset" agreement, which every participant signed: an interview was, in her formulation, an *address* rather than a *record* — a speech act directed at someone who could answer back — and each participant retained the unconditional right to require their own tape to be destroyed, without giving reasons. She used to say that once the intended listeners are gone, a tape is "orphaned address," and keeping it is not remembering but eavesdropping. Thirty-seven of the participants left standing destruction requests, and last winter the project committee honoured them by a vote of six to five. The evening will run like this: we play about four minutes of selected excerpts, then the thirty-seven tapes go into a degausser on the stage, then I speak. In the room will be three of the surviving children of those thirty-seven — one of whom, Dewi Pryce, has said in the local paper that we are "finishing the drowning"; two of the remaining interviewees, whose own recordings are being kept and who now feel, as one of them put it to me, like the guests at a funeral who weren't invited to die; the county heritage officer, who wanted digitised, name-redacted transcripts retained as a "silent copy" and lost that vote too, six to five; a journalist; my brother Geraint, who voted against and who has barely spoken to me since; and in the front row, Mara Selby, the retired sound archivist who trained me, who resigned from the committee over this and who has called the destruction, in print, "professional barbarism."

Two things make this harder than a normal commemoration. First, Elinor changed her mind. In her last year she wrote me a letter saying she had come to think wholesale destruction would be a mistake — not because memory is owed to us, she wrote, but because the addressee she had imagined, "the village," had quietly been replaced by a thinner one: whoever, anywhere, genuinely wants to know about Marsh End. She had come to think that was a real addressee after all. The committee honoured her original promise over her later wish, and I cast the deciding vote to do it. So I have to acknowledge my mother's reversal without disowning her and without staging the family split in front of a hall. Second, the chair has asked me to "say something about healing and the future," and I don't want to: I read Rieff in graduate school and I worry that if we narrate tonight as closure, we turn what we owe thirty-seven particular people into something we are doing for our own emotional hygiene, and a ceremony that does that seems to me to fail precisely where it claims to succeed.

So here is what I need the speech to do and not do. It must not say that the thirty-seven "live on in our memory" or "in our hearts": several of them said in their interviews that they did not want to be turned into a monument, and that formula is the one they were refusing. It must avoid therapeutic vocabulary entirely — no healing, closure, moving on, letting go, nothing about what is healthy for the valley — while still being, in some way I can't specify, kind. It must not smooth the fact that some of these requests were made thirty years ago and never revisited, or that two of them came from relatives rather than from the speakers themselves. It must not imply that the recordings were worthless or that keeping them would have been wrong, because Mara is in the front row and I was her student and I'm not going to insult her in public. It must not thank anyone for "preserving" anything. It must find some honest way of handling Dewi Pryce's "finishing the drowning" that neither concedes it nor defuses it with a joke. It must be speakable aloud, in plain English, by a nervous person, with the philosophy load-bearing and invisible — no named theorists, no distinctions announced as distinctions, and nothing that would make the two elderly interviewees feel they are being studied. If you think any one of my constraints is philosophically mistaken, don't quietly write around it: honour it in the draft and attack it in the note.

Finally, the note, no more than 400 words. I want you to separate, as clearly as you can, three things: what is genuinely settled or close to settled among people who work on memory and forgetting; what is openly contested among them — for instance whether duties of memory are owed to persons and relations or to communities, truths, or the dead as such, whether a person's request not to be remembered can be binding on those who outlive them, and whether the Rieff-style suspicion of therapeutic framing is a good diagnosis or itself a contested cultural claim; and where *my own* reasoning and your draft take a side. Do not present a contested view as the consensus, and where you aren't sure whether something is settled or contested, say that you aren't sure rather than asserting it.
