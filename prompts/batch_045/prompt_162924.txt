I help run a multilingual community choir in a mid-sized city, and somehow I have become the person who commissions and then adjudicates the English singing translations we perform. I have no training in this — I was a logistics coordinator before I retired — but I got interested after a friend lent me Douglas Hofstadter's 1997 book *Le Ton beau de Marot*, in which he takes one short sixteenth-century French poem of Clément Marot's (the poem to a sick young woman, which he calls "Ma mignonne") and retranslates it dozens of times in different registers and shapes. What stuck with me was the idea that a translation's job is not to carry over the words but to carry over the pattern: the tune of the thing. Our situation is a version of that with a hard external constraint, because a singing translation has to fit a melody that is not negotiable — same number of syllables per line, stresses on the same beats, an open vowel wherever the tune holds a note for more than about a beat and a half, and a rhyme at every point where the music cadences. We do eight to ten songs a year, I have one evening per song, my translators are volunteers (heritage speakers, a retired French teacher, one composer), our music director vetoes anything unsingable, and our board has to approve anything that could embarrass us in front of an audience. We perform each song twice in a concert, first in the original and then in English, and the program prints the original text facing the translation. Over time I have accumulated some house rules that I can no longer tell apart from superstition: never change the season or time of day the song is set in; never reverse the emotional direction of a line to make a rhyme land; every dropped image must be "paid back" somewhere else in the same song, which I call the debt ledger; each translation must contain at least one visible anchor to the original, either a borrowed word or a cognate, so the audience can see the connection; and, when a rhyme is impossible, we sometimes let an English line rhyme against the sound of the French word left untranslated, which one of my volunteers calls a ghost rhyme. I want a procedure I can actually run, not a theory: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure just says no, and where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because the issue is one of values rather than facts. It needs to be usable by someone with my level of French and my volunteers' patience, it has to say what to do when I cannot settle a factual dispute about the original (half the time I cannot tell whether a word is slang, obsolete, or regional), and it should tell me which of my house rules are doing real work and which ones I should drop.

Three cases are on my desk right now. First: a 1930s song addressed to a woman named Marguerite, where every stanza's last line plays on her name also being the French word for the daisy, and the melody's longest held note lands on that word. English "Margaret" has no flower in it, "Daisy" fits the music but renames the woman and loses the sense that she is being spoken to by her real name, and my current draft keeps "Margaret" and moves the flower image into an earlier line to pay off the debt ledger, where it feels jammed in. Second, and this is the one I expect to lose: my favourite draft we have ever done, which turns the original's last line of tired acceptance — roughly, "I have waited long enough" — into something like "and still I'd wait for you," which is more beautiful, sits perfectly on the melody, and is the line our singers love; but it flips resignation into devotion and adds an image that is not in the French. Third: a song where the only word that will rhyme and sing at the cadence is period-accurate and would have been unremarkable in 1930 but would now be heard by part of our audience as an ethnic slur, and my volunteer argues — not unreasonably — that singing anything else there is a bowdlerisation.

Please be decisive: run the procedure on all three and give me a definite verdict on each with your reasons, including the verdict you think I will not like, and do not just tell me to hire a professional translator, because there is no budget. Tell me which of your rulings a working song translator would most likely overrule, which parts of the procedure are judgment calls dressed up as steps, and where you are yourself unsure.
