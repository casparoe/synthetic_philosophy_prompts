A colleague in policy work sent me the following argument against Chesterton's fence, and I cannot locate the break, though I am fairly sure there is one.

(1) Chesterton's fence: do not remove an inherited institution until you can say what it was put up for.
(2) The usual Burkean backing for that rule: a long-surviving institution has been tested in ways no individual reasoner can reconstruct, so it carries tacit knowledge, and the burden of proof falls on whoever would change it.
(3) But the test in (2) is only survival over time, and the selection pressure on institutions is nearly nil. Institutions are not competed away for being bad; they persist through inertia, sunk costs, coordination lock-in, and the interests of whoever they benefit. A useless fence and a load-bearing one survive at similar rates.
(4) So survival is no evidence of fitness: the status quo carries no epistemic presumption, and the Burkean asymmetry in (2) is unsupported.
(5) If there is no epistemic asymmetry, there is no reason to prefer Chesterton's caution to any other decision rule; proposals to abolish should be assessed bottom-up, by expected consequences, exactly as a rationalist planner would assess them.
(6) Yet (5) is something a Burkean can reasonably reject, and not because survival is evidence. Call it the deletion asymmetry: abolishing an institution you do not understand destroys information you cannot recover, whereas retaining it is reversible, since you can abolish it later once you have learned what it does. Caution can therefore be warranted on decision-theoretic grounds even if the status quo has no epistemic credit at all.
(7) If (6) is right, the argument breaks somewhere between (3) and (5).

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want a diagnosis, not the verdict that premise (3) is too strong. Two things I especially want priced. First: if the deletion asymmetry in (6) is all the conservative has left, does it really deliver Burkean conservatism, or only an instrumental caution about irreversible acts, with none of Burke's distrust of abstract planning? It is worth remembering that for Burke conservatism was a doctrine about how to change, not a refusal to change. Second: is the move from (2)'s tested through (3)'s survived to (4)'s fit doing any work it should not? If I have to choose which claim to keep, I would rather keep (3) than keep (6) as a defense of Burke, but tell me what that costs me.
