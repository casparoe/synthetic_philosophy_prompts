I serve as Procedural Clerk to the Rules Committee of the Legislative Assembly of the Republic of Sarmatia, and I need a standing procedure for certifying proposed amendments to our Constitutional Charter — specifically the recurring class of proposals that amend the rules about how the Charter is amended. The jurisdiction is fictional and the provisions reproduced below are the whole of the relevant law; please reason from them alone and do not rest anything on cases, commentators, or comparative constitutional practice, since my office has no research support and a certification note must be defensible on the face of the Charter text.

The consolidated text my office maintains includes the following:

Art. 112 — The Assembly may amend this Charter by resolution adopted by an absolute majority of all its members at two readings separated by not less than forty days.
Art. 113 — A provision may be declared entrenched by a resolution adopted under Art. 112 which states that it is entrenched. An entrenched provision may be amended or repealed only at a third reading at which the resolution is adopted by a two-thirds majority of all the members. This Article may be amended or repealed only at such a third reading.
Art. 114 — An amendment takes effect on the day after its adoption and has no retroactive effect.
Art. 115 — A provision of this Charter that has not been reaffirmed by the Assembly within twenty years of its adoption lapses. Chapters I to III do not lapse. (Art. 115 stands in Chapter VI and was itself adopted twenty-two years ago.)
Art. 116 — The Clerk shall maintain an authoritative consolidated text of this Charter. Where two amendments conflict, the one later in date of adoption prevails.

We certify around forty instruments a session; two or three of those raise a self-reference question. The stakes are asymmetric: a wrong certification either admits a defective provision into the consolidated text, where ministries and courts rely on it for years, or blocks a measure with clear majority support and invites a confrontation between the Committee and the floor. I have five working days per instrument, two deputies with law degrees but no training in constitutional theory, and a one-page note as the deliverable. I certify, the Chair of the Rules Committee countersigns, and the Assembly may overrule us by simple majority — which is a reason for caution, but not for pretending that a political question is a legal one.

What I want from you is an operational procedure, not an essay. Please give me:

1. An ordered sequence of questions my deputies can run on any proposal, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to it, who bears the burden at that step, and the standard for saying the question is settled. I would like your own labels for the recurring patterns — I have been calling them "bootstrapping" instruments (a rule used to change itself), "ratchet" instruments (a rule made harder to change than the change it enacts), and "retro-cure" instruments (a rule about validity altered with retroactive effect) — but use whatever vocabulary makes the distinctions stable for someone who has never studied the topic.
2. Explicit stop-points: the questions on which a "no" ends the inquiry and the instrument is out of order, and the places where the procedure must hand the matter to a person or to the floor because the issue is one of legitimacy, reliance, or political judgment rather than of validity. Where you hand off, say precisely what the note should record so that the handoff is auditable.
3. A short, fixed vocabulary of certifications — something like "in order," "in order with a flagged defect," "out of order," "referred" — with the conditions for each, plus a rule for what my office does with a provision whose validity is genuinely doubtful but which has been acted on: whether it is printed, printed with an annotation, or withheld from the consolidated text.
4. A one-page desk version my deputies can work from without me in the room.

Then apply the whole procedure to three live instruments, drafting for each the actual language I would sign:

A. A resolution moved under Art. 112, carried at two readings by an absolute majority, providing (i) that future amendments require the assent of three-fifths of all the members and (ii) that clause (i) "shall not be amended or repealed." The sponsors assure the Committee that (ii) makes the new threshold permanent. Tell me what, if anything, (ii) accomplishes under this Charter, and what my note should say about a clause that purports to do something the Charter gives it no power to do.

B. A single resolution, moved and carried under Art. 112 by an absolute majority at two readings, that repeals Art. 113 and in the same instrument repeals Art. 96 (Central Bank independence), which was entrenched under Art. 113 four years ago. The sponsors argue that the lock and the locked provision fall together: at the moment the resolution takes effect Art. 113 no longer exists, so nothing required a third reading. The Committee is convinced this is out of order and expects a one-line ruling to that effect. Run the procedure and give me what it actually yields, including whether the same result could be achieved by a sequencing that is clearly lawful. I would rather have that answer than win the argument.

C. Three years ago the Assembly adopted three instruments, each after a single reading; the Chair ruled at the time that the requirement of two readings had been substantially complied with, and the instruments have been printed in the consolidated text and relied on by two ministries ever since. A new resolution proposes (i) to repeal Art. 114 with effect from a date three years and six months ago and (ii) to declare the three instruments duly adopted. Note that repealing the no-retroactivity clause retroactively is itself a retroactive amendment, and note the reliance. Tell me whether my office can certify those three provisions, and what the consolidated text should show in the meantime.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do. I have two pressure points in mind and would like you to address them: Art. 115, whose twenty-year clock has run against the very article that creates it; and measures whose legality under the Charter is clear but whose legitimacy is contested, where the Assembly can simply overrule my certification. If there are further limits — places where the procedure will misfire, or where it silently presupposes a view about what makes an amended Charter the same charter — say so. Please write your answer in American English.
