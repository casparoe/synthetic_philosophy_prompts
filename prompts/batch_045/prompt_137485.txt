I am writing a paper on the relation between sunk-cost reasoning and competitive escalation, and I want to use the Watergate cover-up as my central case. I am not looking for a history lesson. I am looking for a disciplined analysis of that episode through two lenses that I think are usually conflated: the dollar auction — the sequential game, from Shubik's 1971 paper, in which one dollar is sold to the highest bidder while the second-highest bidder pays his bid and receives nothing — and the escalation-of-commitment literature that begins with Staw's 1976 study of continued investment in a failing course of action. Both get called "sunk cost." I want to know how far they actually come apart, and what each does and does not explain about this case.

Please take the following stylized timeline as stipulated rather than as something to litigate: the June 1972 break-in at the Democratic National Committee offices in the Watergate complex; the payments of money and the promises of executive clemency made to the arrested men to secure their silence; the attempts to contain and deflect the FBI's investigation; the burglars' trial and the letters and testimony that emerged from it in early 1973; the Senate select committee's hearings and the appointment of a special prosecutor; the dismissal of that prosecutor in October 1973 and the resignations it triggered at the Justice Department; the struggle over the Oval Office recordings, the Supreme Court's 1974 ruling against the President, the House Judiciary Committee's recommendation of articles of impeachment, and the resignation in August 1974. Assume, further, what the standard account holds: that the concealment worked well enough at first to help carry a landslide reelection in November 1972, and that the cover-up unraveled afterward.

What I want from the analysis:

**One. An explicit mapping, stated in the theory's own terms.** Who is bidding, and is there genuinely a second bidder, or is the opposing party better modeled as an adaptive opponent — the investigators, the press, or the men being paid off — whose reservation price rises the more it is fed? What exactly counts as a bid: money, clemency, false testimony, the misuse of an agency, the President's remaining credibility? What is the prize, and is it the same object in 1972 as in 1974? What would "dropping out" have meant in practice, and what did it cost at each stage? I also want you to say whether the decision-making unit is properly one agent or several with divergent exposure — the counsel's position and the chief of staff's position were not the same position — because escalation research generally assumes a single decision maker facing a single feedback signal.

**Two. What each framework says the actors should have done, and when.** In the dollar auction, the marginal step is locally rational: given that you have already bid, paying a little more beats paying what you have already bid and getting nothing. If that is right, the fault cannot be located in any individual step, and it has to be relocated to the entry decision or to the failure to adopt a binding commitment not to respond. Say whether you accept that relocation, and where it puts the blame in this case.

**Three. Where each lens explains something and where it is silent.** Be explicit about silence rather than forcing the theory to speak. I suspect, for instance, that the escalation literature has nothing useful to say about a course of action that *succeeded* at its stated aim before it destroyed its author, and nothing about the moment when the object of commitment quietly shifts — after November 1972 the thing being protected was no longer the reelection but the earlier concealment itself, so that the "project" became the cover-up. If you think the literature does cover these, show me how.

**Four. An assessment of a formal worry I have about the auction reading.** In Shubik's game the prize is fixed, and the auction only extracts money from the bidders. In this case each bid damaged the prize itself, because every act of concealment generated fresh evidence of obstruction, and the damage was caused by the bidding and grew with it. Is that a genuine structural difference, or just a relabeling of the losses? Concretely: suppose the value of the prize declines as a function of the total amount you have bid. Under what condition on the rate of decline does the "bid again to cut your losses" argument stop working, and how much earlier does stopping become optimal? I want the condition, not the intuition.

**Five. Whether a third model does better than either** — a credible-precommitment story, a principal–agent story, or something else — and a verdict on the reverse direction: is Watergate an argument for the psychological escalation literature, or is it a case in which the structure alone produces the catastrophe and the psychology is decoration?

Please write in American English, keep the mapping and the payoffs explicit rather than impressionistic, and tell me plainly which parts of this case neither framework can handle.
