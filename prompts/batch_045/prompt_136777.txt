I'm a retired entomologist — thirty-odd years, first on crop pests, then the last decade on vector control — and a few years back I drifted into a philosophy reading group at the library, which seemed harmless enough at the time. It's turned into an argument I can't let go of, and I'd like someone to actually work through it with me instead of trading slogans.

The row started when someone said that if insects can suffer at all, agricultural insecticide use is by a huge margin the biggest moral catastrophe humans are currently running: the numbers are so large that nothing else comes close. Someone else said insects almost certainly don't feel anything, so there's nothing to discuss. Both strike me as lazy. The first one is just counting corpses, as if every death is the same unit, and the second one settles an empirical question by shrugging.

So I sketched something I've started calling the balance sheet, and I want to know whether it survives contact with a real case. Four separate cells that you have to fill in before you're allowed an opinion:

1. What the intervention does to the target organisms — and not just how many die, but how. Mode of death has to be weighted: a fast neurotoxic knockdown is a different thing from an insect growth regulator that leaves a larva unable to moult, or a Bt toxin that shuts the gut down, or a parasitoid wasp eating something alive from the inside. There's a nasty trade-off I keep coming back to — call it the selectivity paradox — where the more selective, "kinder" tool spares the non-targets but kills the targets slowly, so you can end up with fewer deaths and more suffering.
2. What it does to everything else that gets caught: non-target arthropods, the predators and parasitoids that were holding the pest down, and everything up the food chain.
3. The cell everybody skips: what would have happened to those insects anyway. Most pest insects are r-selected — enormous numbers of offspring, nearly all of them dying as juveniles, mostly by starvation or predation or parasitism, none of it quick. So killing breeding adults before they lay may prevent a much larger number of short miserable lives rather than cut short a smaller number of decent ones. I think of this as the demographic offset, and it's the cell that decides the whole thing, and it's the cell nobody has data for.
4. Human welfare: disease, crop loss, cost, who eats.

Plus a rule I like, the displacement audit: suppression rarely deletes harm, it relocates it — resistance, resurgence once the natural enemies are gone, secondary pests promoted into the vacancy. You owe an account of where the harm went, not just that it left.

Now the case. Take the DDT-and-malaria story, roughly as I remember it, and please just grant me the shape of it rather than arguing with the history, I don't trust my memory on exact figures and that's not the point. DDT from the 1940s; the WHO campaign of the fifties and sixties leaning hard on indoor spraying and doing very well in a lot of places; Silent Spring in 1962 and the backlash; the US cancelling agricultural use in 1972; the Stockholm Convention restricting it from 2004 onward but exempting public-health use inside WHO guidelines, because there wasn't an affordable replacement; and then the case everyone cites, South Africa switching from DDT to pyrethroids in the nineties, resistance in the local vector, malaria climbing sharply, and a switch back to DDT around 2000 with cases falling again. Also relevant, and I think this is right: most of the resistance was driven by agricultural use at vastly greater volumes than vector control ever needed.

What I want is that episode run through the four cells, in both directions.

First, the mapping. Fill the sheet for three moments: the big spraying campaigns, the withdrawal and restriction, and the return to indoor residual spraying. Where does each cell get large, and in which direction does it point? Does the sheet actually deliver a verdict on any of the three, or do the cells come out pointing opposite ways?

Second, where it goes quiet. My suspicion is that the sheet has a structural problem: the fourth cell is in a different currency from the first three and there's no honest exchange rate, and the third cell is the one that dominates the arithmetic and is the one we can't fill. I also think the sheet has nothing to say about who got to decide — the choices in this story were made by donor agencies, trade rules, chemical regulations written in countries with no malaria, and farmers spraying cotton, none of whom were weighing my cells at all. Say plainly where the framework is silent instead of forcing it to speak.

Third, and this is what I really want: what does the case do to the framework? Because here's what bothers me. If you fill in the demographic offset generously, the sheet seems to license more spraying, not less — suppress the breeding population and you've prevented a great deal of suffering that was coming anyway, plus the malaria. Which is the opposite of what the person who started this argument wanted. Is that a reductio of the balance sheet, or a reductio of the "count the deaths" version of insect ethics, or just the lesson that insect numbers and insect suffering are different quantities that can pull apart? And if you think the sheet is salvageable, what would have to be true empirically for the verdict to flip — and if you had one field season and a modest budget to measure it, which cell would you go after and how?

Sorry for the ramble. Talk to me like a retired field person who knows his insects, has read maybe four philosophy papers, and hasn't quite got the vocabulary: define anything technical you need, and where you're guessing please say you're guessing. I'd much rather have an honest blank than a confident number.
