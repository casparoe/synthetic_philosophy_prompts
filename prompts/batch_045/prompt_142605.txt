I'm in a small graduate reading group on anthropic reasoning — six of us, mostly philosophy with two physicists sitting in — and last week's session was on the doomsday argument. One member of the group, who is far more convinced by it than the rest of us, has since produced what he calls the Indexical Indifference generalization, and I have been going in circles trying to locate its weak point. I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis, and please answer in American English.

Here is his chain as I have reconstructed it. Let R be my birth rank among humans (on the usual estimates somewhere around 10^11, though nothing below turns on the exact figure).

(1) Self-sampling assumption: my epistemic situation is that of a random sample from the class of observers of my kind. In particular, conditional on the total number N of humans who will ever live, my birth rank is uniformly distributed over the positions 1 through N.
(2) Take the prior over N to be the usual zero-information one, P(N) proportional to 1/N. Then P(N less than or equal to 20R) is about 0.95: I should be roughly 95 percent confident that no more than some two trillion humans will ever live, which at anything like present population size puts our end within a few centuries to a few millennia.
(3) Now generalize. What (1) really asserts, he says, is that my indexical position within any class I belong to carries no information beyond the bare fact of membership — call this Indexical Indifference. Nothing in the argument depends on the class being humans. It depends only on (a) I am in the class, (b) my position in it is exchangeable with the other positions, and (c) I have no independent grip on the total size of the class.
(4) If Indexical Indifference is sound, it is sound for observer-moments as well as for persons — arguably more so, since what gets sampled is an experience rather than a lifetime, and a lifetime is just a series of experiences. So, conditional on the total number M of observer-moments I will ever have, my present one is uniformly located among them, and with the same indifference prior I get P(M less than or equal to 20m) of about 0.95, where m is my tally so far.
(5) And it is sound for the class of all observer-moments in the history of the universe, not merely for mine. On at least some mainstream cosmological pictures, a long-lived de Sitter phase or an eternally inflating multiverse yields, by thermal fluctuation, enormously more observer-moments of the Boltzmann-brain sort than all the ordinary evolved observers a planet-bound civilization could ever produce — not by a factor of twenty but by a factor that is astronomically large, though the estimates differ from each other by dozens of orders of magnitude. Given (3), I should conclude that I am almost certainly one of those fluctuation observers.
(6) But a fluctuation observer's apparent memories of a seminar, of cosmology, and of having worked through (1) through (5) are noise, uncorrelated with the truth. So the argument in its general form removes its own grounds: accepting it requires assigning near-zero credence to the very premises that generated it.
(7) He also insists that the original argument is insensitive to anything we might learn: whatever empirical evidence we have about humanity's prospects — eight decades of nuclear near-misses, pandemic risk, the shape of our technology — is far too weak to move a prior with twenty-to-one leverage, so evidence cannot rescue us from (2).

What I want from you: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I find (5) and (6) unacceptable and (2) merely uncomfortable, so if I must choose I would rather keep the human-scale doomsday argument than the Boltzmann one — but I want to know what that choice actually costs me, and whether anything distinguishes the two cases apart from my dislike of the second conclusion. One of us thinks the real work is being done by the sampling assumption and another thinks it is the 1/N prior, which is doing all the labor in (2) and then being silently carried into (4) and (5) where it no longer has any claim on us; I cannot tell which is right, because in his presentation the two moves look like a single step. Please name the step, explain why it looked innocent, and price the main alternatives — switching to a self-indication assumption, restricting the reference class, abandoning the indifference prior, denying that observer-moments are the right sampling unit, or biting the bullet on (5) — and tell me which of those prices is the one a defender of the original doomsday argument can least afford to pay.
