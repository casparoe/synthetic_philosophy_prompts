I'm in an art history PhD program (17th-c. Dutch, which is apparently why I got voluntold to referee this) and two people in my department went at it after a colloquium on forgery. The visiting curator put a hypothetical on the table: two canvases, visually indistinguishable, same materials, same everything you can test — one painted by a minor Dutch master in the 1650s, one by a forger in the 1930s. We all know the standard setup: when the fake is unmasked its value collapses, and the puzzle is how that can be when nothing perceivable has changed. The twist she added: during the decades it passed as genuine, the fake was the painting that a whole later generation of artists responded to, while the real one spent those same decades in a crate in an attic influencing nobody.

Then:

1. Dev says the twist kills the historical line. If what we value is the object's place in the causal history of art — what it made later artists do — then the fake wins outright. So the value of the original has to come from the act of making: this is a thing someone actually did, a problem they actually had and solved.

2. Nadia says that commits him to treating the fake's influence as worthless, which can't be right: those later paintings are genuine responses to something, the artists weren't mistaken about what they were doing, and their achievements don't evaporate just because the object turns out to be a 1930s canvas.

3. Dev says influence is real, it just isn't the same as value — a fertile mistake is still a mistake.

4. Nadia says fine, then look at the achievement side, where she thinks his view backfires. The master could take the tradition for granted; the forger had to reconstruct it backwards from surfaces, with nothing to lean on. However you measure difficulty of problem and quality of solution, the forger did the more impressive thing, so if value tracks achievement the original shouldn't win.

5. Dev says achievement isn't absolute, it's indexed to the agent's situation, and the forger's is a different kind of thing anyway — reconstruction rather than invention, working out a joke from its punchline rather than thinking one up.

6. Nadia says that's just relabeling: he's defined achievement as whatever the original did, so of course the original wins. And look where we've landed — whatever the difference is supposed to be, no amount of looking at the picture will reveal it, which is the part that bothered her about the whole literature to begin with.

Can you go through this move by move: where each of them is right, where each is overreaching or quietly switching the subject, and what (if anything) both of them are missing? I want a verdict on this exchange, not a survey of the forgery literature, and I genuienly don't know who's ahead.
