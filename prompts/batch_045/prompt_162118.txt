Me and my roommate got into it again about meritocracy last night and I ended up building an argument that I'm pretty sure proves way too much, but I cant see the joints. He's the one who thinks effort is what makes desert. So I said: imagine the effortometer — a gadget that measures how much genuine effort you actually put in (sustained concentration, exertion, all of it), and the firm pays strictly per unit of measured effort. No talent luck, no health luck, no luck about demand, no luck about which machine you got assigned to. Just effort, paid exactly. He says that scheme is fully just. Then:

(1) A pay scheme is just only to the extent that it pays people what they deserve.
(2) Desert can't be a matter of luck: if two people differ only in respects that are pure luck for both of them, they can't differ in what they deserve.
(3) How much effort someone is capable of, and how much they actually put in — conscientiousness, impulse control, how badly boredom hurts — is substantially a product of genes and early upbringing.
(4) Nobody picks their genes or their upbringing; both are pure luck.
(5) So the effortometer pays people for luck as well. Its not paying for desert at all.
(6) But theres nothing special about pay here. Run (2)–(5) on anything a person does: every intentional action comes out of a character and a set of dispositions that the agent didn't choose.
(7) So nothing anyone does is ever praiseworthy or blameworthy.
(8) So nobody deserves anything, so no pay scheme is more just than any other — the effortometer is no better than paying people by eye colour.
(9) That's the bit I refuse. Paying by eye colour is unjust, the effortometer obviously isn't, and a country where nurses can't make rent while heirs collect billions is unjust in a way that has nothing to do with effort.

If I have to choose I'd rather give up (1) than (2) — the claim that feels non-negotiable to me is that birth and genes are unfair — but he says I can keep (1) and (2) both and just stop at (7), which sounds like cheating. (He also says that even if your character is unchosen, you can still train your own conscientiousness, and that second-order effort is really yours — I have no idea where that move goes either.)

So: which step do you reject, why did that step look fine when I wrote it, and what does each of the alternatives cost me? Lead with the number of the step you'd give up in your first sentence, then spend the rest pricing the others. Everything in under 400 words.
