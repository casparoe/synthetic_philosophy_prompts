I convene a second-year undergraduate seminar of eighteen students on power, knowledge, and discipline, meeting for ninety minutes in a flat room with movable chairs and no screens. They have already worked through the contrast between power as something a sovereign possesses and forbids with, and power as something exercised rather than held, productive rather than repressive, running through practices of observation, record-keeping, ranking and avowal rather than descending from a centre. They have also met the thought that the individual is an effect of such practices rather than their raw material, and that the knowledge these practices generate and the ordering they impose are one operation rather than two. They can state all of this. What they cannot yet feel is the second half: that an apparatus of this kind needs no one in charge, that it teaches you a competence you then want to be good at, and that by the time you want to criticise it you have no vocabulary left that it did not supply.

Design me a single runnable exercise that produces that feeling in the room. My constraints:

The architecture. - In a first phase the group, apparently for a harmless administrative purpose, invents the categories under which something will be sorted and scored. In a later phase those same categories, which no one remembers authoring, are turned on the participants themselves. I want the norm to be visibly manufactured by the act of measurement rather than discovered by it - and manufactured by everyone, so that no one can point to a legislator.
- Observation must be delegated, not centralised: each participant is both watcher and watched, on rotation, and whether a report is actually filed must be unverifiable from below. Compliance should come from the possibility of being recorded, not from being recorded.
- The exercise must produce a skill. Participants should spend the session getting better at something - writing a legible self-account, applying a rubric, sorting a case - whose value collapses the moment the session ends, and at the debrief someone should be able to say, with mild embarrassment, that they wanted to do it well.
- For a sustained middle stretch - I would guess twenty minutes or more - the facilitator must be silent or out of the room. Whatever order obtains during that stretch has to be an order with no author present.

The deliverable. Materials (nothing beyond cards, paper, string, a timer, a wall); roles; a timeline with minute marks; a scoring and record-keeping scheme using no money and no real stakes; the wording of the two or three sentences of instruction the participants actually receive; the debrief; and an honest account of what the exercise cannot do.

The debrief. Map at least three datable moments in the run onto the three mechanisms the students have read about - observation arranged in a hierarchy, judgement that normalises, and the examination that combines them and makes cases out of persons. Then take on the hardest claim: that there is no position outside an apparatus from which to judge it. Say how the run can make that visible - the students should catch themselves appraising the exercise in the exercise's own terms - and be explicit about which part of that claim the room can only ever be told.

Failure modes. Give me at least four things that will predictably go wrong the first time, with a repair for each. I am especially worried about the session being read as a fable about authoritarianism or about digital surveillance, so tell me what to build into the fiction to foreclose both readings. I am also worried about early detection: if the trick is spotted in the first ten minutes the spell is gone, so say how to delay it.

Ethics and framing. Nothing may require a student to disclose anything personal: any self-account must be written about a fictional persona. During the run the facilitator may not use the words power, discipline, surveillance, norm or panopticon. Do not give me a reading list, a bibliography or citations - work only from the concepts. If it helps, invent a fictional institution for the students to be functionaries of.

Close with a one-paragraph statement of what a participant would conclude if they had to leave before the debrief, and a forty-five-minute variant for a room where the furniture cannot be moved. Please write in British English, in under a thousand words.
