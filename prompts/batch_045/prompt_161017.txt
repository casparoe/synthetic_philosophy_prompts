I sat in on a foundations-of-economics reading group last week where an economics PhD student and a philosophy postdoc argued past each other for about forty minutes about what utility is, and I have now been asked, unwisely, to referee. I would like you to do it instead. Both of them granted the following setup: for a finite body of choice data, a utility function fits the data only if the data satisfy the relevant consistency condition on choice, roughly that there are no cycles of the form *x chosen over y, y over z, and z over x*; and the ordering the data support survives any relabeling of the utility numbers that preserves their order. The exchange then went like this.

1. Marc (economics): The representation result is the whole story, in both directions. As a definition: to say someone prefers x to y is to say nothing more than that, were she offered the two together, she would take x. As a testable theory: the consistency condition is substantive, and real choosers violate it. Anything you want to say about utility that cannot be cashed out in possible choices is metaphysics, and specifically the useless kind — untestable in principle, and invisible in the only place anyone could ever look.

2. Dana (philosophy): Two complaints. First, your definition is either too small to be a definition or not a definition at all. If would choose means has chosen, then it is a record of the past and tells us nothing about a person in a new situation; if it means is disposed to choose, then you have helped yourself to a counterfactual disposition, and a disposition is exactly what I mean by a preference — it is not the behavior, it is what explains the behavior. Second, the representation result is close to vacuous as a theory of utility: take any consistent data and infinitely many utility functions fit them, differing only in how the numbers are stretched. A quantity defined only up to stretching measures nothing; it is the data renumbered. That is why people who actually care about utility — about how well someone's life is going — treat it as something with its own nature, of which choice is evidence.

3. Marc: Give me one observation that would refute a claim about the mental-state version. You cannot. Every case where choice and real utility come apart — weakness of will, a misread label, a menu that frames things badly, an addict's fix — gets absorbed by saying the choice did not reflect the agent's preferences. That is unfalsifiability by construction. And your preferred evidence, asking people, is just more behavior, and worse behavior: cheap, unincentivized, and chronically out of step with what people do. We at least use the data where the agent stakes something.

4. Dana: Choice is not behaviorally privileged either. Take Nadia, who pays fifty dollars for an app that blocks her from ordering delivery after nine. The unblocked menu contained everything the blocked one did: she could always have declined to order. So either paying to remove options is straightforwardly irrational, on the usual assumption that extra options cannot make a chooser worse off, or her choices from the larger menu were not expressions of her preferences — at those moments she was choosing against what she wanted. Notice what the natural explanation is: she expects herself to choose badly later. That is a claim about a gap between choosing and wanting, made by the agent herself. Your formalism can be stretched to fit it — define preferences over menus rather than outcomes, or posit two selves with two utility functions — but every stretch concedes that the original identification fails exactly where it matters.

5. Marc: The stretches are the theory working, not breaking. Preferences over menus are still preferences, revealed by choices among menus. Two selves are two choosers, each revealed by that chooser's own choices. And expects herself to choose badly is itself cashed out by the one hard behavioral fact in your story: she paid. Nothing mental did any work. You keep producing cases where the identification has to be made at the right level — the right menu, the right self, the right moment — and I keep saying: fine, make it at the right level. That is a research program, not a concession to hidden mental states.

6. Dana: Then here is a case where no choice of level saves you. Nadia again. Offered cake or fruit, she takes the cake every time. But she also pays two dollars for a standing arrangement under which, once she has ordered, the cake is swapped for the fruit; she renews it monthly and has never cancelled or tried to circumvent it. Both facts are behavior, both are incentivized, both are repeated. Her choice says cake over fruit. Her paid-for, renewed, never-revoked arrangement says fruit over cake. So the data underdetermine the preference, and you now need a principle for which choices count — the calm ones, the informed ones, the ones not made in the heat of the moment. Every candidate is a normative judgment about when someone's choices are to be trusted, and that judgment is precisely the content you said you had eliminated. Your view survives only by borrowing, at the moment of selection, the notion of what is good for her that it was meant to replace.

7. Marc: Two replies. First, which choices count may itself be a choice: ask her which situations she would rather be choosing in, and that is another menu, and the regress is behavioral all the way down. Second, even if it is not: what your case shows is that behavior alone does not settle what we should do for Nadia. That is a problem for welfare economics, not for the meaning of utility. I can keep utility as whatever the choice data are consistent with and let the policy question be settled separately, by whatever normative theory you like.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the seven moves give a rating — clearly right, mostly right, half right, mostly wrong, clearly wrong — and justify it in a few sentences: where a move is right, say precisely what it establishes and whether it establishes as much as its author takes it to; where it is wrong, say whether the mistake is fatal to that speaker's overall position or repairable within it. Then, separately: (a) which moves, if any, were never actually answered by the other side; (b) what both speakers are missing — distinctions neither drew, or a position neither considered; and (c) whether there is a single crux on which the whole dispute turns, and if so which speaker it favors. I have a vague suspicion that the argument keeps sliding between a claim about what preference means, a claim about what our evidence for preferences is, and a claim about what should count for policy, and that Marc and Dana may each be winning a different argument — but I cannot locate the slide, and I have to report back to the group, so I would like the reasoning explicit enough that I can defend it if either of them objects.
