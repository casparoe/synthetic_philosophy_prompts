I hope you won't mind my writing to you out of the blue like this. We haven't exchanged more than conference pleasantries in years, and I'm afraid I need a reader who is, inconveniently, both a friend and a moral philosopher. The situation is this. Nine days from now, on the same Saturday afternoon, three hours apart by train, two things I have promised are coming due, and I cannot do both. A year ago my daughter asked me to walk her down the aisle; our relationship has been thin for a long time, she has told me quite plainly that this is the one thing she needs from me, and that she will read my answer as the measure of whether I am going to be there for her at all. The other commitment is to serve as the external examiner at the defence of the last doctoral student of a mentor of mine who died eighteen months ago; I promised her, more or less at her bedside, that I would see the student through. I am the only person in the country who can examine that thesis, I am named on the paperwork, the date is fixed, and if I withdraw now the defence collapses and with it the student's funding and visa. There is no partial option: I cannot arrive late at either, and the defence cannot be moved to the morning. Both promises were given freely and knowingly; the clash is entirely my own fault, since the wedding date was in my diary when I signed the examiner's paperwork. I've spent the last fortnight trying to weigh the two, and the trouble is that every time I try I find myself quietly changing the units of comparison — a career against a relationship, an institutional undertaking against a personal one, a promise I made recently against a promise I made to someone who cannot release me from it. That last asymmetry is the thing I keep coming back to: I could ask my daughter to release me, and she might well say yes; my mentor cannot be asked. But I don't know whether that settles which one I should do, or only changes what I owe afterwards.

The people around me have been unanimous and unhelpful. My partner says: go to the defence, your daughter will get over it and you can make it up to her for years, whereas the student cannot be compensated at all, so plainly that's the one to keep. A colleague in my department says that one of the two must matter more, that my job is to work out which and then to stop feeling bad about the other, since bad feeling after you have done the best you could is just squeamishness dressed up as conscience. For what it's worth, that has more or less been my own position: I published a paper some twelve years ago arguing that conflicts of this shape always have a single right answer, that the sense of something left over afterwards is a piece of sentiment, and that a rational person who has done the best available thing owes nothing further to anyone and has nothing to reproach herself for. I still think the argument is valid. What I no longer trust is the premise it rests on, because I now notice that I expect, whichever way I choose, to go on owing something to the person I let down — not mere regret that things turned out this way, but a specific debt to a specific person, of the kind that an apology acknowledges and, as far as I can tell, never finishes paying off. In a draft I've been circling for a year I've tried to mark the difference with a distinction between 'closed' and 'open' remainders: a requirement that has simply been outweighed leaves nothing behind except perhaps an explanation, whereas a requirement that survives the choice leaves a debt that no subsequent act can discharge and can only be acknowledged; and my proposed test for telling the two apart is whether an apology is genuinely owed and whether resentment on the other side would be fitting. My suspicion, which is why I'm writing, is that the test is just a redescription of the feeling it was supposed to adjudicate.

So: what would you actually do, and why? More narrowly — should I ask my daughter to release me, and if she does, does that settle the matter or merely alter what remains between us? Is my partner right that compensability is the right thing to look at, and if not, what is? And whatever I end up doing, should I take the residue seriously as a sign that I will still owe the person I let down something that outlasts my having done the best I could — or is that exactly the sentiment my younger self said it was? If you think my published view is right, please tell me why it doesn't misfire here; if you think it is wrong, I'd be grateful if you could locate rather precisely the step at which it breaks. I can recite the dialectic on both sides myself, so what I want from you is not a survey but a verdict with its reasons attached.
