I help run the training day for new volunteer mentors at a youth mentoring charity in Manchester — nothing academic, just a room above a community centre, about eighteen adults ranging from retirees to people on a gap year, and I've got an eighty-minute slot after lunch when everyone is a bit sleepy. I've been reading on my own about the argument that what we do is mostly shaped by the situation we're in rather than by any stable character, and I got into it after a stupid argument with my brother at a family barbecue: he reckons you can tell what someone is really like by how they behave in a crisis, and I said that was rubbish but definately couldn't explain why. So I want to design an activity where the group runs into the idea rather than being told it. Please don't give me any references, reading lists or quotes from studies — I've got no library access and I'm not going to stand up and recite experiments at volunteers, so build the whole thing out of what happens in the room itself.

Here's the rough version I tried once, which half-worked and half-flopped, so build on it or throw it out. Beforehand everyone writes down privately whether they'd stop to help a stranger who'd dropped a pile of papers in a corridor, and also writes down one adjective for the sort of person who wouldn't stop. Then I hand out envelopes with a small errand to do around the building — take this form to the kitchen, ask at the office for a spare key, whatever — and the envelopes are subtly different: about a third say your one-to-one slot has been moved forward and you need to be quick, about a third have a nice pen or a biscuit tucked inside, the rest are plain. Two of my co-facilitators stage a small mishap in the corridor, someone dropping a folder and looking a bit lost. What I couldn't get right was the sequencing: people either twigged that the envelopes were different and started comparing them, or the whole thing felt so obviously arranged that everyone stopped out of politeness, which proves nothing. So I need rules, materials, who has to know what in advance, an exact timeline for eighty minutes, and ideally a version where the manipulation doesn't depend on anyone being fooled — maybe because they're told there is a manipulation but not which one, or because the interesting data is the gap between what people predicted and what they actually did.

For the debrief I want two things to land at once. First, that they'd just written down a character adjective for a hypothetical person who walks past, and that a decent number of them then did the walking past themselves because of one sentence on a piece of paper — I'd like them to notice that gap before I name it. Second, I want the strongest version of my brother's objection to get a fair hearing: that maybe the people who stopped are simply the people who always stop, and that one nudge in one corridor doesn't show that anybody lacks a character at all. So tell me how to run the debrief so the sceptics feel heard rather than ambushed (I've got a couple of quite grumpy experienced mentors who'll hate being messed with), how to map particular moments in the activity onto the different parts of the philosophical claim, what will predictably go wrong when I run it and how to handle each of those, and be honest about what the activity just can't show — I'd rather know now that it doesn't touch, say, whether someone is consistent across their whole life, or whether the kind of character worth having is a rare thing or a local, situation-by-situation thing, and then say that bit myself. Oh, and don't use the words virtue or situationist until the debrief. Write it in British English, and as continuous prose only — no headings, bullets, bold text or tables — because I'm pasting it straight into an email to my co-facilitator and she reads it on her phone.
