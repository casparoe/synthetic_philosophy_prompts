I got pulled into all this by the telescope headlines a few years back and I've been reading around on my own ever since. No philosophy background, no astronomy background, so sorry if I mangle some words. There's something about how the whole "search for life out there" business actually works that I can't tell is deep or just habit, and I want somebody to take it apart for me.

Here's what I mean. Say somebody thinks they've spotted a sign of life: two gases in the atmosphere of a planet forty light years off that shouldn't be able to sit there together, because one of them destroys the other, so something must be topping them up. That gets treated as a big deal. Headlines, "possible sign of life," years of papers arguing about whether some odd non-living chemistry could fake it. And remember this is a place we will never, ever visit — the entire case is a squiggle in a graph of starlight. A couple of these have been announced and then quietly got less exciting later.

Now say somebody thinks they've spotted a sign of intelligence: a narrow spike in a radio scan, or a star dimming in a pattern that doesn't match any natural thing we know. Completely different reaction. Interesting, sure, let's point the telescope back. And if it never does it again, that's basically the end of it: unexplained, not a detection. There's a famous one-off from the 1970s that never came back and it's still just a curiosity, and the same thing happened with a weird star a few years ago — once somebody could tell a boring story involving dust, the megastructure talk evaporated.

So what does that gap in standards actually presuppose? Here are the readings I keep going back and forth between, and I can't decide which one the practice really needs:

- That intelligence is a more extraordinary claim than life, full stop. But why? Because it's a further and chancier step after life gets going? Because we'd react very differently to the news, so being wrong costs more? Or just institutional scar tissue from a long run of false alarms?
- That it's not about extraordinariness at all, but about how many mundane mimics there are. A satellite or a bad receiver can fake a signal; maybe nothing non-living genuinely can fake those two gases together. In which case the asymmetry is really about background rates of confounders and has nothing specially to do with minds.
- That it's about whether you can check. You can re-observe a patch of sky; you can't re-run a distant planet's atmosphere or go look at it. So maybe repeatability is doing the real work and mind-versus-life is a red herring.

Two made-up cases that I think separate these, and I'd like you to run them:

One. We pick up a single burst, thirty seconds, never repeats, from 400 light years away, and what's in the burst is unmistakably artificial content — say the prime numbers in binary, or a simple picture. Zero ambiguity about the source, zero chance of a second look. Does the practice have to call that "not a detection"? If it does, what does that tell us about whether repeatability is the real principle or just a stand-in for something else?

Two. Same distant planet as before, same kind of evidence — one spectral squiggle, no visit possible — but this time the gas we think we see is the kind of industrial chemical that on Earth exists only because we manufacture it, like a refrigerant. It's methodologically identical to the two-gases case, but it implies minds. Does the field now suddenly owe it the stricter standard, on the same evidence? If yes, what does that reveal about where the asymmetry is really living?

And one more thing that makes me think it isn't all epistemology. We bake spacecraft in clean rooms and worry constantly about stowing away Earth bacteria on Mars, and if we ever did find even the tiniest smear of life there, I gather the place would be treated almost like a protected site and everything downstream would change. So we act as though that smear matters enormously — while on Earth nobody thinks bacteria have any moral standing whatsoever. What's doing the work there: rarity, the fact that it'd be a second independent origin of life, what we could learn from it, the fact that we haven't ruined it yet? Does the protecting practice actually need any of those, or is it just caution wearing a costume?

What I want back: which of these the practice strictly presupposes versus which ones it merely sits comfortably with, whether the different bits of the practice presuppose consistent things, and what would concretely have to change in how the field works if the real justification turned out to be a different one from the one people give out loud. And if you replaced the whole thing with a straight "what does it cost us to be wrong each way" view, where would the bar end up — would it still be tilted the same direction? Also: is our own trajectory relevant? We leak less and less narrowband radio every decade, so the standard we use seems calibrated to a kind of civilisation that maybe doesn't exist, us included.
