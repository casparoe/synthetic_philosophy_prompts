I've been teaching Du Bois's 1926 essay Criteria of Negro Art — the one where he insists that all art is propaganda and always must be, but that propaganda which isn't also beautiful is worthless and does the cause no good — and I want to run it against a real case: Marian Anderson's Easter Sunday concert on the Lincoln Memorial steps. Take the facts as I give them, so we don't argue about them: the Daughters of the American Revolution had refused her Constitution Hall because she was Black, Eleanor Roosevelt resigned from the DAR over it, the Roosevelt administration helped arrange an open-air concert instead, and on April 9, 1939 she sang to something like 75,000 people on the Mall with millions more on the radio; the program was classical arias and spirituals, it closed with a patriotic hymn, and by her own account she did not see herself as staging a protest and wasn't the one who organized it.

Analyze that concert as propaganda on Du Bois's terms, and please use my distinction rather than inventing your own: (a) carrier propaganda, where the message rides in the content of the work itself, (b) occasion propaganda, where the argument is made by the setting and the audience, the who-and-where of the perfomance, rather than by what gets performed, and (c) exemplar propaganda, where the mere existence of a Black artist working at that level is meant to refute the inferiority claim, so the work functions as evidence rather than as persuasion. Which of the three does Du Bois's own practice actually license — the pageants, the editing of The Crisis, his taste in song and poetry — and which would he have rejected as accommodation to white taste, given how hard he came down on Harlem Renaissance writers he thought were flattering that taste? Most of all: does his beauty requirement attach to the work, to the staging, or to neither? Then turn it around and say what the episode does to the theory — whether it confirms it, or shows that once occasion counts, all art is propaganda collapses into near-vacuity (any performance by a Black artist in a segregationist capital is propaganda no matter what gets sung), and that the live question was never whether it was propaganda but what sort of work occasion-propaganda can do. Be honest about where the framework is silent; I'd rather you name a gap than stretch Du Bois to fill it.
