I'm an injury epidemiologist — most of my working life is spent writing case definitions, arguing about denominators, separating incidence from case fatality, worrying about notification completeness and coding drift between ICD revisions, and persuading colleagues that a rate computed over an unexposed denominator is not a rate at all. I've been pulled into a reading group on the claim that violence has declined over the centuries, and the participants seem to me to be talking past one another in ways that look a lot like measurement disputes — but I don't trust that appearance, and I'd like a translation rather than a summary. So: restate the declinist thesis and its main critics as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use, and then give me the residue report. I'm not asking you to adjudicate the empirical record or to cite anything; I want the conceptual structure rendered in my terms.

Concretely, here is the vocabulary I'd like you to work with, and a few ideas of my own that I'd like you to take seriously rather than humour. Take "how much violence was there in the world at time t" as a putative measurand, and treat the dispute over what counts as violence as a case-definition problem: a definition keyed to intentional interpersonal physical force applied to a body will capture homicide and assault, will mishandle the large "undetermined intent" residual that swells wherever death registration is poor, will exclude self-directed and collective violence unless explicitly added, and will exclude entirely the things some theorists call structural — preventable death from deprivation, famine, or neglect of civilian infrastructure, where there is no identifiable agent acting on a specific body at a specific time. Take per-capita versus absolute as a denominator problem, but push it: violence requires co-presence, so person-years alive is a partly unexposed denominator, and I want you to consider a risk-set alternative — person-hours spent within striking distance of non-household others, which modernity has inflated enormously through density, mobility and urbanization — and say what happens to the trend when that is the denominator, and whether the choice between the two is a measurement decision or an evaluative one. Note also that a rising absolute count with a falling rate is arithmetically unremarkable once the denominator grows, that "how big is the decline" has no answer until one fixes a scale (rate ratios and rate differences can rank two periods differently, since a fixed proportional fall corresponds to wildly different absolute reductions), and that comparisons across centuries are comparisons across instruments of unknown and varying sensitivity — the epidemiologist's instinct is to demand a validation study against a gold standard, and here there is none, which is why I'd also like you to consider replacing direct counts with excess mortality over a demographic baseline, as one does for conflicts and pandemics where registration fails. Then there are two further distinctions I think do real work: lethal violence is a product of attack incidence and case fatality, so a falling homicide rate can be entirely a trauma-care and weapon-lethality story rather than a reduction in violent encounters; and wars are clustered mass-casualty events, more like outbreaks than like endemic background risk, so individual-level rate models with a world-population denominator mis-specify the exposure almost by construction.

What I want most, though, is what doesn't survive. Please be explicit about which claims translate cleanly into measurement talk, which translate only with distortion (I suspect the question "do deaths capture what matters about violence?" is not a case-definition question at all, since severity weighting and disability weights are themselves value judgments in numerical costume), and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary in any form — I have in mind the meta-question of whether there is a single quantity there to be measured at all, and the moral or teleological claim that the world is getting better in a sense that matters, neither of which my framework has a slot for, because epidemiology is built to take the disease entity as given and then validate instruments against it. For each item of residue, tell me whether you think it marks a defect of my framework — its dependence on a fixed measurand, its silence about what makes an outcome worth counting — or a sign that the original claim was less determinate than the confidence of either side suggests. And if the ratio-versus-difference and denominator choices turn out to be doing all the work in some strand of the debate, say so plainly: I would rather be told that a famous disagreement partly dissolves once both parties pick a scale than be given a balanced survey that leaves me unable to tell measurement from morality.
