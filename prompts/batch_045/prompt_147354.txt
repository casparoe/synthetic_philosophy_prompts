I spent thirty-eight years as an analytical chemist, most of them on instrument calibration and inter-laboratory comparison, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university. We have just finished two sessions on moral psychology — the work associated with Jonathan Haidt and the empirical claim that people on the left draw mainly on concerns about harm and fairness while people on the right also weight loyalty, authority and purity (with a liberty foundation sometimes added) — and the group split, as I gather it always does, over whether any of that tells us anything about who is actually right. I volunteered to write the handout for the next meeting, and I have concluded that another summary of the debate would be useless to us; what I want instead is a classification instrument. Please build me a decision tree of eight to ten branch nodes that takes a scientifically literate agnostic from verdicts on concrete cases to a named position on that meta-question, with a scoring key, plus a short map of the logical space showing which combinations of answers are unoccupied and why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored).

The cases matter more to me than the labels, so please use these, or near variants of them: a firm that closes a plant in a town where it has employed four generations of the same families, compensating every worker at more than their expected lifetime earnings while the town's social fabric collapses anyway; a person who, entirely in private and without damaging anything, uses a national flag as a drop cloth and then burns it; a hospital that replaces triage decisions made by senior consultants with a validated algorithm that beats them on every fairness metric the hospital can measure, after which staff and patients report a loss they cannot name; a survey run in another country in which harm items and purity items load on a single factor and the left–right difference in scores disappears; and, if you think it belongs, the case of a language model trained on ordinary moral talk that reproduces the same factor structure while offering only harm-and-fairness justifications for every verdict it scores. No node may ask which foundations the reader endorses, which political camp they favor, or whether they think morality is objective — the point is to sort people by what they say about cases. The leaves I need are: the symmetric debunking view (none of it survives), the asymmetric debunking view (the extra foundations are by-products, so right-leaning morality loses more than left-leaning morality does), the pluralist view that morality is thicker than harm and each side is half-blind, a perceptualist view on which the foundations are modular capacities calibrated to a local social ecology, a view on which the full human complement of foundations is the well-functioning moral sense (so the psychology does vindicate something, uncomfortably), a measurement-skeptical view that dissolves the question as premature, and a strict view that the psychology is simply irrelevant to normative questions. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and tell me where you would put a position I have been toying with and calling the convergence-floor view: the idea that the two profiles converge as societies grow larger, more anonymous and more affluent, so the findings predict convergence rather than vindicating either side, and that this is or is not distinguishable from the perceptualist view. My own instinct as a chemist is that the tree should open on a question about measurement — whether the factor structure would survive a change of instrument and a change of population — but I leave the structure to you, and I would rather you started where the disagreement is sharpest. One constraint from our group: several of us have no library access, so please do not point us toward papers, books or reading lists, and reason from the cases themselves. Please write the answer in American English.
