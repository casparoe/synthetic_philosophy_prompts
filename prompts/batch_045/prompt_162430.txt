I am writing a short paper on the dialectic between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption, and I want to argue that the presumptuous-philosopher label is attached to the wrong theory. The standard story has the self-indication assumption playing the presumptuous part: the philosopher who retires to an armchair and announces that the cosmos must teem with civilizations. My thesis is that the self-sampling assumption is the one that licenses large, armchair revisions of contingent empirical belief from no evidence at all, and that its reputation for modesty is an artifact of the cases people choose. What I need is one clean case to open with: a case in which the self-sampling assumption delivers the extravagant verdict and the self-indication assumption the modest one, and in which the extravagance cannot be dismissed as the Doomsday Argument in a new costume.

Please construct such a case. Take SSA to be the rule that one should reason as if one were a random sample from a reference class of observers, so that a hypothesis is favoured to the extent that a large fraction of the observers it posits have evidence like yours. Take SIA to be the further assumption that your existence is itself evidence, favouring hypotheses that posit more observers, so that conjoined with the sampling step hypotheses end up weighted by how many observers have evidence like yours, irrespective of how many observers there are besides. State the convention you are working with, and flag briefly what changes if you regard SIA as a rival to the sampling step rather than an addition to it.

The specifications:

1. The two hypotheses must agree about every fact concerning the reasoner's own community, its past, its present size and its future, and must differ only about a population that exists at the same time but that the reasoner cannot currently observe. The reasoner's evidence must contain nothing about their position in a sequence: no birth rank, no premise that they or their civilization are early or late, nothing about how long their community will last.

2. The number of observers who share the reasoner's evidence must be the same on both hypotheses, so that any divergence between the theories is produced by observers who do not share that evidence and by nothing else.

3. SSA must move the odds by at least three orders of magnitude relative to SIA, and in the direction in which SSA is the theory making the large revision and SIA the one leaving the priors essentially untouched.

4. The priors must be stated explicitly and must be ones a reasonable non-anthropic inquirer would accept on the empirical question alone, roughly even odds; the anthropic step, not the priors, has to carry the weight.

5. The divergence must surface in a decision the community has to take before the question is settled by observation: something they must fund, launch, withhold or announce now, on the strength of the anthropic verdict, with the settling observation still in the future and obtainable in the story. It is not enough for the two theories to disagree about a number nobody acts on.

6. No infinities, no merely possible observers, no simulations or ancestor-simulations, no exact duplicates of the reasoner, no minds of disputed observer status, no contested physics or cosmological measure. The reference class must be one both sides of the debate would grant without argument, ideally because the population whose existence is at issue is continuous, biologically and culturally, with the reasoner's own.

7. Keep the scenario to a paragraph or two and set it somewhere recognizable: a committee room, a mission-control room, a department meeting. Do not stipulate what the theories say; derive it.

After the scenario, do the check. State the reference class and the priors, derive each theory's verdict and by what factor, and identify the single structural feature doing the work. Then say what happens to both verdicts if that feature is removed or inverted, for instance if the unobserved observers also had evidence like the reasoner's, or if the reference class were narrowed to the reasoner's own community. Give me the strongest objection an advocate of whichever theory comes out worse would make, and say whether the objection lands on the case or on the theory itself. Finally, and this is the part I most need, tell me honestly whether the case actually supports my thesis or whether a defender of the standard labelling can absorb it with a distinction I have not anticipated, because I would much rather hear that from you than from a referee.

Write the whole thing, scenario and check together, as one continuous piece of prose, the way you would write an email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
