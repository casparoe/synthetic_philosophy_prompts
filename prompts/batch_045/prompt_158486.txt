I've got fourty minutes in front of the working group that's drafting a new bill of rights for our little country, which has never had a court that can strike down a statute. The room will be: two MPs from the governing coalition who basically think this whole thing is a power grab by robed lawyers, one opposition MP who loves rights but used to be a union organiser and distrusts anything that smells like elite procedure, two civil society people who think rights without a remedy are wallpaper, a retired ombudsperson who worries about cost and delay for ordinary applicants, and one comparative public lawyer who has read the whole dialogue literature and will be quietly furious if I don't engage with it.

The thesis I'm defending, sketch version. I want to propose what I've been calling the two-key override. A court that finds a statute incompatible with the rights catalogue does not void it. Instead the statute keeps applying, but any person who shows they would be personally harmed by it gets an exemption from it, and the legislature gets one full session either to let the provision lapse or to re-enact it with a written statement of which right they take to be at stake and why the infringement is worth it. If they re-enact with reasons, the court may look again, but only at whether those reasons are ones a reasonable legislature could sincerely hold, not at whether the court would have weighed the interests the same way. Underneath that is a framing move I care about: the standard counter-majoritarian argument compares a court and a legislature at one instant, and I want to swap it for what I call the persistence asymmetry, the thought that what actually matters is which institution's mistakes get corrected soonest, since a bad statute can be fixed at the next election while a bad constitutional ruling just sits there until somebody manages to overrule it.
