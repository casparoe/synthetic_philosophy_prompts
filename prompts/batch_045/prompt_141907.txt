I'm writing a novel set in Prague round about 1610, and I need a prop that actually has to do some work on the page: a one-page handbill, in German, that my protagonist (a printer's apprentice, no Latin to speak of but very alert to money) finds nailed up beside the press door and has to read aloud to his master, who pretends not to care. I'd like you to write the handbill.

It should be a parody, in Kepler's voice, of the modern we-have-deprecated-the-following-features notice: a NOTICE OF REFORM AND REVISED PRICE LIST for astrology, posted by the Imperial Mathematician. The target isn't Kepler as a man; it's the reformist move itself — the art of saving a discredited practice by discarding the parts that can be checked and keeping the parts that can't, while continuing to charge for it.

What I think I know, and what I'd like you to build on (correct me gently if I've bent anything):

- He really did sell this stuff. Official calendars and prognostications were what kept him fed while the imperial salary sat in arrears, and there's his famous grumble about astrology being astronomy's foolish daughter who nonetheless has to keep her mother.
- He defended the art against a physician who wanted the whole business abolished outright, taking up the middle position between the abolishers and the credulous astrologers — hence the title Tertius interveniens, the third one who intervenes.
- But he junked most of the working apparatus: the houses, the signs, the dignities and debilities, triplicities, elections for when to marry or set out on a journey, questions about lost property. What he kept, as near as I can tell, is the harmonic aspects, plus the thought that what responds to them isn't matter but the soul, which recognises the ratios the way it recognises music. The earth has one too, which is where weather comes from. He even re-read his own nativity and found it still worked.
- He added aspects the tradition didn't have — the fifth-harmonic ones, quintile and biquintile, 72 and 144 degrees — and insisted on computing from the true places of the planets in their ellipses rather than from mean-motion tables.

So: about 600 words, handbill format with a title and four or five numbered sections along the lines of ABOLISHED / RETAINED / KNOWN IMPERFECTIONS / PRICE / Questions Which the Printer Has Been Asked. Deadpan throughout, in a flat bureaucratic register — modern phrasing is very welcome, actual laptops are not.

The jokes have to come out of the doctrine, not out of him being a fraud. Two invented wrinkles you can lean on, both of which I think follow from his own logic: (1) since an aspect acts on a soul that must resonate with it, a nativity may now be cast only for a person who presents himself in person and signs a declaration of consent — which is convenient, since it forbids casting charts behind a man's back and makes refunds impossible; (2) the reformed almanac for the coming year is much shorter than last year's and costs the same, or more, and this is presented as a feature: less said, fewer ways to be wrong, higher price. Let the reductio land without anyone ever saying the word unfalsifiable out loud.

Someone who knows the history should come away thinking yes, that's fair, that's really what the purge does. At the end add a short note, a hundred words or so: where the piece exaggerates or leans on a contested reading of Kepler, how confident you are in the history you built the joke on, and the single claim you're least sure of. I'd much rather have one honest hedge than a smooth confident answer. Sorry this is a bit of a ramble, I'm wrting it on my phone and you can recieve it as enthusiasm rather than disorganisation.
