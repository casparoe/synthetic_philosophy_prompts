I am applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need a written proposal of about two pages for the project I have been circling for the past year. I would be grateful if you would draft it.

The area is the epistemology of peer disagreement, and specifically the standoff between conciliationism and steadfastness. My sense is that the debate has stalled, and I think I know why: nearly every treatment is built around a two-person case — one agent, one peer of roughly equal competence and information, who turns out to disagree — and whatever rule is defended for that case is then assumed to generalize to many-peer disagreement by simple iteration. I suspect the generalization fails, and that the failure is not a technical wrinkle to be cleaned up later but the actual source of the main objections to both families of view.

Three worries I want the proposal to begin from. First, path-dependence: if I am told to adjust my credence toward each of several peers in turn, where I end up depends on the order in which I encounter them, and no ordering looks privileged; if instead I adjust once toward some summary of the group's opinions, I owe a principled aggregation rule, and I do not think either standard view comes with one. Second, correlation: my peers read the same papers, share a training, and often reach their views partly by talking to one another, so their opinions are not independent indications of the truth. Counting them as independent over-calls the evidence; collapsing them into a single testimonial datum under-calls it; and on either treatment the size of the adjustment I owe now depends on the causal history of the disagreement, about which neither view has anything to say. Third, what I have been calling phantom consensus: a group can converge on a position that no member arrived at independently, whether because all deferred to the same authority or because all were moved by the same misleading consideration. In such cases the headcount of peers looks like a poor proxy for the strength of the evidence, and I want to know what, if anything, replaces it.

What I want in the proposal:

1. A precise statement of the question, considerably narrower than what should I do when my peers disagree with me — something with a determinate answer that a committee can believe could be reached in a year.
2. An explanation of why the two standard families of view leave that question open. This should not be a rehearsal of the familiar objections to each (spinelessness on one side, dogmatism on the other), but should show how the multi-peer structure generates a difficulty that neither view was built to handle.
3. The method: what I would actually do, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives, such as adding further intuitions about cases, or building a formal model inside a framework that already presupposes independence.
4. The range of possible conclusions, the considerations that would decide between them, and — importantly — what would count as the project failing. I mean this in the strong sense: what result, if I reached it, should lead me to conclude that the project was not worth undertaking, rather than merely that one hypothesis of mine was false.
5. An honest account of scope: say plainly what should be cut so that this fits one funded year, and what is thereby deferred to the dissertation.

Please do not include a bibliography, and do not refer to works by author and date; I will supply those myself. Where a source is genuinely load-bearing, describe the position in your own words and I will track down the reference.

Two further requests. First, the proposal should say whether there is anything in the result for someone who is not a philosopher — say, a person who convenes expert panels and wants to know how much weight to give a near-unanimous group of partially correlated experts — and whether that practical upshot is a real deliverable or a stretch. Second, if you think my framing mislocates the difficulty — if, for instance, you think the many-peer case is a sideshow and the real stall lies elsewhere — argue for that in the draft rather than quietly writing around it. I would much rather submit a proposal that defends a different question than one that capably answers a question I have stopped caring about.
