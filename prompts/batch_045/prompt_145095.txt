Writing up a chapter on bounded rationality at the moment (third year, philosophy, mostly on Simon and the optimal stopping literature) and I've realsied I've got a pile of verdicts about when satisficing is in good standing and when it isn't, and I have no idea what principle they add up to. My supervisor says it's all just expected-utility reasoning once you price in the cost of deliberation, and I think that's too quick, but I can't say why. So — cases first.

1. Flat hunting. Four viewings across a weekend, had to be out of my old place by the Monday, took the fourth, it cleared everything I actually cared about. Fine. Anyone who says I should have seen forty is being silly.

2. Same person, same four viewings, same flat — except no deadline, a quiet market, another fortnight available, and each viewing costs me an evening. I stop at four anyway. Not fine. Feels lazy rather than reasonable. Note this is the same stopping behaviour as case 1, so if 1 is fine and 2 isn't, the difference has to be in the situation, not in what I did.

3. Two viewings, each of which cost me a day's pay in travel and lost work, stopped at the second. Fine.

4. Two weeks of failing, and I moved my own bar down: dropped the balcony requirement, accepted 35 minutes instead of 20. Fine. I don't think that's self-deception — my bar was just wrong about what the market contains, and the evidence corrected it.

5. My friend does the reverse: finds a place above her bar on day two, then raises the bar, because now she knows places like that exist. I want to say that's not fine. But by symmetry with 4 it should be fine, and I can't say what breaks the symmetry — I can't appeal to the searching she's already done, that's sunk.

6. Groceries. I satisfice on basically every item, every week, indefinitely. Fine, obviously fine.

7. Twelve sub-decisions on a conference I helped organise — venue, catering, dates, invite list, and so on. Satisficed on each one separately. The result is a conference I would have rejected flat if I'd been shown it as a package. Not fine. But this is the same pattern as 6, and I want to keep 6. My only story is that I set the bar on the wrong unit — per item rather than on the whole thing — but that feels like a get-out: satisficing survives every failure by saying you picked the wrong unit, and then it's unfalsifiable.

8. My GP, choosing between two treatments for something of mine that isn't urgent, stops at the first one that clears the guideline threshold instead of reading up. Fine. That's what guidelines are for.

9. My insurer's algorithm does the same thing at the same threshold, and honestly it's better calibrated than he is — it stops at a point that tracks the marginal costs of finding out more better than my GP does. Not fine. Though I don't want to say it's irrational exactly; I want to say it's illegitimate, or that they're someone else's reasons. But if rationality is a matter of the shape of the stopping behaviour relative to the costs, 8 and 9 are the same case, and I've smuggled in something about authority.

10. A seller who knows I satisfice shows me the just-above-bar option first. I take it. The better one was one click away. I don't want to call that irrational — I want to say I was had. Now run it as a sequence: twenty small 'upgrades', each of which I accept, leaving me worse off than I started. That one I do want to call irrational. Same mechanism, same stopping behaviour, opposite verdicts.

11. Coffee in a new city. I went back to the first place that cleared my bar every morning for a fortnight instead of trying the others. Mistake — but not the same mistake as case 2. In 11 the thing I gave up was information, because I'd be choosing again tomorrow. So is that exploration-versus-exploitation rather than satisficing? But then why doesn't the same point apply to the flat, where I'll also be choosing again in a few years?

12. The one I keep going back and forth on. A learned policy that stops searching when some opaque internal signal fires — no represented threshold, nothing you could point to as its bar, no articulable reason for stopping where it does — but its stopping behaviour is well calibrated to the actual costs and benefits of further search, better calibrated than mine. I want to call that satisficing, in good standing. A human who stops looking because they've had enough, with no articulable reason either, whose stopping happens to be equally well calibrated: I want to call that exhaustion, or weakness of will, not satisficing. What's the difference, apart from one of them being software? And if there's no difference, which of my verdicts goes?

13. One more, at the meta-level: I spent about ninety seconds deciding what my bar was, then stopped, on the grounds that fine-tuning the bar was worth less than just getting on with it. I think that's fine too. I'm not sure any principle of the shape I've got can permit it.

My current stab, which I don't much like: satisficing is in good standing when (a) the bar is set on the thing you actually have reason to care about, and (b) you stop at roughly the point where the marginal cost of continuing equals the marginal expected gain. That gets 1, 3, 4, 6, 8; it kills 2; and I think it gets 11 and 13 once you count the value of information and the cost of setting the bar as part of the marginal calculation. But it says nothing that distinguishes 8 from 9, it looks like it permits 5 (which I want to rule out), and on 7 it just tells me I picked the wrong unit, which I've said is too easy.

So: is there a principle that gets these, or nearly all of them? I'd like it stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, and — importantly — a prediction about at least one case I haven't listed, ideally the case where your principle and my gut come apart most. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts I'd have to drop to get a clean principle, and which verdict it would be cheapest to drop. And settle this if you can: is my supervisor right that this is all just optimisation once deliberation is priced in, or is there something in the pattern above — 8 and 9 especially, and 12 — that a pure cost-benefit story can't recover?
