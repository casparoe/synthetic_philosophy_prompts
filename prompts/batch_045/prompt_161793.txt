I'm a PhD student in maths — analytic number theory mostly, though I've drifted into a computable-analysis side project — and I'm writing a short "foundational commitments" paragraph for the intro of that paper. My supervisor read a draft and said it sounds like I'm trying to have it every which way. He's probably right, but I can't tell which bit to drop. I've read the basic constructivism stuff (Heyting, Dummett, a bit of Troelstra), so please don't explain intuitionism to me from scratch.

Roughly where I've landed:

1. Existence means constructibility, BHK style. To assert ∃x I need to be able to produce x, in principle computably. So excluded middle isn't generally OK over infinite domains, and intuitionistic logic is the right logic there.

2. The powerset of N isn't a determinate completed totality. "All subsets of N" and "all reals" are indefinitely extensible, so I'm happy saying there's no fact of the matter about CH and that leaving it independent is the correct outcome rather than a temporary embarrassment.

3. But the natural numbers themsevles are a determinate structure. Goldbach, twin primes, Con(PA): all determinately true or false, and if one of them turned out independent of PA or ZFC that would just show the axioms are weak.

4. Church–Turing as a mathematical principle, not an empirical one: every function N→N is computable. I also accept Markov's principle. I like the recursive counterexamples — bounded increasing computable sequences of rationals with no computable limit, computable functions with a sign change but no computable zero — and I think they're real mathematics about the continuum, not pathologies to be waved away.

5. I also like the Brouwerian picture: choice sequences, the continuum as something not already decomposed into discrete points, "all real functions are continuous" as a guiding intuition rather than a bug.

6. In my actual number theory I use classical analysis wholesale — contour shifts, compactness arguments, Zorn's lemma, non-constructive existence — and I don't intend to stop. More than that: I think those theorems are true, not merely formally derivable, and I'm happy asserting that there exists a non-measurable set in a classical context while also saying that choice doesn't hand you anything constructible.

7. Two things I'm confident are NOT in tension: using proof by contradiction when the conclusion is negative, and using excluded middle freely when the predicate is decidable or the domain finite. Those feel like free lunches.

So: which pairs actually pull against each other, which only look like they do (including among my free lunches, if I'm wrong about those), and please rank by severity rather than flagging all of them as equally bad. For each real tension say what would have to give and what it would cost me — and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I don't mind which one you tell me to drop.
