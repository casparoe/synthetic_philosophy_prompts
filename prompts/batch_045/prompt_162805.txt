Assume I know the standard setup (compossibility, the principle of the best, metaphysical perfection, the usual mockery) and that I dont need it explained to me.

Here's the position I hold, or am very close to holding. It's Leibnizian optimism in a fairly specific form, and I'm stating it my own way on purpose:

1. The choice is over maximal compossible wholes — complete ways a world could be — not over creatures, events or local features that can be adjusted one at a time. You can't improve a world by editing it.
2. What makes a world better is not total happiness added up. It's something like metaphysical perfection conceived as a rate: how much variety of phenomena gets realized per unit of simplicity in the laws. Richness divided by law-complexity.
3. The actual world is the maximum of that ordering. Not the most pleasant world, not a world without suffering — the best package deal.
4. Every apparent evil is local and structurally tied to a greater good within the same whole. Nothing in the world is genuinely wasted or gratuitous.
5. And here's a wrinkle Leibniz didn't face: suppose the world is infinitely large, with infinitely many finite minds spread across it. Then total value isn't a sum you can maximize at all; the only comparison left is limiting densities or averages per region. I want optimism run in that setting too, not quietly dropped.

I'm not asking for textual exegesis. I know there's a lot of argument about what Leibniz himself meant and I don't care right now — I want the view as I've stated it stress-tested.
