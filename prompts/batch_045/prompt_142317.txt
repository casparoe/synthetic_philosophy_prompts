I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of logic, and I'm writing the final: one in-class essay question, 45 minutes, 20 marks, closed book. I've been arguing about this material in blog comment sections and on forums for about fifteen years, so I have an unusually good sense of what the cached answers look like — and I want a question that they don't answer.

The topic is Lewis Carroll's 1895 Mind dialogue in which the Tortoise grants the premises of a one-step modus ponens and still refuses the conclusion unless a further conditional is added to the premise list, and then a further one, indefinitely. The summary every student arrives with is some version of: a rule of inference is not a premise; the Tortoise confuses using a rule with mentioning it; any argument for a rule either uses that rule or another one; so deduction cannot be justified non-circularly. I've written that summary myself more than once, and it is the sort of thing that feels like understanding without being understanding. I want a question where I can tell from a script whether the student followed the argument or retrieved the abstract — a real crux, not a recall test with philosophical vocabulary attached.

Two angles I've considered; take either, combine them, or replace both with something better, but say what you chose and why.

The first is about what kind of thing the added premise is supposed to be. In the dialogue the conditionals are truth-functional: they say that if the earlier premises are true, then Z is true, and the Tortoise can grant every one of them and still not move. Suppose instead we read the added premise as licensing rather than describing — as saying that a reasoner in that position may, or must, move to Z. That looks like it stops the regress, because accepting such a premise is at least very close to being disposed to make the transition. The interesting part is the bill that comes due. On that reading the basic laws of logic look normative rather than descriptive, and then the question is whether the norm is constitutive of believing or meaning anything at all — in which case deductive failure starts to look impossible, or at any rate undescribable — or merely regulative, in which case the Tortoise is perfectly describable as violating a norm, and we still owe an account of what his irrationality consists in that isn't just a restatement of the rule he violates. I'd like the question to push students into that dilemma and make them pay on one side of it.

The second angle is about justification rather than premises. Even granting the standard moral, someone can insist: fine, the rule need not be a premise, but nobody is justified in moving from P and P→Q to Q unless they are justified in believing that the rule preserves truth. Is that the same mistake again, or a distinct and defensible demand? If it is distinct, what shape could a non-circular answer take — an inference to the best explanation, a reliability or entitlement story, a constitutive argument — and what exactly does each of those fail to deliver? The trap I want closed is the student who announces that using logic to defend logic is circular and therefore deduction is unjustified, and thinks the conversation is over.

Requirements for the deliverable:

The question itself, written as it would appear on the paper. It should be self-contained: state in the question any distinctions the student is meant to use, rather than expecting them to guess which frame I have in mind. It must not depend on knowing who argued what — describe positions and arguments, never by author or paper title; I'll circulate the reading list separately and I don't want anyone rewarded for name-dropping. It should also not turn into a question about whether classical logic is the correct logic, or whether logic is revisable; that's a different seminar.

A model answer of roughly 600 to 800 words.

A marking scheme out of 20, built around four or five must-hit moves with marks attached, plus a one-line note for each on how candidates typically get it wrong. Say explicitly what separates an A answer from a B answer — the specific move or distinction the B omits — and what a C looks like.

A short note on what a fluent, low-comprehension answer to this particular question looks like, so I can recognise it in the stack and not reward it.

Finally, the part I care about almost as much as the question: tell me where your model answer is doing the most work by fiat. Give me the strongest answer that a genuinely good student could write which rejects your model answer's bottom line, and say what would change your mind about the crux you picked — which argument, or which pattern in the scripts if I've run this exam before, would convince you that the question rewards verbal fluency rather than understanding.
