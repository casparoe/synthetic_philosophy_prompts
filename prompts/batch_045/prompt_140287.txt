Hey — I need a document, not an essay. Two friends of mine have been going at it for about a year over the classic utility monster case: the being that gets vastly more welfare out of every unit of resource than anyone else, so that any view which adds welfare accross lives seems committed to saying dump everything on it and let the rest of us scramble. One of them is an aggregationist: thinks the monster is not a reductio at all, that the real content is in the assumptions about interpersonal comparison and about how steep a single being's welfare-per-resource curve is allowed to be, and that once you're honest about those the monster's demands turn out to be real but modest. The other holds a separateness-of-persons view: thinks the case is a genuine reductio of welfare aggregation, and that we should move to something like a contractualist justifiability-to-each-individual view, or a sufficiency view where what matters is people getting above some threshold. They've finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration and I've somehow ended up as the one who has to draft the joint statement. Please write it.

What I want in it:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. Not filler like "both sides care about wellbeing" — claims that actually do work: about what the case is set up to show; about whether the monster is being used modally (for any such theory, some possible being) or empirically (some real being might be like this); about what happens to the verdict under prioritarianism or a sufficiency view.

2. Some distinctions I've invented — rename them if you like, but keep all four, and use them:
- the greedy monster: steep welfare-per-resource curve plus a ceiling that never saturates, so it can absorb everything.
- the deep monster: sits at a very low baseline with enormous gains available at the bottom. This is the one that should worry the prioritarian, since the greedy monster is already high up the scale and prioritarianism discounts its marginal gains anyway.
- the wide monster: not one mind but a huge collection of minds organized as a single claimant or agent, so that giving it everything is really giving to many.
- a conjecture I want both camps to take a stand on — call it the monster-elicitation lemma: any axiology that is (i) welfarist, (ii) monotone in each individual's welfare, (iii) anonymous and separable across individuals, and (iv) puts no upper bound on how much a single individual's condition can contribute, is monster-eliciting — for any allocation you like, there is some possible being such that the theory ranks dumping all resources on that being above it. Is the lemma true? Is it a problem if it is? And is the unboundedness in (iv) an empirical question (no such being is nomologically possible, so the lemma is toothless) or a conceptual one (the flaw is in the theory's structure)? Record each camp's answer separately, and have the aggregationist say which, if any, of (i)–(iv) they'd give up.

3. A disagreements table: each camp's residual position in wording that camp would sign, plus a neutral formulation. Flag anything you judge to be merely verbal. I suspect at least one of their fights is really about normalization: each person's welfare scale is only defined up to a positive affine transformation, so whether a monster exists at all depends on which convention you pick (range normalization, variance normalization, equal marginal weight at some reference point, whatever), and on whether any of those is non-arbitrary rather than smuggling the conclusion in. If that's the crux, say so and say what would settle it.

4. Cruxes with pre-registered predictions: for each crux, the case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and what each camp predicts it will show. Cases I'd like used where they fit: (a) a monster who is also the worst off in absolute terms and gains hugely without anyone being coerced or losing their projects or agency — if the verdict still feels wrong, the objection is structural rather than about fairness or sacrifice; (b) a bounded, realistic monster: suppose we had decent evidence that some being's per-resource welfare gains are roughly fifty times a typical human's and don't saturate — does either camp's verdict change in kind, or only in degree? (c) what I'd call the monster of justification: one being with an enormous complaint, such that every alternative is unjustifiable to it while everyone else's complaints are cheap to satisfy — does the separateness-of-persons camp escape the monster, or just relocate it into their own framework?

5. A short "what would move us" note from each camp, one or two sentences each and genuinely concrete — the consideration that would make them change their own formulation, not "further reflection". Then a paragraph from you as drafter: which agreed claim is the weakest, i.e. most likely to be papering over a live disagreement or doing less work than it looks like it's doing, and where the document's reasoning is most likely to be wrong.

Keep it tight, say 900–1200 words, and don't pad the agreed-claims list; five to eight claims is plenty.
