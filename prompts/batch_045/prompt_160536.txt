I sit on a hospital's therapeutics committee as its non-clinical member, and at our last meeting two of the clinicians — Marcus and Lena — spent most of an hour arguing about a proposal I now have to summarise in a memo, and I genuinely cannot tell who won. The proposal is this: formulary decisions (whether to adopt a proposed treatment protocol) are currently made by one senior clinical pharmacologist, whom a retrospective audit of the last few hundred cases puts at roughly 75% correct against our reconstructed gold standard; a working group wants to replace her with the majority verdict of a 15-member panel drawn from a rotating pool of generalist clinicians, each of whom the same audit puts at roughly 60% on comparable judgments. My own decision theory is about fifteen years stale, so I'd like the argument adjudicated rather than summarised back to me.

Here is roughly how it went. (1) Marcus opened with the Condorcet jury theorem: at p = 0.6, a majority of fifteen is right a shade under 79% of the time, which beats the pharmacologist's 75%, and it keeps improving as you add members, whereas a single reviewer's number is a fixed ceiling — with forty-one panelists you'd be above 90% — and a single decision-maker is also a single point of failure (capture, a bad year, an idiosyncratic dogmatism about a drug class). (2) Lena said the independence assumption is doing all of the work: the panelists read the same dossier, the same evidence review, share a training and a set of professional habits, and talk in the corridor, so their errors are correlated; if the effective number of independent votes is closer to three, a majority of three gets you about 65%, well below the pharmacologist. She added that the assumption is really independence conditional on the truth of the matter, and that a shared signal which is merely noisy — right most of the time but not always — is precisely what makes votes co-vary within the set of cases where the truth goes one way rather than the other. (3) Marcus replied that the conditional formulation cuts his way too: if the shared dossier is highly reliable, conditioning on it is nearly conditioning on the truth and independence is nearly intact, so what breaks the theorem is shared-but-noisy evidence, which is a design problem — split the fifteen into three sub-panels of five, commission a separate evidence summary for each, and decide by majority of the three sub-panel verdicts. (4) Lena answered that you cannot manufacture independent summaries of one fixed body of trial data — re-commissioning or rewriting the same evidence yields correlated summaries, so the sub-panel structure is cosmetic unless you change the information rather than the packaging, since what buys reliability is redundancy of evidence channels, not redundancy of heads; that in any case, under the very independence assumption he is leaning on, the hierarchy costs accuracy, because each five-person sub-panel is right about 68% of the time and a majority of three of those is about 76%, a bit below the flat fifteen-person majority; and that the 60% is a reference-class average, whereas the items that actually reach a vote are the contested ones, where everyone's competence degrades — and the theorem has a flip side, so that if effective competence dips below 0.5, majority voting becomes worse than useless. (5) Marcus conceded the agenda point but said it is an argument about the decision rule, not about the decision-maker: since adopting an ineffective or harmful protocol is considerably worse than delaying an effective one, the panel should be run under a supermajority rule — say ten of fifteen, with a default to "do not adopt" — and a panel's threshold is a tunable dial in a way that a single reviewer's judgment is not. (6) Lena closed by saying a single reviewer's threshold is tunable too: you ask for her confidence, she can escalate or abstain, and she can say "this one is hard" — whereas fifteen binary votes discard exactly the graded confidence that asymmetric error costs make most relevant, and the panel cannot abstain collectively without effectively defaulting to inaction anyway.

Please go through this move by move and tell me who is right about which specific point, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, and then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing. I'd particularly like you to (a) check the numbers rather than inherit them, and tell me if any of the percentages quoted above are wrong; (b) be explicit about what the jury theorem does and does not require of the votes — unconditional versus conditional independence, and what a shared evidence channel does to each; (c) take a view on the hierarchical variant in (3) and (4), including whether there are conditions under which the sub-panel design would beat the flat majority of fifteen, and what would have to be true of the evidence channels for that; (d) say how much the agenda-selection and heterogeneous-competence points in (4) actually threaten the case for the panel, given that they seem to threaten the single reviewer as well; and (e) say whether the threshold-tuning exchange in (5) and (6) is a genuine advantage for either arrangement, or whether continuous-confidence aggregation dominates both. Don't hedge into a summary of the literature or a list of considerations — I need a verdict I can put in a memo, including on whether the honest answer is that the audit numbers are too thin to settle any of this.
