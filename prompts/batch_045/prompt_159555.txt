A colleague of mine in the department — her field is social epistemology, and she has an irritating habit of filing every story about crowds into "cascade" and "not a cascade" — refuses to say what she means by the word until she has seen the cases; she claims the definition is what you get at the end, from the pattern of rulings. Over about a year of departmental lunches she has given me these. Two identical lunch counters open side by side in a market; the first few buyers scatter, then everyone joins whichever queue is longer, and the other counter closes early — cascade, she says, and she adds that the winner is close to arbitrary and would often be the other counter if you re-ran the morning. A rumour about a contaminant in a popular breakfast cereal spreads through a messaging group; within ten days sales fall by two thirds and the product is suspended, and the rumour is later traced to a single misread lab report — cascade again. Nearly everyone in a country migrating to one messaging app within a year, each migrant explaining that this is where everyone they need to reach already is — not a cascade, she says, that is coordination: what others do changes your payoffs directly, not your beliefs. Every household in a town buying a generator the day before a storm that had been forecast for a week — not a cascade either: one public signal, nothing learned from anyone else. And when I offered the case of junior analysts all rating a stock "buy" while privately thinking it was overpriced, because being wrong on your own is career-ending in a way that being wrong in company is not, she declined to rule: herding, she said, and if you want to count reputational herding as a cascade she will not fight you, but she does not think anybody learned anything.

I now have a case where the pressure point is different, and I want to predict her verdict rather than ask her, because she is on sabbatical and I have a small bet going with another student about it. It comes from a report on a seed-distribution programme that someone in my development-economics reading group was discussing. A government programme distributed a drought-tolerant maize variety to villages in one district; the trucks reached villages in an order fixed entirely by which roads had been repaired that year, so the earliest recipients were arbitrary, though they happened to sit in the sandier, drier part of the district and harvested noticeably more than their neighbours in the first season. Word moved village to village and within three seasons nearly everyone in the district had planted it. Almost every follower's own first plot did slightly worse than the local variety it replaced, because the new variety only outperforms the local one under water stress and most plots there are low-lying and wet in a normal year; interviewed later, farmers said they switched because everyone around them had, and several said they had attributed their own poor first plot to bad seedbed preparation rather than to the seed. The district then entered a run of dry years which turned out to be a lasting regional shift, not an anomaly; an independent agronomy service eventually confirmed that the new variety genuinely out-yields the local one below a certain rainfall threshold; a neighbouring district with the same soils adopted it four years later purely on the strength of its own trial plots, with no bandwagon at all; and in the original district nobody has gone back to the local variety now that full trial data are public. So: imitation of others' behaviour, private evidence overridden, the crowd vindicated, and the outcome stable and independently rediscoverable.

My own money is on "cascade," on the grounds that the mechanism looks exactly like the lunch counters, while the friend I am betting with says no, because he thinks the word is reserved for processes that lead people astray and this one landed on the right answer. What I would like from you is the reasoning rather than the ruling: reconstruct the rule or rules she could be running that fit all five of her earlier verdicts equally well, say what each of them predicts about the seed case, which of her earlier rulings is the closest analogy and why that analogy might mislead, what you would actually predict she says and why you prefer that rule, and one further case — the single most discriminating one you can think of — that would separate the surviving candidate rules from each other. I do not need chapter and verse from the literature; I just want to be able to win the bet and, more importantly, to know what the disagreement between my friend and me is really about.
