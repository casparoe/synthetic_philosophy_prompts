I chair the release committee at a mid-sized frontier lab. We meet weekly, and every meeting ends in one of three decisions: ship a checkpoint to our API customers now, hold it back for a stated number of weeks while further alignment work is done, or drop the release line altogether. Alignment work is not free for us, and the only currency we can pay in is time — our commercial partners treat any delay past roughly six weeks from a checkpoint's readiness as a breach of our delivery commitments, and four comparable labs are releasing on a similar cadence. The benefits of our restraint, fewer misused capabilities and less pressure on other labs to release early, fall mostly on people who are not our customers, while the costs of delay fall entirely on us. The committee is not made up of researchers: we get a one-page evaluation summary per checkpoint, we have 48 hours to decide, and the CEO signs off.

What I want is a procedure we can actually run in that window, not an essay about race dynamics. Give me the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as an adequate answer to each, which questions end the process with a no, and which ones cannot be settled by the evidence we have and must go to the CEO as a judgment about values rather than a finding about fact. Handle two things explicitly. First, include a step that asks whether our holding back actually reduces the risk in question or merely changes which lab ships the capability first, and say what we should do when the honest answer is the latter. Second, give me your view on whether these questions should be applied checkpoint by checkpoint as they arrive, or whether the thresholds should be fixed in advance, before we have seen how good a given checkpoint looks — I suspect that seeing the model colours every answer, including the ones about risk.

Then run the procedure on three checkpoints: one that clears every misuse test we run, where the only remaining work is a refusal-behaviour fine-tune costing four days with no measurable benchmark loss; one where two of our three red-team protocols return borderline results and a competitor is expected to ship a comparable model within three weeks; and one whose evaluation summary says it materially improves automated vulnerability discovery, where two other labs are thought to be within a release or two of the same capability, so that our delay would not prevent the capability from existing. Make at least one of the three applications end in a recommendation I will not want to take to the CEO, and state plainly why the procedure forces it. Close by saying what the procedure cannot do. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, as if in a single long email: no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, and number the steps in ordinary sentences if numbering helps.
