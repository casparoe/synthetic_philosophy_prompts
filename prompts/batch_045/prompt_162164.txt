I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working on a paper about whether the frame problem is a technical defect in our formalisms for action and change or a symptom that the representational picture of agency is wrong at the root. In my department I convene a small fortnightly seminar where a colleague from the knowledge-representation side and a colleague from the embodied and enactive side have spent two terms on exactly this, and I want to convert that stalemate into the text an adversarial collaboration between them would have produced. Please draft it.

I would like it in three parts. First, a numbered run of claims both of them could sign: what the frame problem was originally a problem about — I take it there are at least three separable worries that tend to get run together, roughly, stating what an action leaves unchanged without enumerating it case by case, handling an action's unstated preconditions and its downstream consequences, and confining attention to the handful of an agent's stored beliefs that bear on what to do next — together with what a solution would have to deliver, and what has and has not been delivered so far. Second, a table of the residual disagreements, one row apiece, each row stating the dispute first in wording the formalist would accept for their own view and then in wording the enactivist would accept for theirs, so that neither column is a strawman. Third, a list of the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of the two — with, for each, the test, case, or argument both would grant bears on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance.

Two matters I want handled head-on rather than gestured at. The first is how much of their quarrel is merely verbal: my suspicion is that they are using the phrase for different items on the list above, so that one is congratulating a solved problem and the other condemning an unsolved one, but I want that argued for or against rather than asserted. The second is a hobbyhorse of mine. The systems that actually matter now are learned latent-state world models with no explicit list of fluents at all, and the ones I care about must also answer for themselves in language: for any factor the agent ignored, it ought to be able to say why it was safe to ignore it — call this the articulation condition. Does a system with no explicit fluents dissolve the frame problem or merely relocate it into the learned dynamics, and does the articulation condition make the problem harder, easier, or simply different? I want each party to commit to an answer, and to say what would count as evidence against their own position. Please keep the whole thing under about nine hundred words, and do not lean on citations or on anything either of them would have to look up: I intend to circulate it to both.
