I sit on the committee drafting a new world history curriculum for fifteen- and sixteen-year-olds, and we have become stuck on one piece of vocabulary that seemed innocent when we voted it in. Our draft teacher's guide asks teachers to have pupils identify the cast of a historical episode — the thought being that a strong answer names not only rulers, ministers and rebels but also the bacillus, the drought and the volcano. A well-known popular book that credits the broad shape of the last several millennia largely to germs, crops and domesticable animals is the obvious inspiration, though the guide names nothing; another member wants the same vocabulary used in our unit on the enormous eruption in present-day Indonesia in 1815 and the year without a summer that followed it in Europe and North America. The vocabulary is meant to do a definite job. Teachers mark roughly four-hundred-word exam answers in about fifteen minutes and almost none of them have any training in the philosophy of history, so the rubric needs to reward a pupil who brings a non-human cause into the story and to mark down the answer that explains everything by policy and personality. It is also meant to block the opposite abuse: our subject association complained that pupils will write that the germ did it, or the volcano did it, and treat that as a full stop, which quietly releases a colonial administration, a landlord or a legislature from any role at all. So we need to know what the cast framing actually entitles a marker to infer.

Two colleagues have now offered rival framings that I cannot adjudicate. One says that germs, climates and volcanoes are not cast but stage — part of the setting in which the human actors act, like the weather in a play; on this view the volcanic eruption changes what is difficult rather than doing anything, and a pupil who calls it an actor has simply been fooled by a metaphor. A second says neither fits, and that a non-human cause is more like a change in the rules made in the middle of a game: it alters which moves are available and what they cost, without moving pieces itself. I would like an audit of all three. For each, give the correspondence table — what plays the role of the agent, the action, the goal, the reason for acting, the counterfactual difference-maker, the thing that can be praised or blamed — then say where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks matter for our use as opposed to being harmless. In particular I want to know what each framing quietly imports from its source domain that the target lacks: intentionality, the possibility of acting for reasons, responsiveness to evidence or to incentives, accountability, having a role in a plot with a beginning and an end, or the thought that if something is part of the cast then it was necessary to the outcome. Then tell me which of the conclusions we care about each framing can and cannot carry: that a monocausal human explanation is incomplete, that a non-human explanation is not exculpatory, that the eruption or the blight can be the most important cause without being an agent at all. Run the three framings on the episodes our draft actually covers — a fourteenth-century plague and the wage legislation that followed it, a mid-nineteenth-century famine caused by a crop blight, and the 1815 eruption and its cold summer — and say in each case what a fifteen-year-old could defensibly write under each framing, because that is what the rubric has to grade.

Please do not send me a reading list, a bibliography or citations; I want the argument, in plain language I can put in front of a teachers' meeting, and if you can give us a sentence or two of actual guide wording that survives your own objections, so much the better. Where you are making a values call rather than an analytic one, say so explicitly, since we can vote on the former and should not pretend to settle the latter. And be explicit about your own footing: mark which claims you hold with confidence and which are contestable, tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and name the argument or consideration that would lead you to a different verdict about the cast framing.
