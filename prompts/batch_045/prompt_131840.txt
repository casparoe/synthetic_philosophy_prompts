I'm writing a paper for an epistemology seminar on self-locating belief, and I want to use a film as more than a decorative example. The candidate is Christopher Nolan's *Memento*: Leonard Shelby has anterograde amnesia, cannot lay down new episodic memories, comes to each time into what is for him an unconnected present, and has to reconstruct where he is and what he is in the middle of from Polaroids, annotated photographs, notes in his own handwriting, and facts tattooed into his skin. I want a reading of the film through the Sleeping Beauty problem and the halfer/thirder dispute. What I don't want is the lazy version of that reading, so let me say what would make it worth having.

First, the disanalogy, which I want handled head-on rather than waved past. Sleeping Beauty's uncertainty is genuinely de se: the structure of the experiment and every non-indexical fact are known to her, and what she lacks is which awakening-now is hers. Leonard's deficit looks different in kind. He can name himself and locate himself in the ordinary way; what he lacks is history — non-indexical facts about what has already happened, which happen to be reachable for him only through artifacts and other people. Say how much of the self-locating apparatus survives that substitution, and whether the film is really about something adjacent: not indexical evidence but testimony from a past self, where the question is whether a note you wrote when you knew is, for you now, more like memory or more like a stranger's report. If the analogy mostly collapses, tell me so and tell me what the right framework would be instead.

Second, the case I have been using to sharpen it. I invented it and don't want it attributed to anyone; call it Forged-Note Beauty. Standard setup: fair coin on Sunday, one awakening on heads, two on tails, amnesia in between. Added wrinkle: at each awakening, before giving her credence, she finds in her hand a card stating that this is not her first awakening. She knows the experimenter's policy: a card is placed in her hand at every second awakening, and also at a first awakening whenever an independent chance device with known probability p fires, producing a forgery. She knows p but cannot tell a genuine card from a forged one. Work out what credence in tails the card supports under a halfer rule and under a thirder rule; say whether and where the two answers come apart, whether the gap tracks the baseline disagreement or has a shape of its own, and what happens at the limiting values of p. Then tell me what general moral you would draw about the evidential force of apparently self-generated evidence: does the card matter because it comes, in some sense, from her, or only because of the policy governing the channel?

Third, the reading itself. Apply what you get to three or four scenes I'd point a seminar at: the tattoos and the annotated Polaroids as externalized memory; the scene in which he burns the photograph of the man he believes he has killed and reflects on what he will then believe; the Sammy Jankis subplot, where a second anterograde amnesiac is presented with the suggestion that his failure was not really a failure; and the closing stretch, where he is told that the investigation is finished and his revenge already had, and where he then deliberately arranges for his future self to be handed false evidence and a fresh task. For the last one I want the hardest version of the question: a rational agent choosing to make his own future centered evidence less informative in order to remain able to act at all. Is that representable as a policy choice in something like the driver-who-cannot-remember-which-exit case, is it straightforward self-deception, or is it a point at which a purely credal treatment of self-locating belief runs out and needs something else?

Finally, the honest-critic part. Where does the film resist the reading? I have one worry of my own: Sleeping Beauty's whole point is that the non-indexical facts are settled and only the indexical ones are not, whereas the film may be deliberately agnostic about whether the final revelation is true, which would make it a poor illustration of exactly the feature that makes the problem a problem. Say whether that worry is right and whether it is fatal. And then say what, if anything, the film adds that the literature doesn't: the standard cases treat the inability to self-locate as an externally imposed handicap and ask for the correct number, while the film treats it as something the agent builds technologies against, and in the end sabotages.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the self-locating reading earns its keep, and if so what it yields that argument alone does not — then give the case, the reading, and the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question.
