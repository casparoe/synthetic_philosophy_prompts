So here's where I'm coming from: I'm retired, spent about thirty years as a research meteorologist doing forecast verification, and last fall I signed up for a philosophy course at the lifelong-learning institute and got hooked. The thing that grabbed me was this accuracy-first program — the idea that the only fundamentally good-making feature of a credence is how close it sits to the truth, scored with a strictly proper scoring rule like the Brier score (plain squared error, which is exactly what we used on precipitation forecasts), and then the dominance argument: if your credences break the probability axioms there's some other assignment that beats yours in every single possible world, so you're guaranteed to do worse accuracy-wise. That move feels like home to me, and the decomposition of a score into calibration and resolution is basically my old day job. But the argument quietly needs the scoring rule to be strictly proper and additive across possibilities, and the more I poke at it the more it feels like accuracy gets used to prove whatever the author already believed. I want the spec before I read another chapter.

Here's what I want from you. The use I want to put this to is critiquing actual scientific practice — p-hacking, models overfitted to noise, people who treat an inconvenient null result as a reason to distrust the instrument rather than the theory, labs that never quite update. So write me the desiderata a theory of epistemic value would have to meet to be good enough for that job, with the case or consideration that makes each one a requirement rather than a taste. The worries I keep circling, which you should organize, fix, or throw out: how fine we carve the space of possibilities (does the dominance argument still work if I slice the same outcomes more coarsely, and does it then start recommending things nobody should believe?); whether the framework has anything at all to say about which questions to form opinions about, or whether it just says 'be accurate about whatever you happen to be asked'; why simplicity or unification should count when two hypotheses fit the data equally well, which is a curve-fitting problem I ran into constantly; what to do with agents who aren't logically omniscient, since scoring across possible worlds seems to punish mere failure to notice a consequence as harshly as being wrong about the world; whether updating has to be derived or smuggled in; whether the theory can say anything about the value of running the next experiment, not just about being accurate with what you already have; and whether it handles the all-or-nothing kind of belief we actually use in lab meetings ('I think the effect is real'), including those awkward situations where you believe each of a long list of claims and also believe one of them is wrong. Add the requirements I've definately missed, and tell me straight out if any item on my list is really just a prejudice of one camp dressed up as a constraint.
