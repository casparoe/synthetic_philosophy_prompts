Germany has now completed its nuclear phase-out, having justified it largely in precautionary terms, across years in which coal still supplied a large share of its electricity; France, facing the same climate arithmetic, has chosen instead to keep and extend a fleet that supplies most of its electricity with low operational emissions but leaves waste that must be isolated over many thousands of years. I want to set aside which country got it right and ask about the shape of the reasoning involved.

What puzzles me is an asymmetry inside a single regulatory culture. Applied to nuclear, the state refuses to let a very small probability of a catastrophic release be traded off against expected benefits: a thick tail disqualifies the technology, no level of expected harm counts as tolerable, and the waste must be shown to be containable over timescales exceeding the life of any institution that produced it. Applied to the fossil-fuel alternative, the same state regulates by thresholds and cost-benefit balancing: it demands reduction rather than elimination, accepts harms that are large in aggregate and well characterised statistically, imposes no comparably strict containment or escrow duty on those who create long-lived costs, and treats diffuse and delayed harm to anonymous people as a different sort of problem.

What view of risk — or of what we owe to those we expose to risk — does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory rather than diagnosing it: say what the practice strictly requires its theory to commit to, what the theory merely sits well with, and where the practice is underdetermined, so that several theories could license it. In particular, can one expected-utility calculation with some discount rate generate the asymmetry, or does it need a qualitative distinction between kinds of hazard — concentrated against diffuse, dread against statistical, hazards that outlive every institution responsible for them against hazards whose victims and beneficiaries are contemporaries? Are the two halves consistent? One defence I have heard is a principle of counterfactual-baseline discipline: a technology may be rejected only if what will actually be built in its place is safer on the same metric, so refusing nuclear for coal fails while refusing nuclear for renewables and storage passes. A second is that the state does not impose the coal deaths but merely declines to prevent them by one particular means. Do these defences carry the weight the practice puts on them, can they be combined, and can either handle the waste objection — that a hazard lasting longer than any responsible institution differs in kind, not merely in degree? Finally, if the implicit theory were replaced by one uniform rule for all energy hazards, what would have to change in regulation, and what would be lost?
