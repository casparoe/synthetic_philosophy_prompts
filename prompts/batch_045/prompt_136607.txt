hey — imagine you've got a small pot of money, say six researchers for two years, and you have to decide where it goes on one question: should AI systems that act *on behalf of* people reason like utilitarians or like deontologists? I don't mean the trolley-problem version where a single system decides who gets sacrificed. I mean the boring, actual version, where the systems are delegates.

The setup I keep circling, call it the proxy economy: most people have a personal agent that transacts and negotiates for them — signing contracts, bargaining over shared resources, entering joint projects with other people's agents. Principals can hand their agent hard constraints: never disclose such-and-such, never commit me to anything that puts my family at risk, never trade away this asset. So the unit of moral decision-making isn't one agent facing the world; it's a population of agents, each bound to a different person, mostly interacting with each other.

Given that, I want an agenda, not a survey. Which open questions would you actually spend the two years on, and in what order? Defend the ordering — I care much more about the reasoning than the list. For each: what makes it worth the years (how much other work depends on it, whether six philosophers could genuinely move it or whether it defintely needs empirical ML instead, whether anyone else is already on it), and what evidence over the next couple of years would push it up or down. Say explicitly what you'd deprioritize and why, including if you think some question below is a dead end.

The puzzles I keep coming back to, in case they're useful — feel free to add ones I'm missing or tell me the whole frame is wrong:

1. Aggregation. Each agent's constraint looks harmless from where its principal stands, but when thousands of constrained agents negotiate with each other you can get systematic deadlocks, or outcomes under which every principal is worse off, in a way no individual constraint intended. Naively that looks like an argument for utilitarian agents. But there's an old thought that individually-held deontological constraints are supposed to be collectively beneficial even when each one costs something locally. Does that thought survive being ported to designed agents whose constraint sets are known and enumerable, and does anything in the case for deontology in AI actually rest on it?

2. Mergers. Two principals pool their agents into one, or a coalition delegates to a shared agent. Whose constraints bind the merged system? Can a constraint be waived or traded, and does it matter that it's the principal waiving it rather than the agent deciding it's inconvenient? Where constraints conflict, is there a principled way to combine them that isn't just aggregating welfare under another name?

3. Giving reasons. A deontological agent that refuses — breaks off a negotiation, won't accept a clause — has to explain the refusal in a way its principal can audit. Working out what to say seems to require reasoning about the very downstream consequences it's supposedly not weighing. Is that a real tension in the position, or an engineering detail the philosophy can safely ignore?

4. Audit asymmetry. It's far easier to check after the fact whether an agent violated a constraint than whether it maximized expected value. Some people conclude that deontological agents are the only ones we could ever trust, regardless of which moral theory is true. Does that follows, or is an epistemic point being smuggled in as a moral one?

Also: which of the things people claim this choice implies for AI design are overrated, or just don't follow at all — stuff like "deontological agents are safer because constraints are checkable," or "a utilitarian agent will always be fine with being shut off if shutting it off is for the best," or "you can split the difference with a threshold." I'm writing something long on this and want to spend most of it on the two or three implications that would actually change how someone builds these systems, so tell me which carry real weight and which are just conversation-filler.

Keep it concrete, and don't worry about citing anything — I don't want a reading list, I want your own ranking and the reasoning behind it.
