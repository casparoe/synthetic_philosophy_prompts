Hey — I'm running a one-off film-and-philosophy night next month and we're showing Groundhog Day (1993, Ramis, Bill Murray as Phil Connors in Punxsutawney), and I want to pair it with Sartre on radical freedom and bad faith. But I want something sharper than the version everyone already knows: Phil is in bad factic faith, then he stops being, the end. That reading is too smooth and I don't think it survives the last twenty minutes.

Here's the angle I keep circling. In the loop, everything Sartre bundles under facticity — my body, my past, my situation, the way the others see me — resets every morning, while what actually accumulates is Phil's knowledge and skill. I've been calling this facticity starvation: a case where an agent's situation gets thinned out faster than they can take it up, so freedom stops being a burden to be shouldered and becomes something closer to vertigo. Phil's stretch of despair and his run of suicides look to me like evidence that freedom without much facticity is unlivable, which is roughly what Sartre says about anguish but never really gets to test. Is that a defensible extension of the framework, or does it break it — does Sartre need facticity to be non-negotiable for transcendence to mean anything at all?

Then there's the homeless man. Phil keeps going back and can't save him, however many times he tries, and what he settles on is being kind to him anyway. I'd like you to sit with that. Is the film saying freedom is real but bounded by death, or is Phil's kindness there a new and more comfortable bad faith — a way of getting to feel like a good person with no efficacy to show for it?

And the ending bugs me. The loop lets go once Phil has become a man Rita could love, which looks like a moralized universe handing out a reward, which is exactly the kind of thing Sartre denies. Does the film need that ending to be watchable, and does needing it give the game away? Should I tell the group the movie is only half an illustration, or is there a reading where the ending is consistent?
