I teach an upper-level undergraduate course on the philosophy of mathematics, and I am marking the fourth question of a take-home problem set on actual infinities. The students had a week with it and had read on Cantor's diagonal argument, the part–whole principle, and the Hilbert's Hotel literature. The rubric I circulated awards 8 marks for part (a), 7 for part (b), and 5 for part (c).

Here is the question I set.

**Question 4 (the hotel, and how big the infinite is).** Suppose a hotel has rooms numbered 1, 2, 3, … and one guest in every room.

(a) The manager moves the guest in room *n* to room 2*n*, and then puts the *k*-th of infinitely many new arrivals into room 2*k* − 1. Explain precisely what is supposed to be troubling about this, and identify the two principles about size that come apart here: the part–whole principle (a proper part of a collection has fewer members than the whole) and Cantor's criterion (two collections have the same size when their members can be placed in one-to-one correspondence). A set that can be correlated one-to-one with a proper subset of itself is standardly called Dedekind-infinite. Say whether, in the set theory used in this course (ZFC), being Dedekind-infinite is equivalent to being infinite in the ordinary sense, and explain why a defender of the possibility of actual infinities takes the hotel to involve no contradiction.

(b) Call someone a Computabilist if she holds three things: the natural numbers form a legitimate completed totality; a real number exists only if some finite algorithm computes its digits; and a function exists only if it is computable. Must the Computabilist accept that her real numbers are countable, in the sense that there is a one-to-one correspondence between them and the natural numbers? What becomes of Cantor's diagonal argument in her setting? State explicitly which reading you are giving to the phrase there is a one-to-one correspondence, and whether the correspondence is required to be one she can admit as existing.

(c) Does the bare possibility of such a hotel, together with the thought that a full hotel cannot take in more guests, already constitute an argument against the existence of actual infinities? Or does that argument need an extra premise which the hotel scenario does not itself supply? Identify the premise if there is one. Where, if anywhere, does this leave someone who accepts arbitrarily large finite totalities but no completed infinite ones?

Here is one student's answer, reproduced in full.

> The troubling thing is that the hotel is full — every room occupied — and still takes in infinitely many new guests, and would have taken in any finite number just as well, without turning anyone away. Nothing in the procedure fails at the level of detail: each guest in *n* goes to 2*n*, all these rooms are distinct, and every odd room is then free. So the hotel contains no contradiction in the sense of standard set theory; ZFC proves that the evens can be correlated one-to-one with all the natural numbers.
>
> The conflict is between two ways of comparing size. The part–whole principle says that if A is a proper part of B then A has fewer members than B. Cantor's criterion says that two collections have the same size just in case their members can be put in one-to-one correspondence. For finite collections the two agree. Cantor's definition of an infinite set is that it can be correlated one-to-one with a proper subset of itself — such sets are called Dedekind-infinite — and in ZFC this is equivalent to having more than *n* members for every finite *n*; indeed the equivalence already goes through in ZF, since no choice principle is needed to show that a set which is not finite has a countably infinite part. Given all this, the defender of actual infinities says the hotel is unproblematic: being full only means that the guests are correlated one-to-one with the rooms, and the guests can be correlated one-to-one with a proper subset of the rooms, so being full does not prevent one taking in more. The opponent must therefore be rejecting the Cantorian criterion of size, and hence rejecting set theory itself, since that criterion is the one set theory uses.
>
> For part (b): the Computabilist's real numbers are the computable ones, given by finite algorithms, and there are only countably many algorithms, each being a finite string over a finite alphabet. So her reals are countable and she must accept that they can be paired off with the natural numbers: list the finite strings in lexicographic order, delete those that are not programs or that do not compute the digits of a real, and pair the *k*-th surviving program with the number *k*. Cantor's diagonal argument is then unsound as applied to her reals, or at best equivocal. From a list of all her reals one can effectively compute a real differing from the *k*-th real in the *k*-th digit, and since the list is itself computable, the diagonal real is computable, so it is already on the list — which is impossible. The lesson is that the diagonal argument works only if the reals are taken as a completed uncountable totality, which is exactly what the Computabilist denies; she therefore escapes it.
>
> For part (c): the hotel is by itself an argument against actual infinities. If an actually infinite collection of rooms and guests could exist, the hotel could exist; and the hotel cannot exist, because a full hotel cannot take in more guests. No further premise is needed. The only remaining position for an opponent of actual infinities is strict finitism, the denial that the natural numbers form an infinite totality at all; and there is no stable middle position, since someone who accepts arbitrarily large finite hotels — for every *n*, a hotel with *n* rooms — already accepts everything the infinite hotel requires, and so can have no complaint about it.
>
> One further worry the defender should address: actually carrying out the move seems to require infinitely many relocations at once, since no guest can move into an occupied room and there is no last guest who could move first. Whether any physical hotel could do this is a separate question from whether the arrangement is mathematically coherent, and the defender owes us an answer to it.
