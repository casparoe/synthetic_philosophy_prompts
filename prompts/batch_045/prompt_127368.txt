I am a novelist, and I need a reusable procedure rather than an explanation.

The situation: I am two drafts into the third book of a sequence whose narrator alternates between two point-of-view characters. One of them was trained, before the events of the books, in a discipline I have modelled closely on Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka — the four-cornered negation, emptiness as the absence of intrinsic nature rather than as nonexistence, the two truths, and the practice of arguing by consequences drawn from an interlocutor's own commitments rather than from theses of one's own. In the fiction the school goes by another name, but for our purposes call it Madhyamaka. Across the manuscript there are roughly thirty scenes in which this character is put a direct question by someone else: a question with a yes-or-no shape, or a question asking her to state what she holds. I have to write her answers one at a time, months apart, and I want them to be consistent without being interchangeable, and I want them to be dramatically usable rather than miniature lectures. My readers have no philosophy background; my editor has no patience; the answers should almost never run past forty words.

What I have in mind is a decision procedure I can run in a few minutes on each of these scenes: the ordered questions to ask about the question she is being asked, what counts as a determinate answer at each step, and where the procedure terminates in one of a small number of permitted speech acts. As inputs it should take only what I can write down about the scene: what is literally asked, what the asker is trying to do (learn something, trap her, force a public commitment, be reassured), and what she has to be able to do next in the plot.

My working understanding, so you can correct it if it is wrong: for a claim the four corners are affirmation, denial, both, and neither; the point of running through them is not to select a corner or to announce a fifth, but to show that the question has no determinate subject to be settled; and this is compatible with, indeed requires, a robustly conventional answer for ordinary purposes — whether the bridge is guarded, whether she intends to cross it. I am trying to keep three failure modes out of the manuscript: the reader hearing her as a nihilist for whom nothing matters; the reader hearing her as a mystic trading on contradiction for atmosphere; and the reader hearing her as simply evasive, which is the failure my editor is most likely to flag.

Two complications I want built into the procedure rather than smoothed over. First, the school I have given her adds a permitted move to the four: answering inside the asker's convention while marking the answer as borrowed rather than owned, which in my draft notes I have been calling "the lent answer," and I want the procedure to say when that move is honest and when it is a dodge. Second, she has to go on acting: the procedure must say what happens to agency and to the plot's stakes once a character has refused all four corners, since a scene in which the correct answer is silence and the next beat requires a choice is a scene I still have to write.

Please give me the procedure, with a worked application to each of three scenes I will describe: a soldier asking her whether the bridge is guarded; a former teacher asking her, in front of students, whether she believes the self survives death; and a hostile magistrate asking her to state, for the record, whether the order she disobeyed was wrong. Then apply the procedure to one further case of your own choosing in which it returns an answer that is dramatically inert or otherwise unusable, and tell me what I should conclude — that the scene is wrongly built, or that the procedure has reached the limit of what it can settle. Finally, a short statement of what the procedure cannot do: which decisions remain matters of craft and judgment no matter how carefully the philosophy is done, and whether the residue is a defect of the framework or a sign that the framework is being asked to do something it never claimed to do.
