I teach a six-week graduate seminar on mental causation, and for the middle session I want to replace my usual handout of labelled positions with a self-scoring diagnostic: participants would answer a set of case-based questions before they have met any of the literature, and a key would tell them which position their pattern of answers has already committed them to. The topic is the exclusion argument, by which I mean the whole family of formulations — the overdetermination version, the supervenience-based version, and the version on which causal efficacy drains downward to the physical level. Please write the instrument.

Three constraints, in the order in which I care about them.

First, construct the logical space before attaching any labels. Fix three or four choice points; the candidates I have in mind are whether every event that has a cause is fully produced by physical causes alone; whether being fully produced by physical causes requires deterministic sufficiency or can be satisfied merely by the physical causes raising the probability of the event; whether mental properties are distinct from physical ones, and whether distinctness together with the dependence of the mental on the physical leaves the two independent enough for any exclusion principle to get a grip; and what something has to be in order to count as a cause at all — a producer of its effect, a difference-maker for it, a variable one could in principle intervene on. Enumerate the combinations your choice points allow, and only then locate the positions in the resulting cells.

Second, the questions. Ten of them, each a concrete case with two or three answer options. No question may contain the name of a position or a term of philosophical art, and no question may ask in the abstract what causation is; a well-read person with no philosophy training should be able to answer every one from the case alone. Each option should be a live option — something that some position in the literature is genuinely committed to, rather than a strawman. Here is one I have written, as a sample of the register:

A patient has a monitor implanted in her motor cortex. The monitor is programmed to detect any attempted hand movement that is failing to be initiated and to stimulate the cortex so that the same movement occurs, with the same force, direction and timing that it would otherwise have had. On Tuesday afternoon she reaches for a glass because she wants a drink and believes the glass contains water; the monitor detects nothing amiss and does nothing. Did her wanting a drink and her believing there is water cause the reaching? (a) Yes: the monitor did nothing, and the reaching happened because of what she wanted and believed. (b) Yes, but only because wanting the drink and believing there was water were, on that occasion, goings-on in her brain that produced the reaching; the wanting and the believing themselves made no difference to anything. (c) Yes in the everyday sense, but what actually brought the reaching about was the neural process, and the wanting and the believing came along with it without doing any of the work.

Write the remaining nine in that register. Among them I want at least: a symmetric overdetermination or preemption case; a case in which the physical antecedent merely raises the probability of the outcome, so that the question becomes whether the mental antecedent has any causal work left to do; a case in which the same behaviour is produced from very different hardware, so that the higher-level description generalizes across instances while the lower-level one does not; a case in which the mental state, conditional on the complete physical state, predicts nothing further about what happens; a case in which the effect to be explained is a change in someone's brain rather than a piece of behaviour; and a case in which two agents are alike in every physical respect at the moment of acting and differ only in what it is like for them.

Third, the leaves: ten to fourteen of them. The following must appear, under whatever names you prefer: reduction by type identity; reduction to functional or realizer properties; eliminativism; epiphenomenalism, with global epiphenomenalism and epiphenomenalism restricted to phenomenal properties as separate leaves; the view that secures mental causation by inheritance from the physical properties of the very same event or particular; the view that accepts overdetermination, together with the variant on which the mental depends too closely on the physical for the two to be genuine causal rivals; relevance-without-efficacy views of the program-explanation kind; proportionality views that turn the argument around and exclude the physical cause rather than the mental one; interventionist or difference-making views; views that deny the causal completeness of the physical; views that locate mentality in the intrinsic or categorical nature of the physical; and non-reductive views that rest on grounding rather than identity. Mark as unoccupied any leaf that in fact has no defenders.

After that, the two short sections I care about most. The first lists the cells of your space that no position fills, and says of each whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to explore it. The second lists the pairs of leaves that your ten questions cannot separate, gives the shared answer pattern for each pair, and says what an eleventh question would have to ask in order to split them.

Format the whole document in three parts: the questions alone, with no names of positions or theorists appearing anywhere in them; then the choice points, the enumerated space, and a table from answer patterns to leaves; then the empty cells and the unseparable pairs. If a participant's answers match no leaf you have placed, say in part two what the key should report back to them. Please include no bibliography, no citations and no reading list anywhere; the key should name positions rather than papers.
