I hold, provisionally, a view about well-being that I worked out on my own and later discovered is not unheard of: the only things intrinsically bad for a person are craving — the felt pull toward something not yet had — and suffering, and nothing is intrinsically good except their absence. I find it easiest to write this the way I would write a scoring function. At each moment, a life gets a value equal to minus the sum of the craving and the suffering present at that moment; the value of a life is that quantity integrated over its duration; more is not better, there is no positive term, and nothing outside the agent's experience — no achievement, no knowledge, no being loved, no moral worth — contributes except insofar as it removes one of the two bad things.

The difficulty I keep hitting is that this function is, in my field's vocabulary, Markovian: it reads off the current state and knows nothing about the trajectory that produced it. That makes it blind to distinctions I am fairly confident are real. Four cases make the point.

First, absorption. I once worked most of a day and most of a night on a problem, forgot to eat, and felt no pull toward anything at all — no itch, no frustration, just the work. My function scores those hours as excellent, which feels wrong in a way I can't quite locate: the quiet of absorption is not the quiet the view was supposed to recommend, and my function has no slot in which to say so.

Second, the same state arrived at by not looking. I can be plainly unsettled about something and simply not bring it to mind for weeks. Nothing is registered, so nothing is counted. The view appears to reward, not the absence of craving, but the absence of attention to it.

Third, two ways of coming to rest. One person stops wanting the thing she wanted for years because she finally has it. Another stops wanting it because the wanting simply switched off — the goal was abandoned without ever being settled, and she doesn't much notice or care. At every moment after the change, the two are scored identically. I think they differ, and not merely in their causes.

Fourth, the residue and its mirror. For weeks after finishing that project I kept half-turning toward it: an objectless tension, the striving gone but the shape of the striving left behind. And symmetrically, the first hour after a long strain lifts has a character that does not feel like a mere zero — it feels like something, and the view says that feeling is an illusion.

The pattern I want a name for is this: a measure defined purely over instantaneous states cannot distinguish routes to the same state, and in particular cannot distinguish tranquility that is the absence of a craving from tranquility that is its resolution, its suppression, its being unnoticed, or its never having arisen. Put more exactly, the thing the view cares about is supposed to be the absence *of* something, and it keeps turning out that the something can be absent without being gone — suppressed, unregistered, redirected — or gone without being absent, satisfied and leaving a residue behind.

I should add a measurement worry, since it may be a separate problem. Because craving is aversive it commands attention, and its absence commands none, so any self-report instrument over-samples the bad moments and under-samples the quiet ones; and a mild chronic craving eventually stops being reported at all. My function is therefore being fed biased telemetry, and I don't know whether that is a defect in the view or only in my access to it.

So my question. Does this failure mode have an established name, or a literature of its own? A couple of people have told me it is just one of the ancient serene-states doctrines, or a Buddhist point about craving, and I don't trust the identification: those views, as they've been described to me, seem to be claims about what to aim at, whereas my problem is about what my measure can see. I won't be satisfied with a label in any case. I want the carving:

1. What exactly is the phenomenon, and what is it not? In particular, how is it different from the familiar worry that we might not want to be wired into a machine that produces good experiences, from the observation that satisfaction wears off and so is never really attained, and from the worry that people adapt their wants to their circumstances and then report contentment? If it is none of those, say which of them is nearest and where the fit breaks.
2. Is the right repair to make the function history-dependent — to add, in effect, a hysteresis term, so that how one arrived at quiet matters? If so, what does that cost? Does it mean suffering can become bad retroactively in a way the view was designed to avoid, and does it quietly re-admit satisfaction-of-desire through the back door, on the grounds that a resolved craving is a craving one once had and got?
3. Where are the edges? Dreamless sleep, general anaesthesia, coma, and never having existed are all scored at or near the maximum: is that a reductio of the view or a consequence one should accept, and does the distinction I'm after survive if one accepts it? What should it say about the absorbed worker, and about someone whose life is quiet largely because few cravings were ever allowed to form in it?
4. If there is no established concept, coin one, define it, and give the boundary cases that would settle whether a given instance falls under it.
