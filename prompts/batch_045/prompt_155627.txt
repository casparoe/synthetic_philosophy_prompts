I'm working on a long Sunday piece about why expert timelines keep being wrong, and I've noticed something uncomfortable: the advice I keep handing out in it — start from the outside view, find the reference class, use the base rate, treat your own story about the case as the least reliable part of your evidence — is not the advice I actually follow in my own reporting, and I can't state the rule I'm really using. So I want to do this from the bottom up, with my own verdicts.

Health-tech founder tells me her app will clear regulatory review in nine months: I discard her story entirely and use the base rate for software in that category, comfortable. Lab director says his machine will reach net energy in three years: I apply the base rate for fusion milestones and discount hard, comfortable. The same lab director, in the same interview, says his shot rate will double next year because the new power supply is installed and has already been run at half power: I take the inside view and never consult a base rate, comfortable. My own features: I ignore how long features take at magazines generally and quote from my personal record of about forty pieces, comfortable. A writer who has finished four books in five years says the fifth will take a year: I narrow the reference class to her own record, n = 4, and accept, comfortable. A well-funded institute says its ten-year project will not slip, because it has dedicated money and no teaching load: I refuse to narrow to well-funded, well-staffed projects and hold it to the base rate for projects of that kind generally, comfortable. A transit authority says a tunnel will cost a couple of billion: I want the outside view here too, but I don't want the mean overrun — I believe that distribution is heavy-tailed, and quoting an average feels like false precision, so I want something like an eightieth percentile. And an AI researcher gives me a scaling timeline on the record: I decline to use the base rate for timelines given by people who agree to be interviewed, because agreeing to be interviewed selects for a certain kind of confidence, but I then have no idea which class to use instead.

Two pairs in that list strike me as hard to reconcile, and they're where I think the real principle lives. First, the lab director's two claims concern the same person and the same machine, and I treat them oppositely; the only difference I can articulate is that the shot-rate claim is nearly a report of something already partly observed and will be scored within months, while the net-energy claim won't be scored for years and sits right on top of the optimism gradient — which suggests something like: trust an inside view in proportion to how much the estimator has already been, or will soon be, scored on claims of that exact kind. Second, I happily narrow the class for the writer and refuse to narrow it for the institute, even though both are narrowing along a feature that favors their own optimism; my guess is that the difference is that the writer's feature comes with counted outcomes (four books, four dates) while nobody has tabulated outcomes for well-funded projects, and funding is also the very feature that manufactures the confidence — which suggests something like: narrow a reference class only along dimensions on which you possess outcome counts, never along dimensions you merely believe matter.

So: take those two candidates as my tentative principle, test them case by case against everything above, and either merge them into one rule or tell me they are genuinely two rules and which one governs when they conflict. I want the finished principle stated precisely enough that I could apply it cold, with the case that motivates each clause attached to that clause, its verdicts on at least two cases I haven't listed, an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would force me to overturn, and — most usefully — its verdict on the case I keep going back and forth on: a friend on her second startup, whose first one exited, where second-time-founder statistics exist but strike me as selected on the outcome, while her own record is a sample of one. One last thing I want addressed rather than dodged: everyone who tells me this time is different has some base rate of being wrong about that, and if I apply the outside view to my own this-time-is-different judgments, I seem to slide into a modesty so global that no mechanism evidence could ever move me — yet I do let a mechanic's observation of a worn clutch override the model's reliability base rate. Tell me where modesty about my own inside view is supposed to stop, and whether that stopping point is part of the principle or outside it.
