The question:

(a) Consider the following argument: if I know that I have hands, then I know that I am not a brain in a vat; I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat; therefore I do not know that I have hands. State the closure principle the argument relies on, and explain how this argument differs from the argument that moves directly from the premise that all the evidence available to me would be the same whether or not I were envatted to the same conclusion. Which premise does each put pressure on?

(b) A well-known argument, associated with Hilary Putnam, holds that the brain-in-a-vat hypothesis is self-refuting: assume, for the sake of argument, that a broadly externalist account of how words come to refer is correct, and it follows that if the hypothesis were true then the words in which it is stated would not refer to brains and vats at all, so that what the hypothesis says would be false. Assess this argument. In particular, consider the following variant: a community of human beings lives an ordinary embodied life for thirty years, acquiring words like brain and vat in normal causal contact with brains and vats, and is then envatted by a team of neuroscientists who preserve their language use, after which their experiences are subjectively indistinguishable from their earlier ones. Does the self-refutation argument go through for this community? If it does not, what follows for the anti-skeptical use that has been made of it?

(c) It is sometimes said that the brain in a vat is simply Descartes's malicious demon with the technology updated. Explain why this is, or is not, misleading.

The candidate's answer:

The argument in the question relies on a closure principle: knowledge is closed under known entailment, so that if a subject knows P and knows (or can competently derive) that P entails Q, then the subject knows Q. Having hands entails not being an envatted brain, since an envatted brain has no hands. So if I know I have hands, I know I am not a brain in a vat; the skeptic's second premise is that I do not know the latter; and the conclusion follows by modus tollens. The argument is valid, and everything turns on whether that second premise can be supported.

The support usually offered is the underdetermination argument, but this is only the closure argument in different words: in both cases the skeptic says that I cannot rule out the vat hypothesis and concludes that I do not know anything. That is an argument from ignorance. It also rests on a premise that is straightforwardly false. The skeptic says that my evidence would be exactly the same in the two cases; but it would not. In the good case my experience of a desk is caused by a desk and by my hands pressing on it; in the bad case it is caused by electrodes acting on a brain. Different causes, therefore different evidence. Indeed, in the bad case the subject is not perceiving anything at all: stimulation of a brain is not a perception of a desk, and the envatted subject's experiences are not evidence of anything beyond themselves. Once this is seen, the underdetermination premise collapses, and the skeptic has no way of supporting the claim that I do not know that I am not envatted.

It is worth noting that not every anti-skeptical response takes this form. Accounts requiring knowledge to be sensitive, or to track the facts, concede the skeptic's point: in the nearest world in which I am a brain in a vat I believe exactly what I now believe, and so I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat. Such accounts resist the conclusion only by denying closure, and they must therefore accept conjunctions of the form: I know that I have hands, but I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat. Many philosophers have found such conjunctions impossible to assert, which is a real cost, and a reason to prefer the direct refutation given above.

On Descartes: it is often said that the brain in a vat is just the malicious demon with the technology updated, but this is misleading in three respects. First, scope: the demon is introduced in order to doubt as much as can be doubted, including simple arithmetic — whether two and three make five — whereas the vat hypothesis is standardly used to threaten empirical knowledge of the external world and leaves a priori knowledge untouched. Second, object: the vat scenario itself contains an ordinary external world, with laboratories, vats and neuroscientists in it, so at most it threatens our knowledge of the world, while the demon is meant to put the existence of an external world in question as well. Third, exit: Descartes escapes by way of the cogito and then by way of the guarantee of a non-deceiving God, a foundationalist and internalist route that no contemporary externalist could accept. So the two are not the same argument in different costume.

The self-refutation argument is best understood as an application of the verificationist principle: a hypothesis which no possible experience could confirm or disconfirm is cognitively meaningless. Nothing I could experience would settle whether I am now envatted, since by hypothesis the envatted subject's experiences are indistinguishable from mine. So the vat hypothesis has no content: it can be neither true nor false, and therefore it cannot be a genuine alternative to my ordinary beliefs. That is why we are entitled to say that we know we are not brains in a vat. The argument is purely semantic and does not depend on any controversial theory of reference: whatever one thinks about how words come to refer, an unverifiable hypothesis is empty.

The thirty-year variant makes no difference. Reference requires causal contact between a speaker and the things the speaker talks about, and what matters is the speaker's present relation to the environment. Once the community is envatted it has no causal contact with brains or vats; its word vat therefore fails to refer to vats; and so what it says is false if true, exactly as before. The earlier history of contact is irrelevant.

The skeptical conclusion therefore fails at every point. The underdetermination premise is false, the vat hypothesis is not a coherent alternative to my ordinary beliefs, and the self-refutation argument survives the variant. The externalist can answer the skeptic without conceding anything.
