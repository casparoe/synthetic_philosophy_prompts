I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere for something like a decade, and one move I keep running into is an argument being dismissed with "that's just Bertrand's paradox." A commenter whose posts I usually trust recently gave the fullest version of the move I've seen, more or less as follows:

People still talk as if the principle of indifference were a constraint on rational belief, but Bertrand's paradox settled that in 1889. Ask for the probability that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and the answer depends entirely on how the chord is selected: two random points on the circumference give 1/3, a random radius together with a random point along it gives 1/2, and a random midpoint inside the disk gives 1/4. Each of those procedures picks chords uniformly, so each is exactly what indifference demands; the principle therefore assigns three different probabilities to a single proposition, which means it isn't merely incomplete or in need of a supplementary rule, it is flatly inconsistent and should be abandoned. The root cause is the familiar one: there is no uniform distribution over a continuum, so "chosen at random" has no content until someone hands you a measure, and that measure necessarily comes from outside the problem. Jaynes tried to rescue indifference with maximum entropy, and did extract 1/2 as the unique invariant answer, but that is just one more assumption smuggled in, and later work has produced procedures yielding essentially any value you like. The practical upshot is that priors can never be read off symmetry or ignorance: they have to come from data or from convention, and every argument of the form "we have no reason to prefer one of these alternatives, so give them equal probability" is void.

I'd like an accuracy audit I can act on. Please work through it claim by claim and separate what a philosopher of probability or a careful Bayesian would grant, what they'd wince at as a simplification or a conflation, and what is simply too strong. I'm specifically suspicious of the "no uniform distribution over a continuum" line — I have a feeling it imports a different problem than the one Bertrand raised — and I want to know whether "inconsistent, not merely incomplete" survives once that diagnosis is corrected.

The reason I care is downstream. Last week someone used this same reasoning to shut down an outside-view estimate of the share of technological civilizations that are older than ours, on the grounds that you can parametrize by elapsed time, by number of civilizations, or by rank among civilizations and get different answers, so the base rate is arbitrary in exactly Bertrand's sense. That felt like overreach to me, but I can't say whether the analogy holds once the paradox is described correctly. Does the commenter's practical upshot support that kind of inference, or does it depend on the parts of the summary that don't survive correction?
