I am writing a novel whose central device I now suspect rests on a philosophical mistake, and I would like it corrected before I build three hundred pages on it.

The setup: my protagonist, a biographer down on her luck, is commissioned by the family of a minor, vainglorious, generally unloved politician to write his life, on two unusual terms — the advance is very large, and it is repayable in full if, in the family's judgement, she has not written *in a spirit of genuine admiration*. She takes the job for the money, spends a year in the archive in enforced and initially unwilling proximity to the man, and slowly comes to admire him: for a dogged patience with constituents' letters, and for one costly refusal to leak a colleague's secret. These are, I mean them to be, real qualities that she would never have noticed without the year of attention the contract bought. Only in the last chapter does she learn the family's actual purpose: they wanted there to exist at least one genuine admirer of him, and selected the money as the means of producing one.

My understanding, picked up second-hand, is that this is an instance of the wrong kind of reasons problem, and that the case is meant to damage the buck-passing account of value — the view that for a thing to be admirable just is for there to be reasons to admire it. Here is how I have been reasoning about my own plot, and I would like to know where it goes wrong: the cheque, like the demon's threat in the standard example, is a reason to be in the state of admiring rather than a reason given by the person admired; the cheque is also what causally explains why she was in the archive at all; therefore her admiration cannot be a response to the reasons that make him admirable, and the qualities she cites at the end are rationalization. And therefore the buck-passing account is refuted, since in my story there is a very substantial reason to admire him and he remains a tedious and vain man — which is precisely the point I want the book to make about bought esteem.

Three things I need from you. First, is that argument sound as stated, and if not, which step fails — I would rather be told the confusion than told what I want to hear. Second, for the purposes of the novel: would it change anything if I made the money purely causal, so that she signs for the advance, never thinks of it again, and admires him in the ordinary way by the end? A friend insists my case is not a case of the wrong kind of reasons problem at all, and that if it is one, it is among the easy ones; I would like to know which of us is right and what the difference turns on. Third, and this is what I actually care about: is there a distinction available between a reason that merely causes or enables an attitude and a reason *for which* the attitude is held, such that my protagonist's admiration could come out as genuine without my having to pretend the money played no part? If the philosophical categories here do not track what I mean by authenticity in a character, say so plainly — that too would be useful. I do not need names, dates, or references; I need the structure of the mistake.
