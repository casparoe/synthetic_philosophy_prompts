I've built a little thought experiment I'm calling the Arno problem, and I want to know what Machiavelli would say about it.

Setup: near future. A middle-rank power — call it the Signoria — can field a new class of autonomous command systems. Fielding them gives a durable first-mover advantage: whoever goes first can lock everyone else out for a decade or more. But there's roughly a 1-in-20 chance per fielding that the thing escapes control in a way that's irreversible on any human timescale and ends the game for everyone, winners included. And the odds aren't fixed. They're much worse if you move fast and alone, better if you've spent years on containment work first, better still if your rivals do the containment work too. Nobody can directly observe whether a rival has fielded. The moment one side fields, every other side's incentive flips to fielding immediately.

So: what does Machiavelli tell the prince to do? I want the reasoning run through his own machinery — fortuna, virtù, necessità, the business of matching one's mode of proceeding to the times, and his habit of treating appeals to fortune as what idle people say once they've already lost.

Specific bits I'm stuck on:

1. Would he even file this under fortuna? His line is that fortune has authority over roughly half of what we do and leaves us the rest, and that its real power shows up where there's no virtù organized against it — the flood only destroys where nobody built dikes and canals. Is a 5% irreversible catastrophe a flood to be engineered against, or is it definately closer to the ruin he thinks princes call down on themselves by sitting still and letting fortune decide for them?

2. There's a real tension: he's the one who says fortune yields to the impetuous and admires the young and rash, and he's also the one who says the prudent prince prepares in calm weather. Which one shows up here? And does it change if the "dike" is a treaty — given what he thinks about men keeping faith only under compulsion, is an agreement to restrain the tech a canal, or a canal built on sand?

3. Endogeneity. Here fortune isn't weather; it's made of other people's guesses about what you'll do, which are guesses about your virtù. Does his distinction survive that hall of mirrors, or does it collapse into reputation management?

4. Necessità. He thinks hard times manufacture virtù and that crisis is where great men get made. But an irreversible ending removes the whole currency of his system — no city, no posterity, no glory, no second act. Is there anything left in his framework that can even register that kind of risk, or would he refuse the premise?

5. Would he reject the framing outright? He has no expected-value calculus, no multiplying probability by payoff. Would he say a prince who thinks in probabilities has already traded virtù for bookkeeping — and if so, what does he put in its place that still tells the prince what to do on Monday?

Give me the memo he'd write to the prince: what to do in year one, what to do by year five, and the one part of our modern framing he'd find most unintelligible.
