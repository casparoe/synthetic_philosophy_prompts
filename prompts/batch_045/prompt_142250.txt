I am preparing materials for a final-year seminar on historical explanation and forecasting, and I have been reading about reference classes, base rates, and the outside view as they are used to model the life-cycle of empires. At a departmental workshop last week, a colleague in political theory invoked the familiar Santayana aphorism from Reason in Common Sense, to the effect that those who are ignorant of the past are fated to re-enact it, in order to settle a disagreement about how we ought to forecast the durability of the present American-led international order. Her claim was that, because we have a reasonably long record of hegemonic empires overstretching and declining, we should treat that record as a reference class, assign a high base rate to great-power decline, and regard any inside-view story about how nuclear deterrence, economic interdependence, or institutional learning make this case different as an instance of the very special pleading the aphorism warns against. She seemed to think the maxim itself told us to prefer the outside view to the inside view.

I want to press on the language rather than the geopolitics. It seems to me that the aphorism is being made to bear far more weight than it can. Could you set out, as a menu, exactly five distinct precise claims that the maxim might be expressing, from the platitudinous to the substantive to the plainly false? For each, I want a verdict: say whether the claim is trivial, whether it is substantive and (so far as one can tell) defensible, whether it is false, and which of these readings the people who repeat it in arguments like my colleague’s probably have in mind. Crucially, say which of the five, if any, would actually support the methodological conclusion she drew—that the outside view and base rates from the imperial reference class should trump an inside-view causal story about novelty. I am not asking for a bibliography, for readings, or for any particular empirical data about empires; I want the conceptual geography of the slogan, distinguishing the reading that is a banality no one should need a maxim to establish, the reading that is an empirical thesis which must be argued for and could be false, and the reading that quietly changes the subject or undermines the very use being made of it.

Please answer in exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two in length, with no introduction and no conclusion: one item per precise reading, stating the thesis, classifying it as trivial, defensible, false, or something else, and saying whether it would do the work my colleague wanted. Please write in British English.
