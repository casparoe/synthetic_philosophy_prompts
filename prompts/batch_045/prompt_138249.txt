The course is a decision and game theory elective, mostly sophomores and juniors. The question was one of four on a take-home; rubric is loose because I wrote it at 1am.

QUESTION 4 (25 points)

Two players, A and B, play the following six-node centipede game. A moves at nodes 1, 3, and 5; B moves at nodes 2, 4, and 6. At each node the player whose turn it is either TAKES (ending the game) or PASSES (moving to the next node). Terminal payoffs (A, B), indexed by the node at which the game ends:

- Take at node 1: (2, 0)
- Take at node 2: (1, 4)
- Take at node 3: (8, 3)
- Take at node 4: (6, 12)
- Take at node 5: (25, 10)
- Take at node 6: (20, 50)
- Pass at node 6 (game ends by itself): (30, 40)

(a) [6 pts] Give the iterative definition of common knowledge. Explain why mutual knowledge of order n, for any finite n, does not amount to common knowledge. Illustrate with an example — the coordinated attack / email confirmation setup is fine, or make up your own.

(b) [8 pts] State the standard result connecting common knowledge of rationality to the backward induction outcome in finite games of perfect information, and sketch the argument for this game. Be explicit about what has to be assumed at decision nodes that are never reached.

(c) [6 pts] Discuss the following objection: if common knowledge of rationality really entailed the backward induction outcome, then actually seeing your opponent pass would be evidence against common knowledge of rationality. So the result is either empty or self-undermining — it only tells you what happens in the case its own assumptions rule out.

(d) [5 pts] Two agents have a common prior over a finite state space, and it is common knowledge between them that the first agent's posterior on event E is 0.6 while the second's is 0.3. What does Aumann's agreement theorem say about this situation? Is the common prior assumption an extra assumption, or does it follow from common knowledge of rationality?

STUDENT ANSWER

(a) An event E is common knowledge when everyone knows E, everyone knows that everyone knows E, everyone knows that everyone knows that everyone knows E, and so on for every finite order — common knowledge is the whole infinite hierarchy, not any level of it. Mutual knowledge of order n stops somewhere: the chain holds up to level n and breaks at n+1, and for any finite n you can build a case like that. The email example is the standard illustration: A sends "attack at dawn," B confirms, A confirms the confirmation, and after any finite number of confirmations there is always a last message whose receipt wasn't confirmed. So the state where attacking is worthwhile becomes mutual knowledge to arbitrarily high finite order as the chain gets long, but it never becomes common knowledge — and the punchline of that example is that the unique equilibrium is for both players to sit tight no matter how many confirmations get sent, which shows the difference is doing real work and isn't just pedantry.

(b) Rationality here means: choose the action that maximizes expected payoff given your beliefs. Common knowledge of rationality, plus common knowledge of the game tree and payoffs, gives the backward induction outcome. At node 6, B takes: 50 > 40. At node 5, A knows (because rationality is common knowledge) that B would take at 6, so passing gives A 20 while taking gives 25; a rational A takes. At node 4, B knows A would take at 5, so B compares 12 to 10 and takes. Same at node 3 (A: 8 > 6), node 2 (B: 4 > 3), and node 1 (A: 2 > 1). So the outcome is A takes at the first node and the payoff is (2, 0) — which is also the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium outcome, since the same induction pins down play in every subgame.

The important subtlety is that almost all of these nodes never get reached on the path we just derived. The induction assigns behavior at nodes 2 through 6 counterfactually, so common knowledge of rationality has to cover what each player would do if a node that the argument itself says won't be reached were reached. You can't run the argument using rationality at reached nodes only: on the backward induction path there is exactly one reached node, so there'd be nothing to induct over.

The argument also doesn't really depend on perfect information. In any finite game each player can run this reasoning in their own head, and since the reasoning is common knowledge they all arrive at the same conclusion, so common knowledge of rationality selects a single outcome in any finite game, simultaneous moves included. In a pure coordination game, for instance, where both players just want to match, common knowledge of rationality forces them onto the same option.

(c) The objection sounds strong but it's mostly confused, though it does point at something real. The result is a conditional: if common knowledge of rationality and of the game holds at the start, then the path is the backward induction path. Since the antecedent entails that nobody ever passes, observing a pass is inconsistent with the antecedent — so the result can't be refuted by data, but it also isn't in the business of predicting a pass. That's not the same as being empty: it tells you that the package of assumptions is inconsistent with what people actually do in centipede experiments, so something in the package has to go, which is a real finding about the assumptions rather than about the world.

The part of the objection that bites is the status of the counterfactual nodes. The proof needs claims like "if node 5 were reached, A would be rational there," but common knowledge of rationality entails that node 5 is never reached, so those conditionals are vacuously true and the induction is better thought of as a fixed point of the assumption across all nodes than as a body of evidence about any node. So the "paradox" dissolves into a point about what kind of claim the theorem is: it's a statement about the model's implications, not a prediction, and the model rules out the data.

(d) The agreement theorem says that if two agents share a common prior and their posteriors on E are common knowledge, then those posteriors are equal. So the situation described isn't improbable, it's impossible: there's no state of the world at which A's posterior is 0.6 and B's is 0.3 and both are common knowledge, given a common prior. So 0.6 and 0.3 means at least one of the conditions failed.

The common prior assumption is doing work, but it isn't an additional assumption on top of rationality — it just is common knowledge of rationality applied to belief formation. If both agents are rational and each knows the other is rational, and so on, then they have to process the evidence they share in the same way, and that's exactly what a shared prior says. So the theorem shows that any persistent disagreement has to come from irrationality, or from a failure to actually communicate: two rational agents who have told each other their posteriors cannot agree to disagree.
