I spent my career as an entomologist, first in insect physiology and latterly in pest ecology, and since retiring I have been reading into moral philosophy — where, I have to say, I find the standards of argument harder to pin down than in my own field. Last month I attended a small workshop on welfare standards for farmed insects, and two of the participants fell into a dispute that I have not been able to put out of my mind. I know the biology well enough and the philosophy not nearly well enough, and I could not tell who was getting the better of it. I would be grateful for a careful adjudication.

The two were Miriam, a welfare economist who advises on husbandry standards for insect-farming operations, and Anselm, a philosopher of biology. Roughly reconstructed, the exchange ran as follows.

(1) Miriam opened with what she called the scale argument: there are on the order of a quintillion individual insects alive at any moment, and commercial insect operations already kill numbers in the trillions; even if one grants each insect a thousandth of a vertebrate's moral weight and only a one-in-ten chance of being sentient at all, the expected-value arithmetic dwarfs every other item on the animal-welfare agenda; and because standards for a young industry are being written now, the intervention is unusually tractable and unusually cheap to influence.

(2) Anselm replied that this is the fanaticism problem in a new costume. The same multiplication can be run on soil nematodes and on bacteria, which are more numerous by many orders of magnitude; if very small probabilities combined with very large populations are allowed to dominate, then one's conclusions are fixed entirely by whichever candidate population happens to be largest, and no evidence anyone could actually gather would make any difference to what one should do. Until a principled stopping rule is produced, he said, the arithmetic is not an argument at all but a way of laundering a prior commitment into a number.

(3) Miriam answered that the probabilities are neither tiny nor arbitrarily assigned, and offered the stopping rule he had asked for: a three-part marker profile. A system receives non-zero weight only if it shows (i) nociception with sensitization rather than a bare reflex, (ii) flexible avoidance behaviour that shifts with motivational trade-offs, and (iii) endogenous modulation of the response of the sort that makes analgesia possible. Bacteria and nematodes fail all three; most adult insects, on the evidence as she reads it, pass. She then volunteered a correction against her own case: at any given moment most insects alive are eggs, larvae or nymphs, and if sentience requires a developed central brain, then the relevant population is the adults alone — which, she conceded, lowers her effective N by two or three orders of magnitude. She maintained that the conclusion is entirely unaffected by this.

(4) Anselm made three points in return. First, that what she calls a probability is not a probability: the disagreement is not about how much evidence there is for a single well-defined property, but about which theory of consciousness is true, and there is no agreed distribution over theories — under one theory nociception plus valenced learning suffices, under another something like recurrent global broadcasting is required, and her marker profile simply encodes the theory he doubts. Second, her demography correction is unstable: if immature stages matter at all they vastly outnumber adults, so she can deflate her N only by assuming the very claim in dispute, and cannot then turn around and describe the result as robust. Third, even granting sentience, the weight to be attached to an experience should scale with the richness of the system that carries it — a fly's few hundred thousand neurons against a vertebrate's tens of billions — so the per-capita discount should be several orders of magnitude deeper than the thousandth she began with.

(5) Miriam said the third point conflates the richness of a life with the urgency of a unit of suffering: a system capable of agony at all can be in full agony, and the intensity of a pain is not a function of neuron count. In any case, she said, her conclusion is robust across the entire plausible range: it survives per-capita discounts far more severe than his, and someone who wants to set insects aside altogether must show that the probability of sentience is lower than that robustness range permits — which, she said, nobody has done and probably nobody can.

(6) Anselm then raised what he called the sign problem. If insects have welfare at all, the populations in question are r-selected: the overwhelming majority of the individuals born starve, desiccate, are parasitized or are eaten before maturity. The aggregate is therefore not merely large but very likely dominated by lives that are net negative, so the honest practical content of taking insects seriously is a reduction in insect abundance — which in the wild means fewer hedgerows, fewer flower margins, more paving. He offered this as a reductio: a framework whose upshot is a policy that no one at the table would endorse, and which its adherents conspicuously decline to state.

(7) Miriam conceded the sign problem but said it separates the levers rather than refuting the framework. For wild populations held near carrying capacity, deaths per unit time are fixed by recruitment, so improving the fate of juveniles while leaving the equilibrium population unchanged may not reduce the total at all; the lever there really is population size, and he is right that the framework's implications for wild insects are ones almost everyone rejects. Farmed insects are different: those populations exist only because we breed them, and not breeding them reduces the total without generating any conflict with conservation — which is precisely why the standards now being drafted are the right target. That, she said, is a reason to take the question seriously, not a reason to shelve it.

There the exchange ended, with neither conceding and the rest of us unable to referee it.

Please go through the exchange move by move — Miriam's four contributions and Anselm's three — and say for each whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. I am especially interested in the places where a move succeeds only by presupposing what is at issue, where two moves talk past each other because they are using a term differently, and where a point is genuinely strong but does less work than its author takes it to do. Then give me your view on who is more right overall, and on what, if anything, both parties are missing: is the deadlock ultimately empirical, or conceptual, or is it a dispute about how to aggregate value across individuals at all? And if you can say what kind of result — an experiment, a formal result, a conceptual clarification — would actually break it, I would value that too. I am less concerned with which policy I should favour than with where the arguments themselves break.
