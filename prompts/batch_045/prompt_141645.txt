Here's my half-formed idea, which I tried out on some old colleagues over Friday beers and they mostly thought I'd had too many. In my old world, uncertainties get evaluated two ways: type A, by statistical analysis of a series of repeat observations, and type B, by everything else — calibration certificates, manufacturer's specs, handbooks, and plain engineering judgement about things like a temperature gradient in the room. The odd thing, as I remember it, is that the official guidance tells you to convert both into a standard uncertainty and then propagate them together as if they were the same kind of animal, even though one looks like a frequency and the other looks like an opinion. None of the three interpretations we read seems to fit the type B case: it isn't my personal degree of belief, because it's auditable, you can be held to be wrong about it, and two labs have to be able to converge on it; it isn't a long-run frequency of anything, since nobody's repeating the world; and a propensity of the measured object looks wrong too, because the mass of a kilogram isn't chancy. My hunch is that the right home for it is a propensity of the *procedure* — a dispositional property of the measurement method, not of the object and not of the measurer — and that the single measurement result inherits its uncertainty from the method's repeatable behavior.
