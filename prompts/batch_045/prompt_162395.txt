After a long argument with a friend last night — we were fighting about who should get a vote on the city's proposed coastal flood barrier, which shifts surge risk onto an upstream municipality outside the city limits — I tried to write down the general position I found myself defending, and I'm no longer sure it holds together. Roughly:

1. Where a collective decision puts someone's significant interests at stake, that person has a pro tanto claim to a say in it.
2. But "affected" has to be read counterfactually over options rather than over welfare: you are affected just in case the decision changes what you can do, not merely how well off you end up. And I also want to say that the real ground of the claim is that the outcome will be imposed on you — you cannot opt out — with affectedness serving only as evidence of that.
3. The constituency should be drawn issue by issue rather than fixed once and for all at a territorial border.
4. Political equality matters, and what it requires is equal power over the whole set of decisions that shape your life, not merely equal weight within each individual decision.
5. Decisions about the franchise itself are legitimately made by those already enfranchised.
6. Children and adults with severe cognitive disabilities are rightly excluded from voting, with guardians answering for their interests instead.
7. On the barrier, I defended consultation and a duty to give reasons for the upstream residents but no vote — even though I would give full votes to long-term non-citizen residents of the city itself.

Which of these actually collide, and how badly? I want the tensions ranked by severity, each with what I would have to give up to resolve it, and I want to be told which apparent collisions are not real ones, because I would rather not abandon a commitment I am entitled to keep. Finally, if there is one revision that does the most work across the set, say what it is and what it costs me.
