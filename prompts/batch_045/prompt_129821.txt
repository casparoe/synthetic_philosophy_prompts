I'm working on a project that assumes an infinitely large universe containing infinitely many lives, and I've settled on what I call the density criterion: rank outcomes by the limiting fraction, within ever-larger regions centred on the agent's own location, of locations whose welfare falls below a fixed threshold — the worse outcome is the one with the higher limit — and where that limit fails to exist, extend the ranking with an ultrafilter-style limiting device. Its appeal is that it sidesteps the standard infinite-aggregation paradoxes: infinite totals that cannot be compared at all, and the familiar impossibility of combining an impartiality-style equity condition with a Pareto condition across infinitely many locations.

What would count against it? I want the strongest defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: verdicts that flip under a different way of exhausting the universe, or under relabelling or finite duplication of locations; the worry that a criterion anchored on where the agent stands smuggles back in exactly the indexical partiality that impartiality was meant to exclude; the fact that it is deaf both to Pareto improvements confined to density-zero regions and to the question whether a sparse infinite improvement of enormous intensity should ever outweigh a dense mild one; and cases where the limit simply does not converge and the extension looks arbitrary. For each, say whether a defender of a density-style view should concede it as decisive or can reasonably treat it as a cost of doing business in an infinite world, and whether the view survives only by multiplying qualifications to the point where it no longer says much. I'd also like to know what it predicts that its rivals — aggregative approaches using extended or infinitesimal number systems, and approaches that compare only finite differences — do not. Where I would personally be most moved: if the ordering can be reversed by a mere redescription of the universe rather than a change in it, I would count that as refutation rather than as an acceptable cost.
