**Question 4 (25 marks). Answer all parts.**

In the seminar we distinguished three theses. Please keep them separate.

**(1) The ambiguity thesis (two operators).** Indicative and counterfactual conditionals differ in truth conditions. English has two conditional constructions — or one construction plus a semantically significant difference in tense and mood — and the two are evaluated with respect to different domains of worlds.

**(2) The unified thesis (one operator), in Kratzer's form.** There is a single conditional construction. The *if*-clause restricts a covert modal: a contextually supplied modal base supplies a set of worlds, and a contextually supplied ordering source ranks them; the conditional says that the consequent holds throughout the most highly ranked worlds in which the antecedent holds. On this view the indicative/counterfactual contrast reflects a difference in which modal base the context makes available, not a difference in the connective. Note carefully that this is a *semantic* thesis about the context-sensitivity of truth conditions. It is not the pragmatic thesis that there is one meaning which speakers put to different uses.

**(3) Adams' thesis (assertibility).** For a speaker with credence function P, the assertibility of an *indicative* conditional "If A, B" goes with P(B|A). Two caveats from the seminar. (a) The thesis is stated for indicatives; extending it to counterfactuals is a further and contested step, not something that follows from the thesis itself. (b) Lewis's triviality result shows that (3) cannot be converted into the claim that the probability of the proposition expressed by the conditional equals P(B|A), on pain of collapsing the conditional into the material conditional. (3) is a thesis about assertibility, not about truth conditions.

**The case.** A panel of three judges — Ferrand, Quill and Ruth — decides a motion. The motion carries if and only if at least two judges vote yes. The outcome is announced publicly: the motion carried, 2 votes to 1. The individual votes are sealed. Dana has not seen them. She is probabilistically coherent; she knows the rule and the announced result; and, subject to what each variant adds, every assignment of votes compatible with her evidence is equally likely for her. (The three assignments compatible with a 2–1 result in favour are: Ferrand yes, Quill yes, Ruth no; Ferrand yes, Quill no, Ruth yes; Ferrand no, Quill yes, Ruth yes.) Assume also, as we did in the seminar, that the semantics is strongly centred, so that where the antecedent is in fact true the conditional is true just in case its consequent is.

**(a)** [4 marks] Consider the indicative: *If Ferrand voted no, then Quill and Ruth both voted yes.* What is its assertibility for Dana, and why?

**(b)** [7 marks] **Variant (i).** Dana also knows that the three judges voted independently: no judge's vote influenced, or was influenced by, any other's, and there is no standing arrangement among them. Assume that the closest worlds in which Ferrand votes no are worlds in which the other two judges vote as they in fact did. Consider the counterfactual: *If Ferrand had voted no, then Quill and Ruth would both have voted yes.* Say, for each of the three assignments, whether it is true, and hence what Dana's credence in it should be. Then explain in two or three sentences why the verdict here differs from the verdict in (a).

**(c)** [6 marks] **Variant (ii).** As in (i), except that Dana now learns two further things: Quill had publicly declared before the vote that she would vote yes whatever anyone else did; and Ruth and Ferrand were paired under the court's pairing convention, which is reliably observed and under which a judge whose partner dissents votes yes. Recompute Dana's verdicts on the indicative and on the counterfactual. Say what has changed between the variants and what has not.

**(d)** [8 marks] What does the pair of variants show about theses (1), (2) and (3)? In particular: does the case tell against extending Adams' thesis to counterfactuals? And does it decide between the ambiguity thesis and the unified thesis?

**Candidate's answer.**

**(a)** Dana's assertibility for "If Ferrand voted no, then Quill and Ruth both voted yes" is maximal. Conditional on Ferrand's having voted no, the announced 2–1 result can only have been produced by both of the others voting yes, and Dana is certain of that result; so P(Quill and Ruth both yes | Ferrand no) = 1. Note that this is a case where the conditional probability is 1 even though Dana is far from certain of the antecedent: her credence that Ferrand voted no is only 1/3. Adams' thesis concerns the conditional probability, not the probability of the conditional, and Lewis's triviality result shows that the two cannot in general be identified, on pain of collapsing the conditional into the material conditional. The proviso that the antecedent have positive probability is satisfied here, so the thesis applies without strain.

**(b)** In variant (i) the judges vote independently, so the closest worlds in which Ferrand votes no are worlds in which the other two votes stand as they actually were. Take the three assignments in turn. If Ferrand and Quill voted yes and Ruth no, then in the closest worlds where Ferrand votes no we still have Quill yes and Ruth no: the motion fails, and the counterfactual is false. If Ferrand and Ruth voted yes and Quill no, the same reasoning makes it false again. Only if Ferrand was himself the dissenter, with Quill and Ruth both voting yes, is it true — and then it is true trivially, because the antecedent is already satisfied. So the counterfactual is false, and Dana's credence in it should be 1/3, since it is true in exactly one of the three assignments she cannot tell apart and she has no reason to prefer any of them.

The reason the two verdicts come apart is that conditionalizing and counterfactual revision are different operations. When Dana conditionalizes on Ferrand's voting no, everything she is certain of is carried along, and she is certain of the result; the result then forces the remaining yes votes onto the other two judges. When she evaluates the counterfactual, the result is not held fixed — it is precisely what is in question — and on the independence assumption Ferrand's no-vote changes it. The indicative inherits Dana's certainty; the counterfactual gives it up.

**(c)** For the indicative, nothing changes. Dana is still certain of the result, so the conditional probability is still 1. For the counterfactual, everything changes. Quill is known to have voted yes whatever happened, and Ruth's vote is tied to Ferrand's by the pairing convention, so in the closest worlds in which Ferrand votes no both of the others vote yes: the counterfactual is true, and Dana can now be certain of it. What varies between the variants is only the dependency structure among the three votes — whether Ferrand's vote is independent of the others or compensated for by Ruth's. That is exactly the sort of fact a conditional probability cannot see. Dana's P(Quill and Ruth both yes | Ferrand no) is 1 in both variants, because it is pinned down by her certainty about the result; the counterfactual tracks the dependency structure and the conditional probability does not.

**(d)** Two morals. First, the case tells against extending Adams' thesis to counterfactuals. Adams' thesis is a thesis about the assertibility of indicatives, and the assertibility of an indicative goes with a probability that is blind to the very dependency structure the counterfactual is sensitive to. If we simply read the assertibility of a counterfactual off P(B|A), the difference between variant (i) and variant (ii) disappears, and with it the difference between a counterfactual Dana should reject and one she should accept. So the extension fails.

Second, the case supports the ambiguity thesis. In variant (i) Dana has a single body of evidence, in a single context, and the two conditionals — same antecedent, same consequent — come apart: the indicative is maximally assertible and the counterfactual is not assertible at all. That is a divergence in truth conditions, and no appeal to context can account for it, since there is one context here and one speaker. On the unified account there is just one conditional construction, and the indicative/counterfactual contrast would have to be pragmatic: the hearer works out from the speaker's evidential situation which of the two the speaker is getting at. But that cannot be right either, because Dana's evidential situation is the same in the two variants, and what changes is how the world is, not what she is conveying. The data therefore favour two operators, an indicative evaluated against an epistemic domain and a counterfactual evaluated against a circumstantial one.

The case does not, I think, show that Adams' thesis is false. It is a thesis about assertibility, and the assertibility data are exactly what it predicts. What the case shows is that assertibility is not the whole story about conditionals.
