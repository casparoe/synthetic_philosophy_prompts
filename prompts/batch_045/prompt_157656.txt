For the past two years, since retiring from a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have been attending a philosophy reading group run through the university's continuing-education programme: ten of us, fortnightly, mostly retired scientists plus one former philosopher who chairs the sessions. Eight months ago we spent two meetings on the Sleeping Beauty problem — Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday, a fair coin is flipped, she is woken on Monday if it lands heads and on both Monday and Tuesday if it lands tails, a drug erases each awakening from the next, and at every awakening she is asked for her credence that the coin landed heads. Two members have been arguing about it ever since: one a committed halfer, one a committed thirder. They are now plainly talking past each other — the halfer keeps returning to the point that Beauty learns nothing new when she wakes, the thirder keeps returning to the point that two of the three possible awakenings occur after tails, and neither has shifted by the smallest amount. What I would like from you is the document they should have produced instead: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the two positions. I do not want a verdict on which of them is right, and I do not want a bibliography or a reading list; I can chase the literature myself.

Three things from our arguments seem to me to be doing real work, and I would like the statement to build on them rather than start from scratch. First, my own instinct — and that of a retired statistician friend — is that this is a sampling-bias problem of exactly the kind I met in my working life: if you run the protocol many times and then sample one awakening uniformly at random, you draw a tails-awakening twice as often, which has the same structure as length-biased sampling or the inspection paradox, where sampling by intervals rather than by instants over-weights the long ones. The halfer replies that the two tails awakenings are not independent replicates but one experimental run measured twice, so the exchangeability the thirder needs fails: the tails pair is perfectly correlated in a way that three genuinely independent draws would not be. I find that exchange the most clarifying thing anyone has said so far, but I cannot tell whether it settles the question or merely restates it in the vocabulary of my old field, and I would like the statement to say which. Second, I have been pushing a variant of my own at the group: suppose that on tails, instead of waking Beauty twice, an exact duplicate of her is created while she sleeps, and each of the two women is then woken exactly once in a subjectively identical room, neither knowing whether she is the original or the copy, each asked for her credence in heads. If a position's answer changes between that case and the standard one, then something in it is tracking temporal self-location rather than self-location among persons, and I want that difference pinned down rather than gestured at. Third, at every awakening Beauty could be asked two separate questions — what is your credence that the coin landed heads, and at what price would you be indifferent to a ticket that pays one unit if the coin landed heads and nothing otherwise. Both camps in our group agree that the second question has a determinate answer and they agree what it is, which makes the relation between credence and betting odds a place where their disagreement should become measurable; use that probe, or a better one of your own, as one of the agreed tests.

Concretely, I would like: (1) a numbered section of claims both camps can sign — thin enough that neither would object, substantial enough that they are not platitudes, and I would rather have eight claims they would genuinely sign than twenty padded out with truisms; (2) a disagreements table with a column for the disputed proposition, one for the halfer's own preferred formulation of her view, one for the thirder's, one for a neutral restatement, and one saying whether that disagreement is genuine or merely verbal; (3) a list of the cruxes, by which I mean the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of the two — my guess is that there are about four, concerning whether self-locating or centred propositions can be objects of credence and whether an indifference principle may be applied to them, whether the three awakenings may be treated as exchangeable samples given the perfect correlation of the tails pair, whether credence must coincide with betting odds in a case like this, and whether a probability claim of this form needs a time-index or a centre at all — and, for each crux, the case, argument or statistic both sides would accept as bearing on it, together with each side's prediction of what it will show, recorded in advance the way one pre-registers a hypothesis; (4) a short closing section on what each side would count as evidence against its own view, whether there is anything both would agree would actually move the debate, and which formulations you expect one side or the other to refuse to sign and why. Please keep the whole thing in neutral wording, flag explicitly any place where you suspect the dispute is only about words, and do not smuggle in a verdict. Finally, since I intend to read this aloud to two people who will scrutinise every line, mark each substantive claim with how confident you are in it and close with a short paragraph naming the single point you are least sure of.
