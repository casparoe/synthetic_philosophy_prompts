On Saturday I have to give the eulogy for Hugh, who taught philosophy at this university for thirty-eight years, supervised my dissertation, and was to the end an entirely serious skeptic about the external world. Not a professional speciality and not a debating posture: he thought the dream argument had never been answered, that nobody knows that there is an external world, and that the famous Moorean gesture — holding up first one hand and then the other, and offering that as a proof that external objects exist — was the most overrated move in the history of the subject. His objection was always the same one. Someone in the grip of the skeptical worry can simply redeploy it one step earlier, against the premise: if I may be dreaming, then I may be dreaming that I have hands. He did not think Moore had produced a proof. He did not think Moore had produced even an argument.

I disagreed with him for twenty-five years, and said so in print. My position is the one that now goes by the name of dogmatism: a perceptual experience as of a hand can confer immediate, non-inferential justification on the belief that there is a hand, so that the skeptic's demand for an antecedent reason is misplaced rather than unanswered. But I also think the position collapses as soon as it is converted into an argument, because it invites the easy-knowledge problem: if the belief that I have a hand can be my reason for believing that there is an external world, then it looks as if it can equally be my reason for believing that I am not a brain in a vat, and no one should be able to get that for free. So I hold that Moore was right about what we know and wrong to think he had proved it. Hugh found this comic, to the last.

He once told several people, including me, that if I stood up at his funeral and held up my hands he would come back and haunt me. Part of the joke was that he did not believe in ghosts.

The room will be split roughly in half. On one side: his daughter, his two sisters, who are devout and will want something in the eulogy they can pray with, and a great many people who have no idea what any of the above is about and to whom I owe the ordinary thing a eulogy owes a family. On the other side: two dozen philosophers, perhaps a third of whom thought his skepticism was a thirty-year indulgence and who will be listening — I am certain of this — for whether I settle the argument now that he is not there to answer.

What I want from you is the eulogy itself: roughly seven hundred words, about five minutes read aloud. These are the constraints as I see them, and I would rather you argued with them than obeyed them.

It must contain no refutation: not the hand gesture, not a compressed demonstration that the dream argument fails, nothing that reads as and now, at last, he cannot object. It must not become a short lecture on why he was wrong, and it must not make anything of the fact that a stroke took the use of his right hand two years ago, so that he could not have performed the gesture himself by the end. He joked about that; he would have loathed its being made into an emblem.

Equally, it must not flatter him with a sympathetic account of his skepticism that implies I have come round to it. That is a different disloyalty, and the philosophers will hear it in a sentence.

It has to praise him in a way he would have accepted. He was notably unwilling to be praised for sincerity, for intellectual courage, or for having followed the argument wherever it led; he thought those are things one says about someone whose conclusions one does not share. What he would have accepted is praise for what he did: for forty years he answered, at length and by hand, everyone who wrote to disagree with him, and he never once used a letter to score a point.

And it has to give his family somewhere to stand without asserting anything false by his lights or by mine. No claims about what he is undergoing now, no claim that he now knows anything, no claim that he is anywhere, and in particular no claim that he is at peace. But I also do not want his daughter to experience the eulogy as being told that her father is simply gone and that that is all. I suspect that tension is the actual problem the speech has to solve rather than a detail to be managed. If you think it can be solved, solve it. If you think it cannot, show me what a eulogy looks like that fails in the right direction.
