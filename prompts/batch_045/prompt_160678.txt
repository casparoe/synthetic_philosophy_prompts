ok so I need help with an argument I got handed at a reading group and I cant find the exit.

I've been defending the knowledge norm of assertion — you may assert p only if you know p — and Jonas wrote this on the back of a handout on the way out. Its short and I think it either refutes me or refutes everything, and I cant tell which.

(1) Permissible assertion requires knowledge: you may assert p only if you know p.
(2) Knowledge is sufficient too, apart from the usual side-constraints about relevance and stakes: if you know p, you may assert p.
(3) Take a 300 page monograph. For every claim c in it, the author knows c. She checked each one separately, on independent grounds, the way people actually do.
(4) She also knows, from base rates about herself and about monographs of that length, that at least one claim in the book is false.
(5) Call this the bridge principle: if each assertion on a list is individually permissible, then making all of them, one after another, to the same audience, in one stretch of discourse, is permissible as well.
(6) So she may assert all 300 claims in one go — a lecture, an audiobook recording, a signed affidavit, take your pick.
(7) But what that stretch of discourse asserts — what her audience is in a position to take her to be committing to, what they would write down afterwards as what she told them — is the conjunction of the 300 claims.
(8) She knows that conjunction is false, since she knows at least one conjunct is false. (If she reads the preface aloud too, the whole performance asserts an outright contradiction, which is certainly not whats going on when someone gives a book talk.)
(9) So by (1) she may not make that stretch of discourse.
(10) So an ordinary, careful, blameless lecture is permissible and not permissible, and the verdict flips if she puts a semicolon where I put a full stop, or splits it into two talks.

Jonas says it gets worse: nothing in the argument uses anything special about knowledge, so run it with truth, certainty, justification, or reasonable belief in the place of knowledge and it sinks those too. Which would mean the argument doesn't show that knowledge is the wrong norm — it shows there is no norm of assertion at all, which can't be right either.

I'd give up almost anything before (1) and (2), and I don't want to give up (3) and (4) together, because the preface situation seems to me real and not a trick.
