I'm a high school senior, and I help run a small replication club at my school: each term we pick one published result from social psychology that looks fragile, redo it with a fresh sample of students, and write up whether it held up. Reading about the replication crisis on my own, I think I've figured out the mechanism behind most of it. Researchers used frequentist null-hypothesis tests, and those are invalid when you look at the data before deciding whether to keep collecting — my statistics teacher called this data peeking and said the 5% error rate is only guaranteed if the sample size is fixed in advance — and even when they didn't peek, a 5% threshold means one in twenty studies of a genuinely null effect will come out significant anyway. So my plan for this term is to switch formalisms rather than patch the old one: instead of a t-test at a fixed n, I'll collect participants in batches of twenty and stop as soon as a Bayesian comparison strongly favours whichever hypothesis is ahead, on the grounds that a Bayesian conclusion depends on the data you actually obtained and not on when you decided to stop looking, so peeking cannot inflate anything. I'll also hold myself to a stricter standard than the usual 5% before calling something a finding, and I've decided not to preregister the design, since as far as I can tell preregistration exists only to fix the frequentist problems of optional stopping and of trying many analyses until one works, and neither of those applies once the inference is Bayesian.

Before I commit to this, I'd like two things checked. First, is my division of labour correct — that frequentist tests are the thing that breaks under flexible stopping while the Bayesian version is safe under any stopping rule I choose, so that switching to Bayes removes both the need to fix n in advance and the need to preregister anything? Second, my club adviser says I have the crisis backwards, and that the largest single cause is that null results rarely get published, so the literature is already a biased sample of everything that was attempted. I don't see how that could touch my own project: I'm not submitting to a journal, I'm running one careful study of my own, so the file-drawer problem looks to me like other people's problem and my inference looks clean. While you're at it, two smaller assumptions I've been relying on: I've been reading strong Bayesian support for a hypothesis as roughly the probability that the hypothesis is true (a factor of 10 in its favour means about 91%, doesn't it?), and I've been treating a replication that fails to reach significance as evidence that the original result was a false positive rather than as an inconclusive outcome.

Please tell me which of these assumptions is doing the real work in my plan and which one is the actual mistake, and explain why the mistaken one fails in terms I could repeat to my club — I'd rather understand the error than be handed a corrected procedure. Assume I know what a p-value, a prior, and a likelihood are but nothing beyond that.
