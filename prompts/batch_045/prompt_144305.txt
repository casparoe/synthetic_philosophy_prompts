Two friends of mine got into a long argument at dinner on Saturday and I've been chewing it over since. I was on my second glass of wine and I've probaly got bits of it wrong, but the shape of it was this.

Earlier that day our friend Nia had asked the group chat a question. She needed the 11:40 from platform 2. She'd checked the timetable app the night before — 11:40, platform 2 — and hadn't looked since. Should she just turn up, or check again? Everyone agreed on the two obvious verdicts: if she's only going into town for a coffee and there's another train in fifteen minutes, she's fine, she knows when her train is. If missing it means missing a one-off job interview she can't reschedule, and the next one isn't for three hours, then she doesn't know, and she'd be an idiot not to check. Nobody disagreed about that, or about the fact that Nia's evidence is exactly the same in both versions. The fight was about what that difference shows.

Ana said it's the word. Knows is context-sensitive, like tall or flat: how good someone's position has to be before it's true to say she knows depends on the standards in play in the conversation. Chatting about the coffee, the bar is low and Nia knows is true. Talking about the interview, the bar is high and Nia doesn't know is true. Both things we said are correct, there's no contradiction, and nothing about Nia changed — so there's no deep puzzle left to explain.

Rhys said that gets the experience of it wrong. When Nia, in the high-stakes version, thinks to herself that she doesn't actually know when the train is and had better look again, she isn't making a remark about the semantics of her own sentence; she's saying something about her own epistemic position. And knowledge is supposed to be the thing that licences action — roughly, you shouldn't act on p unless you know p. On Ana's view, whether Nia may just turn up at the station depends on which conversation the word knows is being used in. On his view — he called it interest-relative invariantism — whether Nia knows is itself partly fixed by how much is riding on it for her, so the norm bites on exactly the thing that matters. Same evidence, different stakes, different knowledge; not just a different meaning of the word.

Ana said that objection only lands if you assume the norm of action is insensitive to context, which is the very thing in dispute: when we ask whether Nia may act, we're assessing her situation, so the relevant context is the one her own stakes set, and contextualism gives the same answer Rhys does. The rest of the time — a stranger with nothing at stake saying offhandedly that she knows — contextualism lets that be true too, which seems right. And she accused him of the same move in reverse: once you let the subject's stakes shift the standard, you've got contextualism with extra machinery and a refusal to admit it.

Rhys came back with what I thought was his best case. On Ana's view the standard isn't set only by stakes, it's set by what's salient in the conversation. So if somebody mentions a specific way Nia could be wrong — the app sometimes serves a cached timetable, say — then Nia doesn't know becomes true even in the coffee version, with nothing about Nia and nothing practical having changed. He thought that made knowledge-attributions hostage to conversational weather. Ana just took it: yes, mentioned possibilities raise the standard, that's the point of the view, and it's how you explain why sceptical arguments feel compelling in a seminar room and irrelevant five minutes later.

Then Ana went on the attack with two cases I haven't been able to stop thinking about. (a) Nia is going for the coffee — low stakes. But someone else, Marta, is about to put a lot of money on whether Nia's train is at 11:40, and Marta is the one saying Nia doesn't know. Ana said contextualism looks committed to Marta's stakes raising the standard, so that Marta speaks truly, and that's mad: Nia's epistemic position obviously hasn't been degraded by Marta deciding to bet. It's the subject's stakes that matter, not the speaker's. (b) Flip it: Nia is off to the interview, but the person saying she knows is a bored stranger on the platform with nothing riding on it. Now contextualism says he speaks truly, while Rhys has to say he's simply wrong. Ana thought (a) was the real problem for her own side and (b) an embarrassment for his.

Rhys's answer to (a) was that Marta's stakes are irrelevant to whether Nia knows, though they obviously matter to something else: whether Marta is in a position to rely on Nia's belief. Knowing, and being able to bet your shirt on someone's say-so, are different things, and only the first is what he's offering a theory of. He didn't seem to have much of an answer to (b) beyond shrugging and saying the stranger's remark is false and we let people talk that way out of charity.

Then Ana went after his view: on it, Nia can lose knowledge purely because the world got more demanding, with no change in her evidence, her reliability or the facts. Practical facts look like the wrong kind of thing to partly constitute a status like knowledge. Rhys said that's only a puzzle if you've decided in advance that knowledge is purely a matter of belief tracking truth, that a whole range of statuses we care about are interest-relative — being a good enough reason, being safe enough, being justified in ignoring a risk — and that this is the only way to explain why knowledge is the thing we care about in the first place.

At which point Ana said the whole thing had started to look verbal: they agree about Nia's evidence, about how reliable the app is, about whether she should re-check, about everything you could actually test; all that's left is a fight about a word. Rhys said it isn't verbal at all, because it shows up in what you're allowed to assert and in whether you don't know is a fair rebuke to someone acting on slim evidence — that's substantive, and the two views give different answers.

Sam, who'd been half asleep in the corner, mumbled something about the stakes changing what question Nia is answering rather than how certain she has to be — that in the interview case what she needs to get right is something finer-grained, like that the 11:40 is running today despite the weekend engineering works, not just that the app said 11:40 last night. They talked over him and I couldn't tell whether it was deep or whether he was just drunk.

So: could you go through this move by move and tell me who's right about what, not just who wins overall? I'm especially interested in whether (a) and (b) really cut the way Ana thinks, whether Rhys's knowledge-versus-reliance distinction is a real answer or a dodge, and whether the argument genuinely is verbal by the end. And what both of them are missing, if anything — including whether Sam was onto something. British English please, and assume I know no epistemology beyond what's in this message.
