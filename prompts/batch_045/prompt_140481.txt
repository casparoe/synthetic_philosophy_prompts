Hey — I'm a novelist (three books, no philosophy since a freshman survey), and I've agreed to run a 90-minute session at a writers' residency this fall. About 14 adults, all fiction writers, mixed ages, no shared reading and no homework. I'm mid-draft on a novel about a woman in an 1890s mill town whose husband never forbids her anything — not one thing, in 300 pages — and I keep failing to make readers feel that she isn't free. So I want a role-play, not a lecture: I want the room to be running a household before they realize they're doing political philosophy, and I want them to end up arguing about who in the house is freest and discovering that they each mean something different by 'free'. Here's the bit I can't get right alone: three rounds under three different sets of house rules, and in one round nobody is stopped from doing anything and nobody is threatened, but the character's deepest wants were deliberately formed by someone now dead — she wants exactly what she was raised to want, and gets it. I don't know if that round earns its place or just reads as a parlor trick. So: rules, roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a real but small stake — I was thinking the scenes they write in-character get read at tonight's open mic in an order decided by the group's own verdict. Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the three notions, without me uttering the labels until after they've fought about it. Tell me what'll go wrong the first time (I'm guessing somebody plays the husband as a villian and flattens the whole thing), and be straight about which part of the trio an afternoon of make-believe simply can't deliver — I'd rather hear it now and say it out loud myself. Last thing: flag whichever piece of the design you're least confident in, and tell me what you'd watch for in the room that would make you cut it or rewrite it on the spot.
