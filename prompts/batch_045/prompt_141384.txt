I'm retired now — I spent about thirty-five years doing intracellular recording from identified neurons in a sea slug, mostly voltage-gated channels and how neuromodulators reshape a circuit's output — and in the last couple of years I've been going to a monthly philosophy reading group at the public library, which is full of people who are much better read than me but have never held an electrode. On Tuesday we got onto consciousness and machine intelligence, and one of the regulars, a retired software engineer, kept shutting things down with the line "the brain is just a computer — the mind is the software running on the hardware." He used it twice, in two different directions: first to say that a fine enough simulation of a person would just *be* that person (so uploading is in principle fine, and if you deny it you're doing vitalism), and second to say that all the messy wet stuff I care about — graded potentials, volume transmission, gliotransmission, ephaptic coupling, the fact that a lot of signalling is continuous and diffusive rather than discrete — is mere "implementation detail" that couldn't possibly matter to the science of the mind. I pushed back and he said the slogan was obvious and I was confusing the substrate with the computation, and we left it there.

Two things from my own work keep nagging at me and I want them dealt with rather than waved off. First, what I'll call the granularity question: at what level of description is the computational claim even supposed to hold — single ion channels, single spikes, dendritic integration, population vectors, whole-organism behaviour? Because at the bottom level there is a lot of analogue, noisy, continuous chemistry, and whether you call a given calcium plateau or a neuromodulator concentration a "symbolic state" or a "bit" seems to me to be a choice we make, not a fact in the neuron. Second, what I'll call the gradual substitution question: if you replace my sea slug's neurons one at a time with silicon devices that reproduce each one's input-output behaviour, the animal presumably keeps behaving normally for a while — but at which swap does the system stop implementing the *same* computation and start implementing a different one, and who gets to say? And a related worry: a real circuit only ever sees a tiny fraction of the inputs it could in principle see, and it's noisy, so any claim about what it "would" do off its beaten track is doing a lot of work.

So here's what I'd like, in American English please. Give me a menu — a numbered list — of the distinct precise theses that "the brain is a computer" (and its cousin "it's the pattern, not the substrate") could actually be asserting, running from the trivially true to the wildly strong; I have in mind things like readings on which it's just a claim about realizability or about modelling, readings on which it says something about counterfactual-supporting state transitions, readings on which it's a metaphysical claim about what minds are, and readings on which it collapses because any old rock can be shown to implement any finite machine. For each reading say (a) exactly what it claims, (b) roughly what kind of evidence would settle it, and (c) whether you think it's trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive and shaky, or just false. Then tell me, reading by reading, which ones would actually earn him his two conclusions — that simulation-plus-uploading obviously works, and that the wet chemistry is irrelevant — and which ones he'd be smuggling in. Finally, say which reading you think a person like him is most likely really expressing when he uses the phrase in an argument, and whether that reading survives the granularity and gradual-substitution worries above.
