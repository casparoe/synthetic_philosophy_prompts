I've got a work-in-progress talk in ten days at a political theory department — it's a WIP series, 40 minutes plus 25 of Q&A, title is something like "Practices, Not Tokens". From the RSVP list the room will be: four or five analytic political philosophers who work on liberal neutrality and public reason; two people who write about hate speech and about pornography from a feminist and a CRT angle respectively (they don't cite each other, but they'll both have a problem with me); a First Amendment scholar from the law school who thinks Mill is basically a 19th century meme with a good slogan; and one historian of ideas who knows the texts better than I do and who is coming specifically because she saw my title. I need Q&A prep, not a defence — I've got the paper.

The thesis, short version: the harm principle gets applied to tokens, it should be applied to practices, and once you make that switch it cuts both ways on speech in a way neither side in the free speech argument wants to hear.

Sketch, four moves:

1. The principle has two working parts that pull apart in Mill's own examples — an assignability clause (harm has to be to the definite interests of somebody you can point to) and a care-or-station clause (duties you've taken on, or conditions that make you unable to discharge them). Mill's examples: the excited mob outside the corn dealer's house, the habitual drunkard he talks about restraining, offenses against decency. In the last two there's no assignable victim of a definite harm and he's still fine with coercion. So the principle was never purely assignability-based, whatever the slogan says.

2. Proposal: the unit of assessment should be a practice — a repeatable type of act, evaluated for its general tendency under realistic partial compliance, where the question is whether that tendency runs to the kinds of setback the principle names, and whether regulating tokens is the least liberty-costly lever you have on the practice.

3. Consequence against the absolutist: the corn dealer case is Mill conceding that expression can be coercible when the uptake conditions on the audience side are degraded — the crowd isn't deliberating, so the expression works as a trigger rather than as a reason. Generalise: coordinated, targeted, high-volume expression degrades uptake for the target. So a Millian can regulate the practice and leave every token alone — reply limits, de-amplification, rate limits. Each individual post is harmless; the practice isn't. I'm deliberately not saying the tokens are punishable.

4. Consequence against the other side: nothing here gives you offense, dignity, or silencing as such. Silencing only bites if you can show a practice-level tendency to wreck the conditions under which the target's own speech gets uptake — it has to be cashed out as a harm in the principle's sense, not as a wrong in itself. So most speech codes fail my test and coordinated pile-ons pass it.

Cost, which I'll own in the last five minutes: once the unit is the practice, the principle stops being a side-constraint and becomes a policy test; its not obvious its still Mill's; and on its face it licenses regulating ordinary demagogic political speech, since false political claims in aggregate get people killed. My gate is that the mechanism has to bypass, degrade, or pre-empt the hearer's deliberative uptake rather than work through it. That gate is where I'm softest and I know it.
