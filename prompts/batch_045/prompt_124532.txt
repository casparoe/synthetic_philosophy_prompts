I'm a graduate student in philosophy of biology, and I've been trying to work out exactly what Gould and Lewontin's 1979 critique of the adaptationist programme — the Spandrels of San Marco paper — entitles its defenders to conclude. I reconstructed the critique as an explicit argument, and it now seems to me to prove far more than anyone has ever claimed for it: not that adaptationist practice is often sloppy, but that no claim about the selective history of any trait is ever warranted, in biology or in any other historical science. I can't accept that, and I also can't locate the break. I'd be grateful for a step-by-step diagnosis.

To make the middle premises precise, I'll use four labels for the non-adaptationist explanations a trait might have. They are my own labels, adopted only for the sake of the argument:

- *Passenger*: the trait is a side effect of selection acting on something else — through linkage, pleiotropy, allometry, or developmental coupling.
- *Architectural*: the trait is a geometrical or material consequence of the body plan being built the way it is. This is the spandrel case proper: nobody selected the tapering triangular panels that appear where a dome is mounted on arches; they are what falls out of the architecture.
- *Relocated*: the trait was selected for doing G and only later came to serve its present function F — the sort of thing the term exaptation was introduced for. Note that a relocated trait is still an adaptation on the standard account; it just isn't an adaptation *for F*.
- *Drift*: its presence is not a selection story at all.

My working example, so the argument isn't purely schematic. In the lineage I work on — a group of extinct semiaquatic reptiles — each rib carries a ventral flange of bone. It is present in every individual, it is densely vascularized, and it currently functions as ballast: it increases the animals' density enough to keep them submerged while feeding, and a crude ballast model reproduces its dimensions rather well. Hypothesis A: the flange was selected for ballast. Hypothesis B: the flange is architectural — thicken a rib in the direction required by the shift to suction feeding, and a flange of roughly this shape is what the geometry gives you — and its ballast function is a use found afterward, not a function selected for.

The argument:

(1) A trait T is an adaptation for F only if T's presence in the lineage is explained by selection *for* F: heritable variation in T caused differences in fitness by way of doing F — selection *for* rather than mere selection *of*. If not, T is passenger, architectural, relocated, or drift.

(2) A hypothesis about T's selective history is warranted only if it has won against its serious alternatives, i.e., only if the evidence discriminates it from the passenger, architectural, relocated, and drift hypotheses available for the same trait. This is what I take to be the live core of the critique: an adaptive story offered without any attempt to specify and test such alternatives is a just-so story and deserves no credence.

(3) For any T and any F, alternatives are always available. Development is holistic, everything is correlated with something, and an unobserved constraint can always be posited. So the supply of rival hypotheses is inexhaustible.

(4) Each rival can be elaborated so as to be consistent with all the evidence we have, and with all the evidence of the kinds we can actually obtain for a fossil taxon: morphology, allometry, phylogeny, sedimentology, mechanical modelling. If the fit to the ballast model is cited for A, hypothesis B replies that the geometry which falls out of stiffening the rib happens to be just the geometry ballast requires. If the comparative method is cited, B attributes the cross-taxa correlation to a shared constraint rather than shared selection. Every proposed discriminator is reabsorbed by the alternative.

(5) Hence no obtainable evidence discriminates between an adaptation hypothesis and its rivals.

(6) Hence, by (2), no hypothesis about the selective history of any trait is ever warranted.

(7) And the reasoning is symmetric: for every passenger or architectural story there is an adaptationist shadow rival — some function nobody has yet imagined, in some past environment nobody has reconstructed — generated by the same procedure as in (3) and (4). So the claim that the flange is a spandrel is exactly as unwarranted as the claim that it is an adaptation. The critique devours its own positive cases.

(8) Nothing in (3) through (7) depends on anything peculiar to biology. Every historical science infers unobservable past causes from present traces, and the same recipe — posit a cause that would leave the same traces — generates an inexhaustible supply of uneliminated rivals. So no historical hypothesis is warranted anywhere: not in paleontology, not in cosmology, not in geology or philology.

(9) Therefore either the demand in (2) is one that nothing could ever satisfy, in which case the critique is a purely negative gesture that can withdraw credence from adaptationist claims but can never itself assert that any trait is a spandrel; or it can be satisfied, in which case (3) through (5) are false and adaptationism is in perfectly good shape as practiced.

Here is where I stand. I want to keep (1) — I think the selected-effect account is right, and I think the selection of / selection for contrast is real. I want to keep (2) — the complaint about untold just-so stories is the part of Gould and Lewontin that I find convincing. And I cannot give up the datum that (8) is meant to reduce to absurdity: we really do know that the camera eye is an adaptation for forming images and that trilobites predate dinosaurs. But I also cannot accept (6) or (7), and I don't see how to keep all of this at once.

What I'd like from you:

1. Name the step — or the pair of steps — where the chain fails, and explain why that step looks unexceptionable in a way that makes the failure easy to miss. If the flaw is an equivocation, say which two readings are being traded on.
2. Price the alternatives you considered and rejected. In particular: if I reject (2), can I still say anything substantive against adaptationist practice, or do I lose the critique entirely? If I keep (1) and (2) and reject (4) or (5), what exactly am I committed to claiming about the flange case — which specific kind of evidence would discriminate A from B, and why isn't it reabsorbed?
3. I suspect (7)'s symmetry claim is where the real work is being done, but every way I try to state the asymmetry between adaptationist and passenger hypotheses seems to presuppose the conclusion I want. If the asymmetry is genuine, what does it consist in?
4. Give me the strongest version of a moderate critique that survives your diagnosis — one that condemns bad adaptationist inference without condemning all of it — and tell me what it costs me.

Two further requests. First, argue the case rather than surveying positions: I don't want a map of who has said what about adaptationism, and I can't follow up references, so I'd rather you reason from the flange example than from what any camp has concluded. Second, be explicit about your own confidence: say how confident you are in the diagnosis you give, which single consideration or argument would most move you to locate the flaw in a different step, and where you think your own diagnosis is most likely to be wrong.
