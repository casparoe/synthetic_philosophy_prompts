Hey — long time lurker, been reading this stuff since the old LW days and I've somehow ended up in a job where it's actually load-bearing, which is annoying because it turns out I only ever learned to argue about it, not to do it.

I'm the person who has to sign off on "nudge review" at the company I work for. We make a savings app: you link a debit card, it rounds up your purchases, it sets streaks, it sends you pushes, it has a little plant avatar that visibly wilts if you miss a day. Every change to any of that goes past me before it can ship — pushes, copy, streak mechanics, anything adaptive or personalized, the onboarding flow itself. Call it 15–20 reviews a month. I get about thirty minutes per review, I have to leave a written rationale in the ticket, my head of product can overrule me but has to say why in the same ticket, and legal only ever looks at the things I flag. So the failure mode I'm scared of is the obvious one: I rubber-stamp everything except whichever thing feels gross that week, and the "rationale" is a vibe with a semicolon in it.

What I want is a procedure, not principles. Questions in order. What counts as a sufficient answer to each one. Where it stops with a no. Where it explicitly hands the decision to a human because what's left is a disagreement about values and no amount of procedure will settle it — and what I'm supposed to write in the ticket when I hand it off. It has to be runnable by a pm who has never read any of this and has roughly half an hour, and it has to survive being steelmanned by our growth lead, who is genuinely sharp and will point out (correctly) that every design choice "exploits" some quirk of human psychology, including the ones I like.

The line I actually care about is manipulation vs. legitimate persuasion, and I want the procedure to do real work on it rather than collapsing into "does it change behavior" (of course it does; the whole point of the app is to get people to save money they'd otherwise spend) or into "would you be comfortable telling the user exactly what this does" (our growth team has learned to write disclosure copy that technically tells you everything and changes nothing). Two things I'd like you to build in, since they're the cases that break the tests I've tried:

- I've started calling one failure mode "vulnerability arbitrage": we can see overdraft events and payday dates, and a nudge can work either by giving someone a reason they'd endorse on reflection or by finding a lever — catching them in a hot state, at the hour our model thinks they're most impulsive, with loss-framing. I want the procedure to separate those two cleanly and tell me what evidence I need, and what to do when I can't get it.
- The opt-in for pushes is default-on during onboarding, so half of what I'm reviewing is a flow the user maybe never chose. Does the procedure need a step that audits the consent architecture itself rather than the individual nudge, and is that even mine to decide?

Then run it on three real-ish ones: (1) streaks plus a "streak freeze" you can earn or buy, plus the wilting plant; (2) a push sent the evening of payday saying people like you saved $40 this week, timed by a model that learned each user's most impulsive hour; (3) adaptive copy that switches to a red badge and a specific shortfall number for users our model flags as high overdraft risk this week. Please include at least one case where the procedure says no to something I'd defend and at least one where it clears something I expected it to kill — I want to know if I'm just laundering my intuitions.

Also: our users say in interviews they want the notifications, and 40% of them have turned pushes off within a month. I don't trust either number. Where does the procedure go for evidence about whether people actually endorse this, and what's the fallback?

Finally, be straight with me about what the procedure can't do — where it'll misfire, what it's structurally blind to, and what I should do in the first month to find out whether it's doing anything at all.
