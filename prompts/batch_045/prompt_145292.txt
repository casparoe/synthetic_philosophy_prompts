Me and my friend had a four-hour argument last night that started about free will and ended up, somehow, about the weather. His whole case rested on one analogy: a chaotic system is like a fair coin. The coin's tumble is just ordinary deterministic physics, but nobody tracks it, so for every purpose that matters the flip is random — and, he says, the atmosphere is exactly the same, deterministic underneath and random in practice. His slogan was that "random" always and everywhere means "I dont know the microstate" and nothing more than that.

From that he drew two conclusions he actually cares about. One: chaos theory is not a discovery about nature, it's a discovery about us and our instruments, so it belongs to engineering and not to metaphysics. Two: so none of it touches the real question, which is only whether the fundamental laws are deterministic. If they are, prediction is possible in principle and there's no room for free will; if they aren't, trading determinism for randomness doesn't make room either. Either way chaos drops out and was never load-bearing.

I pushed back with something I half made up on the spot: that with chaos the unpredictability is in the system rather than in our heads, because the rule itself eats digits — to answer a question twice as far out you need exponentially more precision about where you started, and there's a ceiling on how much precision the world can hand you, or on how much a computer sitting inside the world can even represent. He said that's ignorance with extra steps, and that more sensors and a bigger model would push the horizon back indefinitely.

Two side fights we got completely stuck on. He says the butterfly thing means a butterfly really does decide whether a tornado happens, and I said tiny disturbances get damped out by viscosity below some scale, so there has to be a floor on which causes can matter. And he says if weather is chaotic then climate models are trying to do the impossible; I said predicting the statistics of a system looks like a genuinely different job from predicting its exact state, and he said I invented that distinction to win.

Can you audit his analogy properly? I want the correspondences written out — what plays the role of the coin's microstate, the tumbling, the 50/50, the tosser, our ignorance — then where the mapping breaks, and which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion he's using it to support. Specifically: is a string of coin flips even the right shape, given that chaos is deterministic dependence rather than independent trials? Does his "random just means I don't know" slogan survive the fact that a chaotic system will beat you even when you know the exact equations? Is my finite-precision point metaphysics or just engineering? Is there a real distinction between predicting a trajectory and predicting a distribution, and does it rescue climate modelling? And the butterfly: does the scale of the perturbation matter, or is his version fine? Then tell me whether his conclusion — that chaos does no work at all in the free will argument — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the coin was carrying it the whole time. And if there's a better analogy than the coin, give me that instead, because I have to see him again on Friday.
