I do a podcast about science and ideas and I'm editing the episode we recorded on the paradox of fiction — the one that starts with the Anna Karenina paper (Radford and Weston, 1975, if I got the byline right) and then goes through Walton's "Fearing Fictions" reply in 1978. My guest walked through the usual families of response — we only quasi-emote inside a game of make-believe; we really emote but at imaginings or thoughts rather than at beliefs; or there's something like a momentary illusion — and I came away feeling that all three sound great in a studio and none of them obviously tells you what to expect one level up.

Here's what I mean by one level up, and this is the bit I actually want the poem to be about. The puzzle is always set up with a single frame: a reader, a character, a death, "she never existed." But stories contain readers. A woman in a novel weeps over a letter in a novel; I weep over her weeping; and if you ask me why, "she never existed" is now true twice over and somehow no more damaging than it was once. My guest sort of shrugged and said the nesting is just more of the same, but I'm not sure it is — if the make-believe story is right you'd expect each extra frame to thin the response out, and if the thought story is right the thought just gets longer without getting weaker, and those feel like different predictions. I also keep coming back to something from my own reporting: I sobbed at a death in a tv show I'd only half been watching, and twenty minutes later read a real obituary of a stranger and felt basically nothing, which my producer says is the whole problem in one sentence and which I think is probably just me being tired.

So I want to close the episode with a poem, read aloud, under two minutes, where the form does the arguing rather than the vocabulary. My suggestion is a sestina — six stanzas of six, then a three-line envoi with all six end-words in it, short lines, nothing ornamental — because what I can't get out of my head is that in a sestina the words never change and only their positions do, which is more or less the disagreement in miniature. Concretely: six end-words along the lines of real / believe / pretend / imagine / grief / page (swap them if you have better ones), and each stanza climbs exactly one frame — someone weeping over a death in a story, then someone weeping over that weeper, then someone listening to a podcast about it, then me trying to write the poem, then whoever's hearing it, then whatever the sixth one is, I don't mind if it folds back on itself. Don't let it resolve. I don't want a final stanza announcing which theory won, and I really don't want a punchline about how fiction is truer than fact. It should be a poem a listener who's never opened a philosophy paper can follow, and one an aesthetician would say is right rather than just name-droppy. If a sestina is the wrong machine for this, say so in the note and use some other comparably strict repeating constraint — but the repetition has to be structural, not decoration.

After the poem, a short note, 200 words is plenty, with two things in it. First, separate what's settled from what's live: I'm assuming it's settled that people do have emotional responses to things they take to be fictional, that the modern debate starts with those mid-seventies papers, and that there's no agreed solution — tell me if any of that is itself contested — and then say plainly which bits are genuine live disagreement (whether there's a paradox at all once you drop the cognitivist premise, whether the state we're in is real emotion, quasi-emotion, or something thought-sized, and so on), without dressing one side up as the consensus. Second, flag where the poem quietly takes a side or leans on a minority reading, and be blunt if my one-level-up nesting thing is my own hunch rather than something people actually argue about in print — I'd rather cut that section of the episode than have someone write in to say I passed off a hunch as a debate.
