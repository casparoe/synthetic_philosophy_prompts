I'm looking for test cases for the nonidentity problem that don't look anything like the standard ones. In its usual form: a choice can make things worse without being worse *for* anyone, because which individuals exist depends on the choice itself. Had the agent chosen differently, those very people would never have existed at all; and if their lives are worth living, they cannot be said to have been harmed by being brought into existence in worse shape than they might have had. The familiar illustrations all place reproduction itself at the centre of the decision — a teenager's choice about when to have a child, or a society's choice between a wasteful and a frugal energy policy that determines which future generation comes to exist.

Give me an example with the same structure from somewhere the discussion rarely visits: an institution or practice whose purpose has nothing to do with reproduction, whose participants never consider future people, and which nonetheless, merely by reshaping ordinary life, reliably settles which particular individuals later come to exist. Three constraints. (a) It must be genuinely identity-affecting rather than merely circumstance-affecting: lay out the causal chain from the decision to the fact that a numerically different set of gametes — and hence a numerically different person — was involved, and explain why the same individual could not have resulted from the alternative. (b) The judgement it invites must be one people would actually resist giving up: call it a wrong, a loss, an injustice, or at minimum a serious mistake — even though the person-affecting route to that judgement has been closed off. (c) It must be a case where it is genuinely contested whether the nonidentity problem applies at all, so that the puzzle is not simply stipulated into existence.

Then do three things with your example. First, identify which nearby moral complaint actually survives in it — impersonal badness, unfair distribution among the people who do end up existing, a violated duty not to bring lives of a certain kind into existence, or something else — and assess whether that complaint is strong enough to account for the resistance described in (b). Second, deal with the pervasiveness worry: if the causal chain you describe is as fragile as it must be, then nearly any large-scale social policy shifts the timing, place, or pairing of conceptions and is identity-affecting as well; either show how your example avoids sliding into the conclusion that no policy can be criticised, or argue that the slide is real and say what we should abandon instead. Third, note one way in which your example leaves the dialectic clearer — or murkier — than the standard cases do.

Close by stating how confident you are in the central claim you make about the example, and single out the step in your argument you are least sure of.
