One of the guys in my local rationalist-adjacent Slack has an argument that I'm pretty sure proves to much, but I can't locate the bad step and it's been eating at me for a week. It's about the Allais thing. Here it is, cleaned up.

Setup, the usual textbook numbers. Problem 1: A is $1M guaranteed; B is 89% $1M, 10% $5M, 1% $0. Most people take A. Problem 2: C is 89% $0, 11% $1M; D is 90% $0, 10% $5M. Most people take D. But C and D are just A and B with the shared 89% branch swapped from $1M to $0, so independence says you have to pick A-and-C or B-and-D. A-and-D is inconsistent. Call it the Allais pattern.

(1) A norm of instrumental rationality is binding on you only if it has a pragmatic vindication: someone who violates it should be predictably worse off by their own lights. Money pumps, Dutch books, "you end up with less of what you wanted." If all you have is "your preferences don't satisfy my axiom," that isn't a criticism, it's a taste.

(2) The vindication people actually offer for independence is the Allais pump. Run problem 1 as two stages: 89% of the time you're done and collect your million; 11% of the time you go to stage two, where you choose between a guaranteed $1M and a 10/11 shot at $5M (1/11 nothing). Ex ante, the Allais agent pays a dollar to have stage two replaced with the guaranteed million — that just is A over B. But if stage two actually arrives she takes the gamble — that just is D over C. So a bookie sells her the guarantee up front for a dollar, then, conditional on reaching stage two, sells her the gamble back for another dollar. She ends up holding exactly B, minus two dollars, and every trade was one she accepted.

(3) The pump only goes through because her ranking flips once the 89% branch resolves. If her ranking were invariant to whether that branch has resolved — if her preferences depended only on the final probability distribution over dollar amounts, call it Distributionalism — she couldn't flip and there'd be nothing to sell her twice. So the pump requires Distributionalism.

(4) But Distributionalism is not some neutral background assumption the Allais agent shares with the expected-utility theorist. It's a restatement of independence in weaker clothes: it says the only thing about a gamble that can matter is the final odds on the outcomes, and denying that is the entire content of preferring A to B. So the pump doesn't vindicate independence, it presupposes it; and by (1) a norm whose only vindication is circular has no force. So the Allais pattern hasn't been shown to be irrational, and "shut up and multiply" is, at best, a preference people are allowed to decline.

(5) Here's the part I think goes wrong, but I can't say how. This seems to generalize, and that's why it proves to much. Every vindication of every coherence norm is a pump, and pumps are processes in time: they work by getting you to accept a trade at t1 and another at t2, with some chance event resolving in between, or with you now holding a thing you weren't holding before. So transitivity and dominance need Distributionalism too, in the sense that they need your ranking of two options not to shift merely because some chance resolved or because you've come to hold one of them.

(6) Which means the cyclic agent gets the same excuse. She prefers A to B to C to A and pays a penny each time to switch. If we let the Allais agent say "my ranking legitimately changed when the world changed," we have to let her say "my ranking legitimately changed when I came to hold B." Neither is irrational by the standard in (1).

(7) But (6) is insane. Cyclicity is the paradigm case of irrationality, and the cyclic agent loses money with certainty no matter how the world turns out — that pump doesn't obviously need any assumption about resolution at all.

(8) So something in (1)–(6) is wrong. I'd rather keep (4) than give it up, because I don't want to hand the EU people a blank check to call anyone with non-EU preferences irrational; but I also really don't want to end up committed to (6).

One more thing that's maybe relevant and maybe a red herring. I tried to test (4) on four friends using what I call the Opaque Box version: you mark all your choices on a form at the start, one machine resolves everything at the end of the session, and you never learn anything in between — no moment of being "safe," no moment of discovering you're in the 11% branch. The final odds on dollars are identical, obviously, but anecdotally the A-over-B preference seemed to weaken a lot (n=4, lol). Does that tell me anything real about whether (4) is right, or does it only tell me that people are bad at imagining the box?

So: which step do I kill, why did the step look fine, and what does killing it cost me? And if (4) is the step that dies, is there a non-circular argument against the Allais pattern at all, or does the whole thing just bottom out in "here are my axioms"?
