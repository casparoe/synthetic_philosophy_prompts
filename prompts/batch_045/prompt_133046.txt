My diss defends a pretty specific package: an agent's credences should be represented by a convex set of probability functions rather than a single one, and choice should go by something like Γ-maximin over that set instead of expected utility with a mid-point. The bit I keep leaning on is what I've started calling the no-free-precision principle — never represent yourself as having a credence sharper than your evidence can pin down, since any extra sharpness is information you manufactured out of nothing. I use it on the usual cases (urn of unknown composition, a sensor you can't calibrate) but also on peer disagreement.

What I want is the case against it, ranked fatal to merely awkward, and I want the version a committed imprecise probabilist would themselves concede, not the critics' caricature. Four things worry me most: (i) the exactness complaint — does the set just relocate the sharp boundry rather than remove it; (ii) dilation, where conditioning on ambiguous evidence widens the interval, and whether that's a bullet to bite or a reductio; (iii) scoring — if there's no strictly proper scoring rule for set-valued credences, do I lose any usable notion of accuracy or calibration; (iv) collapse — whether maximin-over-a-set is representable as expected utility relative to one prior plus a risk attitude, in which case I've changed the vocabulary and not the theory. Also: is the whole view insulated by the move that the width of the set is a permissive-rationality matter, so that no behaviour could ever refute it — and if so, is that a problem? I'd personally be most moved by something showing the view can't give action-guiding advice in exactly the cases it was built for.
