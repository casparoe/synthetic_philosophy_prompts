I have noticed an argumentative shape that I keep encountering, and I would like to know whether it has been identified and named, because I keep wanting to refer to it and have no word for it.

The shape is this. Someone wants to establish that some endpoint Z is not worse than some starting point A — or, more combatively, that Z is better — even though A and Z, compared directly, strike almost everyone as not close, and as clearly ordered in A's favour. So they decline the direct comparison. Instead they lay out a series of intermediates, A through Z, and ask you to grant one adjacent pair at a time: B is not worse than A; C is not worse than B; and so on to the end. Every individual step is easy to grant. Then comes the move I will call chaining: if the first is not worse than the second, and the second not worse than the third, then the first is not worse than the third — and repeating this along the series yields the verdict about A and Z that no one would have granted up front.

Two instances, which I think are not the same instance.

First, a municipal planning dispute I sat in on. Plan A keeps a small park and changes nothing else. Plan B converts a corner of the park into affordable housing — a real loss of green space, but the committee agreed it was not worse overall. Plan C adds a clinic beside the housing, taking a bit more of the park; again, not worse. By Plan F the park is a paved plaza with a clinic, housing, a bus interchange and a data centre, and no one on the committee would have voted for that over Plan A in a straight vote. Yet every step had been granted, and the chain had been run.

Second, a series of revisions to a translation of a poem. The translator cuts an obscure image in favour of a clearer line; then restores factual accuracy at some cost in clarity; then reworks the rhythm at some cost in accuracy; then tightens the whole thing at some cost in rhythm. Each revision was defended, and accepted, as an improvement — but each on a different ground, and the grounds are the very features the earlier revisions had sacrificed for. The finished version is, I would say, worse than the first, and worse in a way that is not a matter of accumulated tiny changes: each change was a real trade, not an imperceptible nudge.

My questions:

1. Does the chained comparative argument, as a form, have an established name and a literature? I am not after a reading list — I want the concept carved.

2. Is there a standard diagnosis of why the endpoints in the first case come apart, and is it a different diagnosis from the second? My own suspicion is that in the first case the trouble lies in the threshold: the relation being chained is something like 'not detectably worse', and that relation is not the same relation as 'not worse', however comfortable the slide between them feels. In the second case the trouble looks different: each verdict is presented as all-things-considered, but is in fact reached by letting one consideration dominate and then quietly letting a different consideration dominate at the next step, so that nothing is ever weighed against anything across the series as a whole. I want to know whether that is the right distinction, and whether the second failure has a name of its own.

3. Is there a standard term for a comparative relation that holds between any two items one cares to compare, and that each item bears to itself, but that does not survive chaining, so that a long series of 'not worse' links can connect endpoints that are plainly ordered the other way? And is there a formal account of the minimal weakening of chaining that produces this — something that says how much of the stepwise structure has to be given up?

4. I have been using a rough test: try to build the chain in reverse, from the endpoint back to the start, granting each step by the same standard as before. If chains of equal persuasiveness can be built in both directions, I conclude the relation is not doing the work I had assumed. Is that test sound? I can see two worries — that a relation might permit a reverse chain without being genuinely cyclic, and that the reverse chain might succeed only because the intermediates were chosen differently — and I would like to know whether either is fatal.

5. Finally, the boundary. Some chained arguments are clearly fine: if each step removes a defect and adds none, the endpoint really is better, and I would not want a diagnosis that condemns chaining as such. So: what marks the legitimate from the illegitimate case, and how should the pattern be distinguished from (a) cases where the endpoints differ only because a great many negligible differences have accumulated, (b) cases where the items are not genuinely rankable against one another at all, and (c) cases where a group's collective ranking cycles even though every member's own ranking is perfectly well behaved?

I will not be satisfied with a label. I want to know what the phenomenon is, how many distinct phenomena I have bundled together here, what the nearest established concepts are and precisely where each fits and where each falls short, and — if the thing I am describing has no name — a term for it, with a definition and an explicit statement of the cases it is meant to cover and the cases it is meant to exclude.
