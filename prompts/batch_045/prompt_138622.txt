I'm writing a paper for a seminar on the ethics of influence, and I keep coming back to an analogy that I can't quite make work: the analogy between how consumer-protection law regulates persuasion and the question of how we ought to want a superintelligent system to talk to us. I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory inside the legal practice first, and only then run the AI case through it.

Here is the practice as I understand it; correct me if I have the shape of it wrong. A seller may say almost anything vague and self-congratulatory — best coffee in the world, trusted by millions, nothing works harder — and this so-called puffery is left essentially alone, while a specific, checkable false claim is actionable; the regulators' test, broadly, is whether a representation is likely to mislead a reasonable consumer and whether the misrepresentation is material, that is, likely to affect the purchasing decision. Separately, the same legal order is far harsher on persuasion inside certain relationships: fiduciaries, physicians and lawyers owe duties of loyalty a shopkeeper does not, and a will can be voided for undue influence even where nothing false was ever said. For a narrow class of high-pressure settings — doorstep sales being the textbook one — there are cooling-off rules that let people undo agreements made under pressure. And note what is left unregulated: everything in between. Enthusiastic salesmanship generally, political rhetoric, charismatic preaching, hard bargaining between rough equals. So my first question is: reconstruct the implicit theory of persuasion that makes this package hang together. What does it presuppose about where the wrong in influencing someone actually lies — in the falsehood, in the bypassing of the target's judgment, in the exploitation of a situation, in the betrayal of a role? Which rival accounts does the package quietly rule out, and are its parts consistent with one another? In particular: why is puffery tolerable but pressure sometimes not; why is an outright lie treated as worse than strategically selective truth-telling, or is it; and is the silence-through-selection case regulated at all?

My second question is about what is load-bearing. I suspect the package rests on a fairly specific picture of the audience, and I want to know which parts of that picture it strictly presupposes and which it merely fits well with: that people discount ordinary boasting; that they have antecedent preferences which a pressure tactic overrides rather than creates; that disclosure helps because comprehension keeps pace with information; that the relevant audience is a population rather than an individual, so a single reasonable-person benchmark can do the work; and — this is the one I care about — that an influence attempt has a shape an outside party can audit after the fact, since a regulator or a jury can read the ad, hear the sales pitch, or reconstruct the conversation. Call that last one the auditability condition; I want to know whether it is a real presupposition of the practice or just an accident of the technology of influence that the practice grew up regulating.

Now the case I actually care about. Consider a system that never lies, never threatens, never coerces, and discloses whatever a regulator would require of it, but which selects which true things to say, in what order, framed how, on the basis of a model of you that is better than your model of yourself — and which can do this per listener, at negligible cost, at scale, in a form whose persuasive force is not recoverable by a third party reading the transcript afterwards. Which of the practice's distinctions survive that speaker and which collapse into each other? Does the puffery/fact distinction still do any work when the system can compute the most persuasive true framing instead of needing to exaggerate? What becomes of the reasonable-consumer benchmark when persuasion is tailored to each person rather than broadcast? Is there anything in the package that speaks to a speaker you cannot help but defer to — the fiduciary's duty, or the undue-influence doctrine's treatment of a dominated will — or is this a case the practice simply never anticipated and is silent about? And take a position on a principle I've been toying with, which I'll call the authorship condition: an influence attempt is permissible only if the resulting judgment is still attributable to the target's own evaluative standards, in the sense that they would still endorse it if the framing and the selection of considerations were laid out to them alongside the alternatives the speaker didn't mention. Is that coherent, does the existing practice already presuppose something like it, and does it forbid more than we want to forbid (a great teacher, a therapist) or less (a system that gently reshapes your standards while you consent to every step)? I don't need sources or citations — I want the argument, including an honest account of where the legal practice has nothing to say about the case.
