I am a novelist, and a scene in the book I am finishing has led me to a distinction I cannot name, which makes it hard for me to know what I am even claiming.

The scene is this. A man is set to work at a table on which stand a jar and an inexhaustible heap of marbles, every marble stamped with a number. A mechanism governs him. His first act takes half a minute: he places marble 1 in the jar. His second act takes a quarter of a minute: out with marble 1, in with marble 2. His third takes an eighth: out with 2, in with 3, and so on, each act half the length of the one before. The minute passes; the mechanism stops. Between any two of his acts the jar contains exactly one marble. Afterwards it contains none, since marble n was taken out at act n + 1 and no marble is ever returned. There is nothing vague in my stage directions about which marble goes where; each marble has a complete and determinate biography. My readers grant both sentences, and a good many of them then write to say I have cheated.

I am not asking whether the scene is possible, and the two answers I keep being given both seem to me to miss my difficulty. One is that no one can act infinitely often. The other is that my instructions simply do not say what is in the jar at the end, so I am free to put anything there I like. What interests me is that the jar seems to me to be an instance of something I can produce without any machinery at all.

Here is the second instance. I draw a staircase of twenty little steps from one corner of a square to the opposite corner. Its length, measured with a ruler along the treads and risers, is very nearly the side of the square plus the side of the square again. I halve the steps and double their number: the length is unchanged. I do it again: unchanged. The staircase draws closer and closer to the straight diagonal, and there is no question of its not arriving, because nothing is moving; it is only a picture. Yet the diagonal it lies along is measurably shorter than the staircase ever was, by a factor of about seven-tenths. So here too: a quantity which never varies at any stage of the process, and the value of that same quantity for what the process leads to, and the two are not the same. No one says the picture is cheating.

Is my jar and my staircase one phenomenon or two? And is either of them what I think I have also found in a third case, which I suspect is different: when I measure a stretch of rocky coast on maps of larger and larger scale, the length I get grows each time and, as far as I can tell, never settles at all.

What I keep needing, and cannot find, is a name for the gap itself: the gap between the value a quantity holds all along the way, and the value it has at the place the way leads to, in cases where both values are perfectly determinate and are not equal. If there is an established concept for this, I would like it placed against its nearest neighbours and distinguished from them, since I assume there are several nearby notions it is easily confused with. If there is no such concept, I would be grateful for one coined and defined, together with its edges. In particular: can the arrived-at value ever be larger than the value held along the way, or only smaller? Does the jar have something in it that the staircase lacks, beyond the mere fact that one is completed in a minute? And is the coastline the same failure seen from the other end, or a different failure altogether?

A bare word will not help me. I am trying to find out what I have actually written.
