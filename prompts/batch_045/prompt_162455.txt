Next month I'm presenting a dissertation chapter in political theory to a department colloquium, and what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view.

The chapter argues that the franchise should track coercive subjection rather than citizenship or any threshold of political competence, and it defends two institutional conclusions together: lower the voting age to 16, and enfranchise non-citizen residents who pass a residency threshold. The move I'm leaning on is an exit-cost proviso: subjection by itself does not enfranchise, what enfranchises is subjection a person cannot escape at moderate cost, which is supposed to keep tourists, short-term visa holders, and people who could naturalize cheaply outside the demos while still bringing in permanent residents and 16-year-olds.

The room will be: two democratic theorists who hold that the demos should be defined by who is affected by a decision; a consent theorist in the Lockean tradition who holds that political obligations are owed only by those who have opted in; a civic republican who treats voting as an office and an exercise of civic judgment rather than a bare interest; an egalitarian liberal preoccupied with which groups actually turn out; and a political scientist who will want to know what the proposal does to the composition of the electorate.

Give me the seven or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way the person who would raise it would phrase it; a two-minute reply for each with a note on where that reply is soft; a flag on which objections hit the principle itself and which hit only one of my two institutional conclusions; what I should concede in the opening minutes rather than be forced into later; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
