**Question 3 (20 marks: 8 for part (a), 12 for part (b))**

Gould and Lewontin's 1979 critique of the adaptationist programme is often summarised as the claim that many traits are not adaptations. Say that a trait is *secondarily captured* when all of the following hold: the trait first appears as an architectural by-product of selection acting on something else; at its origin it does no work for its bearer; later it is co-opted for some use; and later still, selection on that use modifies the trait, so that its present form is partly a record of selection for that use.

(a) Say what a spandrel is, in Gould and Lewontin's sense, and say whether a secondarily captured trait is, or is not, a case of one. [8]

(b) Assess the following argument. "If secondary capture is possible, the spandrels critique loses most of its force. The adaptationist can grant that the trait began as a by-product and still maintain that its present functional design is the work of selection for its current use — and that is all the adaptationist ever claimed. The critique therefore survives only as a quibble about historical priority." In your assessment, keep separate the claim that a trait's present form is due to selection for a current function and the claim that the trait exists because selection built it for that function, and state what, on Gould and Lewontin's view, an adaptationist would have to establish before a selective account of a trait's *origin* should be accepted. [12]

The student's answer, reproduced verbatim:

---

Gould and Lewontin's target is the adaptationist programme: the habit of cutting an organism into separate traits and supplying a story about what each is for, on the assumption that selection is the only cause of form worth taking seriously. They are not denying that selection is the creative force in evolution, and they are not claiming that most traits are not adaptations. Their objection is methodological. Adaptationists produce plausible stories about what a trait is for, but they rarely test those stories against alternatives, so the stories are sheltered from refutation and the trait's actual history never gets settled. The alternatives that ought to be tested include architectural and developmental constraint, allometry, pleiotropy and drift. The procedure is Panglossian because, whatever the organism turns out to be like, some advantage is always found for it.

(a) A spandrel is a trait that natural selection built for one function and that was later co-opted for a different one. The type case is the spandrels of San Marco: the curved triangular panels that result when a dome is set on four arches, and which were afterwards covered with mosaic decoration. These panels are not incidental to the building — the design calls for them — but the job for which the design calls is not the job they were later made to perform. A spandrel is thus defined by a mismatch between the role a feature was built for and the role it currently plays. On that definition a secondarily captured trait is plainly a spandrel: its present use comes after, and differs from, the use for which it was built.

(b) The argument quoted in the question is essentially correct. If a by-product is later taken up for some use and then modified by selection for that use, then the adaptationist's description of the trait is correct: its present design is the product of selection for what it now does. The critic is left only with the historical point, that the trait was not selected for this use at its first appearance; but that was never what was in dispute. Gould and Lewontin need the distinction between being built for a function and being maintained for one to do real work, and secondary capture is exactly the case that dissolves it, because exaptation followed by selection is just adaptation spread over a longer stretch of time. The longer a trait has been under selection for its current use, the more of its present form is adaptation rather than by-product, and so the more of the organism comes back to the adaptationist.

What remains of the critique is the demand that an adaptationist eliminate every by-product and constraint hypothesis before offering a selective one. Nobody can eliminate all of them in practice, and a secondarily captured trait is an adaptation now; so the burden has moved to the critic, who must show that the trait is still only a by-product. Gould and Lewontin's real claim, then, is not that adaptationist stories have gone untested but that stories about a trait's origin cannot be tested at all — and secondary capture is the case that shows why.
