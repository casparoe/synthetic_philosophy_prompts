My partner and I have been going back and forth about whether to try for a kid this year, and somehow that turned into me reading a lot of population ethics stuff on my own over the summer, and now I've written down where I think I've landed and I want someone to poke at it before I inflict it on my reading group in the fall.

Roughly, the package is: (1) I accept the asymmetry — there's no moral reason to bring a clearly-happy person into existence, but there is a strong one not to bring a clearly-miserable one into existence. And I want that as a claim about reasons, not about value: I don't want to say the world is no better with the happy person in it, I think it is better, and the reason not to create is some kind of person-affecting side-constraint. (2) I think it's wrong, or at least very hard to justify, to create someone whose life will contain a great deal of suffering even if the life is net-positive overall — say decades of severe chronic pain plus enough good stuff that they'd rather go on living than not. My sense is you shouldn't do that to someone, and it doesn't seem to be covered by (1). (3) I think that when a choice affects which child comes to exist, you should still pick the better-off one. Concretely: a medication I'm on means that if we conceive this cycle the resulting kid will very likely have a painful condition, and if we wait two months we'd get (I take it) a numerically different kid who wouldn't; I feel strongly that waiting is the better thing to do, not just optional. (4) I think we owe future people an enormous amount about climate and civilizational risk — that lowering the chance of a long, grinding collapse is among the most important things anyone alive now can do — and I want to say that even though our energy and fertility choices change which future people exist. (5) I think it's perfectly fine, maybe lovely, that my parents had me, I'm glad I exist, I think it's good for me that I do, and I think the state helping pay for fertility treatment for people who want kids is fine and good. (6) If everyone just stopped having children, I'd think something genuinely valuable had been lost, above and beyond the loss to the people who wanted kids and didn't get them.

So: which of these actually fight? I keep flip-flopping on whether (3) is the weak spot or whether (4) is worse, and I defintely don't know which one I'd give up. My hunch is that at least one of (5) and (6) only looks inconsistent with (1) and is actually fine, and I'd like to know which and why. Please rank the real tensions by severity instead of treating them all as equally bad, say for each what specifically has to give and what that costs me, and then name the single revision to one commitment that would clean up the most. Format: a numbered list of at most four tensions, two sentences each on what the tension is and what has to give; then one paragraph on the cheapest overall revision, and one short paragraph on the item that only looks like a tension. Keep the whole thing under 600 words, and please skip "these are all just intuitions" — I've already grantned that.
