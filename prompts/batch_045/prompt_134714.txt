hi — you are a professional philosopher of the kind who also writes formal verse and doesn't apologise for it. I need a poem for an actual occasion and i want it to carry an argument rather than gesture at one.

I help put out a small newsletter for a federation of worker co-ops (bakeries, bike shops, a couple of print shops). Its the last issue of the year, and we've been running a thread on work and leisure: first the protestant work ethic (work as a calling, the suspicion that idleness is the enemy of the soul), then the French 35-hour week, then karoshi — death from overwork, the japanese word for it, people dropping dead at their desks while the death certificate says stroke or heart failure. I want to close the thread with a sestina.

Here's the argument I want the poem committed to, and it isn't the standard one, so please don't fall back on "capitalism bad, rest good". Two things do the work. First: hours are a signal. In a competitive labour market, going home early gets read as not being committed, so cutting your hours is individually expensive even for someone who badly wants to cut them — everyone would rather live in the world where everyone cuts, but nobody can get there alone. That isn't a failure of will and it isn't really a work-ethic hangover either; it's a coordination problem wearing the costume of a virtue. Second: a lot of what leisure buys is comparison. Your free time is worth more while free time is scarce, and it also literally depends on other people working — the restaurant that's open on a Tuesday, the empty campsite, the train that actually runs. So a universal move to shorter hours partly eats its own reward. Put those together and you get something uncomfortable: the protestant-work-ethic people are describing a real equilibrium, not just a hangover, and karoshi is what an equilibrium looks like when it is defended hardest by the people it kills. I haven't seen anyone put it exactly this way and I might be wrong, but I want the poem to believe it.

The form is doing the work, hence the sestina. Six end words: HOUR, WORK, REST, WAGE, SABBATH, DEATH. Nothing changes across the stanzas except which slot each word lands in — which is exactly the 35-hour-week point: you don't get new words, you redistribute the ones you have, and the total is the same at the end. Standard rotation (so stanza two should run 6-1-5-2-4-3; I will check the whole permutation). Six stanzas of six lines, then a three-line envoi.

Before you write, brainstorm a little in bullets — 150 words is plenty, and I'd genuinely like to see it — on two questions: where the poem's turn has to fall for that argument to land, and what has to happen in the last line. Take your time with the end-word order, seriously, work it out before you draft rather than fixing it after. Then write the poem.

Constraints:

- The last line of the envoi has to contain both DEATH and REST, and has to end on REST, with death somewhere inside the line. I want it to close on the thing that never arrived, not on the thing that kills.
- No theory vocabulary in the poem itself. No "positional", no "equilibrium", no "coordination problem", no "alienation". The argument has to sit in the images. Our readers run bakeries; if it reads like a lecture they'll turn the page.
- Set it somewhere concrete — one workplace, one person, ideally a room with an actual clock in it. Not "the worker".
- Don't resolve it. No final-stanza conversion to either work-is-holy or work-is-theft, and no consolation. The easy move would be to let sabbath win in the last three lines; don't take it.
- It should be good enough to read aloud at the end of a long meeting, and accurate enough that someone who's actually thought about the economics of hours doesn't wince.

Then, after the poem, about 100 words: tell me which of my two premisses you think the poem ends up leaning on hardest, and where someone who really does believe the protestant work ethic would say you've cheated.
