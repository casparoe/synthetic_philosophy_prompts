I'm writing a seminar paper for a final-year module on practical rationality, and I need a thought experiment built to order rather than an illustration of one that already exists.

My working thesis is that the standard "just pick" treatment of Buridan's ass — if two options are exactly equally good, nothing favours either, so rationality permits either and there is nothing further to be said — is incomplete. The gap I want to open is this: exact equality of expected utility may be precisely the situation in which a little further enquiry does the most work, because when you are exactly indifferent almost any informative signal can change what you do, whereas once you lean even slightly towards one option most signals leave your choice untouched. If that is right, then genuine indifference does not license a shrug and a choice; it may obligе the agent to buy some evidence first. I need a case that shows this vividly and survives being pushed on.

Specifications:

1. Two options that differ in kind — two jobs, two flats, two courses of treatment — not two qualitatively identical piles of hay. The agent's indifference must be the upshot of weighing considerations that are genuinely different and happen to balance, not a stipulation that the options are duplicates. If it helps, add a clause on how the balance is arrived at.

2. The agent is indifferent in the strict decision-theoretic sense: equal expected utility on her own evidence and her own values. Not incommensurability, not bare ignorance she ought to be cautious about, not "she can't make up her mind".

3. A cheap and realistically imperfect source of further evidence is available — one conversation, one visit, one test. Informative but nowhere near conclusive. There is a deadline, so exactly one round of enquiry fits and further delay does not.

4. The randomisation escape route must be blocked by the shape of the case rather than by fiat: she has to give reasons to someone the decision affects, or there is some comparable feature that makes a coin flip genuinely unavailable as an answer. If you think it cannot be blocked without stipulation, say so plainly.

5. One paragraph, ordinary life, no demons, oracles, predictors or exotic machinery.

Then show me that the case does what I want:

(a) what the permissivist "just pick" line says here, and why choosing either right now is supposed to be beyond criticism;
(b) what a symmetry-or-randomisation view says, and whether randomising differs from determinate picking in your case;
(c) what the expected-utility-plus-option-value line says, and why, on the numbers you build in, enquiry comes out as required rather than merely permitted — including the condition the cost of enquiring has to meet;
(d) which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to all three verdicts if it is removed — in particular if the agent leans even slightly to one side, if the signal is too weak, or if the deadline rules enquiry out;
(e) the strongest objection a defender of the "just pick" line would press against your case — I expect something about whether rationality can require chasing very small expected gains, or about whether the costs of further deliberation have been undercounted — and how well the case survives it;
(f) an honest note on any point where the case begs the question against the permissivist.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the central claim — that exact indifference raises rather than lowers the expected value of further enquiry, and can therefore convert a permitted choice into a required one — and single out the step in the argument you are least sure of. Please don't give me citations or a reading list; I want the argument itself.
