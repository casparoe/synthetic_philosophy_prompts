hey — I've landed on a position about the bootstrapping problem in epistemology and I want you to try to break it, because I can answer every objection I can think of myself and I don't trust that at all.

Quick version of the setup in case we use different words: the easy-knowledge style argument runs through an agent using one single source over and over — a colour sensor, a thermometer, whatever — noting that its outputs line up with how things actually are in each case, then inferring (by induction, or via some closure principle from the conjunctive list of matches) that the source is reliable, and thereby seeming to buy knowledge of its own reliability for free. Reliabilism is supposed to make that too cheap.

My position: the argument doesn't touch knowledge. What it actually shows is that the bootstrapped belief isn't supported by the agent's evidence — her total evidence is perfectly compatible with the source being a reliable-looking piece of garbage — and that's an undercutting defeat relative to what she has, not a defect in truth or in reliability. So the bootstrapped belief can be knowledge, and the leftover that-can't-be-right feeling is a fact about epistemic rationality, evidential support, and maybe about what she should be willing to say out loud — not about knowledge. Corollary I'm stuck with: most of the industry of replies is fixing the wrong thing. Restricting closure, demanding that beliefs track the truth or be safe, adding an anti-bootstrapping condition on what counts as a basic source — all of these treat an evidential complaint as if it were a knowledge complaint, and any of them that saves reliabilism from bootstrapping by blocking the inference is, on my view, buying relief at the wrong price.

Three cases I made up. I want your verdict on each, but more than that I want you to say what each one actually shows about my diagnosis, if anything:

(a) The shy sensor. A sensor that is genuinely highly reliable in ordinary operation, but whose reliability is sensitive to the operator's own conclusions: as soon as the operator comes to believe, to a high enough degree, that it is reliable, its accuracy collapses to roughly chance. Fill in the mechanism however you like — a maintenance tech who sabotages operators who get cocky, or make it social and let the source be a colleague who gets sloppy once she's trusted. So in the actual world the bootstrapping inference destroys the very reliability it reports on. I seem committed to saying she knows it's reliable only if she refrains from concluding that it is; the inference her evidence supports is the one that makes her belief false. How much does that hurt me, versus how much does it hurt the original argument (which also wants to say something about what happens when the agent actually performs the inference)? And: should the process whose reliability matters be individuated to include her resulting confidence? That's the generality problem biting, and I'm not sure it bites me or the other side.

(b) The dimmer. Same thing but graded and continuous: reliability starts very high when she's agnostic about the source and falls off smoothly as her confidence rises (and comes back as her confidence drops). There's then a fixed point — a confidence level where she thinks the source is reliable to exactly the degree it in fact is, given that she thinks that. Pick the numbers so the crossing point is still comfortably above whatever threshold you think reliable needs. At that point the belief is true, the source is reliable, and the belief came from a reliable process, so on my view it looks like knowledge — except that the reliability is partly made true by the belief itself. Is that knowledge? Does my position quietly collapse into the self-verifying, coherence-flavoured view it was supposed to be an alternative to? And note there will be fixed points for lots of intuitively awful sources too, so I may be committed to knowledge in cases where I really don't want to say that.

(c) The mirror image. Flip the sign: a source that performs better the more it is trusted or relied on, so that confidence raises reliability. Start it at chance, let her sample, get mildly confident, get a better source, get more confident, and watch her walk herself up to a fixed point where her belief is true and the source really is reliable. Reliabilism plus my diagnosis seems to say: knowledge, no problem. But this looks like manufacturing reliability out of nothing, which strikes me as a worse version of the original too-easy complaint rather than a solution to it. Is that a genuine escalation, or is it just the same case with the sign changed?

Same question for a reliabilist who does buy the closure-restriction or tracking reply: I assume their objection is that I've changed the subject from knowledge to rationality, but give me the strongest version of that, not the version I can bat away.
