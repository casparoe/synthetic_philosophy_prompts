Hey — I'm putting together the prospectus for a one-year MA thesis (about 60 pages, but I'm TAing both terms so realistically I've got four or five months of actual writing) and I want it to be on Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain example, but not on the parts everyone already writes about. Here's the half-formed thing I keep annoying people with: everyone treats the argument as establishing that no patterned distributive principle can be maintained without unacceptable interference, but I think patterned is doing about three different jobs at once, and the argument only obviously bites on one of them. What I'm calling (A) end-state principles say the current distribution at any time must match some formula over people's holdings. (B) starting-gate principles say only that everyone gets an equal or adequate initial package, and after that it's nobody's business what happens. (C) diachronic or relational principles constrain the history or the relations between persons over time — the ratio between the best- and worst-off has to stay within some band, or nobody's holdings may grow more than k-fold relative to the median, or the worst-off's share of the social product can't fall below some floor. My suspicion is that the Wilt Chamberlain story is a knockdown against (A), largely irrelevant to (B), and that for (C) the familiar thesis that free transfers will always disrupt any favored pattern stops being an all-or-nothing moral claim and turns into an empirical question about rates and magnitudes: how fast does a population of voluntary transfers drift outside the band, and how much interference per unit time does it take to pull it back? Which suggests the thing I actually want to build the thesis around: an interference budget for a principle — not does maintaining P require interfering with liberty, but how much interference, how often, and of what kind — because a tax on the ticket price leaves the transaction available at a price, whereas banning it doesn't, and I think a lot of the argument's rhetorical force comes from hearing the second when what's on the table is the first. So: at the end of the day, does the argument reach redistributive taxation at all, or does it only reach prohibition?

Give the approach and say why it's better than the obvious alternatives — e.g. than just writing a taxonomy paper, or than trying to model the dynamics formally, which I don't have the maths for. Tell me what the possible conclusions are and what would decide between them. Give me failure conditions that are real: what would it mean for this to have been a footnote.
