Right, so my mate and I spent about three hours in the pub last night arguing about whether you're ever allowed to just *assume* independence when you're giving a heuristic argument, and I've come away realising neither of us had any idea what a decent rule would even look like.

I was defending the number theory way of doing it: treat divisibility by each prime as an independent coin flip, read off an estimate, then go back and patch it for the local obstructions you can actually see — the classic one being that once you've ruled out divisibility by 2 and 3 and so on, the candidates that are left aren't spread as evenly as the naive model says, which is why the naive estimate for how often twin primes turn up is off by that product-over-primes correction factor. He said that's just assuming what you want to prove, and that when these models break they break for reasons you can't see from the inside: he reckons there's a result from the eighties showing the random model gets the fluctuations in the number of primes in a short interval badly wrong, because of correlations with smooth numbers that no local check would have flagged. I think he's right that it breaks and I still think the method is fine, and I couldn't say why, which is definately the annoying bit.

So: don't give me a theory, give me the spec. What would a formal account of the presumption of independence have to deliver for me to trust the numbers that come out the other end — good enough to decide whether a back-of-envelope estimate is worth betting on or pointing a research programme at, not good enough to prove anything. Every requirement with the case that motivates it, and mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preference.

Things I want covered, but add what I've missed:

- What the relata even are — events, propositions, or the *reasons* backing them — and whether independence of reasons aggregates the way probabilistic independence does when you chain twenty weak ones together.
- What the probability is over. Every heuristic I can think of is secretly a frequency in some chosen ensemble, and you can usually pick the ensemble to get roughly the answer you like, so the rule has to say which ensemble and either be stable across reasonable choices or tell you when it isn't.
- The regress: saying p and q are independent needs its own independence assumptions somewhere further up. Does the rule have to have a stopping point, and if so what earns it.
- No free lunch: it can't manufacture confidence. A dozen shaky but independent-looking considerations shouldn't compound into near-certainty, and I want the total capped somehow by how good the independence claim itself is.
- Locality: whether the verdict on a whole argument has to match what you get by checking its parts, given that the failures we actually know about are the global ones no part-by-part check would catch.
- What defeats it and what shouldn't: a hint of a real mechanism should, but mere logical entanglement shouldn't — on any honest probability everything is a bit dependent on everything, and if the presumption dies to that it was never alive.

Then: which pair of requirements do you think can't be jointly satisfied, because that's the bit I most want to see, and which single one rules out the most candidates — my guess is the maximum-entropy move dies on the ensemble one.
