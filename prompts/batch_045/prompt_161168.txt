I've been going to this meetup for about a year — it's the local effective altruism / longtermism crowd, maybe twelve or fifteen people, mostly software engineers and a couple of finance guys, plus one actual moral philosopher who shows up maybe half the time (she's pretty skeptical of the whole future-generations literature) and a guy who works in climate policy and visibly checks out whenever the conversation goes more than fifty years out. They think in expected value and QALYs, they're completely used to the "there could be trillions of future people, so even a tiny slice of a percent of extinction risk swamps everything else" style of argument, and they get fidgety with anything that can't be turned into a number. Next month I'm the one giving the talk, and my thesis is going to annoy them a bit I think.

What I want to argue: there's this philosopher named Samuel Scheffler who has this thing he calls the afterlife conjecture — roughly, that an awful lot of what we care about (our projects, our relationships, whether discoveries or achievements or a culture outlast us, even a lot of ordinary enjoyment) quietly depends on there being a human future after we die. My argument in three steps: (1) if that's right, then human extinction is bad in a way that isn't just the deaths involved and isn't just the missing future lives either; (2) that extra badness is non-aggregative — it doesn't come in units of people, so it doesn't scale with how many future people there would have been, and it's what I keep calling "the residue"; and (3) it's partly retrospective and uncompensable, in the sense that it reaches backwards and damages the point of things that have already happened, and there's nobody left afterward for whom anything could make up for it. The bit that's really mine, and that I'm least sure about, is a little test I've been trying out on friends: if you found out you'd been wrong about the future — you'd assumed humanity would go on, but it turns out your generation is the last — would you feel that something had been taken away from you, or merely that you'd been mistaken? I've been calling it the surprise test, and I want to use it to seperate genuine afterlife-dependence from just having a preference that humanity continues. The reason any of this matters to me is that I think the numbers-driven version of the case is fragile: if the future turns out to be short or small — we never really leave the solar system, the whole space thing never happens — the trillions-of-people argument more or less evaporates, and I want a reason to care about extinction that survives that.

So what I actually need is a Q&A prep sheet for the room I described, not a defense of the view. Give me the seven or eight objections I'm most likely to really get, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each one phrased the way that audience would phrase it — so some in expected-value / "what's the upshot for where the money goes" terms, some in philosopher terms, and one or two from the climate guy who thinks we're ignoring people who are alive right now. For each: a two-minute-ish reply, what I should just concede up front instead of fighting, where my reply is soft or where I'd be helping myself to something I haven't argued for, and how strong the objection really is versus how strong it sounds. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — my guess is something about whether the residue, being the same across every option on the table, can't actually change what we should do at all, but tell me if you think the really dangerous one is a different one. Also, and this matters to me because I don't want to overclaim in front of people who know more than I do: flag clearly which parts of what I'm saying are reasonably settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested — especially whether I'd just be asserting Scheffler's conjecture rather than defending it, and where my own surprise-test bit is doing work that the actual literature doesn't support. Please write it in American English.
