I work on product policy for a large consumer platform, and part of my job is anticipating how users and regulators will describe what our systems "do." I recently read a piece in a behavioral-ethics newsletter that leaned on the Knobe effect to support a recommendation about how we should talk about responsibility for automated ranking systems. I'd like to know how much of it I can safely rely on before any of it ends up in an internal memo.

The passage ran roughly as follows:

"Joshua Knobe asked people about a company chairman who is told that a new program will increase profits and will also affect the environment. The chairman replies that he does not care about the environment at all and only wants profits. When the environment is harmed, almost everyone says he harmed it intentionally; when the environment is helped, most people say he did not help it intentionally. Crucially, participants read both versions of the story, so they could see for themselves that the only difference was whether the outcome was good or bad. The lesson is that moral judgment drives factual judgment: people first decide that someone is blameworthy, and then reach for the word 'intentionally' in order to justify blaming them. That is why the same effect has been found for every related notion — whether the chairman knew what would happen, whether he foresaw it, whether he caused it, whether he is responsible for it. And it travels well beyond philosophy classrooms. If a team ships a ranking system that predictably raises engagement and also predictably increases the spread of harmful content, users will say the team intentionally amplified the harm, because the bad outcome is what they attend to and people treat bad outcomes as deliberate. More generally, this is why experimental philosophers hold that intuition can't be trusted as evidence: our ordinary concepts are contaminated by moral judgment, so there is no stable folk psychology there for conceptual analysis to recover."

My own instinct is that the sentence about "intentionally" being nothing but a blame-label is too simple, but I can't say why, and I have no view on the rest.

Please audit the passage claim by claim. I'd like you to separate four things: what the actual experimental result does support; what is one contested interpretation among several that the passage presents as settled; what is a straight overgeneralization from the original finding to other words or other notions; and what is simply invented in the move to the algorithmic case. In particular, I want to know how much turns on the description of the experimental design, whether the mechanism the passage asserts is the one the data establish, whether the extension from intentionality to knowledge, foresight, causation, and responsibility is licensed or is a substantive further claim of a different kind, and whether the philosophical moral the passage draws about intuitions and conceptual analysis is one that researchers in this literature would accept — including whether it is the conclusion the original work is usually taken to support.

Then tell me what survives for my purposes. If the defensible version of the finding is narrower than the newsletter's, does it still support anything actionable about how a company should expect attributions of intentionality, purpose, or responsibility to respond to the way an outcome is described, to whether the stated attitude of the designers is one of indifference, or to whether a harmful side effect is certain rather than merely probable? And where I should be careful to distinguish a claim that is well supported from one that would need empirical work in my own setting before I repeated it.

I don't need citations, references, or a reading list — I can't check them, and I'm not writing an academic paper. Just tell me what's fair, what's contested, and what the passage made up. Please answer in American English.
