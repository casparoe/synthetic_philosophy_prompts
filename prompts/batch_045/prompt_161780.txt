I work in formal verification — model checking, SMT-backed refinement proofs, and Coq and Lean when I can get away with it — and I've been sitting in on a graduate reading group in early modern philosophy that my flatmate runs, mostly as an excuse to argue with her about whether Hobbes's geometrical ambitions are a curiosity or the load-bearing part of his political theory. Her position is that you can discard the mathematics, which was largely wrong and was publicly ridiculed, and keep the politics. Mine is that the methodological claim is what makes the politics look like a science rather than a tract for the times, and that if it fails the whole enterprise changes shape. We've agreed to stop shouting, and I want to test my side in a vocabulary I actually trust: specifications and models, soundness versus completeness, refinement between abstract and concrete machines, trusted computing base, capabilities and authorization, assume-guarantee reasoning, safety versus liveness properties, oracles and interpreters, and the difference between deriving a theorem and getting anyone to run the checker.

Here is the position I hold, stated in six claims with my own labels so we don't talk past each other. (1) The drawn-figure premise: geometry is certain not because we grasp some independent realm of forms but because its objects are things we ourselves draw, so a definition is a description of our own construction, there is no gap between what is defined and what is, and a definition cannot be false. (2) Resolution and composition: a science proceeds by resolving a composite into its simplest parts — body, motion, appetite — and then recomposing demonstrations from those definitions, rather than by accumulating observations. (3) The made-so-knowable move: civil philosophy is a demonstrative science of exactly this kind, because the commonwealth is likewise something we make; Hobbes opens by saying that Nature (the art whereby God hath made and governes the world) is by the art of man, as in many other things, so in this also imitated, that it can make an Artificial Animal. (4) Nominalism: names are conventional marks, there is nothing universal in the world answering to them, and truth is a property of speech rather than a relation between language and mind-independent kinds. (5) Fiat-termination: geometry needs no enforcer, because geometers do not war over definitions, whereas politics does, so the interpretive regress is stopped by a sovereign whose definitions and judgments are authoritative — not by a better argument. (6) The author-inside-the-artifact problem: the geometer stands outside the circle, but the makers of the commonwealth are its matter and its subjects, most of them born into it long after it was instituted and connected to its acts only through authorization. A wrinkle I especially care about: the laws of nature are supposed to be theorems derivable from self-preservation, yet Hobbes concedes they bind only inwardly in conscience and only when others comply as well, and that without a visible power to enforce them they oblige no one.

What I want is a translation and a residue report. Restate each of the six claims as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary — the wrinkle included, mapped onto assume-guarantee or conditional safety properties if that is honest, and flagged if it is not. Then tell me what did not survive, in three labelled groups: claims that translate cleanly; claims that translate only with distortion, with the distortion named; and claims my vocabulary cannot express at all. Flag anywhere the translation quietly imported an assumption of my framework — for instance treating definitions as axioms, or treating the commonwealth as an implementation of an external specification — rather than rendering Hobbes. Two things would move me personally: whether the drawn-figure premise does real work or merely relocates the problem of knowing that what I specified is what I wanted, from the semantics of geometry into an unnoticed idealization of what drawing comes to; and whether the sovereign-as-trusted-base analogy strengthens Hobbes or quietly destroys him, since I suspect normativity is the largest residue — nothing in my vocabulary makes trust in a base anything but pragmatic. Please structure the answer as a numbered list of translations, at most two sentences each, then the three-part residue report, then one closing paragraph of verdict, and keep the whole thing under 1,000 words. I'd rather have it sharp than complete, and I have the reading list already, so no citations.
