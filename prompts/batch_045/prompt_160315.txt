**Draft: Two currencies — what Solomonoff's razor is actually spending**

The thing that got me into this was a pattern from my own work. Give me thirty noisy data points off a spectrometer and I can fit them with five Lorentzians or with twenty. The twenty-parameter fit always fits the old data better and always predicts the new data worse. Everyone says "Occam's razor." But it slowly dawned on me that the advice was doing two quite different things, and that the standard formalization welds them into one number.

The formalization I mean: take a universal Turing machine as your reference machine, weight each program by 2 to the minus its length, and let the prior for the next observation be the total weight of all programs consistent with what you've seen so far. The choice of reference machine only shifts every complexity by a constant that doesn't depend on the data, so the "simplicity" here isn't arbitrary in the way people sometimes worry it is. Two facts about it matter for what follows. First, there's the convergence result: if the source is computable at all, the predictions converge to it, and the cumulative error stays bounded rather than growing with the amount of data — with the bound scaling with the complexity of the true source. Second, everything here is uncomputable: you can approximate the prior from below, you never get it, and in fact the weights only sum to something less than one, because some of the weight sits on programs that never produce anything. Which means this object is a research program, not an algorithm anyone will ever run.

Now here's the thing I want to flag as possibly wrong. It's often said that the 2^{-K} weight "embodies" Occam's razor: simple hypotheses get more prior. But count hypotheses. The number of distinguishable hypotheses at complexity n grows roughly like 2^n, and each gets weight roughly 2^{-n}. So the penalty and the count very nearly cancel: the prior isn't tilting toward the *class* of simple hypotheses in aggregate. What it's doing is spreading the mass very thinly across the enormous number of complex ones, so that no *particular* complicated hypothesis gets much. Those are different claims, and only the second one is true. That matters because scientists mostly reason class-wise ("don't go to a fifth parameter"), not pointwise.

So here's my split. Call them two currencies.

**Currency A, the risk currency.** Penalizing a hypothesis for how many different ways it *could* have bent itself to the data. This is not a claim about the world. It's risk management: it trades a worse fit now for a better expected fit later.

**Currency B, the structure currency.** The bet that the world is low-order — that most of what happens is generated by a small number of compositional, reusable mechanisms, so that a short description will keep working. This *is* a claim about the world, and it could have been false. We could have found ourselves in a world where every extra decimal place of precision required a new law. Nothing about logic or probability rules it out.

The reason I think the split is real is that Solomonoff's formalism doesn't need Currency B. The convergence result holds for *any* computable source, however complex. If the source is maximally complicated the bound is just worse. So the machinery's success is entirely independent of whether nature is simple. If you'd told me that the razor was justified because simplicity is truth-conducive, I'd expect the justification to break when the world isn't simple. It doesn't break. That suggests the formalism is buying you Currency A, and that Currency B is a separate, empirical, and largely unargued-for assumption that most people slide in without noticing.

And here's where I think the two currencies come apart sharply, in a way the formalism can't see. Consider two hypotheses. Hypothesis one: a very short program that contains a big table of real numbers tuned to yesterday's data. Hypothesis two: a long, ugly, rigidly specified mechanism with no tunable knobs at all. Bits-wise, hypothesis one is cheap and hypothesis two is expensive. But every working scientist I know would treat hypothesis two as the theory and hypothesis one as a curve fit. The felt razor tracks *degrees of freedom* — how many independent directions the thing could have wiggled to accommodate the noise — and not bits.

My strongest claim, then: degrees of freedom is not a property of the hypothesis. It's a property of the hypothesis together with the data set and the noise. A twenty-parameter model is dangerous with thirty data points and harmless with thirty million. But a prior is a function of hypotheses alone. So no prior over hypotheses, Solomonoff's included, can be doing the noise-protection job in the way people assume it is. Whatever protects you from overfitting is not something you can pack into the prior; it's something about the relationship between your hypothesis class and your sample.

If that's right, the slogan "simplicity is a guide to truth" is a coincidence of two things: a truth-conducive empirical bet about the structure of the world (Currency B), and a purely pragmatic rule about how fast to commit (Currency A). They coincide often enough that nobody noticed, and they get welded into one number in the universal prior because in that setting bits can be read either way.
