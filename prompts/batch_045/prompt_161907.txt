I direct a small excavation with a six-week methods field school attached, and each year I print one short poem at the back of the end-of-season report — something the students and the diggers will actually read, and that says something true about the work rather than decorating it. This season handed us a problem I would like the poem to hold open rather than resolve, and I would be glad if you would write it.

The site is invented and should be treated as given. Call it Aldermere: an Early Iron Age settlement of eleven houses on a gravel terrace, all of identical plan and floor area, each with its hearth in the same corner and its storage pit in the same position relative to the doorway, each closed by a single fine-ware vessel set mouth-down over the ashes of its hearth. Just beyond the terrace lies a cemetery of nineteen inhumations, every one of them furnished with exactly one grave good: a bone pin of the same type, at the left shoulder. No house is larger than the others, no grave richer, no object unique. The use-wear and residue reports have not yet come back from the lab.

The pattern of uniformity can be read three ways, and the poem should contain all three without adjudicating between them. First, a community with strong levelling norms, in which no household is permitted to stand out: the sameness is achieved, maintained at some cost, and the identical mouth-down vessel is a closing rite performed alike in every house. Second, a dependent population housed to a single template by someone else's decision — an estate, a temple's households, a garrison, a workshop settlement — in which the equality is what the plan imposes rather than what the people chose, and identical houses are the signature of an authority, not of a norm. Third, an event rather than an order: eleven houses raised in one building season by one crew working from one template, the vessels set in a single act of closure, the nineteen burials a short sequence; on this reading there is no long-lived egalitarian community to infer at all, and the uniformity belongs to a construction episode that may have been preceded and followed by something quite different.

What makes the three hard to separate is what I would like the poem's arrangement to show rather than state: the same static pattern is the expected material signature of opposite social arrangements, so the pattern itself cannot discriminate between them. Two further things belong in the background and may be gestured at once. One is that we read a deposit as a living village caught in an instant, when what lies on the terrace is what survived abandonment, collapse and discard. The other is that getting from a pattern in the ground to a way of life requires an independent link — an experiment, a use-wear study, an observed case — between a behaviour and the deposit it leaves; and the facts that would discriminate here are precisely the ones not yet in hand, whether the eleven houses are contemporary or successive, whether the vessels saw use over generations or were set once, whether the fine ware is one workshop's output or eleven households', whether the bone is one feast or years of ordinary meals.

For the form I would like a pantoum: six quatrains, twenty-four lines, in which the second and fourth lines of each quatrain reappear, word for word, as the first and third lines of the next — so that every line that returns returns in new company, and the reader meets the same sentence beside different neighbours. Let most of the returning lines be things one would write in a site notebook: counts, materials, measurements, positions. One or two may be an interpretation, and those are the ones that should feel altered the second time, though not one word of them may change. The poem's first line should come back as its last, altered only in punctuation — a full stop where there was a comma, or a question mark where there was a full stop. Rhyme is welcome but secondary; the interlacement is what carries the poem, and it should carry the argument: the evidence stays fixed while its meaning shifts with what it is placed beside, and nothing in the poem settles which reading is right.

Two things to avoid. No mysticism of objects and no shrug: not "the past is silent," not "we can never know," and equally not a confident promise that the lab will tidy this up. The verse should use no technical vocabulary at all — the report has a glossary and does not need the poem to repeat it. It should be readable as a poem by a nineteen-year-old who has never thought about inference from material remains, while someone who has thought about it should find the three readings and the reason they cannot yet be separated rendered correctly, and should be able to say why the form was the right choice. Please give it a title in the drab register of a site catalogue.
