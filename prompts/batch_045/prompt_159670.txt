I'm TAing an undergrad philosophy of mind seminar this term and I'm putting together a problem set on theories of consciousness that don't build behaviour into the criterion. I want one question built around Integrated Information Theory, but not the usual "explain IIT and say whether you're convinced" — I want a designed case, and I've been going in circles trying to build one myself.

Here's the spec. Construct a scenario in which IIT says there is a conscious subject — a genuine complex with structured experience — while every behavioural, functional and report-based criterion says there's nothing there: the system's ouputs are exactly the same whether or not the relevant part is running, nothing the subject "does" or could do depends on it, and no possible interrogation of the system would reveal it. Constraints: (1) one paragraph of scenario, that's it; (2) ordinary hardware someone could actually go out and buy these days — no uploads, no brain organoids, no worm brains wired to GPUs, no exotic physics; (3) the subsystem that carries the consciousness has to be doing something an engineer would plausibly have put there anyway (a diagnostics path, a self-test, a redundancy check, a debug module nobody reads), not a component whose only job is to have high Φ; (4) IIT's verdict has to fall out of the postulates rather than be stipulated — no "suppose the Φ of this bit is very high." The way I'm thinking about IIT, so we're on the same page: consciousness is a system's intrinsic cause–effect power; it has to be integrated (not decomposable into independent parts), definite (one specific way of being, not a mush of alternatives), and — this is the bit I want doing the work — exclusive: complexes can't overlap, and among overlapping candidate systems only the one with the highest Φ is a real subject, so a big recurrent core can be the subject while the output path bolted onto it gets excluded. If I've mangled any of that, say so before you start building. I already have the stock examples in my notes — the camera sensor with plenty of information but no integration, the giant grid of logic gates that supposedly lights up, the lookup-table-implements-a-person argument — and none of them work here: the first two aren't realistic and the third gives the verdict in the wrong direction.

What I want after the scenario: walk the case through the postulates and tell me which units are in the complex, which get excluded and why, and what the minimally disruptive partition is likely to be — no numbers, argue structurally from the architecture. Then say what experience IIT would attribute and how much IIT can actually say about its content. Then give me the contrast verdict from a functionalist / global-workspace-ish view, and — the part I care about most — tell me which single feature of the design is doing the work, whether removing that feature collapses the case (what if you let the core drive the output? what if the output path feeds back into it? what if the core shares a clock and power rails with the rest of the device, so that the whole device is arguably one big complex?), and what each side's best reply is. My flatmate, who does ML, says the case would just show IIT is profligate and that the honest thing is to say the chip isn't conscious; I gather an IIT person would say consciousness was never for doing anything. Don't just report that standoff — tell me which side the case actually helps, whether the "behaviourally silent subject" is a prediction IIT should own or a reductio it should dodge, and be explicit if you think no case can meet all four constraints at once and why, since the thing I'm really testing with this question is whether IIT's criterion and our ordinary evidential practices can come apart at all.
