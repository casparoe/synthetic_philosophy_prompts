I work on hybrid systems / cyber-physical stuff — plants with continuous dynamics plus a discrete mode, guards, resets, invariants, and the thing we call a Zeno execution where you get infinitely many discrete switches inside a finite time. My team's usual response is to declare the model valid only up to the accumulation point and then either complete it by taking a limit, or regularise it: add hysteresis or a minimum dwell time, switch to Filippov sliding modes, or reach for a finer-grained model (the bouncing ball isn't really bouncing at the limit, it's rolling). I'm fluent in all that; I've read basically no philosophy.

Please restate Thomson's lamp and the philosophical argument around supertasks in that vocabulary, as honestly as you can, and then give me the residue report: what maps cleanly (I suspect their "there's no determinate state at the end" is close to our "the semantics simply doesn't define the state at the Zeno point"), what you had to bend to make it fit, and what has no expression at all in how I talk. Specifically: is regularisation a genuine answer to the puzzle, or just a refusal to answer it?

The case I actually care about: suppose switching is driven by comparing a continuous signal to a threshold, but the dwell time halves after each switch, so switch times accumulate at t = 1. We can nearly build this — what stops us is that the actuator has finite bandwidth and goes dead or jitters before the accumulation point. Does that physical limit touch the philosophical argument at all, or is it engineering pedantry that misses the point?
