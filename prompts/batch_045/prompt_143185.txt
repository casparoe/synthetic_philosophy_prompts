After 35 years poking at invertebrate ganglia I retired and joined a philosophy reading group, and I've definately brought some bad habits with me — I keep asking what you'd actually measure, and getting told that's the wrong sort of question. We're on personal identity now and I've been writing down the verdicts Hugh (who runs the group) has given, because to me they don't obviously add up. His record so far:

1. Replace one neuron at a time with a functionally identical silicon unit, patient awake and talking throughout, old units destroyed: same person the whole way through.
2. Scan at molecular resolution, destroy the body, rebuild an identical one on Mars from local feedstock: the original dies, the thing on Mars is a duplicate.
3. Same scan, except the original is only put into suspended animation and wakes up a year later: now there are two people, and the copy isn't him.
4. Scan, destroy, build two identical copies: the original dies and neither copy is him, but being either one would be as good as surviving, so he'd sign up for it in advance.
5. One-at-a-time replacement done over a month under deep anaesthesia with no experience at all, each new unit run in parallel with the one it replaces for an hour before the old one is switched off: survives. Whether he was conscious during it is irrelevant — the system never stopped running.
6. As in 1, except the entire left hemisphere is taken out in one operation and replaced with a prosthetic hemisphere built and trained beforehand on a year of that same patient's recorded hemispheric activity, matching the removed tissue's input–output behaviour almost perfectly: that's a death, not a continuation. He was disassembled and reassembled; it doesn't matter that the spare part fits.

Here's the case I actually want. No scan anywhere, nothing ever stops running. Each unit is replaced not by one unit but by a pair, both wired to the same inputs and outputs. For a week the pair just recieve copies of the traffic while the original does all the work; then over three weeks the mixing is ramped slowly from 100/0/0 to 0/50/50, and the original is switched off; then the two branches are disconnected from each other and each is given its own body. Every step is tiny, no stage is a copy made in one go, and both branches were trained in place, in situ, by running alongside. At the end there are two awake subjects with exactly equal claims.

What will Hugh say — does the original survive, is either of them him, and would he take the deal? More useful to me: what rules fit 1 through 6 equally well, what does each of them predict here, which is your best guess and why, and which single variant of this case would pry apart the rules that agree on it? I'd especially like to know whether his 5 commits him to anything, since my case is just his parallel-running trick scaled up and never resolved. Please keep the whole answer under 500 words.
