I'm a couple years into a PhD working on Dennett stuff, and I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about real patterns — roughly, the idea that a pattern is real when there's a way of describing the data that's more compact than the raw bit map and licenses predictions better than chance. My gut verdicts on cases dont line up with the criterion I keep writing down, so here they are, in no order:

- glider crossing a Life board: real, obviously.
- the shape we project onto some scattered stars (constellation-style): not real, it predicts nothing.
- the basins of attraction in a chaotic dynamical system: real — and I want to say it's because the system's own dynamics does the coarse-graining rather than us imposing a grid on it.
- a hurricane 'heading for' warm water, 'trying to' hold itself together: not real, even though the hurricane itself is.
- my cat wanting the tuna: real — her belief and desire are real patterns.
- a chess engine 'not wanting to' trade queens: not real, merely instrumental, even though it predicts its moves about as well as the cat case predicts the cat.
- a million digits that came out of a forty-line generator program, but encrypted so we'll never recover the program: real. The pattern's there.
- a million digits with a 'pattern' that only an agent with 10^40 bits of memory could ever exploit: not real, or barely. (I know these two pull apart and I cant say why.)
- a pattern a data-mining script found in stock prices that fits in-sample and dies the moment you go out of sample: not real. So the criterion needs something about the description beating the exceptions, or about novel instances — not just compressing data you already have.

Then two cases I don't think anyone's written about and that I keep flipping on. Call the first stacking: in Life you get gliders (level 1), gliders arranged into gates (level 2), gates arranged into a counter whose output stream happens to draw a crude picture if you know the architecture — and suppose nobody ever actually looks at the picture. Is the picture as real as the glider, or does realness thin out the further up the stack you go? Call the second self-occlusion: the pattern that is the entire trajectory of some closed system, whose shortest description is that system's own laws, but where exploiting it would require an agent too big to fit inside the system. I lean 'real but useless,' which I think just breaks the exploitability clause.

Is there one principle that gets all of this — presumably with a clause about whose computational resources count, a clause about genuine prediction vs curve-fitting, and a clause about whether the system itself does the carving — or am I stuck picking between a messy principle that saves every verdict and a clean one that kills some? Which verdicts does each candidate force me to drop? And does whatever principle you land on need extra conditions to seperate the cat from the chess engine, and does it say anything about a case I haven't listed: the 'beliefs' of a large language model, where the pattern over internal states predicts output better than chance and compresses it nicely, but the system was trained to produce plausible text rather than to track the truth?
