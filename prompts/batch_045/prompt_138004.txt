I have to give the best-man-style toast at my oldest friend Maren's wedding in three weeks, and I have written and thrown away six drafts. Maren did her doctorate in ethics on a view she defends under the name tranquilism: the only thing that matters in how a life goes is the absence of craving and suffering. On this view the satisfaction of a desire is not itself a good — a life in which you come to want something, strive, and get it is not thereby better than a life in which the wanting never arose, and it is worse than a life in which it never arose in the only respect that counts, since it contained a stretch of craving. She is entirely serious and entirely consistent about it, and I don't want to get up and say things she thinks are false in front of two hundred people. The problem is structural, not cosmetic: a wedding toast is basically a machine for wishing people goods, and I can't see what is left to wish for once the satisfaction of a want is off the table. Telling two people that I hope they suffer little and crave little is thin, faintly insulting (it implies they currently do), and it isn't a benediction at all — it's a wish for less bad rather than a wish for more good. Meanwhile the obvious replacements are traps: flourishing, fulfilment, purpose, a life full of meaning — all of those, as her department will instantly notice, are just desire-satisfaction with the serial numbers filed off.

The room makes it harder. About a third of the guests are philosophers from her old department who will catch any smuggled-in good; most of the rest are her family from a small farming town who want warmth and who have been telling me for months how lovely it would be if I said something about dreams coming true; her mother has described tranquilism to the neighbours as something off a meditation app and will be listening for a reason to think her daughter married into a cult. Theo's grandmother buried her husband in January and will be at the top table, so I also can't afford a line that implies grief is just another craving to be got rid of. Useful specifics about the couple: they met in a queue at a hardware store, arguing about whether they were buying the same odd compression fitting for a leaking radiator (she says yes, he insists his was a different size); they have a fifteen-year-old whippet with one eye called Pilot; they are buying a house whose chimney doesn't draw and have decided not to care.

Please write the toast: four to five minutes, roughly 600 words. It has to (a) contain an actual good wish for them that a tranquilist could endorse and that the ethicists in the room won't boo, or, if you think no such wish exists, (b) find an honest way to say so that still lands as affection rather than as a lecture or a downer; either way it must not be built on any sentence of the form may you get what you want, and it must avoid the idioms may all your dreams come true, everything you've ever wanted, he would have wanted you to be happy, and a life full of purpose. It needs at least one joke the farming half and the philosophy half can both enjoy — I've been toying with the thought that the most efficient tranquilist wedding present is something that removes the capacity to want, but every version of that I've tried makes it sound like I'm insulting the marriage. And it needs one concrete, non-abstract image drawn from the details above, not just talk about views. After the toast, add a note of about 150 words saying which of (a) or (b) you went with, what you take the real crux to be, and where, if anywhere, you quietly cheated.
