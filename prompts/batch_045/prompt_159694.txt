I am writing a paper on prioritarianism and the levelling-down objection, and I want to organise it around Ursula K. Le Guin's 1973 short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas rather than around the stylised cases the literature normally uses. So I need a reading of the story that takes both it and the view seriously, and I need to know where the fit breaks down.

The story, as I have it: Omelas is a city of genuine and abundant happiness, without kings, soldiers, priests or slaves, and its whole felicity — the beauty of the city, the health of its children, the quality of its friendships — is said to depend entirely on one child kept in filth and darkness in a cellar, terrified and alone. Citizens are taken to see the child once they are old enough; most are at first shocked and then come to accept the bargain, and a few, mostly young, walk out of the city alone into the darkness and do not come back. The narrator offers no account of where they go, does not say that the child is freed or helped, and does not say that Omelas changes. As I read it, one further detail matters: the child's condition is presented as possibly the product of fear, malnutrition and neglect rather than of any birth defect, so that what has been taken from it may in part have been destroyed by the arrangement itself.

What I want from you:

1. Whether prioritarianism is the right lens at all, and where it cuts. Prioritarianism holds that a benefit matters more the worse off the person is who gets it, and it is standardly advertised as escaping the levelling-down objection: unlike a view that values equality intrinsically, it need not say that blinding the sighted so that everyone shares the condition of the blind improves things in any respect. But Omelas is not a levelling-down case as usually posed. On the story's own terms, lifting the person at the very bottom is not a free good: freeing or succouring the child is said to wither the felicity of everyone else. So the structure is one person rising enormously from the very bottom against a large population falling by a comparatively small amount each, with no one, so far as we are told, pushed below the level of a decent life. Tell me, structurally rather than arithmetically, how population size and the steepness of the priority weighting interact here, at what point the verdict turns, and whether the turn is a defensible implication of the priority view or a reductio of it. In particular, say whether the case shows that prioritarianism reproduces the very insensitivity to the worst off that it was introduced to correct, or whether it shows something else — that the view is unsettled between being person-affecting, comparative, and purely aggregative.

2. Three variants the story does not give me, which I want constructed and used as probes. First, divisibility: the same total misery borne by a hundred children instead of one, the dependence running on the total rather than on any particular bearer. Second, rotation: the same total misery spread over time, each citizen taking a turn as the child, so that at any moment one person is at the bottom while across a life nobody is singled out. Third, a version in which the citizens do not know about the child and the dependence runs on their believing the child to be there rather than on its actual state. For each, say what a prioritarian should say, and more importantly say what the divergence among the three reveals about what the view is really tracking: a person's level, a person's claim against others, or the shape of a distribution.

3. The walkers-away, and the option the story omits. Those who leave do not free the child, and on the most natural reading nothing changes for it or for the city. I want an account of whether this is a morally significant act, a merely expressive one, or a refusal of complicity that the consequentialist structure of prioritarianism cannot represent — and then a verdict on whether that is a gap in the view or a point in its favour. Take seriously here the possibility that the arrangement has itself degraded the child's capacities, since on that reading the size of the loss to be weighted depends on capacities the arrangement destroyed, and a simple claim that the worst off has the strongest claim becomes harder to apply than it looks.

4. Where the reading should stop. I do not want the story reduced to an illustration, and I do not want the view bent to fit it. Tell me which features are doing philosophical work and which are narrative scaffolding that a paper should not lean on: the deliberately vague and slightly unreal quality of the utopia, the ritual of being taken to see the child, the narrator's refusal to say where the walkers go. And give me an honest conclusion on whether anything survives the exercise: does the story give us a reason to accept or reject anything about prioritarianism and levelling down that the standard stylised examples do not, or is it only a memorable way of staging the aggregation problem and the problem of dirty hands?
