You are a philosopher who works on causation, agency, and explanation in both law and historiography. Please think carefully and slowly, and before giving me your answer, spend a paragraph or two brainstorming the two or three candidate "implicit theories" you think could be doing the work here, plus what would discriminate between them — I would rather see the space of options than only your favourite. Where you need a technical term (actual causation, interventionism, probability-raising, moral luck), define it in a clause: I am a catastrophe risk modeller at a reinsurer by training, entirely comfortable with expected values, return periods and hazard curves, and much shakier on philosophical vocabulary.

Here is the practice I want you to take apart. My industry, and the legal doctrine it sits on top of — "act of God" and force majeure exceptions, natural-peril covers, the whole architecture that treats flood, windstorm, quake and eruption as exogenous — works on what looks like a definite, and to me now slightly strange, picture: hazards are stochastic, given, and nobody's doing. A "1-in-100-year flood" is defined against a baseline that is assumed stationary, and the law's reason for not attaching liability to the flood seems to be that the flood is an event rather than an action, so there is no agent to whom blame could stick. But the very same legal order that treats the flood as nobody's act also obliges me to build to a code written against a 1-in-N-year wind, prosecutes officials who failed to order an evacuation before a predictable surge or lahar, and increasingly entertains suits that trace a particular storm or fire season back to the cumulative emissions of identifiable defendants, leaning on attribution science that says the hazard was made several times more likely by what people did. Meanwhile, reading outside my field, historians seem entirely happy to let non-human things be actors: the catastrophic post-1492 depopulation of the Americas is offered as a cause of the collapse of the Aztec state, the 1815 eruption of Tambora and the "year without a summer" that followed are offered as causes of famine and migration across the North Atlantic, and the malaria and yellow-fever environment of West Africa is offered as an explanation for why European expansion took the territorial form it did there and a different one in the Americas. When I accused a historian friend of crude environmental determinism — continents, domesticable animals and disease pools deciding everything — she replied that she invokes the mosquito only where it is genuinely doing explanatory work and would never do so where, say, a colonial administration's forced resettlement is the thing to be explained. I realised I cannot state the rule that tells you which is which, and I suspect my industry's rule and hers are the same rule wearing different clothes.

So, concretely, this is what I want from you:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory of causation and agency that the act-of-God / natural-peril practice presupposes. I have in mind something like the old legal-philosophical distinction between causes and mere conditions, on which the item picked out as "the cause" is characteristically either a voluntary human action or an abnormal natural event, with everything else demoted to background — the Hart-and-Honoré-style point, if that is indeed the right machinery. Say whether that, or something else, is what is really load-bearing.
2. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. My own guess, which I want you to attack, is that the metaphysics of "nature is not an agent" is decoration, and that what the practice actually needs is (a) a stable baseline against which abnormality and return periods are defined, and (b) an administrable rule for not tracing liability through every link of a causal chain. A pure risk-allocation story — put the loss on whoever can cheapest take precaution or insure — seems to generate most of the same practice. Tell me where that guess fails.
3. Say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. which several theories would each license it, and what the hard cases are in which they diverge.
4. Then stress-test it on the case my industry now actually faces: agency exercised on the baseline rather than within it. Emitting, introducing a pathogen, diverting a river — these are actions that work by shifting the hazard distribution rather than by intervening directly on the outcome, and the abnormality/normality contrast seems to be defined against precisely the baseline they have moved. Does the practice have the resources to handle this, or does it collapse into either universal liability or universal excuse? Note that no single emitter is a but-for cause of any particular flood, so the doctrine cannot simply reach for its usual test; if the honest fix is a fractional, probability-raising or "attributable share" notion of responsibility — the way some jurisdictions already handle cases where no one defendant can be shown to have caused the harm — say so, and say what that costs.
5. Carry the reconstruction over to historiography: state, as exactly as you can, the rule a historian would have to apply to decide when a germ, a drought or a volcano is a historical actor and when it is background; say whether that rule is genuinely epistemological (counterfactual contrast, explanatory interest, whether the factor is something we could intervene on) or whether its selectivity is doing political work; and use it to deliver a diagnosis of what the "peril of environmental determinism" really consists in. A sharp test case: does the difference between "the conquistadors destroyed them" and "smallpox destroyed them" survive once we notice that the pathogen's arrival was itself something the conquistadors did?
6. Finally, tell me what would actually have to change — in the doctrine, in my industry's pricing and modelling practice, and in ordinary historiographic habit — if the presupposed view were replaced by one on which the background is endogenous, i.e. on which there is no longer a "nature as given" for acts of God to come from. If you think any part of my framing above is confused or begs a question, say so plainly before you answer the rest.
