I don't have any training in any of this — I keep bees, got into an argument on a hobby forum about why worker bees are sterile and never breed, and ended up reading a couple of popular books about the gene's eye view. I built the argument below out of what I understood from those, and now I'm fairly sure it's broken somewhere, because where it lands is ridiculous.

(1) For a trait to be an adaptation there has to have been selection for it: some history in which the individuals that had it left more descendants than the individuals that had the alternatives.
(2) A trait can only be shaped by selection if differences in that trait get passed on. If it isn't heritable, selection can't accumulate it, whatever else it does.
(3) The only thing actually transmitted between generations is DNA. A forager that got better at finding flowers during its lifetime, or one that lost a wing to a bird, passes none of that on. So genes are the only thing with the right kind of heredity.
(4) So genes are the units of selection, and anything that isn't a gene is just the machinery genes use to get copies made.
(5) That kills group selection: a colony or a herd doesn't have a genome it hands on as a unit and doesn't reproduce as a whole, so there's no group-level heredity, so no trait can ever have evolved "for the good of the group." Sterile workers have to be explained gene-wise, not colony-wise.
(6) But run (2) on the individual organism as well. A queen doesn't pass her genome on as a whole — it gets shuffled up in making eggs, and her daughters differ from her and from each other. So there's no organism-level heredity either, in the same sense that there's no group-level heredity.
(7) So organisms aren't units of selection either, and nothing is really "for" the organism. Eyes aren't for seeing in any real sense, a stinging worker isn't doing anything for her colony, and the fact that animals look designed is an illusion we read onto gene-level bookkeeping.
(8) That's obviously nonsense. Eyes are for seeing, and explaining why animals look designed is most of what biology does.

So: which step do I drop? My guess is the trouble is around (3) or (6), but I keep flip-flopping and I can't tell which one is doing the real work. Someone on the forum said the words "replicator" and "interactor" at me and told me to go read something, which I'm not going to do, and they wouldn't explain it either.

I'd rather hold on to (5) than to (8) if I have to pick — I do think people reach for "for the good of the group" way too fast — but tell me what I'd be paying for that. Please don't just hand me names of theories or books; explain what distinction is actually doing the work and why the step I should reject looked fine when I wrote it.
