I am assembling a seminar handout for advanced undergraduates on whether supererogation can survive when the cost to the agent is trivial. The dialectic I want to stage is usually run in one direction: the standard Urmson-style treatment — an act is supererogatory when it is morally optional to omit (not wrong, not blameworthy), yet praiseworthy or admirable to perform, and so in some sense beyond duty — is illustrated with costly, risky, heroic acts, and it is consequentialism that is said to have no room for the category. I want a single case that reverses that burden and isolates one structural feature, namely the act's membership in an indefinite, unbounded series of similar opportunities, with nothing else available to do the work.

The scenario

Give me one paragraph, no more than about 150 words, set somewhere ordinary — a workplace, a university department, a clinic, a neighbourhood — in which an agent S faces an open-ended supply of opportunities to perform acts of a single type A. Each act costs S almost nothing: a little time, attention, or minor inconvenience; no consequential money, no physical risk, no reputational cost. Each confers a real but modest benefit on a different stranger, and S can tell without any investigation that the benefit would accrue. There is no upper bound on how many such opportunities S will face and no non-arbitrary point at which the series ends. Then single out the first act of the series and call it A₁.

Constraints I will check

No saints, heroes, martyrs, rescuers, or life-saving; the stakes of any single act must be small enough that it could be done on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon without anyone remarking on it. S has no special relationship to the beneficiary, has promised nothing, occupies no role that generates the duty, did not create the need, and is not uniquely placed; nor is anyone else waiting to step in. The optionality of A₁ must not be traceable to S's ignorance, to uncertainty about whether the benefit would accrue, to any competing obligation, or to the thought that someone else might handle it — close those exits explicitly. And do not stipulate the verdicts: no "intuitively this is clearly supererogatory" and no "everyone agrees S is not required." The verdicts have to fall out of the accounts below applied to the facts you have given me.

The three verdicts the case must generate, with the reasoning shown

First, on the standard analysis, A₁ is supererogatory: omitting it is permissible and not blameworthy, performing it is praiseworthy or admirable, and A₁ lies beyond what duty requires. Show why this holds even though the cost to S is trivial, which is the whole point of the case.

Second, on act consequentialism equipped with a genuine Scheffler-style agent-centred prerogative — the agent may give her own interests extra weight by a fixed factor or proportion, not by an arbitrary or gerrymandered allowance — A₁ is required and omitting it is wrong. Show why the prerogative cannot shelter this act: the cost falls well inside what the prerogative permits an agent to absorb.

Third, on a Kantian treatment of beneficence as a wide or indeterminate duty — one we are required to adopt as an end, required in kind but indeterminate in degree, and whose fulfilment counts as meritorious rather than merely owed — A₁ falls inside duty: performing it is meritorious, omitting it is not culpable, and the only latitude concerns how much. Be explicit that this is a distinct verdict from the first even though the two agree that omission is not blameworthy: the disagreement is over whether A₁ lies beyond duty or within it.

Diagnosis

Name the single feature doing the work — I expect it to be the unbounded series structure together with the absence of any principled stopping point — and demonstrate that it is doing the work by removing it. Rewrite the case as a one-off: same act, same trivial cost, same benefit, a single opportunity that will never recur. Tell me which of the three verdicts change and how. If the one-off version comes out required on the standard analysis too, say so plainly; that is the result I expect, and it is what makes the series structure load-bearing. Then run a second diagnostic: make each act's cost significant — a real sacrifice, though still not heroic — leaving the series structure intact, and say which verdicts change. If the second verdict collapses, that confirms the case is not secretly a cost case.

The defences

Tell me what the consequentialist and the Kantian will each say to stop the case from counting against them. I expect the consequentialist to try converting the requirement into an aggregate one — some quantity of helping overall, with full latitude about which opportunities fill it — and the Kantian to argue that latitude in degree is exactly what ordinary supererogation talk is tracking. For each move, say whether it works and whether it costs the theorist anything; in particular, say whether the consequentialist's aggregate move concedes the instance-level optionality that the supererogation theorist wanted in the first place.

An honest note

If the three verdicts cannot be jointly generated from the accounts without smuggling one of them in, say so: identify which conjunct of the specification fails, explain why, and tell me which conjunct you would relax and what you would substitute for it.

Keep the scenario tight and the whole answer under 800 words.
