I'm in a graduate reading group that has just worked through Putnam's Twin Earth case, and we got stuck on a variant none of us had seen discussed, so I want a number rather than a survey of positions.

Call the scenario Isotope Earth. It is a community like an ordinary Earth community in every respect except this: roughly half of the clear, odourless, drinkable liquid in its lakes, taps, clouds and bodies is ordinary water, and the other half is a substance I'll call β-water. β-water has the same chemical formula as water — two hydrogens, one oxygen — but each oxygen nucleus carries two extra neutrons, so it is a genuinely different physical microstructure. It is nonetheless indistinguishable from water by any means available in that community: no reaction they can run, no separation they can perform, no measurement they can make tells the two apart, and their science contains no theory of isotopes or of microchemistry at all. They have a single word, used exactly as we use "water", applied indiscriminately to samples of both, and they have deferential practices just like ours. One further stipulation: there is a compound, not synthesizable by that community for another three centuries, that reacts visibly with β-water and not with water.

Here is the proposition I want your credence on: as used in that community, the term's extension is the union of the two substances — water and β-water together — so that it is neither the case that the term picks out exactly one of them nor the case that it has no determinate extension at all. Give me a number, and then decompose it. I'd like rough probabilities and a one-line justification for at least these: (a) that externalism about natural-kind terms is on the right track at all; (b) that, given externalism, reference is fixed by microstructure as such rather than by whatever hidden property best explains the observable behaviour and licenses the community's inductions — since here the two substances differ microstructurally while sharing the entire explanatory profile that matters to these speakers; (c) that a difference detectable only in a merely possible future interaction, involving substances no current speaker can produce, can be reference-relevant now; (d) that a term used evenly and indiscriminately across two substances can have a disjunctive extension rather than being simply a case of indeterminacy or partial reference; (e) that what competent speakers say when told the facts is evidence about what their word referred to, rather than only about their theory of reference. Then combine them, tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and tell me which term you are least confident about.

Two of us ended up at 0.75 and 0.2 and we can't locate the disagreement; tell me which of the sub-questions most likely separates someone at 0.75 from someone at 0.2. If you think the proposition is indeterminate in a way that makes a number inappropriate, or that the question is defective, say so and put a number on that — and then give me your credence conditional on the question being in good order. Finally: what would move your number most — a further thought experiment, an argument from the philosophy of science about kinds and induction, or something empirical about how children actually acquire natural-kind words? Please don't answer with "it depends on your theory of reference"; adopt whichever reading you think is best, say which one it is, and note how much the number would shift under the main alternative.
