I serve on a small committee charged with designing the commemorative and archival practices of the newly founded Bentham Institute for Adaptive Systems, which is being established on the grounds of a former colonial agricultural research station in a region with a painful history of dispossession. Our committee is divided. One faction wants to preserve the station’s remaining buildings, ledgers, and local rituals essentially intact, arguing that this continuity will give our fledgling institute a sense of rootedness and identity; another insists that we should publicly condemn the station’s role in historical injustices, perhaps even demolish or symbolically break with its most visible monuments, in order to clear ground for a genuinely new beginning; a third urges us to ignore the mundane and oppressive parts of the station’s record and instead curate only its rare moments of bold scientific achievement, presenting those as inspiring precedents for our researchers. While reading for a seminar on nineteenth-century philosophy, I encountered Nietzsche’s essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, with its distinction between three ways of relating to the past. As I understand it, monumental history looks to great and exemplary moments in order to inspire present greatness; antiquarian history reverently preserves the past of a community and fosters piety toward its origins; and critical history judges, condemns, and breaks up the past in order to free the living. Nietzsche seems to think that each of these modes is necessary in some measure but dangerous in excess, and that a vital culture needs both the ability to remember and the ability to forget.

Given this framework, please describe the most important implications of Nietzsche’s distinction for how our committee should reason about the trade-offs among the three factions. In your answer, please focus on the underlying logic of his account rather than on scholarly commentary: explain what, according to that logic, is likely to go wrong if the institute elevates one mode to the exclusion of the others, and what kind of balance or priority among monumental, antiquarian, and critical history would best serve an institution that, unlike a long-established nation, has almost no shared past of its own but must nonetheless decide what to inherit from a contested predecessor. Since our institute is committed to both local reconciliation and ambitious innovation, please also indicate how Nietzsche’s emphasis on a bounded horizon—the idea that a culture must be enclosed within a limited perspective in order to act and create—and on the active transformation of inherited material might apply to the practical choices before us, such as whether to maintain, reinterpret, or destroy the station’s archives and monuments. Please make the discussion concrete by using the fictional details of our situation, and aim it at a thoughtful non-specialist reader.
