QUESTION (20 marks)

A conference with 400 registered participants has its badges printed by a supplier who makes an error: sixty of them come out bearing the name and title of the conference's plenary speaker, R. Okafor, and are handed out to ordinary participants at registration. Ada, a first-year graduate student who has never met Okafor and would not recognise her, has been asked to find Okafor and hand her a handheld microphone before the plenary. She scans the crowded lobby, sees a woman standing by the north doors wearing a badge that reads R. Okafor, walks over and hands her the microphone. The woman is Okafor. Assume the following: (i) Ada's belief that the woman by the north doors is Okafor is justified — a name badge is the sort of thing one is entitled to rely on, and she has no reason for doubt; (ii) Ada holds no belief at all about whether the badges were printed correctly and has never considered the matter; her inference from the badge to the identity is immediate and involves no intermediate premise she could articulate; (iii) had she looked instead at any one of the sixty participants carrying a misprinted badge, her grounds would have been just the same and she would have formed a false belief of the same kind.

(a) State the justified-true-belief analysis of knowledge, and explain, using one of Gettier's 1963 cases, why its three conditions are not jointly sufficient. (6 marks)

(b) Apply two standard post-Gettier repairs to the conference case: the no-false-lemma condition (S's justification for p must not essentially depend on any false belief) and the safety condition (in nearby possible worlds in which S forms her belief that p in the way she in fact does, p is true). Distinguish the latter carefully from the sensitivity condition associated with Nozick's tracking account, which requires that if p were false, S would not believe that p. Does either repair handle the case? If your diagnosis appeals to a false assumption Ada never entertains, explain why that appeal is not question-begging. (9 marks)

(c) Now suppose that no badge was misprinted: the badges are all correct, and Ada forms her belief in exactly the same way, with grounds indistinguishable from her point of view. Intuitively, she now knows. What does each of the two accounts in (b) predict about this variant, and which, if either, matches the intuitive verdict? (5 marks)

STUDENT'S ANSWER

Knowledge is standardly analysed as justified true belief: S knows that p if and only if p is true, S believes that p, and S is justified in believing that p. Gettier's 1963 paper showed that these conditions are not jointly sufficient. In his first case, Smith has excellent evidence that Jones will get the job — the president of the company has told him so — and has just counted ten coins in Jones's pocket, so he infers and believes that the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket. In fact Smith himself gets the job and, unknown to him, has ten coins in his own pocket, so his belief is true and justified but is not knowledge, because its truth depends on how things happen to turn out rather than on the evidence he has. Gettier's cases therefore show not only that the three conditions are not jointly sufficient but also that none of them is individually necessary, since each of them can be present in a case in which the subject still fails to know.

In the conference case, Ada's belief is true and justified but intuitively not knowledge, and the reason is the same as in Gettier's own cases: her justification depends on a false assumption, namely that the badges were printed correctly. Any inference from a printed name to an identity presupposes that the printing is reliable, so the no-false-lemma condition correctly rules that Ada does not know. The interesting feature of the case is that nothing in Ada's evidence distinguishes it from the variant in (c): her grounds are the same in both, so any condition stated purely in terms of the evidence available to her will treat the two cases alike, and the difference has to be located in the environment rather than in her reasons.

That is why the modal condition is the better repair. Safety, as Nozick argued, requires that if p were false, S would not believe that p; equivalently, that in the nearby possible worlds in which S relies on the evidence she actually has, she is not wrong. Ada's belief fails this test, since the lobby is full of people whose badges misidentify them and she could very easily have relied on one of them, whereas in the variant no such nearby error is available and her belief is safe. So the safety condition handles both cases, and it follows from this that knowledge requires justification strong enough to rule out every possibility of error: any fallible justification leaves room for luck, and luck is what generates Gettier cases in the first place.
