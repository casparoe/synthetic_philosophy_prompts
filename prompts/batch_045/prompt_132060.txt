I am a retired structural biologist who has lately taken up philosophy: for most of my working life I inferred three-dimensional structures from a few hundred noisy diffraction spots, so the business of getting something general out of a handful of samples is not new to me, and the conceptual puzzles surrounding it have become an obsession. This term I gave a guest lecture in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on concepts and concept learning, in which I inflicted Bongard problems on the students; I have now been asked to set and mark the exam question on that material, and I would like your help in writing it.

Please work as a professional philosopher of cognitive science who also has a working knowledge of machine learning. Begin with a brief planning phase in which you settle what the crux of the question is, and then open your reply with a single paragraph headed Rationale stating that crux, followed by the deliverables below. Think the matter through carefully rather than quickly; I would much rather have a sharply aimed question than a comprehensive one.

The constraints of the assessment: one essay question on the final paper, closed book, forty-five minutes of writing time, a target length of about 900 words, marked out of 25. It is a second-half-of-the-degree seminar, so the students have read the standard material on concepts, similarity, and the riddle of induction, but they should not need any prior familiarity with the puzzles themselves. The stem of the question must therefore define a Bongard problem from scratch, in a sentence or two, and must supply everything else a candidate needs.

The condition I care most about is this: the question must not be answerable by reciting the standard line about these puzzles — that they demand abstraction, that they are hard for neural networks, that they are a pretty illustration of the underdetermination of theory by data or of Goodman's problem of projectible predicates. All of that is true and all of it is in the readings, and a candidate who produces nothing but those labels should earn a middling mark at best. I want the question to force a candidate to locate a specific difficulty and take a position on it. To that end I want you to build the following two stipulated problem families into the stem, refining or renaming them as you see fit.

First, what I call mate problems. Six boxes on the left, six on the right, one figure in each box. The intended principle is that the twelve figures fall into six congruent pairs, each pair having exactly one member on the left and one on the right — congruence up to rotation and uniform scaling, with position within a box irrelevant. The point I want extracted is that no property of the individual boxes can sort them, not even a property defined by reference to the rest of the page: the property of containing a figure congruent to some figure in the other half belongs to every box in the problem. What separates the halves is a one-to-one correspondence between two sets. So the usual characterization of the task — find the property that all and only the left-hand figures have — is false as a general description of what a solver is doing, and whatever is induced here is not a predicate over individual figures.

Second, what I call the degenerate rule. In any standardly presented problem, the rule that a box belongs on the left just in case it stands in the left-hand column has perfect fit with the data and is about as simple as a rule can be, and yet no solver has ever offered it and no constructor would accept it. I want candidates to say exactly what excludes it, and to notice that neither fit nor simplicity can do the excluding, and that the rule is not gerrymandered in the style of Goodman's artificial predicates — it is a perfectly ordinary spatial property. Then ask, as a further twist, whether a constructor could fairly build a problem whose intended answer is of that kind, and if not, why not.

Between them, the five must-hit points of your marking scheme should cover the following moves, though you should organize and name them yourself and are free to add: that underdetermination by the data is the least interesting part of the story, the harder part being that the vocabulary in which the rule is stated is not given in advance but constructed during the solution, so that any story which conditions on a fixed hypothesis space or measures simplicity within a fixed language fails even to describe the task; that a tacit assumption of fairness — that the constructor intends a unique, findable, non-gerrymandered rule and is cooperating with the solver — does real work in making the problems solvable and is nowhere present in the stimulus, together with what becomes of the task when it fails, as under adversarial construction; that the mate family shows the induced item to be a correspondence or structure rather than a classifier of individuals, with the consequences of that for views which treat a concept as a device for sorting particulars; that the degenerate rule forces a position on what projectibility amounts to and on whether the capacity to classify newly presented figures is part of what it is to possess the concept at all; and finally the methodological question whether success at these puzzles is evidence for anything general about concepts — structuredness, compositionality, an innate bias toward figural properties — or is an artifact of a shared visual culture playing a cooperative game, and what a non-artifactual test would look like.

Deliverables, in this order: the question exactly as it should appear on the paper, self-contained and with the two families defined; a model answer of eight hundred to nine hundred words, written as a genuinely strong candidate would write it under exam conditions and honest about what it leaves out; and a marking scheme of five must-hit points with the marks each is worth totalling twenty-five and, under each, a line or two on how candidates characteristically get that point wrong or half-right. Close the scheme with two or three sentences on the boundary between an A and a B script, naming the specific move that an A must make and a B may miss, and tell me which of the five points you would expect markers themselves to disagree about.

Two further requests. A competent candidate who has done the reading but has never encountered my two variants must still be able to reach a solid B in the time allowed; please check your question against that standard and say briefly how a B-grade answer would look. And a note on the epistemics of the model answer: end it with a short examiner's caveat of three or four sentences stating how confident the answer as a whole ought to make a marker, and identifying the single claim in it that you are least confident of — the one a specialist in the field would be most likely to reject. Please do not cite or refer to any literature, and do not appeal to empirical results about how well any current system performs on such puzzles: the exam is closed book, neither you nor the students can check such claims, and I want the answer to rest on argument alone. Write in American English; I am told the department style guide follows Merriam-Webster.
