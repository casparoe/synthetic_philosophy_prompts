MORAL EPISTEMOLOGY (PHIL 4010), TAKE-HOME FINAL, QUESTION 4 — 30 marks; suggested length 700–900 words

Background from the seminar:

- A belief is *testimonially acquired* when a subject forms it solely on the basis of what someone else says, without independently grasping the considerations that support it.
- Call a moral belief *morally robust* when the agent could sustain it, and act on it, without continued reliance on her informant's say-so.
- The *Recantation Condition* (RC): S's testimonially acquired moral belief that φ-ing is wrong is morally robust only if S would continue to believe that φ-ing is wrong, and would continue to refrain from φ-ing, were her informant to recant — provided S receives no further evidence either way. RC is offered as a necessary condition on moral robustness only. Whether moral robustness is required for knowledge, or required for acting for the right reasons, is exactly what you are being asked to consider.

(a) [10 marks] Two claims are often run together in this debate. The *Transmission Claim* says that moral testimony cannot transmit knowledge or justified belief at all: uniquely among subject matters, morality is one about which you cannot come to know things by being told. The *Understanding Claim* says that moral testimony can transmit knowledge, but that the recipient's resulting state falls short in some further respect — she lacks moral understanding, or a grasp of the reasons. Explain why a defender of an asymmetry between moral and non-moral testimony should prefer the second claim to the first, and say what work the second claim would still have to do in order to establish the asymmetry.

(b) [10 marks] Apply RC to the following case, which we discussed as *The Apprentice*:

Nadia is a junior accountant, newly hired, who has never thought about the ethics of her profession. A senior colleague at the firm, whom she has excellent reason to trust on questions of this kind, tells her that the firm's standard practice of backdating client invoices is morally impermissible. Nadia believes him, refuses to take part in the backdating, and quietly reports the practice to the compliance officer, knowing that this may cost her the job. She has no view of her own about backdating and could not say what makes it wrong; had her colleague told her the practice was permissible, she would have joined in without demur, and she has received no further evidence on the matter.

Does Nadia know that backdating the invoices is impermissible? Does she act for the right reasons? Does *The Apprentice* support the asymmetry between moral and non-moral testimony, or does it support parity between them? Explain.

(c) [10 marks] Assess the following objection to the asymmetry: *If dependence on another person's say-so were epistemically defective, then the same would hold for testimony about mathematics, history and medicine. But plainly it does not. So there is nothing epistemically defective about moral deference either.* Does the objection succeed? Does it refute the asymmetry, or only some version of it?

CANDIDATE 17 — ANSWER TO QUESTION 4

(a) Both claims are attempts to capture the thought that something is missing when you take a moral claim on trust, and it is easy to slide from one to the other. The Transmission Claim says that what is missing is knowledge itself: moral testimony, uniquely, cannot put its recipient in a position to know. The Understanding Claim says that the knowledge gets through well enough, but that the recipient lacks something further — a grasp of the reasons that make the claim true.

An asymmetry theorist should prefer the second. The first is not credible. Testimony transmits knowledge everywhere else: I know that the Battle of Hastings was fought in 1066, and I know that Fermat's Last Theorem is true, and in neither case can I produce the evidence myself — I take both entirely on the word of others. If moral testimony could not transmit knowledge, then almost nobody would have any moral knowledge, since most people acquire nearly all of their moral views from parents, teachers and the people around them. That consequence is unacceptable. So the asymmetry theorist should concede that moral testimony transmits knowledge and locate the defect in what the recipient lacks besides truth and reliability: she does not have the reasons.

What work does the Understanding Claim still have to do? It has to show that this missing understanding is itself an epistemic deficit — that a moral belief acquired without its reasons is, for that reason, unjustified and so not knowledge after all — and that this is what distinguishes morality from the other domains in which deference is unproblematic. This is the hard part, because it is not obvious that understanding is the kind of thing knowledge can require.

(b) Nadia fails RC. Had her colleague told her that backdating was permissible, she would have taken part in it, and she has no view of her own to fall back on and no further evidence. Her belief is therefore not morally robust, and that is why the case feels uncomfortable.

It follows, I think, that she does not know. Her belief tracks her colleague's utterance rather than the moral fact: in the nearest possible world in which her colleague tells her that backdating is permissible, she believes that it is permissible, even though the moral facts in that world are exactly as they are in the actual one. A belief that varies with what someone says, holding the facts fixed, is not sensitive to those facts; it is sensitive to the speaker. And a belief that is not sensitive to the facts it is about is not knowledge. So whatever other virtues Nadia's state has, knowledge is not among them.

Does she act for the right reasons? She certainly does the right thing, and she does it at real cost to herself, which makes it hard to say that her action is defective. But in the demanding sense we used in seminar she does not act for the right reason: she could not say what makes backdating wrong, and she would have joined in had she been told to. I am inclined to say that she acts well but not from understanding. The case therefore gives some support to the asymmetry, though less support than it first appears to give, since Nadia's action is not obviously defective in the way an asymmetry theorist would want it to be.

(c) I think the objection succeeds, and I think it shows that the asymmetry should be given up. If dependence on another person's say-so were epistemically defective, the same would have to be true of my belief that Fermat's Last Theorem is true, which rests entirely on testimony and which is a paradigm of knowledge. It plainly is not defective. So there is nothing epistemically defective about moral deference either.

The residual discomfort with cases like Nadia's must then be explained by something other than an epistemic defect. The natural explanation is that we hold agents responsible for their moral views in a way we do not hold them responsible for their views about history: we want a person's moral beliefs to be *hers*, and to be able to answer for them, because moral beliefs guide action and express character. That is a moral or social expectation, not an epistemic one.

Someone might reply that the mathematical case is disanalogous, because there the proof is public and could in principle be checked, whereas moral reasons are not like that. But this is not a real difference. I will never in fact work through the published proof, and the moral reasons are in principle available to Nadia too — she could simply ask her colleague to explain himself. The two cases are on a par. Moral testimony is just testimony, and the only thing special about the moral case is what we expect of people, not what they can know.
