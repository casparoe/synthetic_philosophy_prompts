I'm in a final-year seminar on explanation and design in biology and mind, and for the past few weeks we have been working through Dennett's contrast between cranes and skyhooks together with his reply to the charge of greedy reductionism. The contrast has quietly become the seminar's default instrument. Whenever someone proposes that some posit X does real explanatory work, the first question asked is whether X is a crane or a skyhook; if it looks like a skyhook, the proposal is set aside without further argument. I want to know how much weight the instrument can actually bear, because I suspect it is carrying more than the principle behind it.

It has been used to license two things in particular. First, in our session on content: the claim that any explanation of a creature's behaviour that cites what its states mean, or what it has reason to do, is committed to a skyhook unless the cited content can be replaced by a structural or functional property, and that this is precisely what makes the resulting reductionism good rather than greedy. Second, in our session on cumulative culture: one paper argued that culture is a second crane erected on top of the genetic one, another that it is a skyhook in disguise, because a cultural crane has to be operated by minds that are already products of design. That dispute was never settled, and my guess is that the metaphor itself is the reason: it does not say whether a crane is allowed to stand on the structure it is raising.

My own worry is that the picture is doing two jobs that can be pulled apart. The defensible one is a ban on circular explanation: you may not account for design by positing something that already possesses the design. The other is a ban on top-down influence: anything acting on the process from above is out. The first does not entail the second. A causal loop in which the products of selection reshape the selection pressures acting on their own lineage is not obviously circular, but it does run downward, and I cannot tell which way the metaphor rules on it — which matters for the culture case and for the content case both, since reasons-explanations also look downward.

The repair I have been toying with is to drop the vertical axis and replace it with two others: (i) does the explanation import the design it is meant to account for, or generate it from conditions that do not already contain it; (ii) does the explanation preserve the phenomenon it explains, or replace that phenomenon with something else. My thought is that the second axis is exactly where the difference between greedy and good reductionism lives, and that the crane/skyhook picture has no room for it, so it cannot by itself deliver the anti-greedy conclusion the seminar draws from it. As a replacement image I have been trying out a ratchet with a pawl: no builder, no up, no target height, irreversible accumulation, and it stops holding the moment it stops turning.

So I would be grateful for three things. First, an explicit mapping: what plays the load, the crane, the skyhook, the ground, the direction of lift, and — the one I keep tripping over — the crane's designer, given that cranes are themselves artifacts of intelligence. Second, the disanalogies, sorted into those that bear on the two conclusions above and those that are merely decorative: I would rather know which two or three do the damage than be handed a list. Third, a verdict on each conclusion separately: does it survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if not, what is the minimal supplement that rescues it — my two-axis version, or something better? And is the ratchet an improvement on the crane, or does it lose something the crane was quietly keeping?
