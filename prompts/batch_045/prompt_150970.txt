I'm trying to work out what I actually think about when someone's information environment counts as epistemically bad, going by gut on cases rather than by any theory. Here's my list:

1. A climate scientist who follows only other climate scientists, never reads the contrarian blogs, and couldn't reconstruct the objections if you asked her: fine. I don't count this as a problem at all.
2. A survivors' forum for people with a stigmatised chronic illness: members only, outsiders banned, and a norm against engaging with outside commentary on their condition. Fine, maybe even good.
3. A family group chat where everyone quietly unfriended the relatives who voted the other way, so political news only ever arrives in one framing: bad, but only mildly, and it feels like a different kind of bad from #4.
4. A conspiracy community with a standing doctrine that every outside institution is captured, so any debunking just confirms the doctrine: very bad — and I feel this is a different kind of bad from #3, not just more of it.
5. An observant religious community that restricts what members may read and teaches that outside scholarship is corrupted by worldliness: bad.
6. A closed online space for a marginalised group that does structurally the same thing — restricts what members read, and teaches that outside commentary is corrupted by the culture that produced it: fine. I've just given opposite verdicts on two structurally identical cases and that bothers me.
7. Someone who reads nothing, trust no source, and has no opinion on anything: I don't want to call this bad in the same way, but I also don't want to call it fine.

The one I keep flipping on: someone who has outsourced all their filtering to an AI assistant that summarizes the news, always presents both sides, and weights each side by how much engagement it got. Some days that seems obviously worse than just reading one newspaper; other days it seems better, since at least stuff gets through.

My first pass at a principle: an environment is bad to the degree that (a) it leaves you worse placed to form true beliefs about things you care about, and (b) the filtering is the kind that exposure to the excluded material wouldn't fix — bubbles you can pop by walking in with evidence, chambers you can't, because they've already discredited the people who'd bring it. But I think this gets #3 wrong (that one does look fixable by exposure, and I still want to call it bad), gives me no grip on #7, and flatly ties itself in knots on the #5/#6 pair.

Can you give me a principle, or two or three candidates, that does better? I'd like each clause tied to the case that motivates it, verdicts on cases I haven't listed (a scientist who blocks cranks on social media, say, or someone whose feed is purely algorithmic with nobody curating it on purpose), and a straight statement of which of my verdicts your principle just can't save — I'd rather drop a verdict than keep a principle with a special clause bolted on for every case. If it helps, say what your principle predicts about a case where the excluded sources really are untrustworthy versus one where the group only believes they are.

Also please be clear about which bits are settled among people who actually work on this and which are live disputes — I don't want a contested claim, e.g. about whether the bubble/chamber distinction is real or whether exposure actually helps, presented as if everyone agreed. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give me the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
