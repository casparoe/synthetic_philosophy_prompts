I'm in a small reading group working through Chalmers's *The Conscious Mind* and the conceivability argument, and we've been arguing about a case that one of us insists is the argument's photographic negative. Take the facts as I state them: in 2006 Adrian Owen and colleagues published a study, in *Science*, of a young woman diagnosed as being in a vegetative state — a diagnosis that is defined purely behaviourally, by sleep–wake cycles with no reproducible sign of awareness — five months after a traumatic brain injury. Given instructions to imagine playing tennis and to imagine walking through the rooms of her house, she produced task-evoked fMRI activity in the supplementary motor area and in parahippocampal and parietal regions that was, on the relevant measures, largely indistinguishable from healthy volunteers given the same instructions; later work extended the method to yes/no communication with other patients. I want this treated as, roughly, the inverse of a philosophical zombie: a being with respect to whom the behavioural evidence for consciousness is empty — not merely degraded, as in locked-in syndrome, where some channel of behaviour survives — and with respect to whom the only positive evidence is neural. Call it the anti-zombie case. Analyse it through the lens of the conceivability argument, and be disciplined about the mapping: what plays the role of the physical or functional duplicate, what plays the role of the phenomenal property, what the theory says the clinicians were in a position to know before the scan and after it, and what the argument implies about the evidential standing of the scan itself.

The parts my group can't settle are these. First, a distinction one of us introduced: the classic zombie is a behavioural-and-functional duplicate without consciousness, but this case seems to force a different conceivability claim — a *neural* duplicate without consciousness, i.e. a being with exactly this patient's pattern of task-evoked activity and no experience whatsoever. Is the conceivability argument already committed to that claim, and is it anywhere near as clearly conceivable as the behavioural one? Second, the cost my friend Priya keeps pressing: if zombies are possible in the relevant sense, then no evidence of any kind — behavioural, neural, or as fine-grained as you like — could ever establish that another being is conscious, so the argument's success would render the 2006 result evidentially worthless and return us to a problem of other minds with no exit. Is that a cost the anti-physicalist should simply swallow, or does it show that the conceivability argument is at bottom an epistemic thesis about the limits of inference rather than the metaphysical thesis it advertises? Third, the scope question: patients who recover and later report having been conscious while unresponsive seem to close the gap by first-personal testimony, in a way the argument has nothing to say about. Does that leave the argument untouched, on the grounds that it was never about that kind of case, or does it reveal that its bite is confined to a class of cases — the permanently uncommunicative — that it cannot help us with? And please be explicit about where the theory falls silent: I would rather you say the argument has no verdict here than force it to pronounce on, say, whether the patient should be treated as conscious.

I'd like a committed answer, not a survey of positions. Give me a definite verdict on whether this episode confirms the conceivability argument, disconfirms it, or leaves it exactly where it was, and put numbers on it: your credence in the argument's soundness before you considered the case and after, and whether you moved by more than five points. Then tell me which of the standard moves — the phenomenal-concept strategy, the Kripkean modal version, the two-dimensional route from ideal conceivability to possibility, or a flat rejection of any conceivability–possibility link — the case does the most damage to, and close with the strongest objection to your own verdict, in two or three sentences.
