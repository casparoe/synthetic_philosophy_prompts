I have an argument I built for a graduate seminar on moral responsibility whose conclusion I am not willing to accept, and I would like you to tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. Please answer in American English.

Background as I understand it. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" treats the participant reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, guilt, indignation — as responses to the quality of one person's will toward another, and treats those attitudes as partly constitutive of the interpersonal relationships we care about. The "objective attitude" — regarding someone as a thing to be managed, explained, handled or avoided — is in order toward the immature, the deranged, the coerced, and others who are not fit objects of the reactive demand. But, on the reading I want to defend, Strawson's answer to the pessimist is not that determinism is false or that it is compatible with responsibility; it is that the general question whether determinism (or its denial) is compatible with the attitudes being in order cannot be answered in a way that could rationally require us to give the attitudes up. Call that the idleness claim.

Here is the argument.

(1) Whether resentment toward an agent is warranted turns on two things: whether the agent is a fit object of the reactive demand at all (someone whose will can be addressed — what children, the severely deranged, and the coercively compelled are said to lack), and whether the agent's action manifested a disregard of the person on whose behalf the attitude is felt.

(2) The second of those questions is settled entirely by the actual sequence: what the agent believed, wanted, noticed and chose. It is not settled by what the agent would have done under conditions that did not obtain.

(3) The general question the pessimist raises is idle, in the sense just described.

(4) Suppose — this part is my invention — that we had a perfectly reliable "counterfactual profile" of an agent: for any variation in that agent's circumstances, in the considerations made salient to them, or in their upbringing, it tells us what the agent would have done. Consider Mira and Nils. Their actual sequences are indistinguishable: each knowingly breaks a confidence for a small professional gain, fully aware of the cost to me and without reluctance. Their profiles are not. Mira would have refrained had any of a wide range of moral considerations been made salient to her, or had the cost to me been even slightly greater. Nils would have done exactly the same thing under every such variation, and under any upbringing we care to specify; call this counterfactual fixity. My reaction is to resent Mira and to withhold resentment from Nils. Nothing in the actual sequence explains that difference, since the actual sequences are the same.

(5) So some counterfactual facts bear on whether resentment is warranted.

(6) But (2) and (3), taken together, say that they do not. If what matters is the actual sequence, and if the modal question the pessimist cares about cannot be brought to bear on the practice, then no merely counterfactual fact is doing justificatory work anywhere in the practice. So (2), (3) and (5) cannot all be true.

(7) Nor, it seems, can we save the view by restricting the idleness claim to "general" modal theses while admitting "particular" ones. Two reasons. First, the particular excuses the practice actually runs on — that he could not have helped it, that she is only a child, that he did not know — are themselves modal claims about agents, and if modality is idle for the general thesis it is idle for these, which would strip the practice of its central exhibit. Second, if determinism is true, then the counterfactual facts that (5) relies on are unavailable anyway: nothing would have gone differently, so Mira's sensitivity is as much an illusion as Nils's fixity is a fact, and the contrast drawn in (4) collapses into precisely the thesis the pessimist asserts.

(8) So Strawson's answer to the pessimist is empty. Either the idleness claim is general, in which case it destroys the excuses; or it is not general, in which case the line between the modal claims we may appeal to and the modal claims we may not has been drawn ad hoc, and the metaphysical question is not idle after all.

I do not want (8). I am attached to (1) through (3), and I want to keep my reaction to Nils, which felt to me like the real datum rather than a philosopher's intuition. My friend says the argument is valid and that I must give up one of (2), (3) or (5); I cannot see which, and I suspect something slipped earlier than that.

So: name the step you reject, and explain why that step looked fine enough to get into the argument. Then price the alternatives — for each other step you might reject instead, say what you would have to accept and how much it would cost a Strawsonian. Three sub-questions matter to me in particular.

(i) Is "he could not have helped it" one excuse or two? If it is two, do its two readings pull in different directions in the argument above, and does that difference settle which step is faulty?

(ii) Does my reaction to Nils track warrant at all, or does it track something adjacent — that resentment toward Nils would be pointless, or unreceivable, or merely ineffective, rather than unwarranted? If it tracks something adjacent, which of (4), (5) and (6) is misdescribing it?

(iii) Can the distinction between the counterfactual facts our practices need and the counterfactual facts the pessimist needs be drawn in a principled way, or does any way of drawing it amount to selecting the modality that happens to survive determinism? If it can be drawn, say what it costs; if it cannot, say what a defender of (3) should say instead.
