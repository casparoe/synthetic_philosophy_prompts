I write about science and ideas, and I'm finishing a feature on a deceptively small question: what makes every 'a' an 'a'. The occasion is the Letter Spirit problem — building a program that, handed three or four letters drawn in some typeface, has to produce the rest of the alphabet in that same style. To do that it must be able to say of a candidate glyph that this one is an 'a' and that one is not, and it must know what to vary and what to hold fixed while it varies.

Please stage the central disagreement as a dialogue between two equally competent theorists, each arguing in good faith and responding to the other's actual points rather than restating their own:

One holds that there is a style-invariant schema for the letter — a relational description (a closed bowl, an aperture, a stem on one side, a characteristic distribution of stroke weight and counter) that every 'a' instantiates and no non-'a' does — with style supplied by a separate set of parameters applied on top.

The other holds that 'a' is a graded category with no sharp structure: a similarity gradient over the cases one has seen, anchored at best by the glyph's role in a system (it contrasts with o, e, g), so that membership comes in degrees and shifts with context.

Have them work through three cases: the single-storey 'a' of geometric sans faces versus the double-storey 'a' of old-style serif faces, which share almost no shape; the blackletter 'a' that looks to an untrained eye like an 'o' or a 'u'; and a glyph I had drawn for them — a closed circle with a short tail descending on the right — which readers call an 'a' inside c_t but hesitate over in isolation. By the end, each should say honestly whether there is any glyph on which their two accounts would issue conflicting verdicts, or whether the argument was never really about glyphs, and whether drawing an 'a' requires anything that merely recognising one does not.

Below the dialogue, on a final line, give your own verdict in machine-readable form: \answer{substantive}, \answer{verbal}, or \answer{both}, followed by a phrase of at most six words locating where the real disagreement lies.
