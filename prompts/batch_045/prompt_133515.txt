So I'm a novelist — I've got three books out and I'm slogging through a fourth, and the new one is written entirely in external narration: no interiority at all, no "she felt," just what people do and say, where they look, how long they wait before answering. Which means I spend every day doing something that feels uncomfortably close to what I gather ML people call inverse reinforcement learning. I watch an invented person do stuff and then announce what she wants. My cousin works on something adjacent to alignment and tried to explain it over Thanksgiving and honestly did a bad job of it — he kept saying "reward function" like it was obvious what one was, and when I pushed he said something about how you have to assume the person is behaving pretty well or you can't conclude anything about what they want, which sounded like my problem of deciding how much of a character's behavior to read as intentional and how much as noise. I can read a little math — I did one of those online ML courses during lockdown, I know what a loss function and a gradient are roughly, and I can mostly follow a blog post — but I don't want the equations. I want it in my words.

Here are my words: scene, beat, subtext, what a character wants in the scene versus what she wants in the book, dramatic irony, the old rule that anything you put in early has to pay off later, genre as a shared prior with the reader, retrofitting a motive so a weird bit of behavior suddenly reads as inevitable. Also the distinction I lean on constantly: what she wants, what she'd want if she knew what I know, and what she'd say she wanted on reflection — three different things, and I assume your side has its own version of that but my cousin didn't have one. And there's a problem I keep hitting in my own draft that I've started calling the understudy problem: a whole family of different wants would generate exactly the behavior I've written in the scenes I actually wrote, and they only come apart in a scene I didn't write. The understudy wants are indistinguishable from the lead wants on the page, and I have no procedure for firing one of them, I just pick the one that makes the book better.

So here's the ask. Take the problem of inferring a person's or an agent's preferences from observed behavior, the way people in ML actually set it up, and restate the whole thing as faithfully as you can in that vocabulary: the setup, the main moves people make to get around the fact that behavior on its own doesn't pin down a reward, and the reasons people are nervous about whatever they end up with. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about — what translated cleanly, what you had to fudge or force to make it fit, and what has no counterpart at all in how a novelist thinks about a character. Is assuming the demonstrator is near-optimal really anything like assuming a character is coherent? Is "the reward" more like a want, or more like a want plus a license to extrapolate it into every scene you didn't write — and do I have anything on my side for that second half, or is that exactly where my vocabulary runs out? Does the understudy problem map onto something your people have a name for, and if so what do they actually do about it? And is there anything in their framing that a novelist has no use for? My guess is it's the bit where you take the thing you inferred and then optimize it hard, since nobody in a novel goes around maximizing a character's inferred motive, but you tell me. Be straight with me where the translation fails: I want to know whether the failure is my vocabulary being too thin, or their problem being less well-posed than it sounds when someone says "reward function" with a straight face.
