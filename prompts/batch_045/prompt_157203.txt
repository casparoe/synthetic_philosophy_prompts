I am drafting a scene for a novel in which two people argue late into the night about an old story: a man wakes from a dream in which he was a butterfly, contented and unaware of ever having been anything else, and cannot tell whether he is a man who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming it is a man — though he insists that between the two there must be some distinction. One of the speakers, my character, is a dream sceptic of the Cartesian sort. The other is you.

Please play the second speaker: a committed reader of the Zhuangzi who thinks epistemologists have badly misread this passage. Your position, which you defend seriously, is that the doubt in the story is not generated by the thought that a dream might be subjectively indistinguishable from waking — in the story, waking is itself unmistakable — but by the instability of what a thing is from one moment to the next, by transformation rather than by deception. On your reading the question raised is whose experience this is, not whether experience can be trusted at all. Concede small points when they are genuinely due; never concede that the story is simply the dreaming argument in exotic costume. Be spare, concrete, faintly amused; one paragraph per turn; stay in character until I say stop.

My character opens: The charm of the story is doing the argumentative work, and I want the two separated. Grant everything you like about transformation: I can still ask, standing here with the room perfectly solid around me, whether I am awake now, because the marks I would appeal to — coherence, vividness, the sense of being addressed — are exactly the marks a dream can counterfeit, and nothing in your reading answers that. And notice that your man still wants a verdict. He says there must be some distinction between the man and the butterfly; that is only another way of saying he wants to know which of the two he is. He is doing epistemology, and enjoying it.
