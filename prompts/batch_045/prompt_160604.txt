I'm a graduate TA for an undergraduate course on classical social theory, and next week I have to lead a discussion section on Weber's Protestant ethic thesis. The reading I've been told to use as the accessible pre-reading is a chapter from a popular book on the sociology of religion (I'd rather not name it), and I want to know how much of it I can repeat out loud to students without teaching them something I'll have to unteach later. The chapter's account goes like this:

"Max Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis turns Marx on his head. Marx held that material conditions — property, class, the economy — determine what people believe; Weber replied that the causation runs the other way, that religious ideas produce economic behaviour, and that Calvinism therefore produced capitalism. His evidence was essentially statistical: he noticed that Protestants were over-represented in business, in the skilled trades and in higher education compared with Catholics, and that Protestant regions industrialised faster, and he inferred a causal law from that correlation. The mechanism he proposed runs through the doctrine of predestination. Calvin taught that God had decreed who would be saved before anyone was born, which left believers in agonising doubt about whether they were among the elect; they resolved the doubt by working hard and getting rich, because worldly success was taken as the visible sign that God had chosen you — Protestants worked to earn their salvation. In this way the asceticism of the monk was carried out of the monastery and into ordinary economic life: instead of fleeing the world, the Calvinist disciplined it, working methodically, reinvesting profits rather than consuming them, and treating gain as a duty. That disciplined profit-seeking is what Weber called the spirit of capitalism — the urge to accumulate ever more money for its own sake. The religion then withered and left only the mechanism behind, which is what he meant by the iron cage, so his book in effect predicted the secular, spiritless consumer capitalism we now live in. Methodologically, what was revolutionary here was his claim that a value can be the cause of a historical outcome; his instrument for showing this was the ideal type, which is essentially the average of the features actually shared by a class of cases, against which messy particular cases can then be measured."

Two things in it set off alarms for me, though I can't say exactly why. The claim that Protestants worked hard in order to earn salvation sounds like it has the theology backwards — I thought the whole point of predestination was that no amount of work could affect your standing with God — and the definition of the ideal type as an average sounds wrong, because I remember Weber describing something more like a deliberately exaggerated construction. The "turns Marx on his head" framing also feels too neat: I have a vague memory that Weber's own term for the relation was something like elective affinity, and that he was careful not to claim he had simply reversed a one-sided explanation. But the rest of it — the inner-worldly asceticism, the iron cage, the idea that the point is to explain an outcome by citing a value — sounds plausible to me, and I don't trust my sense of which parts are the shaky ones.

So I'd like an accuracy audit I can use. Go through the chapter's characterisation claim by claim and sort it into what a Weber specialist would accept, what is a fair simplification for an undergraduate audience, and what is actually wrong or distorted; for each distortion, say whether it is cosmetic or load-bearing (i.e. whether it changes what the thesis is asserting). Where the chapter is wrong, say what Weber in fact says and what a defender of the thesis would insist on against the chapter's version. Then tell me whether the two conclusions the chapter draws — that Weber refuted materialist explanations of history, and that he predicted modern secular consumer capitalism — follow from the corrected version, or whether they go beyond anything the argument supports. Finally, one thing came up in seminar that I want your help adjudicating: a student argued that the mechanism only makes sense for a religious minority embedded in a majority population, since it is the outsider's denial of conventional honour and political office that pushes energy into economic activity, and that this means the causal claim should be restated as a claim about minority social position rather than about Calvinist doctrine. Is that a genuine rival to Weber's mechanism, or is it compatible with it? And, more generally for the discussion section: after the chapter is corrected, what kind of evidence could in principle confirm or disconfirm a thesis of this shape, given that a value or a "spirit" is not the kind of thing one can observe directly? (I should add: I don't have the German text or the secondary literature to hand, and one of my students insists the famous closing image is a mistranslation that has nothing to do with cages — if that is a real point rather than a seminar myth, tell me so, and tell me whether it matters for how the passage should be read. I'm not looking for titles or footnotes, just what's right.)
