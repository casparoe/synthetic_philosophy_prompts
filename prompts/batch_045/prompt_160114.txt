I'm a doctoral candidate in political theory, and the chapter I'm currently revising defends a specific sortition-based design rather than sortition in the abstract: for each of a fixed schedule of policy domains, a separate assembly of roughly 120 members is drawn by stratified lot from the adult resident population; members serve staggered nine-month terms; each new cohort inherits a non-voting carry-over tier of about twenty members drawn from its immediate predecessor in the same domain, whose job is to hold institutional memory and brief the newcomers; and a permanently staffed Framing Office, itself not selected by lot, converts the assembly's findings into statutory text, with one power to send a proposal back for reconsideration but no power to alter its content. The reason I'm writing is that two analogies keep doing all the argumentative work around this design, in opposite directions, and I've never seen either of them audited carefully.

The first comes from sympathizers, including a senior commentator on my panel at a workshop last spring who waved away my institutional detail with the remark that this is just jury service scaled up: ordinary people, drawn at random, given a bounded question, presented with evidence and testimony by interested parties, deliberating in private, returning a decision that everyone accepts. It was offered to license a strong conclusion — that no electoral filter for competence is needed, that the arrangement is not the institutional novelty I was making it out to be, and that my Framing Office and carry-over tier are decoration on a familiar machine. I want that audit done properly: give me the correspondence table (venire and panel, voir dire, the twelve, the judge, the indictment, the rules of evidence, the instruction on the law, the verdict, the mistrial, appellate review, and whatever else carries weight), and then separate the disanalogies that matter for the use it's being put to from the ones that don't. I have a suspicion I'd like tested rather than flattered: at n = 12 randomness buys impartiality, and only at a few hundred does it buy descriptive representativeness, so scaling up is not a change of size but a change in what the lot is for. I also want to know what the analogy quietly imports — a compulsory venire, a single question someone else has already framed and bounded, a supervisory figure who rules on what may be put before the deciders, a decision rule with a settled default, a body whose errors someone else can correct — and what happens to the conclusion once those imports are made explicit, as in my design the Framing Office is, and once the target is a chamber that in practice frames its own questions and stakes out multi-dimensional policy over months rather than returning a binary verdict on one charge.

The second analogy comes from the other side, and it arrived in a referee report in essentially this form: you would not pick your heart surgeon, or the pilot of your aircraft, by lottery, so no amount of procedural machinery fixes the competence problem. I'd like the same treatment for it — a correspondence table, then the disanalogies, then a verdict on whether it survives contact with a design in which the randomly selected person is not unaided, not alone, not under time pressure, and not choosing a technical means to an agreed end but an end on which reasonable people differ and for which there is no outcome measure analogous to a patient's survival or a safe landing. In particular I want to know which features of the source the analogy smuggles into the target: a task with an uncontested success criterion, a population in which the relevant skill is unequally distributed and legibly credentialled, a decision made by one agent rather than by a deliberating collectivity whose aggregate performance is the object of interest, and a decision that cannot be deferred for weeks of briefing. Then, putting the two audits together, tell me what follows for institutional design: whether there is an assignment of powers — agenda-setting, gatekeeping or veto, binding decision on a single framed issue, budgetary or treaty authority — that one analogy supports and the other undercuts, whether either conclusion was ever being carried by its analogy rather than by an argument, and whether some third comparison (a scientific review panel, a constitutional convention, a deliberative poll, an audit committee, a lay assessor alongside a professional judge) gives my design better support than the jury does.
