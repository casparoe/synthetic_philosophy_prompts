I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about why thought experiments travel so well outside philosophy — the runaway-trolley sort of hypothetical, the person wired up to an unconscious violinist, the scientist who sees color for the first time — and about how often they talk people into something the story never actually argued for. Before I go interview a real philosopher for the piece I want to pressure-test my own skeptical angle, so please play the other side: I'll be the hostile interviewer, you be the defender. I've been chewing on this for weeks and I keep fliping sides, so I need someone who won't fold.

You're a philosopher who thinks hypothetical cases, done properly, are a legitimate tool, and you hold a specific version of that view: a good thought experiment is an argument in disguise. The story is a compressed way of getting someone to notice which facts are actually present in a situation they'd otherwise misdescribe, and what does the justifying work is the argument you can reconstruct afterward — the claim about what's true in the case, plus whatever principle connects that to the verdict. The intuition is a detector, not a verdict. So you'll cheerfully concede that most famous cases are badly built, that irrelevant narrative detail leaks into people's answers, that verdicts move with framing, and that philosophers have been way too quick to treat a shared reaction in a room as data. You won't concede that the method is rigged, that hypotheticals should be replaced by bare arguments with no story attached, or that polling non-philosophers tells us anything about whether the argument is any good. Sharp rather than a strawman — concede small points, never the main one. Stay in character until I say "stop", keep each reply to about two short paragraphs, and sound like a person talking, not like an encyclopedia entry. Please write in American English. And no paper titles, no bibliography, no reading list: I can't check them and my editor won't print them, so argue it in your own words.

Two bits of vocabulary I'm using in the piece, which I want you to use back at me. Call it framing leakage when a detail everyone agrees is irrelevant — the time of day, the bystander's job, whether the case is written in first person or third — still moves people's answers by a lot. And call two cases pump twins when they contain exactly the same facts and raise exactly the same principle, but one is told with the emotionally loaded bit up front and the other buries it halfway down. Now stipulate, just for the argument, that a psychologist friend of mine ran a pair of pump twins past a few hundred people and got a twenty-point swing in the verdicts. Here's my opening move: if the answer swings that far on packaging, the detector is broken, and you can't rescue the method by saying 'well, really it's the argument that matters, not the intuition' — because nobody ever finds the argument until the intuition has already told them which way to look. The intuition does the discovering, the discovering is contaminated, so why isn't the honest conclusion that the whole thing is a persuasion technology in a lab coat? Answer that in character, and feel free to ask me for details about the cases if you need them.
