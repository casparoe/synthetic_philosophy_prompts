I'm a third-year PhD student working on free will and I've been asked to open a fortnightly reading group on divine omniscience with a twenty-minute presentation on Molinism. Here is the paragraph: "Molinism holds that God has three kinds of knowledge: natural knowledge of all necessary truths and possibilities, free knowledge of everything that will in fact happen, and, logically in between, middle knowledge of the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom — what every possible free creature would freely choose in every possible situation. These counterfactuals are true independently of God's will; not even God can determine them, since a choice he determined would not be free. God therefore surveys the possible worlds, notes that only some are feasible — the ones consistent with what creatures would in fact freely do in the circumstances those worlds contain — and actualizes the best feasible world. The theory was designed to solve the problem of divine foreknowledge: if God already knows today what you will do tomorrow, it looks as if your action cannot be free. Its fatal weakness is the grounding objection: since the creature's choice never takes place and God's own nature does not settle which way it would go, there is nothing to make these counterfactuals true; they would be brute facts, true for no reason at all, and nothing can be true for no reason. Molina's opponents took this to show that middle knowledge is empty, and most of its present-day defenders have quietly retreated to treating it as a useful model of providence rather than a real kind of knowledge."

My own suspicion, which is why I agreed to present, is that the chapter mislocates the disagreement. It makes the crux whether God can know counterfactuals of freedom; I want to argue that the crux is instead whether God has any counterfactual control over what creatures would do — and that answering that requires splitting what the chapter simply calls "providence" into circumstantial providence (God's choice of which situations to bring about) and compositional providence (God's choice of which agents exist to populate them, given that the counterfactuals are indexed to individual creatures rather than to bare situations). I don't know whether that is a distinction Molinists already draw under another name, whether it collapses under pressure, or whether it is orthogonal to the real dispute.
