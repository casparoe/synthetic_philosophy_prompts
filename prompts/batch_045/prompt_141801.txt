I teach a graduate seminar on contingency and lock-in in history, and for the last session I want to hand them a short story instead of another paper. They can all run through the usual greatest hits — the keyboard layout, the videotape format war, the standard increasing-returns story — and I've found that the more fluently they can recite the machinery, the less they actually feel how weird the underlying question is. So: write me fifteen hundred words or so of fiction that makes the question strange again, ideally something I could assign alongside a paper and then spend an hour pulling apart.

Here's the stuff I actually want the story to carry, and I don't want any of it carried by a character delivering a lecture. First, a distinction I've started calling canalized outcomes versus plastic reasons: my hunch is that for a lot of big historical outcomes, a counterfactual rerun with the dice shaken would land you in very nearly the same place (same standard adopted, same city on the same river bend, same treaty), but the reasons the people in the rerun would give for why it had to happen would be altogether different from, and incompatible with, the reasons our actual ancestors gave. If that's right, then "would it have happened anyway?" isn't one question — it's a question about whether the identity of an outcome includes the description under which it was arrived at and defended. Second, and this is the bit my students will definately push back on: I suspect a good deal of what we call lock-in isn't in the technology or the institution at all, it's in the vocabulary. The alternatives didn't lose a competition; they became unsayable, so that nobody in the rerun could even formulate them — which, if true, means a rerun was never a fair test of anything. Call it lexical lock-in if you want a name for it inside the story. Third, the archive problem: what survives is the justification of whoever won, so the near-misses are exactly what we've lost, and any rerun anybody stages is built out of materials that the outcome already sorted.

Setting, if it helps — use it or don't, I just read fiction better when it has somewhere to stand: a provincial Bureau of Retrospective Coherence (or whatever you'd call it) in a mid-sized industrial city, whose job every decade is to issue the official report explaining why the city's infrastructure has the shape it does and why it could not have been otherwise. Your protagonist could be a junior analyst who notices that one particular decision back in the 1950s — the rail gauge, the dam site, the ring road, pick one and commit to it — was justified four different ways across four successive reports, each presented as self-evident at the time, and none of them mentioning the option that was actually on the table. I'd love some version of a rerun in there — a simulation, a war game, a bureaucratic exercise, a fever dream, I don't care — but I want the rerun to come out wrong in an interesting way rather than to neatly prove the thesis. Constraints: characters with real stakes, no Socratic dialogue in costume, no footnotes or citations or real historical names, and if a character absolutely must say something philosophical, have them say it badly, or half-drunk, or in a memo they later regret. Please also resist both readings my students will reach for on reflex — the econ one where all of this is really about market failure, and the other one where it's really about power — in the sense that it should be possible to read the story either way and come away feeling slightly cheated.
