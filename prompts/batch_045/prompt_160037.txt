I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I've spent the last few months reporting on the fight over whether habitat destruction actually reduces wild-animal suffering — the whole net-welfare-of-ecosystems mess, where one side says a damaged landscape holds fewer lives and therefore less misery, and the other says that's a monstrous way to think about a marsh. Long story short, I got voluntold into giving the toast at the retirement dinner of the director of a coastal marsh restoration program, someone I've known since I was a kid, who spent thirty years buying back drained farmland and letting the tide back in, and who thinks the welfare-biology crowd is grim nonsense. The room is going to be split roughly in half: one half is agency staff, old volunteers, and her family, who want to hear that she brought the marsh back and that the birds and the fish are better off for it; the other half is a younger set of welfare-biology and animal-ethics people I've been interviewing for the piece, who genuinely take seriously the possibility that a healthy marsh is a machine for producing enormous numbers of very short lives with brutal juvenile mortality, and that some of what she restored may have added suffering rather than removed it. I don't want a toast that flatters the first half by pretending the second half's worry is settled, and I don't want one that turns her retirement dinner into a seminar.

Two things I've been chewing on that I'd like the speech to be able to survive rather than stumble over. First, what I've started calling the replacement problem: the lives that fill restored or remaining habitat aren't the lives that used to be there — habitat work tends to swap a modest number of longer-lived specialists for big pulses of short-lived generalists whose infants mostly die, so "we restored the habitat" and "we raised the total quantity of suffering happening in it" can both be true at the same time, and I'd like the toast to never quietly assume that the first cancels out the second. Second, the credit-for-absence problem: if the good supposedly done by destruction is that a lot of possible bad lives never get started, then the benefit is an absence, and it's a strange thing to praise or blame a person for — which feels like something a toast could actually say out loud in a way that isn't a lecture. I was disscussing this with an editor who pointed out that both of these cut against the standard move in speeches like this, which is to tot up the birds. So: write me a five-minute toast, plain language, no jargon and no citations, that lands as real affection for a woman who'd be embarrassed by any of this, honors a career whose value half the room thinks is contested without sneaking in either a verdict or a wink about it, and ends somewhere both halves can raise a glass to. After the toast, add a short note listing any line that only works because it silently takes the net-negative-welfare premise for granted, or only works because it ignores it, and say which and why — I want to know what I'm getting away with.
