I'm part of a small reading group working through formal epistemology on our own — we've covered probabilism and ordinary conditionalization, and last week we got onto what happens when evidence is not learned with certainty. Over dinner afterwards an acquaintance from a machine-learning background, who is confident, probably well-informed, and not a philosopher, gave me a brisk account of the standard Bayesian treatment and then dismissed it. I can't tell how much of his confidence is warranted, so I want an accuracy audit before I repeat any of this to the group. As I understood the rule he was describing (Jeffrey conditionalization, or probability kinematics, as he called it): you have an old credence function over a space; some experience shifts your credence in each cell of a partition to a new value; and your new credence in any hypothesis is the sum, over the cells, of the new credence in that cell multiplied by your old conditional credence in the hypothesis given that cell. The load-bearing assumption is rigidity: the experience may change how confident you are in the cells, but not how you would distribute credence within a cell. His example was inspecting a piece of cloth in poor light, where you become considerably more confident that it is green without becoming certain; my own case, which I think is harder, is a blurry photograph that raises my confidence that a bird is a warbler to about 0.7 without making it certain, while it also occurs to me that the blurriness may be telling me something about the camera.

His claims, as closely as I can reconstruct them, were these. First, that the rule is nothing new: ordinary Bayesian conditionalization applied to a proposition you have not quite learned, adding no commitment beyond standard Bayes. Second, that its real content is the instruction to change your overall opinion as little as possible while matching the new cell probabilities — so it is essentially a minimal-change or minimal-distance rule, and the two characterizations come to the same thing. Third, that rigidity is not a separate assumption at all but follows from the fact that only the cell probabilities changed, so that if rigidity fails you simply chose too coarse a partition and refining it always restores the rule. Fourth, that there is a Dutch book argument showing that any departure from the rule is straightforwardly irrational, which puts it on the same footing as ordinary conditionalization. Fifth, that it is a complete account of how experience should change your mind, since the new cell probabilities are themselves read off the experience. And his punchline: that the rule is in any case refuted, because it is not commutative — two soft experiences taken in one order can yield a different final state from the reverse order — and any updating rule worth having must be order-independent; from this he concluded that the project of modelling uncertain evidence Bayesianly is broken, and that one should abandon updating norms altogether rather than tinker with them. My own inclination is that the order-dependence point is a genuine worry but that he is overstating what follows from it; I have no view on the rest.

So: going through what he said, please tell me which claims are accurate, which are defensible simplifications a careful proponent would accept with a footnote, which conflate two things the framework keeps apart, and which are overstatements or misplaced confidence. In particular I'd like to know what a sympathetic defender of the rule would concede to him and what they would insist is a misrepresentation; whether the commutativity failure is a cost, a refutation, or an expected feature of uncertain evidence; whether refining the partition really does always rescue rigidity, and what my blurry-photograph case shows about that; and whether his final conclusion follows from the parts of his account that are true — or whether abandoning norms is one option among several rather than the only one left. Please also correct my restatement of the rule above if I have garbled it. Finally, be explicit about your own confidence: separate the points that are settled and would appear in any competent presentation from those that are genuinely contested among people who work on this, say where a dispute is merely verbal rather than substantive, and mark any place where you are extrapolating beyond what the formal results actually establish instead of presenting a contested position as the consensus.
