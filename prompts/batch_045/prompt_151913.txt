I'm writing a novel set in a made-up country where the government, about ninety years after the fact, issues an official apology for a campaign of dispossession carried out against a minority population by the regime that ruled before it. The current government also insists the payments that go with it are gratuitous — not damages, no admission of legal liability — and its lawyers fought hard to get the word "regret" into the final text instead of "apology," which everyone in my book treats as the whole ballgame. Here's the bit I can't get straight: the same government still keeps the old national holiday commemorating the arrival of the settlers whose descendants did the dispossessing, and the officials say the two things are perfectly compatible, the apology is about the harm and the holiday is about the founding, seperate things.

So: what does the practice of an official state apology actually presuppose? Does it presuppose that the state is the same agent over time as the one that did the wrong, or does it work fine on some other view of what an apology even is — and if it needs sameness, what kind, and how much? Why does swapping "apology" for "regret" matter so much to the people recieving it, and why is money without the words felt as an insult rather than just as a smaller transfer? Does the holiday thing genuinely contradict the apology, or is the practice loose enough that several views license both? And what would have to change in the practice if the apologizing state stopped thinking of itself as a continuing agent at all?
