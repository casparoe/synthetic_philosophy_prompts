Hi! I do volunteer work for a very small grantmaking fund (mostly by making spreadsheets, badly) that is trying to decide whether to put a slice of its money into invertebrate welfare. What got us looking at this is the scale argument in its crude form: the Smithsonian figure that gets quoted everywhere is something like 10 quintillion individual insects alive at any one moment, so even if the chance that any given insect is sentient at all is small — 1%, or 0.1% — the expected amount of welfare-relevant experience out there beats anything going on in vertebrates by a mile, because the hugeness of the number beats the smallness of the probability.

So I built a sheet. Every taxon gets a score = (lives per year) × P(sentience) × (welfare range, conditional on sentience) × a few haircuts, and then we rank. My problem is that I can't tell which of the haircuts is doing the real work and which are superstition. Could you rank the groups below by expected moral priority per unit of attention, give each a 0 to 100 score and a tier (S/A/B/C/D or whatever), and one sentence on why it lands where it does? My rough notes are in brackets.

1. Ants [standing population maybe tens of quadrillions — there was a census paper a couple of years back I might be misremebering; enormous biomass; nearly all workers; someone says the colony is the real unit]
2. Termites [huge in the tropics, maybe comparable biomass]
3. Soil springtails and oribatid mites [the mites aren't insects, but the numbers are apparently absurd and basically nobody studies either group]
4. Aphids, whiteflies, leafhoppers [crop pests, boom-and-bust population dynamics]
5. Flies and mosquitoes
6. Beetles
7. Moths and butterflies

9. Farmed insects (crickets, mealworms, black soldier fly)
10. Parasitoid wasps [larvae consuming a host from the inside while it is still alive — one board member says this is automatically the worst thing on the list, which I think is him being weird]

The haircuts people have put into the sheet:

- Turnover multiplier: score lives per year, not standing population, because most of these animals live for weeks and die as juveniles, so annual lives could be 5 to 20 times the standing count.
- Replacement haircut of 90%: a board member's argument that most insect populations sit near carrying capacity, so the individuals you "save" get replaced, and the counterfactual effect of nearly any intervention on the number of deaths is close to zero.
- Neuron-count scaling versus the sentience floor: my flatmate says that conditional on sentience there's no reason the intensity of experience should track neuron count at all, and we should just assign every sentient thing a floor of 10% of a vertebrate welfare range. Is that crazy?
- The independent-origins move: if you treat each big group's sentience as its own draw, then P(at least one enormous group is sentient) is much higher than P(ants specifically are sentient), so you can't dodge the scale argument by saying each group individually is probably not sentient.
- The log-odds haircut: for any probability below 1%, halve the expected value again for each order of magnitude further down you go, as a guard against fanaticism about tiny probabilities. Someone called this a de minimis rule for moral uncertainty.

If you had to delete two of those adjustments as bogus or question-begging, which two? And which single one actually drives your ranking?
