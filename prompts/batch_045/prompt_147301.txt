A handout from a survey course on the mind summarizes Daniel Dennett's essay "Where Am I?" roughly as follows. Dennett imagines that his brain is removed from his body and kept alive in a vat in a laboratory in Houston, linked by radio to the body, which goes on walking about and talking while the brain stays in the lab; at one point the link fails, and the body does things of which the brain-bound Dennett knows nothing. The handout then draws the moral: a person is literally wherever their brain is, so Dennett in his vat is in Houston, and the walking body is only a remote-controlled vehicle; we are our brains, and the familiar brain-transplant intuition that you go where your brain goes turns out not to be an intuition at all but a discovery. Two conclusions are said to follow. First, the story refutes any account of personal identity built on psychological continuity or memory, since the person in the vat and the person in the body can come apart in what each remembers and experiences. Second, Dennett's real moral is eliminativist: there is no fact of the matter about where a person is or who a person is, the self is a useful fiction, and a theory of personal identity is not something we should want. The handout closes by saying that the puzzle is therefore a pseudo-problem, hard only because we confused a person with a body, and that on Dennett's own view we would absurdly have to blame the man in the vat for whatever his disconnected body got up to.

I read the essay once, and this is not what I took from it. My impression was that Dennett is doing something quieter and odder than laying down a brain criterion, and that he treats the story as an intuition pump rather than as an argument, which I took to mean he does not think it settles anything on its own. But I cannot tell where paraphrase stops and editorializing begins.

Please go through the handout and tell me which parts a careful reader of Dennett would accept, which are simplifications a specialist would wave through in a survey course, and which misrepresent what he is up to. Is Dennett offering the claim that you are where your brain is as his answer to the title question, or is the route from the story to that answer more indirect? Is the statement that there is no fact of the matter a fair rendering of his conclusion, or does it inflate a deflationary point into an eliminativist one? Does the claim that the story refutes psychological-continuity accounts follow from anything accurate in the summary, given that the case looks to me like exactly the sort of case such accounts were devised to handle? And is the closing point about blame a genuine reductio, or something Dennett could accept without discomfort? Write in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
