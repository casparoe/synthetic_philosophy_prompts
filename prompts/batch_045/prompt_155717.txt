I'm not a philosopher, I do UX research, and for about a year now I've been keeping a running list in a notes app of a thing I can't find a word for. It started when a friend of mine, completely sober, said out loud that it was raining but he didn't believe it, and the whole table cracked up — that shape of sentence is just unusable, and I gather there's some famous paradox about why. But the reason I started the list is the opposite half: the same shape of sentence is often completely ordinary, even moving. My sister-in-law does bereavement counselling and she hears variants of "I know he's dead, I just don't believe it" several times a week, and nobody laughs, nobody thinks she's clients are contradicting themselves. People say the same thing about a scan that came back benign, about winning something, about being told the glass floor on the observation deck is rated for twenty tons. Its not only grief, either — someone at 2am the night before a deadline saying "I know the deadline is real, I just don't believe it" isn't joking, and I've caught myself saying it about plane flights while buckling in.

What I want is a name for whatever is going on in the second group, and, more than a name, a way of sorting cases into the two groups. My own guess is that "believe" in the fine cases isn't being used for belief at all, but for something like whether the news has landed, whether it's been emotionally taken in, whether the person would act on it without hesitating — and that the raining case is different because there's nothing there to fail to take in. But the guess has a problem I can't get past: these people pass every ordinary test for belief. They'd assert the proposition, defend it, cite the evidence, act on it, and be surprised if it turned out false. So if the thing they're denying isn't belief, what is it — a feeling, some kind of expectation about their own behaviour, a matter of salience or vividness, a second mental state that tracks belief but lags behind it? And is the "I know" in the first clause doing any work, or would "he's dead, but I don't believe it" be just as fine on its own?

I won't be satisfied with a bare label, and honestly I'd rather have the carving than the word: if there's an established concept for the second state, or for the contrast between the two groups, tell me what it is and what its boundary conditions are, and tell me how it differs from the neighbouring things it could be confused with — from ordinary partial belief, from self-deception, from just having a low confidence, from merely being surprised. And give me the edge cases, because that's where I can't decide: the person who says "I know the plane is safe but I don't believe it" on every single flight and means it every time; the person who says it about a fact they've known for years and have every reason to accept; the person who says it about a stranger's name thirty seconds after being introduced. If nothing established covers it, coin a term and define it tightly enough that I could classify those three, and say what would count as evidence that the thing being denied is a genuine state rather than just a way of talking.
