I'm retired now — I used to work in invertebrate neurophysiology, mostly motor control and escape reflexes in crayfish, of all things — and for the past couple of years I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university. This term it's moral psychology, and we spent a session on weakness of will that left me convinced the standard move in that literature is being sold as more of an explanation than it actually is. The move I mean is the one where you explain akrasia by saying that agents discount the future hyperbolically (or quasi-hyperbolically, the beta-delta thing), so that preferences reverse as a delayed option gets close, and what looks like acting against your own better judgment is really just a later temporal self getting its turn. I've sketched my complaints below as rough notes toward a handout rather than a paper. Could you go over them the way a good but slightly unkind referee would — not copy-editing, I'll do that later, and please don't bother with a bibliography or a reading list, I'll chase references myself when I can get into the library.

The notes:

Setup. Akrasia as it's usually described is synchronic: at a time t the agent judges that A is better, intends to do A, and freely does B instead, while still judging A better. Hyperbolic discounting gives you a diachronic phenomenon: preferences over the same pair of options reverse as a function of temporal distance. I want to argue the second does far less work on the first than people assume, and that what's actually going on in the near field is better modeled as a cost on control rather than a discount on time.

1. The near-field problem. Discount functions are fit to intertemporal choice data at delays of days, weeks, months — would you rather have this amount now or that amount later — and that's the only regime in which their shape has been measured. But an akratic lapse is a choice where the temptations reward and its cost are both in the present: the cigarette, the third glass, the snooze button. At delay zero every discount function sits at its maximum and is equal to every other one; the curvature that's supposed to generate the reversal is doing nothing exactly when the akratic action happens. So the theory is calibrated in the far field and applied in the near field, and nobody has checked whether the extrapolation holds, because at sub-second delays the functional form is pretty much unconstrained by data. This isn't a quibble about measurement, it's that the mechanism is idle at the moment it's invoked.

2. The synchrony problem. Even granting the reversal, a change of preference across time isn't the same thing as acting against your own judgment at a time. If the earlier self and the later self have genuinely different preferences, then neither of them is weak-willed; there's just a population of agents with divergent tastes and some pre-commitment devices. The discounting story thus doesn't explain akrasia so much as dissolve it into something that isn't akrasia. What's lost is the thing I take to be central: the agent who, right now, thinks don't and does.

3. The missing variable. In the cases I care about, what makes the bad option win isn't that its reward is nearer, it's that it's the prepared response. The judged-better option requires an act of inhibition, that act has a cost, the cost is paid in full right now, and it goes up steeply with arousal, time pressure, fatigue, and how well-rehearsed the alternative is. So the natural quantity to put in the model isn't a discount rate over delay, it's a cost on control — and notice this predicts different things: failures should track prepotency and time pressure rather than temporal distance, and they should be fixable by rehearsal and implementation habits even when the availability of the tempting option is unchanged. Discounting says you fix it by making the bad option unavailable; the control-cost story says you can also fix it by making the good option the prepotent one.

What I'd like from you: which of 1, 2, 3 is carrying the real weight and which is the weakest link; what a fan of the discounting account would say in reply, especially whether they can just shrink the temporal self down to milliseconds so that even synchronic conflict becomes diachronic at fine grain, and whether that reply saves the theory or quietly makes it unfalsifiable and leaves the intending subject with nothing to do; whether my constructive bit in 3 is genuinely a different model or just discounting in a costume; and, honestly, whether any of this is news to someone who works on the topic or whether it's the sort of thing everyone notices in week two and drops for good reasons I haven't seen yet. I also have a bad habit of overstating the first version of an argument and then having to walk it back in section four, so tell me where I've definately gone too far.
