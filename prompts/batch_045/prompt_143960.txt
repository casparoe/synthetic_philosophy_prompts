I'm a high school senior, and this year I have one semester, a faculty advisor, and a small grant from my school to write an independent research paper of about twenty-five pages. Before I can start, I have to hand in a two-page research proposal, and I'd like you to help me write it. Here is the half-formed idea I'm working from.

I've been arguing with friends about AI systems that can imitate a living musician's voice or a working illustrator's style. What struck me is that the objections people raise don't line up with each other. Some say she is being robbed of income. Some say the system is impersonating her. Some say it was trained on her work without her consent. Some say listeners are being fooled. Those look like four different complaints, and I suspect the phrase *owning your ideas* is quietly doing two or three separate jobs that would not be fixed by the same remedy — in particular, a claim over the economic value of what you make versus something more like a claim that you, and not a machine, get to speak in your own voice, or that your name not be attached to things you didn't make. Those two claims have different shapes: one looks transferable and permanent-ish, the other looks personal, narrower, and maybe mainly about attribution rather than about exclusion.

Please turn this into a proposal with the following parts.

1. A single, precisely stated question that has a determinate answer. My guess is that the question is something like: should there be an enforceable interest in artistic style or voice *as such*, and if so is that an extension of intellectual property or a different kind of protection with a different shape (narrower, non-transferable, aimed at attribution or at substitution rather than at copying)? But you should sharpen it, and you should say explicitly whether the question is being asked at the level of what the law should do or at the level of what is morally wrong, and why that level is the right one for a project this size.

2. Why the standard positions leave the question open — not a literature review by citation, just the arguments. Include at least: the incentive-style justification (which was always about getting works made, and which seems both to support and to choke on style protection, since protecting style also blocks the imitating, learning-from, genre-building work that new artists do); the labor or personality style of justification (which seems to support style protection strongly, and then has trouble saying where it stops); the familiar point that ideas are not scarce, and my own worry that the scarcity argument is being borrowed from physical property where it doesn't obviously apply and that nobody has told me what should replace it; and the traditional refusal to protect style or genre at all. Say what each of these arguments settles and what it leaves untouched.

3. A method section with a month-by-month plan for one semester and no empirical work. The core of it should be a small set of constructed cases that pull the variables apart: imitating after training on someone's actual works versus arriving at a similar style independently with no access to them; a working artist versus one who has stopped publishing versus one long dead; output openly labelled as an imitation versus output that audiences take for the real thing; a single fan's style study versus industrial-scale output that competes with the original in the same market. For each case say which features are supposed to matter, and make the proposal commit in advance to where it thinks the cut-point lies — I want the cases to be able to embarrass the theory, not merely illustrate it.

4. The possible conclusions, listed, together with which considerations would decide between them. Please don't write it so that the only interesting outcome is that IP should expand; one real possibility is that the voice-cloning worry is genuine but isn't really about ownership at all, and the proposal should be able to land there.

5. Honest failure conditions. My own fear is that everything collapses: that once you press on the cases, every one of them turns out to be about lost income plus audiences being misled, so that there is no distinct style-interest and I've just written a standard IP essay with new labels on it. Say what I would look at, partway through, to tell whether that has happened, and what I'd do if it has.

6. A scope section: what to cut to keep this to twenty-five pages written by someone who is not a professional, what the biggest risk of the project ballooning is, and what a finished deliverable that someone could actually use would look like — ideally a decision rule that says, for a given imitation, whether it is objectionable and which remedy fits, rather than a general verdict for or against.

Format it with short headed sections, in a fairly formal academic register but not a pretentious one. One constraint: don't give me a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations — I'm required to find and check my own sources, and I'll add them. You can name positions and arguments in general terms (the incentive-based account, personality-based accounts, the idea/expression distinction) and you can refer to the kind of case law or statutes involved without naming or dating specific cases.
