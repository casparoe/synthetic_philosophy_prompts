I write reported features on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am building an interactive sidebar to accompany a piece I am finishing on a question that keeps surfacing in policy form: when a hospital, a relief agency, or a government can help only some of the people who need help, what exactly is gained by helping the people who are worst off? Readers have strong instincts here and no vocabulary for them, and the philosophical literature that bears on the question is a thicket of positions named after their technical differences. I want the sidebar to sort readers by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they find attractive.

What I need is a decision tree that begins from a single commitment almost everyone shares — that helping someone who is badly off matters more than helping someone who is comfortable — and routes the reader to a named position. Concretely:

Deliverable and shape. Six or seven branch nodes, each of which presents one concrete allocation case with two or three options the reader chooses between, followed by a routing table, eight to ten leaves, and a short glossary. Each leaf carries the standard name of the position (or unnamed if it has no settled name), one sentence on what the position claims, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Cases should be everyday: a transplant waiting list, a flood-defence budget split between two towns, a clinic with one course of an expensive drug, a disaster shipment that can reach one valley. Where different people's stakes need to be compared, state them as numbers on a scale you define briefly at the top. No technical vocabulary should appear inside a branch question — a reader should never have to know the words priority, weighted, aggregate, comparative, or threshold to answer one — and every option should be something a thoughtful person could actually pick rather than a straw man.

The distinctions I need the tree to earn. Please make sure the branch questions jointly discriminate the following, splitting or merging them as the logic of the space requires:

- Whether a fixed gain counts for more when it lands on someone worse off at all; and if so, whether the extra credit depends only on how badly off that person is in absolute terms, or also on how they stand compared with everyone else nearby (the same gain given to someone at a low level when everyone around them is very well off, versus the same gain given to someone at that level when everyone around them is only slightly better off).
- The case that has historically caused trouble: a change that makes the worse-off person slightly better off and the better-off person substantially worse off, so that the overall pattern is more even but the total is lower and nobody is helped as much as someone is hurt.
- A case turning on risk: two people equally badly off now, where one option is a fair coin flip that fully restores one of them and leaves the other untreated, and the other is a certain but modest improvement for both. This should separate views that apply their weighting to outcomes from views that apply it to people's prospects.
- Numbers versus the bottom: a very large number of moderately well-off people each getting a small gain, against a single badly-off person getting a somewhat larger one.
- A case where both candidate recipients are comfortably above the line at which a life is going decently, one a little lower than the other: does the claim of the worse-off one persist all the way up the scale, or does it switch off somewhere?
- Whether a gain that lifts someone out of misery counts differently from an equal-sized gain that takes someone from fine to thriving, over and above the difference in level.
- A case involving time: someone badly off now who was well off earlier, against someone well off now who was badly off earlier. Is the standing that matters the whole life, or the moment the benefit arrives?

Leaves. I want the tree to be able to reach at least the following, and I would rather you tell me honestly that one cannot be reached than force it: an unweighted sum view; a view that weights gains by how well off the recipient is in absolute terms; a variant that weights by relative standing or rank rather than absolute level; a threshold view on which the extra credit switches off once people are doing well enough; a view that attends only to whoever is worst off; a version of weighting that applies to prospects under uncertainty rather than to outcomes; an explicitly comparative view on which what matters is the gap itself; and a variant that weights suffering more heavily than satisfaction. Add up to two more leaves of your own choosing if the space has room for views I have not listed.

After the tree, two short sections. First: combinations of answers the tree permits but that no one actually defends — say for each whether the combination looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Second: the pairs of leaves your questions cannot pull apart, and why; do not disguise such pairs by adding a question that only sounds diagnostic.

Closing paragraph. I would like one paragraph on which leaf, if any, can be pushed into endorsing a change that leaves nobody better off and somebody worse off purely for the sake of the pattern, and which leaves escape that result by their own construction rather than by luck.

A note on calibration: I am not looking for citations or further reading. What I do want is for you to mark, at each branch, how confident you are that the case really tracks the distinction it is meant to track rather than some other one, and to single out the one step in the tree you are least sure of, with a sentence on what would settle it. Flag as well any place where the tree depends on a contested reading of one of the views, or on a particular choice of how steeply the weighting falls off.
