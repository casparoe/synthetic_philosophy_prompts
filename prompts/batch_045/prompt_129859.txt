I have been trying to reconstruct Galen Strawson's so-called Basic Argument against moral responsibility in a form I could actually put pressure on, and my reconstruction now bothers me for a reason I can't localize: it seems to reach well beyond moral responsibility, and I can't tell whether that is a feature of the argument or a symptom that I have built something unsound. I'd like a diagnosis of the chain below, and I'd rather be told what each available repair costs than which one to make.

The chain, as I have it:

(1) Provenance condition. A person is morally responsible for an action in the desert-involving sense only if they are responsible for being the kind of person who would do it — that is, for the character, motives, and standards that actually explain why the action issued from them. Praising or blaming someone for what their character produced while excusing them for the character seems like stopping one step too early.

(2) Only-by-making condition. One can be responsible for being a certain way only by having intentionally brought it about that one is that way, in an act of self-formation whose outcome was under one's control. Causing it, or being caused to cause it, or growing into it, is not enough: those are things that happen to a person, not things they are answerable for.

(3) Regress. Any act of self-formation is performed in light of motives, principles, and standards the agent already has. To be responsible for what that act makes of them, they must already be responsible for those prior motives — which by (1) and (2) requires a further act of self-formation, and so on without end.

(4) Therefore no one is ever morally responsible in the sense specified by (1), since a series with no first member cannot be got through by a finite agent, and adding indeterminism does not help but only inserts chance where control was wanted.

So far this is, I take it, the argument as intended. My discomfort begins when I generalize it.

(5) Nothing in (1) through (4) appears to depend on the word 'moral'. The same structure runs for any standing that is supposed to depend on how its subject came to be the way it is: epistemic justification (you can be justified in a belief only if you are responsible for the reliability of the faculties that produced it), credit for achievement, the bindingness of a promise, ownership of what your labour produced, even the plain fact that an event was *your action* rather than something that merely happened in you. If the argument is sound, it seems to show that no belief is ever justified, no achievement ever creditable, no promise ever binding, and no event ever really an action.

(6) And it turns on itself. I can be responsible for asserting this argument only if I chose the standards of good reasoning by which I judge it, which by (2) and (3) requires a prior choice, and so on.

(7) But a sound argument presumably cannot establish that there are no agents, no reasons, and no justified assertions, this one included. So something in (1) through (4) is wrong.

Before I tell you where I think the trouble is, consider a case I invented to test the chain, which I call 'the long way round.'

Dana at twenty is impulsive, vain, and evasive — a character she did not choose and could not have chosen. Over the next forty years she deliberately rebuilds herself: she arranges circumstances, rehearses motives, uses the motives available at each stage to alter the motives that will be available at the next, exploits events she cannot fully predict, and at every stage consciously endorses the direction of change, including the direction she endorsed at earlier stages. At sixty she performs a costly act of honesty that the twenty-year-old Dana would not have performed, that she performs for the reasons she spent those decades installing, and that she calmly endorses at the moment of acting and afterwards. There is no single act of self-creation anywhere in the sequence, and no first link she laid; there is only a long chain, each link forged with the resources of its predecessors, all of them now and then endorsed.

What I want to know:

Where does the chain go wrong, if it does? Name the step. Explain why that step looked sound — what makes it plausible enough to have been accepted without argument. And price the alternatives: what is lost by rejecting (1), by rejecting (2), by rewriting the regress in (3) so that it demands only that each link be properly the agent's own rather than that the series have a first member, and what is lost by keeping (1) through (4) intact and simply accepting the generalizations in (5) and (6).

Tell me in particular what work the Dana case does. Does she satisfy the provenance condition in (1) while failing the only-by-making condition in (2), and if so, does that show that (2) is doing all the real work — or does it show that (4)'s conclusion is best read as being about a maximal, self-creation-involving kind of responsibility, leaving ordinary moral responsibility untouched? A friend suggested that (5) can be blocked by dividing standings into ones that are source-involving and ones that are merely state-involving: desert, credit for achievement, and the authorship of action on one side; epistemic justification and the bindingness of promises on the other. I could not get her to say what makes desert source-involving if justification need not be, and I suspect the distinction is doing less than it appears to.

If I must state my own attachments: I am most attached to (1), since excusing a person for the character from which the action flowed is precisely the move I want to block, and least attached to (2), which nonetheless strikes me as close to analytic. I would rather keep (1) and (4) than keep (5)'s generalizations, but I would like to know what I am paying for that preference rather than merely having it confirmed.
