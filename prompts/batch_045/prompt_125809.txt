i run a monthly data-literacy hour for the analysts on our growth team — about fifteen people, very fluent in SQL, nervous around anything causal. last month we had a small incident: a new onboarding flow lost on the topline conversion number but won inside both the mobile and the desktop cuts. two of them walked away with opposite morals. one says this proves topline numbers are junk and you should always read the segments. the other says it's just a weighting artefact, nothing to see, report the topline and move on. i've got 50 minutes and a whiteboard and i want to actually fix the confusion, not just show them a cute trick and have them be more confused.

so please don't explain Simpson's paradox to me — plan how i should teach it, to this exact room:

- the order the ideas have to arrive in, and what depends on what. my instinct is that the "a rate is just a weighted average" bit has to land before any reversal is shown, but tell me if there's something that has to come even before that.
- the one example that carries the whole session, with actual small numbers. it should be about their world (experiments, funnels, platforms, traffic mix), small enough to hold in your head, and it should make the flip feel inevitable rather than like a card trick. also: tell me what the example must *not* have — i'm wary of an example where the right answer is obviously "the subgroups, obviously," because that teaches them the wrong lesson in the other direction. and say why i should skip the two cases everyone reaches for (the kidney-stone one, the Berkeley admissions one).
- the misconceptions, in the order this room will actually produce them, and where in the sequence i should head each off. the ones i'm betting on: "the topline is wrong / someone messed up the math"; "more data would fix it"; "the subgroups are always the true answer"; "we randomize our A/B tests so this can't happen to us" (i suspect that last one is only half true and i don't trust my own explanation of why); and "ok, then just segment harder" — which i also have a feeling isn't automatically right, but again i can't say why.
- one question i can ask in the last five minutes that would tell me they got it rather than just learned to be suspicious of averages.
- what i should openly refuse to get into at this level.

commit to things: pick one example, tell me what to cut, and give me a straight verdict on both of my colleagues' morals — which one survives and why, even if the answer is that they're both wrong. where your plan rests on a contested call, say so and tell me what you'd do anyway.
