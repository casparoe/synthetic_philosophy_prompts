I'm TAing for an intro ethics course this term and we did Taurek last week, and I got into an argument with a friend afterwards that I've been going around in circles about ever since. His move was: fine, grant Taurek that you can't just add up six people's deaths into one giant death, and grant that you're sometimes allowed to save the one rather than the five. But then, he says, if nobody has any special claim on you and nobody is worse off than anyone else, the honest way to respect six equal claims is to give each of the six people an equal chance at surviving, i.e. to randomize. And I said: sure, but isn't that just sneaking the numbers in through the back door, given that the only reason randomizing does any good is that there are more people on one side? He said no. Neither of us got anywhere, because we kept arguing over cases where the answer was basically built in by how we described them.

So what I want from you is a case, built to order. It needs two feasible actions, and the only difference between the people on the two sides should be how many of them there are: no age gaps, no one of them is a surgeon or a parent, no quality-of-life differences, nobody responsible for the emergency, no consent or promises or prior claims floating around, no relationship to the agent, and everyone involved should be equally badly off if unhelped (they all just die) and equally fine if they make it. Realistic setting, please: a hospital, a disaster site, a fire crew, a rescue boat, a supply drop, something like that, and definitely no trolleys. Probabilities are allowed, and I actually think they're where the action is: if everything is certain under both actions then equal-chances-to-each just collapses into a plain coin flip and Taurek's permission is the only other thing on the table, so nothing separates the two positions. So build it out of risky prospects, with the kind of numbers a rescue team could actually estimate, not one-in-a-billion stuff. Two more design constraints: (1) in the base case the expected number of lives saved should come out exactly equal across the two actions, so that plain numbers-counting has no verdict at all and the disagreement has to get settled by something else; (2) the equal-chance procedure should still return a definite verdict that isn't 50/50 and isn't merely a permission — it should tell you that you must do something specific.

Then do the analysis. Say what each of these three things says about your case and why: plain save-the-greater-number reasoning over expected lives, the Taurek-ish package (numbers don't count, each person's loss is theirs alone and isn't made worse by the others', so you may save the fewer), and the equal-chance-to-each procedure. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work — the thing that makes the first one fall silent while the other two come apart — and show what happens if you remove it, e.g. what happens if you de-risk the case and make all the outcomes certain again. Then do one extra check that matters to me more than the rest: re-run the case with the bigger group scaled way up, five people to five hundred, holding each individual's chance of surviving fixed, and report which verdicts move and which don't. That's the crux of my argument with my friend — whether equal-chances-to-each is genuinely insensitive to how many people there are, or whether it tracks the numbers after all. Finish by telling me what the side that comes out worst would say to wriggle out of your case, and then, in a sentence or two, what you think should actually be done in it.
