I do policy/responsible-AI work at a small company that makes an email client, and on the side I sit in on a pragmatics reading group run by a professor who is extremely terse and extremely consistent about examples. I've been writing down her verdicts because our product shows people *suggested replies* — three tappable chips under an incoming email — and we're arguing about who is on the hook for what one of those conveys. I'd rather guess what she'd say from her track record than flip a coin in a meeting.

Her verdicts as best I can reconstruct them:

1. "Some of the students passed" — the not-all bit is a conversational implicature: cancellable without contradiction (some, in fact all of them), and it disappears once you embed the sentence under "if" or negate it.
2. "She took off her glasses and got into bed" — the sequence isn't part of what was said, but it also isn't cancellable (adding "though not in that order" is bad), and she denied it's a conventional implicature of "and". She called it a default that you get unless something rules it out.
3. "The meeting's at 7am, but I'll be there" — the contrast rides on "but"; you can't get rid of it, and you can't deny it by saying "that's false", you'd have to say "there's no contrast there".
4. "Even the TA came to the 8am lecture" — unexpectedness; still there inside "did even the TA come?"; same deal, denying the main content doesn't touch it. She added that you can't get out of it by saying you hadn't meant to suggest it was surprising.
5. Someone describing a bad singer as having "produced a series of sounds loosely approximating the national anthem" — the sneer is a conversational implicature, not detachable (say it flatly and you lose it), and weird to reinforce out loud ("and I don't mean to suggest she sang badly"). She also said the sneer sticks to whoever *picked that phrasing*: the same description inside a report of what someone else said still left the sneer with the reporter, not the person quoted.
6. Asked what proposition "Jill is ready" expressed, she said the bit about *what* Jill is ready for isn't an implicature at all — it's not an extra message on top of a complete proposition, it's what you need to have a proposition in the first place, and you can neither cancel it nor deny it on its own.
7. Once, about a drafted-but-not-meant message that got sent by accident, she said something like: what you sent is what you said, but whether you meant it is a separate question. I'm paraphrasing badly and it's the only time she's gone anywhere near this.

Our case. Priya, a user, gets an email from her department chair at 11pm asking people to confirm they'll be at a 7am Saturday meeting. The three chips the model offered were "I'll be there", "Works for me", and "Wouldn't miss it!". She tapped the third one 1.2 seconds after opening the mail, on a train. She later told us she hadn't read the wording: she was annoyed, she'd already decided to go, and she tapped whatever was under her thumb, and she's been grumbling about the 7am start to colleagues all week. The chair took it as enthusiasm and now feels played. Nobody chose the *form* — the model did — but Priya chose to send, and she did intend to convey that she'd attend.

So: what would she say — did Priya's message implicate that she was eager, or only that she'd be there?
