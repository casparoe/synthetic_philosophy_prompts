I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am writing a long feature on where the Anthropocene stands now. As you may know, the proposal to formalize it as a series/epoch was voted down in 2024 by the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy and then formally rejected by the International Commission on Stratigraphy and the parent body, with the stated grounds being the shallowness of its sedimentary record and the extreme recency of the proposed start date; the official statement nonetheless conceded that the word will go on being used by scientists, social scientists, politicians and the public as a descriptor of human impact on the Earth system. My piece includes a counterfactual sidebar: I drop the Anthropocene Working Group's formal proposal — mid-twentieth-century base, plutonium fallout spike, a varved lake in Ontario as the candidate golden spike — onto the desk of Charles Lyell and ask what he makes of it.

I need you to help me write that sidebar. Please take the role of a historian of geology who is also a philosopher of science, with a working command of stratigraphic nomenclature, and work in two stages.

Stage one: before drafting, think it through out loud. List the specific commitments in Lyell's own practice that bear on this question, and for each, say which way it would push him. To get you started, here is the background I have established, which you should treat as given rather than as something to recall. In the third volume of the Principles of Geology, Lyell subdivided the Tertiary by the degree of affinity of fossil shells to the living fauna, on figures the conchologist Deshayes produced by comparing some three thousand fossil species against five thousand living ones: roughly three and a half per cent of living species in the oldest group, about seventeen in the middle, thirty-five to fifty in the upper, and ninety to ninety-five in the most modern beds; he named these Eocene, Miocene and Pliocene, and later distinguished the Pleistocene (recent shells, but extinct fossil quadrupeds) from the Recent (everything living). In his 1863 book on the antiquity of man he looked back on this and noted that the terms were invented with reference purely to conchological data, that they had since been stretched to cover strata which do not conform to the original definitions, and that wherever one tries to draw a line between adjacent units the difficulties will multiply rather than diminish as gaps in the record are filled.

Stage two: write the sidebar. It should run about nine hundred to eleven hundred words, in prose an educated general reader can follow, with no invented dialogue and no fabricated quotations — attribute nothing to Lyell that is not traceable to arguments he actually made, and mark clearly where you are extrapolating. Please adjudicate, rather than gesturing at, the following cruxes:

1. Measurement. Lyell's own index of geological time was faunal similarity, and by that index nothing happens in 1950: no extinction pulse is yet visible in shells. Would he conclude that his proxy is not the thing it measures and so does not settle the question, or that a boundary invisible to his method is not a boundary? Note that the extinction signal that would make the case in his terms lies in the future, which raises the question of whether a unit can be defined prospectively, by a cause now in operation whose stratigraphic expression has not yet accumulated.

2. Rate. Lyell is the patron of slow, cumulative, still-operating causes, and the proposal rests on a claim of unprecedented abruptness. Did his uniformitarianism require uniformity of rate, or only of law and kind of cause — and which reading makes him friendlier or less friendly to a rupture of this sort?

3. Diachrony. Human influence spread across the globe at very different times, so on one view the proper category is not an epoch with a single synchronous base in one lake but a drawn-out, diachronous event. This seems to me the most Lyellian objection available, since a gradual spread of a cause is exactly what his own picture predicts, while a single global marker is not. Say how much weight you think he would give it, and what he would make of the reply that a golden spike is a convention for correlating, not a claim that the change was instantaneous.

4. Choice of date. Give me his ranking of the rivals — the mid-century bomb spike, the early seventeenth-century dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide associated with the collision of the Old and New Worlds, the industrial revolution, and the hypothesis that agriculture began altering atmospheric methane thousands of years earlier — and the principle he would rank them by.

5. Authority. Lyell named epochs in a book, with no commission, no code and no vote, and they stuck because colleagues found them useful. What would he think about the fact that a committee decision has settled the formal status of the term while doing nothing about its currency outside geology, and about the argument that a word's usefulness to climatologists, lawyers and activists is a reason either for or against ratifying it?

After the prose, add a short confidence ledger: five or six lines, each naming one claim you have made about Lyell and rating it high, medium or low, with a clause on what would settle it. Where you think the historical record genuinely underdetermines his answer, say so plainly rather than inventing a position; where you think the question is anachronistic in a way that blocks an answer, explain why.
