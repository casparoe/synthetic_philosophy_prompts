Hey — I run the final for a grad-level seminar on ML safety and philosophy (mixed phil/CS crowd; most of them have read the usual LessWrong and ACX fare but defintely haven't had real decision theory). I need one take-home essay question on reward hacking / Goodharting that isn't just explain why optimizing a proxy fails. Everyone in the cohort can already recite that, plus orthogonality, instrumental convergence, and the bit where the AI isn't your enemy, you're just made of atoms it can use for something else. I want a question that forces them to actually analyze a setup they haven't seen before.

Here's a setup I've been kicking around — modify it if you've got something better, but please keep the basic shape. You have a preference dataset labeled by a big pool of human annotators. You randomly partition the *annotators* (not the examples) into K disjoint groups, and you train K reward models from the same base model with the same recipe, one per group, so they're the same model trained on different labelers. Now you do RL. Call the objective that optimizes the mean of the K rewards MEAN-K, and the one that optimizes the minimum MIN-K. The min version feels like it should be the safe one: the policy only gets credit if *every* reward model likes the output, so it can't find some quirk that fools one model the way it fools a single mean-pooled reward model.

The question should make them say what MIN-K does and doesn't buy you, and why. At minimum I want the answer to have to grapple with: the difference between errors the models disagree about and errors they share (they're all fine-tuned from the same base model — how independent are they, really, once the policy walks off-distribution); the fact that the min is an order statistic, so its behavior as K grows is nothing like the mean's, and a single pessimistic subgroup can end up holding a veto; and what the optimization pressure selects for once you need all K models satisfied at once (does that suppress shared spurious features like length or confidence, or amplify them?). Worth having them think about what MIN-K would do to output blandness/hedging and why, and whether any fix has to come from changing the aggregator at all or from getting new information in.

Deliverables: the question text (48h take-home, 1200–1500 words); a model answer of about 900 words that argues to a verdict rather than listing considerations, self-contained, no footnotes or citations — names of ideas are fine, bibliographies are not; and a marking scheme worth 20 marks built around five must-hit points with the marks per point and one line on how students typically get each one wrong. Add a short note on what separates an A from a B, and one line on where a student could reasonably push back on the model answer.

Oh, and write the whole thing in American English — spelling, punctuation, all of it. The department style guide is weirdly fussy about that.
