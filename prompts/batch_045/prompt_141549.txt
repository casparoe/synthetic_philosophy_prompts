I am a novelist working on a book set some forty years from now, and I need one self-contained chapter that dramatizes, rather than argues, a problem I keep circling. Here is my conceit. A woman in her sixties has had a slab of lab-grown cortical tissue implanted to replace what a stroke destroyed. The graft was never programmed — it was only placed — and over two years it taught itself to talk to the rest of her brain. The engineers can record every all-or-none pulse and every chemical gradient in it, but since no one specified the code, no one can say what any of it means. My narrator is a field linguist, trained on unwritten languages with no bilingual informant, hired to do fieldwork on a piece of someone's brain.

The philosophical material I want the chapter to be faithful to is the position von Neumann sketched in the posthumously published Silliman lectures that appeared in 1958 as The Computer and the Brain, as I understand it: first, that the nervous system looks digital at the level of the single pulse but is also continuous — pulse frequency, chemistry — so its real code is neither one, and in any case is not the language of mathematics or formal logic, which is our language rather than the brain's; second, that the brain's individual components are far less reliable than a vacuum tube, and that its reliability is achieved by organisation — redundancy, massive parallelism, many imprecise operations yielding an accurate result — rather than by precision; third, that a genuine theory of the brain would therefore have to be stated in the brain's own terms, which we do not know.

What I want the reader to feel is the asymmetry this sets up: that fluency and translation can come apart. The linguist should end up understanding the graft — able to respond to it, changed by it — while being unable to report what it says in any notation available to her, and the graft's visible unreliability should be the evidence that she is not dealing with a calculus at all: a third of the pulses misfire and the conversation is still flawless, which no logic we possess would survive. Do not let the chapter become a lecture; let the argument arrive through the characters' frustration with their own instruments.
