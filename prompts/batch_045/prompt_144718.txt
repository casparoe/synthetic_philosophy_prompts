I run a one-day working group — four philosophers, a health economist, and two people who work in disability policy — that has to produce a single-page diagnostic: a decision tree sorting anyone who calls themselves an egalitarian into a named position on the question of equality of what. Everyone in the room agrees that inequalities matter and disagrees about what is supposed to be equalized, and the disagreement keeps resurfacing as a practical one about what a public health system should itself be trying to equalize. So the branching should be organized around distinctions that bite on allocation decisions, not around the history of the debate.

Please write the tree under the following constraints.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then the actual positions located in that space. Every node must be a question a participant could answer without already knowing which view it leads to — preferably a concrete case — and every branch must be labeled by the claim that separates it from its siblings.

Currency is not the only axis, and I don't want the whole tree to be the familiar menu of welfare, resources, capabilities, and access to advantage. Include at least two further axes: the timing or aggregation question (is the unit of assessment a whole life, a time slice, or the worst period a person can reasonably expect?), and the question of whether equality is doing fundamental work or merely proxying for something else — sufficiency, priority, non-domination, or standing as an equal. Luck egalitarianism, and the non-egalitarian neighbors it shades into, should appear as leaves, as should the relational view.

Each leaf carries either the standard name of the position or the label "unnamed — nobody defends this," plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment or, for unoccupied regions, on why nobody is there (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Where two views give the same answers throughout your questions, flag the pair as unseparated rather than splitting them with a cosmetic distinction.

One leaf should be a position I have been toying with and cannot place: call it the Recoverability View. What should be equalized is not welfare, resources, or capabilities but each person's expected ease and speed of getting back to a decent baseline after the worst setback that could reasonably befall them — illness, job loss, bereavement, a bad but reversible choice of their own. It is meant to capture something about security that resource- and capability-based views miss, and to come apart from them on cases where two people have the same bundle and the same functionings but very different prospects of recovery. Place it on the tree; name the nearest existing position it collapses into, if it collapses into any; and give the single case you would use to separate it from that neighbor.

The cases at the nodes have to be usable by people whose day jobs are health policy and disability services: voluntary expensive tastes; tastes formed under deprivation and later endorsed; a person with a disability whose adaptive preferences are genuine; an outcome that is the person's own fault in a fully voluntary way versus one that was merely foreseeable. Add at least one case of your own construction that separates two views the above do not separate.

Audience and size: ten branch questions at most, eight to twelve leaves, written so that a smart non-specialist can work through it in twenty minutes. Include a short scoring note on how to read an answer pattern, including what to do with someone whose answers land in an unoccupied region of the space.

Two further things I want, briefly. First, mark the placements that rest on a contested reading of a position — the points where a specialist in that view would say you have misdescribed it — and say where the tree's construction is most likely to fail: which distinction you suspect is doing less work than the tree implies. Second, say what would make you redraw it: which answer pattern from a real participant, or which case, would force a different branching order.

Hard limit: the entire response — tree, scoring note, flags, and caveats — must fit in 1,000 words, with no more than 300 of those written as continuous prose rather than as tree or notes.
