hey — I'm a PhD student in metaphysics, writing the chapter where I try to show that restricted answers to the special composition question are unstable: they either quietly collapse into universalism or they turn into some kind of brutalism with extra steps. I want a pair of cases to open the seminar with and my own attempts keep falling apart. Can you build me one?

What I need: two cases, A and B, such that —

1. At the level of description a contact- or cohesion-style criterion can see — what's touching what, what's bonded to what, the topology of the arrangement, what's flowing where and at what rate, what moves together when you poke it — the two are as near identical as you can get them. Ideally a working biologist told only that much couldn't guess which case she was being shown.
2. Under the life criterion (the van Inwagen-ish answer: some things compose something further iff their activity constitutes a life) the verdict clearly flips. Yes in one, no in the other. Direction is up to you, but I need an actual flip, not just a case that's hard for one theory.
3. No cheating off vagueness. No viruses, no prions, no "well, is a slime mold one organism or many" shrugging, nothing on the life/not-life borderline. Both verdicts should be ones a holder of each theory would state confidently.
4. Realistic. No sci-fi, no demons, no exotic physics. Ordinary biology or ordinary artefact-y setups are fine. Pls skip corpses, coma patients and brain-dead bodies on ventilators — I tried all three and they're doing some other job entirely, plus people just start arguing about death.
5. Nothing conscious in either case. Every time I put a subject of experience in, forty minutes vanish into personal identity. Plants, fungi, colonial marine inverts, cell cultures, industrial machinery — anything that isn't a subject.
6. The annoying constraint: state both cases without using a single singular term for a composite object. Plural talk about the simples only (or about cells/molecules/parts, if you want to fix a level and treat it as simple enough for the purpose), plus topological, causal, and functional vocabulary. It's a pain, but half the room are nihilists and if my setup says "the organism" they'll just say I begged the question.
7. Stretch goal: make A and B the two ends of one continuous process, where the contact-level description from (1) never jumps discontinuously anywhere in the middle. If you can't, tell me why it can't be done and I'll quietly drop that bit.

Then, after the cases:

- Verdicts in both cases under (a) nihilism, (b) universalism, (c) the life criterion, (d) a plain contact criterion, and (e) a hobbyhorse of mine I want stress-tested: call it Non-Redundancy — some things compose something further iff there is some activity they perform together that no proper subset of them could perform on its own. I made it up, so don't go looking for it; what I want to know is whether it peels off from the other four on your case or just shadows one of them.
- A sentence or two of why for each verdict, not just the verdict.
- Which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if you delete it. If removing it makes all five theories say the same thing in both cases, I need to know — that's arguably the result I want for the chapter, so don't hide it.
- Last thing: steelman the parity objection. Whatever feature you used to generate the flip, why isn't the moderate just gerrymandering — picking whichever feature delivers the answer she likes and then calling that feature composition-relevant? Give me the strongest reply available to the moderate, and then give me your honest read on whether this pair helps her or hurts her.

A paragraph each for the cases, tops; the discussion after can be as long as it needs to be. thx!
