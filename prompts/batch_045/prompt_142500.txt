I'm in a small reading group working through theories of welfare, and we've spent two sessions on the view we've been calling tranquilism: what is basically good for a person is only the absence of craving and of the suffering that goes with it, while unsatisfied craving and suffering are what is basically bad; things like pleasure, achievement, knowledge and love count, if at all, only derivatively — because their absence would be craved, or because pursuing them quietens a craving one already has.

Last week someone ended an argument with the line "Pain is inevitable, suffering is optional" (it circulates everywhere, never with a source), and used it for two moves: first, that pain itself is prudentially inert, so resources spent on pain relief do nothing for anyone's welfare while resources spent teaching people to stop craving do all the real work; second, that we are therefore committed to saying a chronic-pain patient who has learned acceptance is, welfare-wise, exactly as well off as a pain-free person. Both moves feel too fast to me, but I can't tell which precise claim the one-liner is supposed to be contributing, and my suspicion is that it is sliding between a definitional trick and a very strong empirical claim.

Please give me the distinct precise theses the sentence could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and a verdict on each. My own attempts keep collapsing into two: a stipulative one (if "suffering" just means pain-plus-aversion, then of course you can have the first without the second) and an empirical one (with enough training, the aversive layer really can be peeled off while the pain sensation stays). I'd like you to get beyond those: readings about what suffering is constituted by, about whether the "optional" is a claim about what is possible, what is within someone's control, or what they owe others, and about whether pain retains any prudential weight at all. For each reading say whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and doubtful, or straightforwardly false, and what commitment it saddles the speaker with.

Two further constraints. One: I don't want any bibliography, citations or reading suggestions — I need something I can say out loud, and I have no library to hand. Two: please test each reading against a problem I've been calling the "three routes to quiet." The same craving-free state seems reachable in at least three ways — by satisfying the craving, by extinguishing it through long training, and by suppressing it pharmacologically with something like a hypothetical anti-craving pill — and also against the case of the meditator who now craves to be free of craving. For each reading, say whether it has the resources to treat those routes as equivalent in welfare, or is forced to; and if every reading ends up unable to distinguish them, say so plainly rather than rescuing it, since that equivalence is the thing I most want to know whether I've inherited.

Format: give me the following four sections, in this order.

1. "The menu of readings": a table of six to eight readings, columns for the reading stated in one sentence, the kind of claim it is (definitional, empirical, normative, constitutive), a verdict, and its most awkward commitment. Aim for roughly 600–750 words, and flag any pair of rows that are really the same claim reworded.
2. "What each of my interlocutor's moves needs": which reading my interlocutor must be relying on for each of the two conclusions, whether that reading is one of the defensible ones, and whether it actually delivers the conclusion. Roughly 250–350 words.
3. "The three routes to quiet": the results of the test above, reading by reading, including the second-order meditator case. Roughly 250–300 words.
4. "What I should say on Thursday": three or four sentences I can actually use — what to concede, what to press — with no hedging and no jargon. Roughly 120–150 words.
