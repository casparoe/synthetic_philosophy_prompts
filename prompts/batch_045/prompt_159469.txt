I install acoustic treatment in recording studios for a living, so I know my way around decibels, but I have no formal training in philosophy and I have been stuck for months on an argument with a friend who does have a philosophy degree. She says that 'better than' has to be transitive — that if one option is better than a second and the second better than a third, the first must be better than the third — and that this is not a discovery about value but part of what a comparative like this means. Her favourite move is the analogy with 'taller than': heights lie on a single scale, and any relation defined by position on such a scale is automatically transitive, so if my reasoning appears to produce a cycle, the mistake must be in my reasoning. I want to argue the opposite, and I would like the strongest version of my case rather than a survey of the territory. My own hunch is that her argument quietly assumes that betterness is one-dimensional in the way height is, that once value is spread across several dimensions transitivity becomes a substantial constraint which can in principle fail, and that a carefully built case can show that it actually does fail. Please develop that hunch if it is sound, correct it if it is not, and give me the argument for intransitivity in the form I could hand to her.

Here is the case I have been working on. Imagine a rental website listing fifty thousand otherwise identical apartments, sorted so that each one is a single cent a month cheaper than the one before it and a thousandth of a decibel louder. I am happy to stipulate what makes the case work: a difference of a thousandth of a decibel is far below anything any human ear can detect, and it has no other effect on the resident either — no effect on sleep, on health, on the value of the place, on anything anyone could care about. Apartment 1 is $2,000 a month at a quiet 45 decibels; apartment 50,000 is $1,500 a month at about 95 decibels, roughly the level of a lawnmower running in the room. Now, each step up the list looks to me like a clear improvement: you are a cent a month better off and nothing about your situation is worse in any way at all, since the extra noise is not merely negligible but undetectable and inert. And yet I am perfectly confident that apartment 1 is better than apartment 50,000: five hundred dollars a month is nowhere near worth permanent hearing damage and living inside a lawnmower. If 'better than' were transitive, those two sets of judgments could not both be right. So either betterness is not transitive, or the fifty thousand step judgments are not all correct, or my endpoint judgment is wrong, or the principle that an undetectable, inert worsening cannot make an outcome worse is false. I want an argument that the first of these is the right conclusion, and that it is the step judgments, not transitivity, that deserve to be preserved.

Three replies from my friend I need dealt with, please, and I want them engaged on their merits rather than listed. First, she says that in any long chain of comparisons of this shape, at least one pairwise judgment must be mistaken, even if nobody can say which, and that we should simply suspend judgment rather than conclude that betterness cycles. I take my version to block this, because a standard spectrum argument only gives you steps whose losses are small but real, whereas mine gives you steps whose losses are literally no difference to anyone — but tell me whether that really blocks it, and whether the reply reappears at some other point in the argument. Second, she says an inaudible worsening is still a worsening: ten thousand tiny losses add up to forty decibels, which is why the end of the list is bad, so each step must have been a step down in some respect after all. I want this rebutted — or, if it cannot be rebutted, I want the argument for why I should give up transitivity rather than this. Third, she says the whole thing shows only that 'better than' is vague, or else that I am equivocating between two relations, something like 'incrementally better' and 'better overall', and that vagueness or equivocation is a much cheaper explanation than genuine intransitivity. I would especially like your treatment of the vagueness version, since it seems to me that even a vague relation cannot have a cycle whose every link is definitely true. Finally, two consequences I want handled rather than dodged. If betterness really can cycle, what am I supposed to do when faced with the actual list — is there still a rational way to choose, and does the fact that a clever landlord could walk me up the list one imperceptible step at a time, collecting a fee at each one and leaving me in the lawnmower apartment, show that betterness must be transitive after all? And be honest with me about where the argument for my side is weakest: I would rather know than win the dinner table.
