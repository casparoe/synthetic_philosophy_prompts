For the past three years I have been writing a two-stranded historical novel — one strand follows a district magistrate in rural Bengal through 1943, the other a Dublin clerk who ends up on a relief committee in 1847 — and I have reached the point where I have to be able to defend, in public, the political arguments my characters make. Reviewers who know the literature will happily dismantle a novel whose politics are only atmospheric, so rather than leaving the book's thesis implicit in the prose, I have tried to write down, as plainly as I can, the position I take myself to be arguing for.

What I am committed to is roughly this: (1) famines are failures of entitlement rather than failures of aggregate food availability — the right unit of analysis is what a household can command through its own production, its labour, its purchases and its legal rights, and I treat this as a claim about how famine is to be analysed, not as an empirical hypothesis; (2) accordingly, a famine is a political crime rather than a natural disaster, and I need that to be a substantive and contestable claim rather than something true by stipulation, because my magistrate's disgrace is supposed to consist in something; (3) as a working vocabulary I have been distinguishing 'thin' famines, where the food exists in the country but particular households' entitlements have collapsed, from 'empty' famines, where aggregate supply has genuinely fallen, and I want to say that the thin ones are crimes and the empty ones are tragedies; (4) Bengal in 1943 was a thin famine — a harvest not dramatically below the preceding years, wartime spending inflating prices, panic buying and hoarding, the removal of boats and rice from the coastal districts, and agricultural wages failing to keep pace with the price of rice — so it was a crime; (5) Ireland in the 1840s was likewise thin, since grain continued to leave the country while the potato crop rotted, and I also want it to sit comfortably beside the claim usually attributed to Sen, that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press — Ireland was, after all, formally part of the United Kingdom, with elections and newspapers; (6) the Great Leap Forward famine, by contrast, was empty, since output really did collapse, and yet I still want to call it a crime, on the grounds that the emptiness was itself manufactured by policy; (7) the test I would like to use for 'crime' is counterfactual — a famine is a political crime only if the authority in charge had some feasible alternative course of action that would have averted most of the deaths; (8) I do not want to blame individual actors — the trader who hoarded, the household that bought ahead of need, the official who followed procedure — since the wrong is structural and nobody had to intend anyone's death; and (9) I do not think any of this is undermined by the fact that most of the excess mortality in these episodes came from malaria, cholera and dysentery rather than from literal starvation.

My worry is that two or three of these are quietly incompatible and that I cannot locate the join: whenever I press one of them, it seems to take back what another one gives me. Would you audit the set for me? Please reply as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion: the genuine tensions in descending order of severity, each with what specifically would have to be given up; one item identifying a tension that is only apparent and dissolves once the terms are made precise; and, as the final item, the single revision that would clean up the most, together with what it costs me.
