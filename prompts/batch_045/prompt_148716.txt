Next week I'm running a seminar on cognitive penetration of perception, and I grabbed what looked like a reliable survey chapter to hand out. Here's the paragraph where it introduces the idea:

Perception is cognitively penetrable when what you believe, want, or expect literally changes what you see — not just what you say about it or how you act on it. We've known since the old New Look coin studies that this happens: kids who cared about the coins saw them as bigger, so top-down influence reaches all the way down into early vision, and Fodor's picture of the visual system as an encapsulated module is dead. That matters because perception is supposed to be our neutral referee: if what we see is already soaked in our expectations, then observation can't adjudicate between competing theories and science starts to look circular. It also settles the question of perceptual content — if expecting a cigarette changes your experience, then experience must represent high-level kinds, and anyone who says perception only ever represents shapes, colours and motion has been refuted by the data.

Most of it reads fine to me, but the last two moves feel too fast, specially the jump to high-level kinds. Can you go through it and separate what a specialist would grant from what is overstated, and from what rests on running together distinctions the literature keeps apart? I've been kicking around a distinction between trigger penetration — your beliefs just change which low-level features the system picks up — and content penetration — they actually contribute the property the experience represents — and my hunch is that only the second one would support the chapter's conclusion about content. Is that a real distinction that does any work here, or am I just restating something the chapter already helps itself to?
