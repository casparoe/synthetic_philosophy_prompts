so I'm taking a class this term on science and religion (early modern heavy, we're on Galileo week) and I also sit on the curriculum committee at my church, which is the reason this isn't just an essay question for me. Basically: we're a mid-size, mildly conservative congregation and we write the little explanatory notes that go in the adult Sunday school packets — one line per passage, max. Half a dozen times a year somebody emails in asking whether we should be saying "this is figurative" or "this is just how it looked to them" or "this assumes an ancient cosmology" about some verse that seems to clash with something science says. The five of us are a retired civil engineer, a high school biology teacher, our pastor, and two non-specialists (me). We meet an hour a month, nobody reads Greek or Hebrew, and we have to land on exactly one of four verdicts in the note: plain sense, describes appearances, not making a claim about the physical world, or leave it open. People complain at us no matter which one we pick. We inherited this pretty useless rubric from a denominational statement in the 90s that just grades the scientific claim as demonstrated / well-confirmed / contested, and nobody knows what to do with the middle grade, which is where almost everything actually sits.

What I want is a decision procedure we could actually run in about ten minutes per item, built out of Galileo's own rules for what to do when a passage and a scientific result seem to collide — the stuff in his 1615 letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, the two-books idea that nature doesn't accommodate itself to us but scripture does, the bit he borrows from Cardinal Baronius about the Holy Spirit aiming to teach us how to go to heaven and not how the heavens go, and his insistence that you only get to reinterpret a passage when the science is genuinely certain rather than merely probable. So: first reconstruct, as carefully as you can, what Galileo actually thought the rules were and why he thought they were the right ones, then translate that into an ordered list of questions with a clear account of what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops and says no, and where it has to hand the question to a person because it's a values call and not a factual one. Two things I want handled specifically. First, my professor has a name for something he calls retreat risk: if you re-read a passage to fit the science of the day and that science later falls apart, you've spent credibility you can't get back, and Galileo's own answer to this was basically that there's no risk when you have a real demonstration — except that he thought his tidal argument was a demonstration and it wasn't, so the safety valve he was relying on doesn't work. Somewhere in the procedure there needs to be a way of pricing that risk when the most we can say is "well-confirmed." Second, we need to separate three verdicts that we keep muddling: the passage is describing how things look from where the writer was standing, the passage isn't making a claim about the physical world at all, and the passage is making a physical claim that's straightforwardly wrong and we should just say so in the note. And tell us what happens when the plain-sense cosmology is tangled up with a doctrinal commitment, since that's where our committee freezes.

Then run the thing on three cases so I can see if it's usable. One easy one: the geocentric-sounding language, the passages where the sun stands still or the earth is said to be firmly fixed and immovable. One where the science is well-confirmed but only probabilistic and the passage is doctrinally loaded: all of humanity descending from a single original pair, against what population genetics implies about the size of our ancestral population. And one where I expect the procedure to tell us something we'll hate and I'll have to go defend it to the pastor — I was thinking the biblical language about the heart as the seat of thought and decision against neuroscientific claims that all of that is really the brain, where it's genuinely unclear to me whether the passage is making a physical claim at all. Please include at least one case where your procedure's answer is one our committee will not like, and finish by being straight about where the judgment calls can't be squeezed out, what the procedure simply can't do for us, and which parts of it are you improving on Galileo rather than following him. Also please don't just point me at books or tell me which scholars to read — we don't have library access and I need something that runs off what we already half-know.
