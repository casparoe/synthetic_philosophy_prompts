I'm in the final year of secondary school and I've been teaching myself philosophy of mind and ethics outside class, because our school only runs a general religion-and-ethics course and the questions I actually get stuck on are the ones about artificial minds. One of my teachers has kindly offered to mark one timed essay for me each half-term, but she's a religion teacher and has asked me to supply the question *and* the rubric, since she'll mark to the scheme rather than to her own instincts. So I need a complete package: a question I could attempt in about 90 minutes and roughly 1,000 words, pitched at the standard of a demanding first-year undergraduate exam rather than a school-leaving one, together with a model answer and a marking scheme she can apply mechanically. Two practical constraints: I have no library or journal access, so I don't want any suggested readings, footnotes, or references to specific papers or authors — assume the candidate knows only the standard basics (functionalism versus behaviourism versus identity theory, Turing machines, philosophical zombies, Parfit-style fission cases, and Singer-style equal consideration of interests), and build everything else into the question itself.

The topic I want this one on is which computations matter morally — specifically the attribution problem rather than the headline question of whether machines could be conscious. I can already write the paragraph that says mental states are multiply realizable, that what matters is the organization and not the wetware, and that we should extend the circle of concern to whatever turns out to have the relevant organization; the question has to be one where that paragraph earns almost nothing. The two cases I keep circling are these. (1) The multiplex case: a single processor runs ten simulated agents by time-slicing, handing each stream the same sequence of internal steps it would have got on a machine of its own, so that each stream is behaviourally and counterfactually indistinguishable from a separately housed agent and the only difference from ten separate chips is that the ten histories are interleaved on shared hardware (and, in the version that troubles me most, they are near-identical copies or even exact duplicates). Does the room contain ten units of moral concern, one, or something in between — and if the answer is ten, what *makes* it ten rather than one mind with ten streams, given that nothing about any single stream differs between the two setups? (2) The cheap-implementation case: any physical system can be described as implementing a great many computations, including ones nobody thinks matter, so it looks as though it cannot be enough for a system to merely implement the morally relevant computation; something has to select the privileged description, and if what selects it is our explanatory interests, moral status starts to look partly conventional, which I find alarming but can't yet argue against. My suspicion — and I may be confused — is that these two cases squeeze from opposite sides: the first tempts me to be generous about which computations are really there, the second tempts me to be stingy, and I can't see how to be both without contradiction.

So please write the question so that it turns on that squeeze. It should force the candidate to say what the criterion is a criterion *of* — a physical system, a causal process extended over time, an abstract program-type, a causally explanatory description, or an interest-relative one — and it should make it impossible to get top marks by counting computations liberally in the multiplex case and then restricting them in the cheap-implementation case without explicitly resolving the tension and biting at least one bullet (for instance by accepting a form of conventionalism about moral status, or by paying per unit of physical capacity, or by denying that duplicate streams multiply what matters). I'd like: the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, including any short preamble that sets up the cases; a model answer of about 800 words written in the voice of a strong candidate rather than as a survey; and a 20-mark scheme organised around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong, plus a brief note on what distinguishes a bare pass from a first-class answer. Finally, end your reply with one line in exactly this form, containing fifteen words or fewer: \answer{the single move that most separates a first-class answer from a bare pass}.
