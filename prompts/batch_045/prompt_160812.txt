I grew up in Norway, moved back a few years ago after finishing a masters in public policy, and somehow every family dinner now ends in a fight about the oil fund. My uncle's line is that we are sitting on this enormous pile of money while hospitals are short-staffed, and that a country which refuses to spend its own money on its own people has tied itself to a mast by choice. I keep trying to explain that the whole point is that future governments shouldn't be trusted with it, and Ive realised I don't actually have a good account of what the binding consists in, so I want to work it out properly.

So: take the Norwegian oil fund and its fiscal rule and analyse them as a case of Ulysses-contract / constitutional precommitment. The facts as I remember them, so you don't have to guess: oil found in the North Sea from the late sixties onward, the fund created by a parliamentary decision in 1990, first money paid in during the mid-nineties, renamed the Government Pension Fund Global in 2006, and invested almost entirely outside Norway in foreign equities, bonds and property; the fiscal rule introduced around 2001 says the state may spend roughly the expected real return of the fund over the cycle, set at four percent and later trimmed, and it is not constitutional or legally enforceable at all, just a parliamentary guideline that any majority can set aside, with a built-in escape hatch for downturns, which it used very heavily during covid; on top of that there are ethical guidelines that have pulled the fund out of coal, tobacco and various weapons manufacturers.

What I want is the two-way thing. First, map the case onto the theory carefully: who exactly is Ulysses (the parliament of 2001, the continuously sitting Storting, or Norway as some intergenerational agent), what are the Sirens, what is the mast, and who or what plays the crew — because there is no court, no creditor and no external enforcer here, and the same body that tied itself can untie itself by an ordinary majority, which I think breaks the standard story. I have two hypotheses about what is actually doing the work and I'd rather you attacked them than agreed: (a) the offshore lock, that the money cannot be turned into Norwegian hospitals without selling foreign assets and moving the krone, which looks more like hostage-posting than like a rule; (b) what I'd call legibility-binding, where the rule doesn't remove the option but produces a single number that makes any deviation instantly nameable and politically costly, so the constraint is epistemic and reputational rather than coercive. Second, turn it around on the theory: is a rule with an escape valve and no enforcer a precommitment at all or a stabilisation rule in precommitment costume; is this intrapersonal self-binding, the prudential case where the dead-hand objection has no bite, or a partisan lock-in against future opponents, where it has plenty; what observable difference the rule would make if it were genuinely biting as opposed to pure decoration, so I can tell real binding from theatre; where the theory just goes silent — especially whether it has any way of distinguishing temptation from legitimately changed circumstances, which is the bit my uncle keeps landing on. Skip the literature and citations entirely, and don't spend time on Dutch disease or the macroeconomics, I know that part.

Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no conclusion.
