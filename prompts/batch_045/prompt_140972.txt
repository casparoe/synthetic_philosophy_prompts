I started a new job a few months ago doing intake at a legal-aid clinic, and part of what I do is check whether someone's income falls under the cutoff for us to take their case. I found a podcast episode by a philosophy-of-language guy who seemed to know his stuff, and he summarizes the sorites paradox like this:

"Vagueness is really just ignorance. Here's the puzzle. One grain of sand doesn't make a heap, and if n grains don't make a heap then n+1 grains don't either, because a single grain can't make that kind of difference. Chain that together ten thousand times and you get that no number of grains makes a heap — but obviously some piles are heaps, so something has to give. There are three camps. The fuzzy logicians blame classical logic: being a heap isn't a yes-or-no matter, it comes in degrees — basically probabilities — so every step of the chain loses a sliver of truth, the conclusion ends up only half true, and 'either it's a heap or it isn't' stops being a safe inference. The supervaluationists blame our language: 'heap' admits lots of equally good precise sharpenings, and a borderline case is true on some of them and false on others, so it has no truth value at all — which is why the law of excluded middle breaks down for borderline cases. The epistemicists blame us: the cutoff is perfectly sharp, set by nothing more than however we happen to use the word, and we simply can never find out where it is; that's what higher-order vagueness is. It's the smallest camp, and deservedly so, because our use of a word is a messy fact about millions of speakers, so there's no sharp line there to be ignorant of in the first place. And this has teeth: any rule with a threshold in it — an income limit, a blood-pressure number, an age cutoff — is arbitrary in exactly this way, so the honest thing is to stop writing cutoffs into rules and let trained humans judge each case on its own."
