I'm leading a fifty-minute problem class for second-year undergraduates on an introductory game-theory module; whiteboard only. They can find pure-strategy Nash equilibria and best responses, and they have been told what a mixed strategy is, but have not yet used one for anything. Next week I have to get them to see why iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies must be allowed to use mixed strategies as dominators — that a strategy can die to a mixture when no pure strategy kills it. Don't explain the idea to me; plan how I should explain it.

What I want from you:

1. The order the pieces have to come in, and what breaks if I reorder them. I want this to be their first real use of a mixture, and I want that first use to be one where the mixture is a measuring stick rather than something anyone plays; my worry is that if 'randomise so the opponent cannot predict you' gets in first, it poisons the well. Tell me where that worry is justified and where I am overthinking it.

2. One game to carry the whole session, which I would like you to construct rather than describe. It should be a two-player normal-form game, small enough that at the decisive moment I can plot each of the relevant player's strategies as a point in the plane, with the opponent's two surviving strategies as the axes, so that 'some convex combination lies up and to the right of it' is visibly what being dominated means, and the mixture that does the work is visibly a point on a segment that no pure strategy sits on. It should contain no weakly dominated strategy anywhere, so that strict and weak dominance do not collapse into each other in their notes. And it should contain a strategy which neither a pure strategy nor a mixture beats on the first pass, but which becomes strictly dominated — by a mixture, and not by any surviving pure strategy — once some other deletion has gone through; I think the mechanism has to be that deleting one of the opponent's strategies shrinks the set of contingencies the mixture has to cover, but correct me if that is the wrong way to get there, or if my three demands cannot be met together.

3. The misconceptions to expect, each with a place in the sequence where I should head it off. My candidates: that a mixture cannot beat a real strategy because nobody would ever play it; that the mixture has to be checked against the opponent's mixed strategies as well as the pure ones; that because order matters for weak dominance it must matter here; and that if a mixture beats a strategy, each strategy in the mixture must already be at least as good as it against everything.

4. What I should refuse to explain at this level, and how to say so in a single line (I would guess: the three-or-more-player case, where a strategy can be a best response to no independent belief at all and still be undominated, and the word 'rationalisability').

5. One closing question that tells me they have understood what domination is for, rather than having followed the arithmetic.
