Hi — long one, sorry in advance, I've got a bad habit of writing seminar handouts that are twice as long as the seminar. I'm teaching a grad seminar on modal epistemology this term and I've had a paper draft sat on my desk for about a year (working title something like 'Conceivability, Calibrated') in which I defend a deliberately narrow thesis, call it R. R says: for identity claims that link a phenomenal concept with a structural or dynamic one, if an ideally rational and fully informed subject can *positively* conceive of the phenomenal thing obtaining without the structural thing, then that identity is not necessary. Two features matter to me and I'd like you to respect them. First, R is only negative: I'm not claiming conceivability hands me possibilities wholesale, only that it reliably *rules out* necessities. Second, I'd rather not lean on the zombie case at all — my central example is one I made up, 'slow phenomenal drift': a creature functionally and physically identical to you over an hour whose colour experience rotates steadily through the spectrum, with nothing behavioural, dispositional or computational differing at any point. I take that to be positively conceivable, and I take it to tell against any necessary identity between the experience and the functional/physical story.

So here's the ask: what would count against R? I want a map of its exposure, not a summary of the debate. Rank the defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and for each one mark whether a committed defender of R would *concede* it as counting against R or would *dismiss* it — and name the exact move the dismissal would consist in (e.g. reclassifying my case as merely prima facie conceivable, or as conceiving-under-the-wrong-mode-of-presentation). I already know the obvious ones — Kripke-style necessary a posteriori identities, the gap between prima facie and ideal conceivability, the worry that conceivability tracks epistemic rather than metaphysical possibility, the possibility that phenomenal concepts just are the kind of concept that generates an appearance of gap regardless of what the world is like — so give me the ones a graduate student who's read the standard stuff wouldn't volunteer, and be specific about which of them bites on the *negative-only* version rather than on the slogan version.

The thing that's actually been nagging me, though, is calibration, and I'd like you to spend most of your effort there. Conceivability is unusual among modal-epistemology tools in that we can in principle check its track record, because there are domains where the modal facts are settled independently of anyone's conceiving. Grant me for the sake of argument that each of the following was positively conceivable for competent, careful people before the relevant proof, and each is flatly impossible: trisecting an arbitrary angle with straightedge and compass alone; a general solution in radicals for the quintic; a counterexample to Fermat's last theorem; a perpetual motion machine of the first kind. That's a calibration set with a 100% false-positive rate, drawn from cases whose difficulty (I'd argue) is not so different in kind from my phenomenal cases. What should I conclude about the expected reliability of my own conceivings in the phenomenal domain? Is there a principled way to say why logic, number theory and thermo are domains where conceivability is unreliable while consciousness is one where it's reliable — or does any such principled distinction collapse into assuming what R needs? And connectedly: is R in danger of being qualified into unfalsifiability? If every apparent counterexample gets absorbed as 'not really ideal conceivability,' what does R still risk, and is that insulation a defect in a thesis like this or just what an a priori bridge principle is supposed to look like? Finally, give me your honest read: if you had to bet on what kills R in the next twenty years, is it an empirical result about how people actually make modal judgements, a formal result about the two-dimensional framework I'm implicitly borrowing, or something nobody has thought of yet? I'd find an empirical result more persuasive than an armchair one, mostly because I don't trust my own intuitions about cases any more after looking at that calibration set — say if you think that's an incoherent thing for someone defending R to say.
