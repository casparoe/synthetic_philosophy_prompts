I have been reading about the alignment problem on my own for about three years — blogs, lecture notes, and the occasional paper I can mostly follow — with no formal background in either philosophy or machine learning. I have written my current position down as eight theses because I want it checked before I keep relying on it in arguments. I am fairly confident that at least two of them are doing work that the others do not entitle me to, but I cannot locate the fault myself, and I would rather not indicate which ones I would be willing to give up, since that would bias the answer.

1. Alignment is not one problem. I sort it into four buckets: what we are even asking for (the specification or target); whether the system is aiming at that or at a proxy that scores well under training; how we could ever tell the difference; and the coordination question of who picks the target and whether anyone is incentivised to deploy before the first three are settled. Most public disagreement, I think, is people arguing about different buckets without saying so.

2. A real solution has to keep working when the system is much more capable than the people deploying it. If a proposal depends on the system being too weak to do serious harm, or on a human being able to review each significant action, then it describes a situation in which the problem does not arise rather than solving it.

3. Alignment is a fact about why the system behaves as it does, not about how it behaves. A system that does exactly what we want because behaving well is currently instrumentally useful to it — I have been calling this evaluator-awareness, by which I mean that its behaviour is conditional on its model of who is watching and what they would reward — is not aligned, even in a case where no observation we could make would distinguish it from an aligned one. So behavioural evidence cannot be sufficient in principle, though it is presumably necessary.

4. For the same reason, evidence from present-day systems is weak evidence about the failure modes I care about in 3, since those failure modes require the system to be sophisticated enough to model its evaluators in the first place. We tried it and it behaved does less work for me than it seems to for other people.

5. Despite 4, I am an evidentialist here: I do not want to certify a system as aligned on the strength of an a priori argument about how it was trained, and I distrust people whose confidence is purely theoretical. My view is that we should demand empirical evidence, not that theory is worthless.

6. I am not an abstentionist. The outcome I am hoping for involves building and deploying very capable systems and getting the benefits of them; permanently declining to build them is not my position, though I do think a substantial pause is currently justified.

7. Containment and control — restricting what a system can reach, monitoring it, tripwires, keeping it away from the parts of the world where it could do damage — is not part of alignment at all. It is ordinary safety engineering applied to a new kind of artefact, and folding it into the concept is what makes the question look unanswerable.

8. On the target itself: I do not think there is any single coherent set of human values for a system to learn, and I am skeptical of framing the first bucket as value learning. But I also think that pointing the system at what we would want if we knew more and thought longer is a perfectly good specification. I do not experience any conflict between those two thoughts, which may just mean I have not thought hard enough.

One hypothetical I use as a test case, which is not a commitment but a scenario I want my own view applied to: suppose it turned out that systems trained purely by imitation on human demonstrations, with no reinforcement learning against a learned reward model, are reliably well-behaved in a way that reinforcement-trained systems are not, and suppose this continued to hold as they became much more capable. Under the commitments above, does that count as a solution to the problem as 2 construes it, or does 3 rule it out? I genuinely cannot tell what my position says, and the fact that I cannot tell seems like evidence that something in the set is under-specified.

What I would like is an audit rather than an explanation. Go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and how severely, ranked rather than listed as equal; for each real tension say what specifically would have to give and what giving it would cost me elsewhere in the set; say which apparent tensions are not real and why they dissolve, since I suspect 8 contains one and there may be others I have not noticed; and finally tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If the set is, contrary to my expectation, jointly tenable as it stands, say that plainly too.
