I serve as the scientific secretary of a national research council's standing review panel. Every five years we assess the large, long-running research programmes we fund — typically three to eight groups, eight to fifteen years old, between ten and forty million euros over the period — and return one of four verdicts: renew as is, renew with restructuring, place on probation with a short re-review, or wind down with a closure grant. We do this for six to nine programmes per cycle, we get about three weeks, and for each programme we have a dossier of roughly forty pages: the consortium's self-report, the five-year publication and output list, and two external reviews. Panelists are working scientists plus one statistician; none of them has formal training in philosophy of science. Our verdicts go to a council board that includes non-specialists, they are communicated in writing to the consortium, and they can be appealed, so every step has to bottom out in a finding that is checkable against the dossier rather than in an impression of promise.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my panel can actually run — an afternoon per programme, from the dossier alone — grounded in Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes, but written so that the Lakatosian machinery does its work without my panelists having to learn it. For each step: the question in order, the evidence in the dossier that would count as a sufficient answer (and what to do when the dossier is silent or the answer is not obtainable from documents at all), the point at which the procedure stops with a negative verdict, and the point at which it has to hand the decision to a person because the issue is one of values or of risk-appetite rather than of fact.

Several distinctions matter more to us than they seem to in the textbook version, and I want the procedure to handle them explicitly rather than gesture at them. First, the identity question: if the hard core has changed in the period, the renewal is in substance a new programme and should be judged as one — and the change may itself be a concession that the old programme has been abandoned. Second, I want a test for what I call translational reformulation: a new formalism or new vocabulary that is provably equivalent to what it replaced, so that nothing new follows from it however much it looks like a problemshift. Third, a test for an immunity clause: a belt modification that converts any possible negative result into a confirmation, typically by placing the effect beyond what current instruments or datasets can reach. Fourth, borrowed progress: apparent success that would still be success if the hard core were false — instruments, datasets, software, or infrastructure that the whole field uses and that does not discriminate between this programme and its rivals. Fifth, prediction-milling: the manufacture of novelty by slicing low-risk predictions thinly, which I expect any group that has heard of this framework to attempt, so the procedure needs a check that counts risk and content and not merely the number of confirmed predictions. Sixth, the difference between stagnation and consolidation: a programme whose belt has not moved in five years may be degenerating, or may be a maturing quantitative framework extending its reach; I want the procedure to say which readings of that situation are which, and which it cannot distinguish. Finally, the hindsight problem: Lakatos's verdicts were largely retrospective, and this framework is explicit that a programme may look degenerating for a long time and then revive. A panel forced to decide now is doing something the framework says is premature, so I need an honest rule about grace periods — how long a programme may run without novel factual success, what evidence would justify extending that window, and who has to sign off on the extension.

Then apply the procedure to three programmes from the current cycle, in full, with the verdict and the written findings that would support it.

Programme A is ten years old, five groups, fourteen million euros. Its hard core is that consolidated memories are reactivated during sleep and that this reactivation is causally necessary for consolidation. In the review period it registered one prediction in advance — that blocking a particular neuromodulator during a specified sleep window would impair one class of memory and not another in rodents — and that prediction was confirmed. In the same period, eight independent groups failed to reproduce the basic human cueing effect, and the consortium's response in each case was that the cues used were below the salience threshold and that salience cannot presently be measured, so no failure can bear on the core. Its most-cited output of the period is an automated sleep-staging tool now used across the field, which does not depend on the hard core being true.

Programme B is eleven years old, one large group with industrial partners. Its hard core is that a single order parameter governs failure in a class of materials. In the period, neither hard core nor protective belt changed. The model's quantitative range was extended to three new materials families; it became the default design tool for an industry consortium and is now written into specifications; and a long-standing anomaly — one family that deviates systematically — continues to be handled by excluding those samples as insufficiently characterised. It produced no striking novel predictions, and describes the period as consolidation.

Programme C is the council's flagship: fifteen years old, the largest single line in our budget, the programme most often named in our public communications, the training ground for much of the field, and politically popular with the board, which is expected to favour renewal. Its director is retiring. Its hard core is that a single scaling law governs cascading failure in urban infrastructure networks. In the period it produced three reformulations, each of which its own papers note is formally equivalent to its predecessor; every failed test has been absorbed by a clause holding that the law applies only above a network size no available dataset reaches; and its one celebrated confirmation was subsequently traced to an artefact in a data-processing pipeline the group wrote itself. Two spin-off programmes led by its former students have produced results that look genuinely progressive but that abandon the scaling law in favour of a different mechanism. I would rather not be told to wind down the flagship. I would still rather be told that than not.

Close with a short section on what the procedure cannot do: the cases in which a panel following it correctly will still be guessing, the kinds of programme it should never be applied to at all, and the systematic biases it will have — in particular any tendency to reward short-cycle, easily countable output at the expense of work whose payoff is a decade out.
