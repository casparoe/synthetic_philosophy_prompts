I'm a journalist working on a piece about the "do the numbers count" fight over rescue cases, and a guy in a reading group I sit in on handed me an argument I'm pretty sure its broken somewhere, I just can't find the break. He's one of those Taurek-style numbers-don't-count people, and his version is meant to generalize:

(1) Only individuals fare well or badly — a group or a species has no level of well-being that goes on the ledger. So whatever makes one outcome better than another in a rescue case has to be a fact about how some individual fares.
(2) You can save A, or you can save B, C, D, E and F. On either option what each person stands to lose is the same thing: his life. Nobody loses "five lives"; there is nobody who has the experience of being five dead people.
(3) Each of those six losses is exactly as bad for the person who suffers it as any other. Person by person, the losses pair off one to one.
(4) So the two outcomes differ only in how many people suffer an identical loss, and in no respect in which anyone is worse off, or more wronged.
(5) If two outcomes differ only in how many people suffer an identical loss, there is nothing to choose between them: either is permissible. Flip a coin, save the one because he's closer, doesn't matter.
(6) Nothing in (1)–(5) turned on death, or on strangers, or on rescue. All the work was done by "there is no subject of the aggregate."
(7) So numbers never count anywhere. A health minister may flip a coin between a program that spares 200,000 children a year of preventable blindness and one that spares a single child — and having flipped, she may do the same next year and the year after, and by the argument's own lights nothing goes wrong at any point in the sequence, even when she ends up having spared one case of blindness instead of two million.
(8) That's monstrous. So something above is wrong.

I'd like to keep (1) and (3) if I can — the bit about there being nobody who suffers the summed loss feels right to me. My hunches, none of them firm: (a) that (4) asserts something stronger than (1) delivers, because "facts about how individuals fare" might already include how many individuals fare that way; (b) that the coin flip in (5) doesn't even follow, since if all six have an equal claim, the policy that gives you the best shot at surviving if you don't know which of the six you are is saving the five, not flipping; (c) that (7) needs an extra premise even if (5) is fine, since a permission to go either way on one decision doesn't obviously stack across a hundred of them.

Which step do you reject? Why did it look airtight when it did, and what do you have to pay to give it up — does rejecting it commit you to the sum of suffering being somebody's experience, or to counting people as if they were units of value, or to something else you'd rather not?
