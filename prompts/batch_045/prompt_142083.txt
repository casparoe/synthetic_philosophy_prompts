I spent thirty-eight years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, mostly on phase transitions and critical phenomena, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a graduate reading group in philosophy of mind at the local university. I have come to understand the motivations for panpsychism tolerably well, including the Russellian thought that physical science characterizes its objects only structurally and dynamically and stays silent about their intrinsic nature, so that experience is available as a candidate for what fills that silence. What I cannot get a grip on is the so-called combination problem. Every way I try to state it in terms I actually trust — microstates and macrostates, coarse-graining, distributions over phase space, extensive versus intensive quantities, the thermodynamic limit, order parameters and symmetry breaking, critical thresholds, renormalization-group flow and universality, scale decoupling and effective theories, weak versus strong emergence, identity versus realization versus grounding, and the fact that the partition of the world into subsystems is something we impose rather than read off — it either dissolves into something I already know how to handle, or it turns into a demand I have no idea how to evaluate. I am suspicious that both outcomes are artifacts of my vocabulary, and I want to know which.

Please take the role of a philosopher of mind who is also genuinely fluent in statistical mechanics and effective field theory, and think carefully and slowly here: I would rather have a careful, somewhat messy answer than a polished summary. Do the job in four parts, in American English, roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words, and please do not pad the answer with references or suggested reading, since I want the argument and not a map to it. First, inventory the combination problem into its distinct sub-problems and translate each separately, because I doubt they behave alike: the subject-summing problem of how many subjects yield one; the quality-combination problem of how micro-qualities are supposed to compose into the qualities I actually enjoy; the structural or mismatch worry that the phenomenal character of experience does not seem to have the shape of anything physics describes; and the grain problem of what counts as a subject at every scale, given that my laptop and my thermostat are also composites. Second, deliberately test three mappings I have produced myself rather than quietly adopting them: (a) treat micro-subjects like spins on a lattice and macro-consciousness as a phase that appears only above some threshold of organization, which worries me because it predicts borderline systems and a critical point I have never seen anyone try to locate; (b) treat the macro-subject the way I would treat a macrostate, as a coarse-grained description that throws away which microstate is realized, which worries me because a coarse-grained description is not an additional entity and panpsychism seems to need it to be one, and because averaging over subjects seems to presuppose exactly what is in question; and (c) a suggestion from my reading-group partner that the bottom-up composition approach versus the top-down decombination approach, on which the cosmos is the one basic subject and creatures are aspects of it, is just the old particles-versus-fields and whole-state-versus-subsystem priority quarrel in a new suit, with entanglement already settling it. My instinct is that the third one is glib, and I want to be told precisely where the glibness lies, including whether the renormalization-group insight that micro-detail washes out at macro scales helps the panpsychist or quietly undercuts them, since it seems to me to cut against the claim that macro-experience is constituted by micro-experience.

Third, and this is what I actually want: give me an explicit residue report. Sort the claims, distinctions, and commitments involved into those that translate cleanly into my vocabulary, those that translate only with distortion — and say exactly what was bent to make them fit — and those that cannot be expressed in it at all. Before writing the answer proper, do a short preliminary pass in which you list candidate mappings you tried and abandoned, since I would like to see the ones that failed your own first attempt and not just the tidy final table. Fourth, for each item in the third column, give me your verdict on a question I genuinely cannot settle: does that residue reveal a real blind spot in a physics-trained description of the world, something my framework is structurally incapable of saying, or is it a sign that the original philosophical claim is less determinate than it looks? Where you think the latter, tell me what would have to be made precise before we could even tell whether there is a problem to be solved, and whether the debate as conducted has already smuggled in an assumption — about identity, grounding, intrinsicness, or the unity of a subject — that my vocabulary neither contains nor needs.
