I've been volunteered to run the next meeting of our little philosophy group — about twenty of us, mostly retired engineers and lab scientists, i was a chemist for thirty-odd years and I've only started reading philosophy properly since I packed it in. The topic I stupidly agreed to is whether the brain is a computer. I've got fourty-five minutes, a whiteboard, no slides. Don't explain computationalism to me, I've read the basics — tell me how to run the session: what has to come first and why (what depends on what), the one example or case that should carry the whole hour and what that example has to have and has to lack for it to do the job, and the wrong turns the room will take in the order they'll take them, with where in the sequence I should cut each one off.

You should know the audience. Half of them will open with "obviously yes, a neuron is a switch and I spent my career building things out of switches." One of them will say "obviously no, neurons are analogue and messy and there's no clock." Someone will assume the question is really about whether we can upload ourselves onto a disk. And there's a chap who'll think it's all just a loose way of talking and brains are wet.

What I actually want them to reach by the end is the difference between "the brain computes" as a redescription of what it was already doing and "the brain computes" as a claim that does real work in explaining how it does it. I want them to feel the pull of both sides of that, and I want them to have to deal with why "the heart is a pump" doesn't seem to raise the same sort of question, and then say for themselves whether that comparison is fair or a bit of a cheat.

The one thing I'd like built in, if you think it works: my own rough test for whether a computational story has earned its keep — that if a system really is computing something, there should be a usable difference between the thing being broken and the thing miscomputing (a bit of damage versus the system running fine and getting the world wrong, like a hallucination). Tell me where in the hour that belongs, what concrete case makes it bite, and if you think the test is a bad one, say so and say why.

Also give me one question to put to them at the end that'll show me whether they've got the distinction or just enjoyed the argument, and tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and where to say so out loud rather than bluff. Please don't name-drop anyone the room won't have heard of, and no dates or references. And write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — the way you'd explain it to me over coffee.
