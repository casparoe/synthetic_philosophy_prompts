Roughly where each of them stands (I've tried to be fair; correct me in the doc if I've made one of them weaker than they are):

Marcus: chunks of nervous tissue literally perform computations over representational states; when a model's variables map onto cell types and its operations onto causal interactions in the circuit, we're not just redescribing the tissue, we've found real structure; the decades of predictive success of computational neuroscience is evidence for that (inference to the best explanation); and there's a fact of the matter about which computation a given circuit implements, once you get the criterion of implementation right.

Ines: what's in the skull is a high-dimensional dynamical system continuously coupled to a body and an environment; singling out some of its states as 'representations' and some of its transitions as 'computational steps' is a modeling decision constrained by what we want to predict and control, not a discovery about the tissue; the dynamical/ecological descriptions are at least as good and arguably get the time-course and the body-looping right in a way that input-output function talk doesn't; there's no privileged 'the' computation a piece of cortex implements.

Things they've already both conceded (go ahead and add more, but only ones both would really sign, not cheerleading):
- the brain is not a von Neumann machine: no stored program, no central clock, no clean CPU/RAM split, no error-corrected digital code. Whatever 'is a computer' means here, it doesn't mean that.
- there's a serious triviality worry: with enough gerrymandering of the mapping you can arguably read any computation off any physical system (rocks, stomachs, the weather). Both agree a criterion of implementation has to block this.
- a lot of neural signaling doesn't look obviously computational at all: dendritic plateau potentials, gap-junction coupling, volume transmission of neuromodulators, astrocytic calcium waves, ephaptic effects, stochastic vesicle release, one neuron sitting in several assemblies at once.
- computational models and dynamical models are both useful. The fight is about what that usefulness shows.
- whether some machine could be conscious is a different question from whether the brain computes, and neither view settles it by itself.
