I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, and this term I'm taking a seminar on infinity, probability, and rational choice. I also host my department's graduate-student podcast, and in two weeks I'm recording a forty-minute interview with Dr. Renata Halvorsen, a decision theorist whose forthcoming paper "Pasadena Is a Process, Not a Sum" defends what she calls procedural expectation: the expectation of a gamble is not a property of its payoff distribution alone, but of the sum of payoff-times-probability taken in the order supplied by the physical procedure that generates the outcome. As she sets the case up, the Pasadena game pays as follows: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; if the first heads occurs on toss n you receive (−1)^{n+1}·2^n/n dollars. Since the probability of first-heads-on-n is 1/2^n, the expectation series is 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., the alternating harmonic series, which converges but only conditionally; by the Riemann rearrangement theorem its terms can be re-ordered so as to sum to any real number you like, or to diverge. Her claim is that the correct value is ln 2, and that it is ln 2 because the tosses arrive in a temporal order, not because ln 2 is the privileged sum of an un-ordered family of terms.

Please draft eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, that press on the details of that position rather than letting her restate it. No opening biographical questions and no "who else has written about this" questions — she has asked not to do a literature survey on air. The ground I want covered:

1. The same payoff distribution implemented without any temporal order — say, a device that samples n from the geometric distribution in a single readout and pays off from a lookup table, so that no outcome comes "before" any other. Does the game still have a value? Her likely move is that the gamble is a process, not a distribution; the follow-up should push on whether two gambles that agree on the probabilities of every payoff, and differ only in how the chance is realized, can really differ in value when the difference shows up nowhere in what the agent receives.
2. Relatedly: if the unordered implementation has no determinate value, is that a discovery about the gamble or an artifact of the way we sum, and why is it not simply skepticism about infinite state spaces?
3. Her probable appeal to finite approximations: the game capped at N tosses (equivalently, thegame restricted to its N most probable outcomes) has a determinate expectation that differs from ln 2 by less than 1/(N+1). Press on why truncation by toss-number, or by decreasing probability, is the canonical way to approximate, given that other truncation schemes pick out different finite subsets and yield different limits or none.
4. An audience member who has read a little nonstandard analysis is likely to suggest that the expectation be taken as a hyperfinite partial sum up to an infinite index: since the ordinary partial sums converge to ln 2, every such hyperfinite sum should be infinitesimally close to ln 2, giving ln 2 plus an infinitesimal. Ask whether this vindicates her or merely encodes the ordering she already assumed — a re-ordered enumeration would give 100 plus an infinitesimal — and whether the infinitesimal tail ever does any real decision-theoretic work if comparisons go by standard parts.
5. Additivity. The sign-flipped twin of the Pasadena game pays the exact negative in every state, so holding both tickets pays zero for sure, even though each is worth ±ln 2 on her account. She will probably restrict additivity to cases where both expectations converge absolutely; the follow-up should ask what replaces additivity when an agent is choosing bundles or adding a ticket to a portfolio, and whether a package can be worse than one of its parts.
6. Exploitability. One can construct, for any real c, a game on the same chance setup whose procedural expectation is exactly c, by fixing a conditionally convergent series summing to c and setting the payoff on outcome n to 2^n times its n-th term. An agent who maximizes procedural expectation will pay a cent to trade up from the c-game to the (c+1)-game, for every c. Ask whether her view licenses an endless sequence of such trades, whether the sequence can be arranged so that the agent does worse in every state, and — if she rules out infinite sequences of decisions — whether that ruling also undercuts her own use of limits over finite truncations.
7. Second-order regress. If the agent is unsure which implementation she is facing, she must in effect take an expectation over procedures; but if the class of admissible procedures is rich enough, that higher-order sum is again only conditionally convergent, or unbounded. Ask what fixes the procedure — the physics, the agent's concepts, the phrasing of the offer — and whether the view has relocated the problem rather than solved it.
8. Contrast with the St. Petersburg game, where the difficulty is infinite rather than order-sensitive expectation; ask whether her apparatus gives any unified diagnosis or whether these are simply two independent problems that both arise in infinite state spaces.

Her stock deflection throughout is some version of "you are describing the gamble as a sum; I am describing it as a procedure." Please order the questions so that this move becomes progressively harder to fall back on, and close with one question about what evidence could count against the view. For each question, add a line or two in brackets saying why it is a genuine problem for her and not just a request for clarification.
