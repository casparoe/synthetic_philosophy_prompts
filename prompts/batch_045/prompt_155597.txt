I am writing a short position paper for a graduate seminar on the Meno problem — why, if at all, knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief — and I want to audit an analogy that keeps doing a lot of work in the discussion.

The analogy comes from Plato's own Meno. Socrates there concedes to his interlocutor that a man who has a true belief about which road leads to Larissa is, on a given occasion, just as good a guide as a man who has travelled the road and knows the way; the difference, he suggests, only shows up across time, because true beliefs drift off like the statues of Daedalus unless they are tied down by working out the reason why.

A member of the seminar uses this to close the value question rather than open it. Her argument is that the example isolates the only real difference: knowledge is true belief plus diachronic stability, the stability is conferred by the reliability of the process that produced and sustains the belief, and therefore the entire value gap between knowledge and true belief is the expected gain in guidance across a run of occasions. On that basis she concludes (i) that there is no need for any achievement, understanding, or credit condition in an account of knowledge; (ii) that a process reliabilism with a stability rider is sufficient; and (iii) that the search for a further, non-instrumental value of knowledge is a blind alley.

I want to test that, and I have been trying to stress the analogy with three variations.

First, the external tether. Imagine a guide who carries a sealed itinerary written by someone else, consults it unfailingly, never loses it, never doubts it, and is never tempted to improvise. His true belief about the road is as stable as anyone's, and it is stable for a reliable causal reason — but no account of why the road runs where it does is available to him, and he could not reconstruct the route if the itinerary were destroyed. If the analogy is really about tying the belief down, is this a tether? If it is, does her conclusion entail that the itinerary-carrier is as well off as the man who knows, and if not, what exactly in the Meno picture is carrying the extra weight?

Second, the shifting road network. Suppose the routes to Larissa are rerouted every spring by floods, and guides are hired by the season rather than by the trip. Now the knower's superiority seems to consist less in persistence than in the capacity to recompute a route from terrain, landmarks, and reports — a capacity the analogy's picture of a fixed road and a single journey has no slot for.

Third, the interrogative environment. Suppose the traveller never merely arrives but travels with passengers who ask why this road and not the other, and who must afterwards be able to find the way themselves. Here the value of the guide's state seems to depend on features of the social setting rather than on anything intrinsic to the belief.

Please give me the following: an explicit correspondence table for the analogy (what plays the destination, the road, the journey, arriving, the guide, the traveller, the tether, and the drift); the points at which the mapping breaks; and a separation of the disanalogies that matter for her intended use from those that are inert. In particular I want to know which features of the source the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks — a single fixed terminus, a task exhausted by arrival, one traveller, one occasion, a silent passenger, a stable network — and what each imports. Then say whether her three conclusions survive once those are on the table, and whether the analogy in fact generates the swamping worry it is used to dissolve: if arriving is the whole point, why is the way the belief was produced worth anything at all? Finally, if the Larissa picture cannot carry the work she wants it to carry, propose a better analogy and say what it would make visible that this one hides.
