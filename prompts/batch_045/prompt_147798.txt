I've been going to a sit-and-discuss group at a Buddhist center for a couple of years now — half the people are philosophy-adjacent, one is a therapist, and one is a software engineer who read Parfit in college and won't shut up about him — and we keep having the exact same argument about anatta and personal identity, and it never goes anywhere. The two camps are basically these: one says the no-self claim is straightforwardly a metaphysical thesis that lands right next to Parfit-style reductionism, i.e. a person is nothing over and above the aggregates (the skandhas) held together by causal and psychological continuity, nothing further is needed, and that's what the self's emptiness consists in. The other camp says that's a category error: anatta is a claim about what is ultimately real as opposed to conventionally real, or a therapeutic prescription about which kinds of clinging produce suffering, and at the conventional level the tradition is perfectly happy to talk about persons, memory, karma, rebirth, ordinary prudence and everything else. Every single time, the reductionist side produces arguments about what does the explanatory work — what makes memory veridical, why prudential concern tracks psychological continuity rather than a further fact, what we should say in fission cases — and the other side replies that those are precisely the conventional-level facts nobody was denying, so the argument is just passing straight through without touching anything.

My own hypothesis is that it's worse than a verbal dispute but not quite a substantive one either: I think there are two different questions under one word. Call them (a) the compositional question — is there anything more to a person than the aggregates and their causal-psychological relations, and is the unity of a life something discovered or something constructed? — and (b) the normative or prudential question — is the fact that I will be the one who undergoes some future experience a further fact that does real rational work, or does it drop out entirely once the continuities are in place? My suspicion is that the early texts were mainly aimed at something like (b), at de-indexing concern rather than at denying diachronic unity, and that the two-truths machinery is what later lets a tradition answer (a) and (b) differently, which is exactly why neither camp can land a punch: one side is answering (a) and the other is answering (b), and both think they've thereby settled the whole thing. I also keep pushing a pet distinction on people — call it 'thin' vs 'thick' no-self. Thin no-self only denies that personal identity is what matters in survival and prudential concern, which a card-carrying reductionist can accept, even welcome. Thick no-self denies that there's any diachronic unity at all, even a merely conventional or constructed one. I think the stall happens because people slide between thin and thick without noticing. The case where I think it shows: someone whose psychology is gradually spliced with another person's over a couple of decades, such that at the end there is no fact of the matter about which of the two original people the end-state is. The thin theorist has a tidy answer there; the thick theorist has to say something that starts sounding like a claim about experience rather than about metaphysics, and then everyone starts talking past each other again.

So what I actually want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right about the self. Is this a verbal dispute, two questions sharing a name, a genuine substantive disagreement about the metaphysics of the aggregates, or a clash of bedrock starting intuitions — say, about whether the anticipation of your own death is a datum to be explained or a symptom to be dissolved — that neither side can argue for at all? Engage with what each camp actually says rather than surveying positions at me; I've read the surveys and they haven't helped. And then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress here. Would a distinction like mine, or a better one, actually dissolve it? Would it take an argument about whether conventional truth can bear any metaphysical weight? Would it take something from outside philosophy — empirical work on how the sense of self comes apart in certain kinds of meditation practice, or in depersonalization, or in split-brain and dissociative cases — and if so, what exactly would that literature have to show to be relevant, since I suspect it mostly wouldn't be? Or would progress require putting the tradition's soteriological aims on the table as data rather than treating them as background noise? Give me a committed answer: pick the diagnosis you think is right, say roughly how confident you are in each part of it, and flag which piece you'd drop first if someone pushed back hard. And please be honest if you think the thin/thick thing is doing much less work than I think it is.
