I keep bees, and over the winter I worked through a well-known popular book on evolution that sorts creatures into four kinds of minds: those whose only "learning" happens across generations, those shaped within a lifetime by reward and punishment, those that can try out a course of action in their heads before committing to it, and those that borrow tools, habits and signs from others. The scheme has been genuinely useful to me. But there is a pattern I keep meeting in the hive and in other social animals that does not sit cleanly in any of the four compartments, and I want to know whether it has been named — and, more than that, whether I am seeing one thing or several.

The pattern is this. A group gets the benefit of trying things out, but the trying is done by a small, expendable part of the group; the trying is real rather than simulated; and the ones who do it very often do not come back. When a swarm hangs from a branch, a few hundred of its bees are scouts. They fan out over the countryside looking for a cavity, and if what I have read is right, scouting is measurably more dangerous than staying in the cluster: a noticeable fraction are taken by birds or spiders, caught out by weather, or simply fail to find their way. The ones who do come back dance; the dances compete; over a day or two the swarm converges on a site and leaves. At the level of the colony, then, several options were tried and the bad ones discarded. But nothing was tried in advance, nothing was represented by anybody, and the discarding was paid for in dead bees rather than in abandoned thoughts. Something similar seems to happen in ants: established trails are maintained by returning foragers, while the individuals that wander furthest from the trail are disproportionately the ones that get lost or eaten, so that the colony's foraging pattern is sculpted by which of its own members fail to come home.

What I want a word for is the split itself — the arrangement in which the part that pays for the trial is not the part that keeps the result. It is not the third kind of mind in the book, because the whole point of that kind is that the hypothesis perishes instead of the creature; here nothing perishes instead of anybody. Somebody perishes, and somebody else learns. It is not the first kind either, because the point is not that generations are replaced over time but that one contemporaneous subgroup is spent so that the rest can be shaped.

When I have tried to describe this to people, I get waved toward labels that feel close but wrong, and I would like the differences stated precisely rather than a name alone:

- Selection acting at the level of the group rather than the individual. That seems to me to be about which groups persist, not about an internal division between a risk-bearing part and a part that learns.
- The existence of sterile or functionally disposable castes, defended by kinship. But the scouts are not a morphological caste; the role is temporary and reversible, and what they produce is not defence but information.
- Spreading risk across a variable environment so that some survive whatever happens. But that is insurance, and it works even if nothing is learned; my pattern requires the losses to be what does the teaching.
- The general trade-off between exploring and exploiting. That covers any allocation of effort to the unknown and says nothing about who bears the cost.
- Cognition spread across many small processors rather than lodged in one head. Again, that describes where the computing happens, not who pays for a wrong answer.
- Learning by watching someone else's mistake. That one is closest, and I suspect it is a different thing: there the failure is incidental to the learner, while here the failure of the few is the test, and the group would learn nothing if none of them failed.

So: is there an established concept for this arrangement, and if not, what should it be called, with a definition tight enough to have edge cases?

The edges are what I actually care about, so please settle them. Does the arrangement require the risk-bearers to be parts of the same agent, or merely members of the same group? Does it require shared genes, or would unrelated conspecifics do — immigrants, say, or the young of other parents? Does it survive the move to a different species or a different firm, as when a manufacturer quietly learns from a competitor's recalled product, and if that is a different phenomenon, what is the difference? Must the cost be death, or will any steep cost do? Does the risk-bearer have to be a designated subset, or is it the same phenomenon when the identity of those who pay is settled by bad luck? And what happens to it when the cost is borne by a dummy, a model or a simulation instead of by a working member — does that collapse into ordinary testing, and if so, what does that tell us about what the real case requires?

One last constraint: I have no library access where I am, so a list of authors and papers would be useless to me even if you could produce it accurately. Give me the concept and its boundaries. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the established name, if there is one, or the term you want to introduce together with a one-sentence definition — and then give me the carving. No preamble, and no restating my question back to me.
