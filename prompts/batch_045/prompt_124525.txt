I'm completing a seminar paper on whether disease attributions are factual or evaluative, and I'd like to use an exchange I observed last month as a worked example. A memory clinic held a case conference on a 61-year-old teacher with no complaints and normal scores on standard cognitive testing who, as part of a research cohort, had a plasma assay for a phosphorylated tau fragment. The result was strongly positive. The prognostic model the clinic uses puts her well above baseline risk but is far from certain, and the only things on offer are the vascular-risk and exercise advice she would receive anyway. The question on the table was whether to record a diagnosis. A neurologist and a bioethicist on the committee disagreed, roughly as follows.

(1) Neurologist: She has a disease now. The assay tracks an ongoing departure from the species-typical functioning of the systems underlying memory, and disease is a matter of how the organism is working, not of how anyone feels about it. On your kind of view, a community that stopped minding memory loss would make dementia disappear.

(2) Bioethicist: The word does work in clinical practice: it opens the door to the sick role, surveillance, and insurance, and it marks something we take ourselves to have reason to do something about. Attach it to someone with no symptoms, no measurable deficit, and nothing to offer, and the only effects are anxiety and stigma. And a pure dysfunction criterion is far too broad: plenty of statistically subnormal function — a sixty-year-old's filtration rate, olfactory acuity after a viral illness — is not disease.

(3) Neurologist: Both of those are handled. Reference classes are age-relative, and hypertension is a disease while it is asymptomatic. Your view has the harder problem. Since learning the result she has retired early and moved nearer her daughter, and she reports being content; some of the harm your account requires exists only because the label was applied. And consider someone whose expectations have quietly shrunk to fit advanced memory loss and who reports being perfectly happy. No harm, no disease?

(4) Bioethicist: That case I grant you, but it decides nothing, because I am happy to say there is a factual notion of pathology and, separately, a clinical and evaluative notion of disease; your reductio only shows that the first is factual. And the harm here is not her anxiety — it is the prospective loss of a life she has reasons to care about, which does not depend on how she takes the news.

(5) Neurologist: Then the dispute is only about which notion the word picks out, and the burden is yours: clinicians do diagnose and treat asymptomatic pathology. Besides, reasons to care about imports a judgment about what matters in a life — whose reasons, and why those?

(6) Bioethicist: Whose reasons is answerable — hers, under informed and uncoerced reflection, so the account is not hostage to how she happens to feel today. And your view needs values too: you had to select which systems and which end-states proper function is measured against, and biology does not hand you that.

Could you adjudicate move by move? For each of the six, give a rating (clearly mistaken / partly right / right) and two or three sentences of justification identifying what is doing the work and where it fails. Then add one paragraph on who is more right overall and what both of them are missing. I care less about which side the literature favors than about whether these particular arguments land. Please keep the entire answer under 600 words.
