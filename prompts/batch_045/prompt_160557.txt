I'm in an undergrad seminar on judging historical figures and public memory — we've spent the last couple weeks on the 'man of his time' defence, mostly with Jefferson, Churchill and Columbus as the cases — and the final assignment is a short creative piece instead of an essay, thank god. I want to do a parody of an offical municipal form: a 'Standard Application to Retain a Statue or Named Building', the sort with checkboxes and a cramped little box for 'describe the objectionable conduct, max 30 words'. Give it to me as exactly five numbered clauses, each one or two sentences, no headline, no intro, no signature block, no form number — just the five clauses.

The target should be the defence itself, not the three guys, and I want the laughs to come from actually getting the defence right, so a tutor who's taught it alot laughs rather than cringes. Please work in three things our seminar got stuck on. First: the contemporaneous-objector test — the defence quietly assumes nobody at the time said the thing was wrong, and whether that's true is an empirical question that can come out false, so 'couldn't have known better' turns into a claim about the historical record that can just fail. Second: symmetry — whatever excuses him for the bad parts also drains the credit for the good parts, so if he was merely a man of his time then the stirring words were his time's as much as the slaveholding was. Third: the forward version — if the defence is good for him it's good for us, and our grandchildren owe us the same exemption.

Keep it deadpan: the clerk filling out the form should be applying the rules correctly and it's the rules that are the joke, no winking, nobody ever says 'can you believe this'. By clause five a reader who has done none of the readings should feel that the form cannot honestly be filled in, without being told so.
