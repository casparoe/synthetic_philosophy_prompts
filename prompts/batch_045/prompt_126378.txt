My friend Rae has a hobbyhorse she calls the inviolate level. Rough gloss: in a system with stacked levels (rules, rules about the rules, and so on), the inviolate level is the one nothing can violate — you can't change it from anywhere you can get to, and you can't get outside it either. She keeps issuing verdicts on whether particular systems have one, and I've started writing them down because I genuinely can't tell which test she's applying. Her rulings so far:

1. Escher's Drawing Hands — yes, and it's the sheet of paper. The hands can draw each other forever but neither can draw the surface they're on, and there's no move available from inside the picture for getting outside it.
2. A constitution with an eternity clause (the amendment procedure can't be used to repeal itself or to delete the first article) — yes, the clause is inviolate, with the caveat "from inside the legal order. a revolution isn't a legal operation, so it's not a counterexample."
3. A card game that ships with a card reading "players may change any rule, including this one" — no. Her words: "the only thing you can't change is that you're sat at a table and I don't count furniture."
4. An endless nest of stories — a boy reading about a boy reading about a boy, no outermost frame — no. "stairs not a loop, you can always climb one more."
5. A self-hosting compiler / metacircular interpreter running on fixed hardware — yes, the hardware. Programs can rewrite themselves all day, they can't rewrite the fact that they're running on this silicon.
6. A commercial online game: an EULA, company-run arbitration, the company can ban you and rewrite the rules, and real courts sit above the company — no. "players can escalate straight out of it, and the rules get changed from outside the game anyway."

New case she hasn't ruled on: a decade-old collaborative fiction wiki.

- anyone registered for 30 days can edit any page, including the Editorial Policy and the page describing how the Council of Editors gets elected
- disputes go to the Council, and the Council's powers are spelled out on an ordinary editable page
- the software keeps an append-only revision log; nobody, including the site owner, can delete or alter a past revision
- once a year there's a Charter vote; the Charter can override any page including the Editorial Policy, and the ballot procedure itself is hard-coded in the source
- the source and the server belong to the site's owner, who is also a contributor, plays a character in the fiction, has publicly promised never to edit anybody else's contribution, and has root
- forking is blessed by policy: any contributor may copy the entire wiki, log and all, and start a new one; forks count as legitimate continuations, two have happened, and the main wiki links to them
- there's an in-wiki page, edited by contributors, arguing about whether the log is inviolate

So: does this thing have an inviolate level, and if so where? I care much less about the bare yes/no than about the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her six verdicts just as well, where those rules come apart on the wiki, and what single change to the wiki would settle it.

Give me a markdown table, one row per candidate rule, with these columns: rule_label | rule_in_one_sentence | verdicts_it_fits (which of 1–6, and flag any it only barely tolerates or plain gets wrong) | verdict_on_the_wiki (yes / no / depends, plus which level, if yes) | flip (the one change to the wiki that would flip this rule's verdict) | p_is_raes_rule (0–1).

Then a JSON object with exactly these fields: {"best_guess": "...", "level": "...", "confidence": <0–1>, "closest_analog": "<which of 1–6, and why>", "decisive_next_case": "<the minimal case you'd put to her next>", "hidden_variable": "..."} — where hidden_variable is whatever she'd have to fix before her verdict is even well defined.
