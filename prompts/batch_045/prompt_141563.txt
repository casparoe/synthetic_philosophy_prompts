Hey — long-time lurker (LessWrong since the Sequences were going up, plus the ususal adjacent blogs), and I want a case built to spec, for an argument I keep having in comment threads.

The argument: people wheel out Aumann's agreement theorem as if it handed you a norm. Two people, both trying to be rational, both able to see the other's number, therefore they converge — so if you and I have swapped numbers six times and we're still sitting at 0.6 and 0.3, one of us is doing something wrong. I want a case where that inference just doesn't go through. Not because anyone is being sloppy, but because the theorem never properly grips.

Spec:
- Two agents, one genuinely common prior, both scrupulously Bayesian, both honest, both reporting exact posteriors, and they swap numbers repeatedly.
- Realistic setting: a small organisation, a lab, a committee, two people running a thing together. Normal humans, stakes a normal person could face. No demons, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics, no simulations, nobody with private revelation.
- At the end they're stuck at genuinely different numbers, and each can give an honest story for why their number is right given what they know — a story the other one actually grants, not one the other thinks is confused.
- The mechanism must not be: different priors, bad Bayes, lying or strategic misreporting, bounded rationality, or failure to communicate. And it must not be the merely-verbal move where they were never disagreeing about the same proposition — I've seen that one a thousand times and it's definately not what I'm after.
- The lever I want you to try: the proposition they're disagreeing about is performative. Its chances partly depend on what the two of them end up believing about it, because their beliefs feed into what they do. (Whether the joint project works, where it works only if both keep putting in effort, and each eases off when they get pessimistic.) If that lever can't actually be made to work, say so plainly and pick a different one, but then tell me why the performative version can't be made to work.

Deliverable:
1. The scenario itself in one short paragraph.
2. A miniature model: state space, what each one observes, what the event is, and why the prior is really common rather than just stipulated to be. Doesn't have to be numbers, but I want to see the structure.
3. One round of the exchange worked through: each one's posterior, what each does with the other's number, and why it doesn't pull them together. In particular show me why the pessimistic fixed point is self-confirming rather than self-correcting — why learning the other person is at 30% behaves like confirmation instead of evidence.
4. Which premise of Aumann's theorem fails, in one sentence, and why that premise rather than a different one.
5. Ablation: replace the performative event with an inert one, same everything else, and say what happens — does the usual "keep swapping numbers and you converge" result (Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis, in finite state spaces) now bite, and roughly how fast?
6. The strongest reply from someone who thinks the theorem is untouched: presumably "expand the state space until the event is well-defined and the theorem applies again". Give that reply properly, then tell me whether it's a real dissolution or just relocating the bump. Be fair to both sides.
7. Where you're unsure: how confident are you in the case overall, and which single step of it would you bet against?

British English throughout, and please don't just assert "and therefore they rationally disagree" — I want the disagreement falling out of the setup rather than being written into it.
