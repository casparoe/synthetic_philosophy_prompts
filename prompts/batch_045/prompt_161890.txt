I came to philosophy late, after a working life in protein crystallography, and I now sit in on a mechanistic interpretability reading group at the local university — mostly graduate students, with one philosopher who comes along to keep us honest. At the last meeting a speaker argued that a language model represents truth. Her evidence was twofold: a linear classifier trained on the model's residual stream — the vector handed from layer to layer inside the network — separates true statements from false ones well above chance and generalises to data it was not trained on; and adding a multiple of the resulting direction back into the activations at inference time makes the model likelier to assert truths and less likely to assert falsehoods. Two complications she volunteered. The direction was not read straight off the activations but came from a sparse autoencoder trained to reconstruct them from a large dictionary of sparsely firing directions, and when that autoencoder was retrained with a larger dictionary, the truth direction split into several. It also fires on some widely repeated falsehoods, the sort of claim that is asserted constantly in training text.

A member of the group closed the discussion with a line I have since heard him repeat: just because you can find a direction, doesn't mean the network uses it. He has proposed that we drop the remainder of the term's programme as methodologically naive. I want to be fair to him, but I suspect the line is doing several different jobs at once, and that some of them are platitudes dressed up as objections.

Please lay out, as a list I can take back to the group, the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making, ordered roughly from the least to the most demanding, and mark each as a platitude, a substantive thesis that would need arguing for, or something false. I would rather the distinctions were separated than run together: information being available to a probe trained after the fact, versus the direction doing causal work in the model's own computation; the difference between carrying information about truth and exploiting it; whether the residual stream has a privileged basis at all, and what superposition does to that question; the gap between there being no fact of the matter whether the model represents X and the fact of the matter being that it does not; and whether a state whose content looks indeterminate — truth-or-assertability, say — can be a representation of anything in particular.

Then: which of those readings would actually license dismissing the speaker's conclusion, and which would only counsel further experiments? Which of them is he most likely reaching for, given the use he wanted to make of the line? Does the steering result rule any of them out, and does the splitting of the feature bear on any of them? If none of them licenses the dismissal, say so plainly and say what he would instead have to establish.

Finally, a thought from my old trade. In crystallography we worried about model bias: a feature one thought one could see in an electron density map had sometimes been put there by the model used to phase it, and the remedy was to hold part of the data out of refinement and check the map against that. Is that a fair analogue of any of the readings above, and if so, what would the corresponding check be here?
