I'm writing a paper on whether the priority view really answers the leveling-down objection, and I need the analogy that keeps doing the work in my department audited rather than either accepted or waved away.

At a seminar last week an economist colleague dismissed prioritarianism as bookkeeping. Giving extra weight to the worse off, she said, is nothing but a concave transformation of well-being, and the right way to see it is as a progressive income tax schedule: one currency, one total to be distributed, and a marginal rate that falls as you move up the scale. The schedule itself redistributes nothing and expresses no moral view distinct from the view about what income is for; it is a scaling convention. She then used the analogy twice. First: there is no substantive moral disagreement between prioritarians and utilitarians, only a dispute about how to calibrate the currency. Second: prioritarianism's much-advertised immunity to the leveling-down objection is dialectically cheap, because it escapes the objection only by declining to say that equality itself matters — that is, by dropping the claim that made leveling down tempting in the first place.

What I want from you:

1. A correspondence table: what plays the role of pre-tax income, of the marginal rate schedule, of the marginal rate itself, of post-tax income, of total revenue, of the legislature that sets the schedule, and of taxpayers' behavioral response to it. Say explicitly what, inside the tax frame, corresponds to leveling down — and check whether the analogy even has the resources to represent the egalitarian's claim. My suspicion is that the closest analogue is the state burning money rather than collecting it, so that the frame can only ever register a loss of total and begs the question; tell me whether that's right or whether the analogy can be extended to a schedule whose rates are set by reference to dispersion (a Gini-indexed rate schedule, say).

2. The disanalogies, sorted by which of her two conclusions they actually bear on. The one I care most about: the diminishing marginal value of money is an empirical and psychological matter on which the schedule's shape can be checked, whereas the diminishing marginal moral weight of well-being is a normative stipulation with nothing independent to be checked against — unless we read the transformation as a re-measurement of how much well-being a given change involves rather than as a weighting of how much a given amount of well-being matters. Say which reading the analogy needs and whether it can get it. Also flag, if you think it's fair, that nobody regards a progressive tax schedule as mere bookkeeping about income, and that this cuts against her first conclusion at least as hard as for it.

3. What is doing the work in her second conclusion. As far as I can see, for any fixed weighting function the rejection of leveling down follows from separability plus monotonicity alone: any strictly increasing separable transform rejects it, concave or not. So concavity is not what buys immunity, and the immunity is inherited from the aggregative structure prioritarianism shares with utilitarianism rather than achieved by it. Say whether that supports her charge that the escape is cheap, or whether it shows the charge is aimed at the wrong feature.

4. My own variant, which I want stress-tested. Call it variable-schedule prioritarianism: the concavity of the weighting function is itself indexed to the dispersion of the distribution being evaluated, so that priority gets steeper as the spread widens and flattens toward utilitarianism as the spread closes. Concretely: order the distribution, set the schedule exponent by the relative range, and evaluate a distribution as the sum of each person's well-being raised to that exponent, with the exponent kept below one so the schedule is always concave and always increasing. On my arithmetic, with two people, V(1,10) is about 2.52 while V(1,6) is about 2.67, so leveling down is favored; and V(1,11) is about 2.49, so a pure Pareto improvement lowers the total, because gains at the top steepen everyone's schedule. Check those numbers, then tell me: does the variant escape the leveling-down objection; does it violate the Pareto principle; is it prioritarianism at all, or egalitarianism with the priority weighting doing the work of a relational term; and — the point I actually care about — does it refute my colleague's analogy, or merely fall outside it, given that a tax schedule is pointwise and separable while an indexed one is not?

5. If the tax-schedule analogy is the wrong one, say what a better one would be; if it's right for some purposes and not others, say which.

A table is fine, aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, and don't give me citations or a reading list — I'll add references myself. Finish with two machine-readable lines and nothing after them: first "Variant: [ESCAPES | DOES-NOT-ESCAPE | ESCAPES-ONLY-GIVEN-SEPARABILITY]", for whether variable-schedule prioritarianism escapes the leveling-down objection; last "Verdict: [HOLDS | HOLDS-PARTIALLY | FAILS]", for whether the tax-schedule analogy carries her conclusion that prioritarianism's immunity to that objection is dialectically cheap.
