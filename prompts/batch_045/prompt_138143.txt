hey — I'm in a joint-listed comp-pol / political theory seminar this term and we just spent two sessions on corruption and I've come out with a thing I can't name and it's bugging me more than the actual readings did.

So we did the usual stuff: whether "corruption" is basically a western category that gets exported to places where it doesn't fit, gift-giving vs. bribery, clientelism, the anthropological point that a gift moves obligations around rather than just moving goods, the arguments about whether Transparency-style rankings are measuring anything real. Fine. But in the discussion I kept noticing a pattern that nobody in the room had a word for, and when I tried to describe it the TA said "that's just Goodhart's law" and I really don't think it is.

The pattern: an anti-corruption rule targets the visible, countable form of a practice. But the practice has two versions — one where the transfer sits inside an ongoing relationship with real mutual obligations and some enforcement on both sides, and one where it's a one-off extraction with no follow-up and no relationship at all. The rule kills version one, and version two grows into the space. Measured corruption goes down. The thing people were actually complaining about gets worse. And the nasty bit is that this doesn't feel like sloppy implementation: the two versions look identical from the outside, the only thing that separates them is the relationship, and the relationship is exactly what the rule destroys.

Two cases I keep coming back to. One: a rural clinic where families used to bring the nurse small things — eggs, a chicken, cash when they had it — and she'd see them quickly, and, more importantly, would treat people she knew when they had nothing at all, because the earlier gifts had made a relationship that she'd lose face by walking away from. A rule comes in banning gifts from patients. Gifts stop. Now there's a posted fee payable in cash up front, the nurse has no relationship with anybody, and the people who used to be seen on credit just aren't seen. On paper: gift-taking went to zero. On the ground: nothing changed except that access got worse for exactly the people the reform was supposedly for.

Two: road contracts. A minister used to award to a contractor from his own region. That contractor finished the road, badly sometimes, because his standing back home depended on it and everyone knew who to shout at when he didn't. Procurement reform: open competitive bidding, lowest bid wins. Connected awards drop to nearly nothing. The winner has no one to answer to locally, the road is graded and abandoned, the cost overrun is bigger than the kickback ever was.

So: is there a name for this, and if there isn't, can you carve it properly? Goodhart doesn't work, I think, because the measure keeps tracking the thing it measures — the thing itself changed under it, which is different from a measure decaying once it's a target. The cobra effect is closer but that's about incentives to produce more of the very thing being suppressed, whereas here the suppressed practice doesn't come back, it gets replaced by a worse variant of itself. Someone in my department said "isomorphic mimicry" but as far as I can tell that's about organizations copying forms without the function, which isn't this. And there's the with-theft / without-theft distinction in the econ literature, but that seems to sort kinds of bribe, not to say anything about what happens to a kind after you ban it.

What I actually want is the carving, not a label to stick on my paper. Is this one phenomenon or two bolted together — a substitution effect plus a measurement artifact — and does it matter for the answer whether the reformers' original definition of corruption was right or wrong? I also can't tell whether the "are western standards universal" frame is even the right frame here, or whether this is a different problem wearing the same clothes: not "is the label imposed from outside" but "applying the label makes the thing the label names." And I want boundary cases — what would be a case that looks like this but isn't, so I know I'm not just pattern-matching over two anecdotes I happen to like. Like, is it still an instance if the replacement turns out to be better for the poor but more demeaning, or if the old relational version was already collapsing on its own?
