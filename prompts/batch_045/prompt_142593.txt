I am a retired evolutionary ecologist who spent my career modeling population dynamics, resource limitation, and extinction risk, and I have recently begun reading philosophy, especially population ethics, now that I have the leisure to do so. I am preparing a discussion note for a local philosophy-and-film group and would like a careful reading of the Marvel films Avengers: Infinity War and Avengers: Endgame, focused on the character of Thanos, through the lens of the repugnant conclusion and population axiology.

As background, in case it helps: Thanos is portrayed as having foreseen the ecological and social collapse of his home world, Titan, as a result of runaway population growth, and as having proposed the random elimination of half of all life as a remedy; his proposal was rejected and his civilization fell. He later acquires cosmic power and carries out a universal version of this policy, erasing half of all living beings at random in an event usually called the Decimation, on the stated ground that the survivors will then have the resources to flourish. In the second film, an earlier version of Thanos learns of his future success and judges it insufficient; he resolves instead to destroy the entire universe completely and to create a new universe in its place, populated by beings who will have no memory of what came before and who will, in his view, be properly grateful simply for existing. I am not asking for any dialogue to be quoted; please paraphrase any scenes you discuss.

I have encountered the repugnant conclusion and the surrounding debates only in a general way, so I do not want a bibliography, reading list, or citations. What I want is a reading that takes both the films and the philosophical problem seriously. In particular, I would like you to use a small set of stylized policies as a fixed reference point. Call them Policy A, Policy B, and Policy C. Policy A is to leave the existing cosmic population as it is, with its current size, current average quality of life, and current risks of scarcity and suffering. Policy B is Thanos's first plan: randomly remove half of all existing subjects, holding the resource base fixed, so that the average quality of life of the survivors is greatly increased. Policy C is a stylized version of his second plan: replace the entire current universe with a new universe containing a larger number of subjects than the original, each of whom has a life that is positive but only barely worth living, none of whom remember the old universe, and who are subjectively content or grateful for what they have. I stipulate the barely-worth-living condition because that is what makes the repugnant conclusion vivid; if the film's own rhetoric implies instead that the new beings will be very well off, please say so and explain how that would change the evaluation.

Against that background, please consider the following. How does Thanos's first plan look from the perspectives of total, average, and person-affecting approaches to population axiology? Is his move from Policy A to Policy B better understood as an elitist or average-intuition response to overpopulation, and does the philosophical literature give any reason to think that a smaller, better-off population could be worse than a larger, worse-off one, or vice versa? How does his shift to Policy C change the axiological picture? Does the fact that the new subjects do not remember the old universe, and are said to be grateful, make any difference to whether Policy C is acceptable, or is that merely a rhetorical disguise for what population axiology would regard as a standard repugnant conclusion? Is there a person-affecting objection to Policy C that does not apply to Policy B, or a totalist objection to Policy B that does not apply to Policy C?

More generally, I would like a reading that is faithful in both directions. It should not reduce the films to a mere illustration of a formula, nor should it distort the philosophical ideas in order to fit the plot. Where the story illuminates something that abstract population ethics tends to miss—for example, the role of memory, history, and gratitude in how we evaluate the value of a population, or the way in which a policy of random culling violates our sense of the separateness of persons—I want that brought out. Where the films instead resist or even undermine the axiological reading—because they are adventure stories that need a villain to be stopped, because the horror of the Decimation is presented as self-evident regardless of any utilitarian calculus, or because Thanos's motives are dramatized as psychologically tragic rather than as a coherent policy proposal—I want the limits of the reading stated plainly rather than smoothed over. If, in the end, the films add something to the philosophical debate that a journal article does not, please say what that is.

I would be grateful for a response in American English, written in a formal but accessible register, and for the answer to avoid verbatim quotation from the films and to rely on paraphrase throughout.
