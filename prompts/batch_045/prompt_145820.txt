I've been reading the same corners of the internet for about fifteen years, and I'm in a small reading group — six of us, most with that background — working through one of those bestselling recent books about how democracies die, the kind that opens with the Gracchi and closes with the last few American election cycles. One member of the group, easily the most historically literate of us and also the most temperamentally conservative, has been filtering the entire discussion through a single analogy, and after three sessions it is doing more work than I think it can bear. I want the analogy itself audited. Please don't pad the answer with citations or a suggested reading list; I want the argument, and I'd rather it not depend on anything you'd have to look up.

Answer as a historian of the late Roman Republic who also works on comparative democratization and institutional economics, and who argues like an analytic political theorist: what matters to you is not whether a claim sounds right but what it can actually support. Steelman the analogy before you criticize it, and please take your time: before writing the audit, brainstorm — dump every correspondence between a fence and an unwritten constitutional norm that occurs to you, every disanalogy, every place where the picture might be quietly importing a feature the target doesn't have; then sort that list into what bears on the specific conclusions the analogy is being used for and what is mere decoration; then write the audit from what survived, and tell me briefly what you discarded and why. Where a specific historical detail matters and you're not confident of it, say so rather than filling the gap.

The analogy is Chesterton's fence: the principle that you shouldn't tear down a fence whose purpose you don't understand, and that you earn the right to remove it only by first finding out why it was put there. He applies it to the unwritten restraints of the mos maiorum — the annual round of magistracies, the tribunician veto, the bar on armed force inside the city, the conventions about prorogued commands and about what a commander did with his army and his veterans. Nobody in 133 BC could have handed you the full design rationale for that arrangement; the Gracchi, Marius, Sulla and then Caesar each pulled out a fence; the result was a century of civil war and the end of the Republic. The conclusions he draws from it are three, and I'd like each treated separately: (1) a strong, near-absolute presumption against ever breaking an unwritten constitutional norm, however archaic or inconvenient; (2) a symmetrical moral verdict — the blame belongs to whoever breaks first, and matching a broken norm is always the wrong response; (3) applied to us, the claim that the correct response to constitutional hardball is unilateral restraint, and that the way to keep a republic is simply to leave the fences standing.

I've raised four objections and he's waved them off, and I genuinely can't tell whether he's right to do that or whether I'm being clever. First: Chesterton's case is one of *ignorance* about the fence's purpose, but in most Roman cases the purpose was perfectly well known — everyone understood why commands were short and why armies stayed outside the pomerium; what changed was that the reasons had stopped outweighing the payoffs. If the rationale is visible and merely defeated, the fence principle seems to me to have nothing to say. Second: the fence picture assumes a builder who was solving your problem, whereas a norm may be the product of selection rather than design — an equilibrium held up by a selection pressure that may no longer be operating — in which case what matters is not whether I can name the reason but whether the pressure that maintained it still exists. (I've started calling the Roman version of this the "Carthage premium": the thought that the Republic's restraints were affordable while an existential external threat kept the elite's time horizon long, and became unaffordable after 146 BC, when the shadow of the future shortened and every restraint turned into an expensive gift to your rival.) Third: fences are unilateral and non-strategic, but norm observance is a strategic complement — the cost of leaving your fence up rises with the number of people climbing theirs — so a picture with no room for that cannot even represent the unraveling it is being used to argue about, let alone justify unilateral restraint. Fourth: fences are physical and have a location, whereas norms are vague and re-describable, and my impression is that most Roman restraints didn't die by being removed; they died by being kept in form and emptied in substance, by exceptions and precedents and legal fictions, which the fence picture can't see at all.

The deliverable I want, in roughly 1,200–1,600 words, is the audit: an explicit correspondence table (fence, builder, field, reformer, the thing you don't know, the destruction, the consequence — mapped onto norm, source of the norm, republic, breaker of the norm, and so on); a statement of which features of fences the analogy smuggles in that norms don't have, including the normative one — that the builder was protecting the common good rather than entrenching an incumbent's rent; a separation of the disanalogies that matter for his three conclusions from the ones that don't, with my four objections rated as to whether each actually bites; and a verdict on whether anything survives — is there a salvageable core, say an epistemic or precautionary version about visible benefits being unreliable evidence of total effects, or does the moral version do all the work? Finally: if the fence is the wrong picture, what's the better one — is his cartel-price-and-shadow-of-the-future frame the right replacement, is there something better (I've been toying with load-bearing structures in a building you didn't design and can't see the load path of), and what would have to be true of a modern democracy for the fence principle to be the right frame after all? It's also worth telling me whether the outside view — the base rates on how republics have actually ended — should change how much weight any of this gets.
