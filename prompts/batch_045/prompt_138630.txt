I do audience research at a mid-sized media nonprofit, and I'm putting together a short internal session on whether we should keep using generational buckets (Millennial, Gen Z, and so on) in our segmentation work. Half my team thinks the labels are junk, half thinks they're doing real explanatory work, and we keep going in circles. I figured a dialogue would make a better handout than bullet points.

Could you write me one? Two colleagues on the same team, both genuinely good at their jobs — I really dont want either of them to be a dumb version of the position, and I want them to actually respond to what the other person just said instead of each delivering a speech and then agreeing to disagree. Somewhere around 1000–1300 words, whatever it takes. Name them whatever you like.

Roughly the beats I want covered, though don't feel obliged to follow them in order:

- The skeptic opens with a technical point I only half understand: in cohort data you can't cleanly seperate age effects, period effects and cohort effects, because cohort is just period minus age, so any smooth trend can be re-described three different ways. The realist should get to actually answer this rather than wave at it.
- A contrast case: some societies sort people into formally recognized age sets, with sharp entry dates, real legal roles, and public rituals, so membership is not a statistical guess. If cohorts can obviously be real there, does that help the case for 'Millennial', or does it just make our categories look shabby by comparison?
- The vagueness move: the birth-year cutoffs are arbitrary and get quietly revised every few years, so push the realist on whether they're committed to mountains and colors being unreal too, and let them say what separates a vague-but-real thing from a gerrymandered one.
- Two tests I made up and can't tell whether they're fair: (a) the swap test — take two people born a year apart on opposite sides of a cutoff and ask whether anything besides their age explains any difference in the outcome; (b) the portability test — do these labels still cut at anything real once you leave the rich Western countries they were basically designed around?
- The self-fulfilling bit: once a label catches on, people start performing it, and then the numbers shift. Does that make the kind real, or does it make it an artifact? And if it's an artifact, what do we do with the fact that it now genuinely predicts behaviour?

Also — both of them should concede something real somewhere, not just hold the line for symmetry's sake. And if you think one of my two tests is actually a bad test, have a character say so and explain why. Please don't pad it with references or further reading, I can't chase any of that up anyway.
