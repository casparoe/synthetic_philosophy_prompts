I ran a materials characterization lab for about thirty years -- XRF mostly, endless argumens about calibration curves and whether a peak was real -- and now that I'm retired I've started reading epistemology at night. Nozick's tracking account got under my skin, and I've managed to talk myself into a corner. I've swapd brains in vats for spectrometers, because brains in vats never moved me.

(1) Tracking: you know p by method M only if (i) had p been false, you wouldn't have believed p by M, and (ii) had p been true, you would have.
(2) Closure: if you know p, know that p entails q, and come to believe q by deducing it from p, then you know q.
(3) I read the display: 3.2 wt% chromium. Take M as reading this spectrometer. The nearest worlds where the sample doesn't have 3.2% Cr are worlds where the sample has no Cr and the machine is working fine, so the display reads zero and I don't believe p. Both halves of (1) hold, so I know p.
(4) p entails q: the machine was not stuck replaying yesterday's stored spectrum. A stuck machine reports a number without registering anything, so a real chromium signal rules that out.
(5) By (2) and (3), I know q.
(6) Now test q against (1) directly. The nearest worlds where q is false are the firmware-hang worlds: same bench, same display, same 3.2, nothing I had access to any different. I'd still believe q there. So (i) fails, and I don't know q.
(7) Contradiction. (1) and (2) can't both be right.
(8) The usual move is to drop (2), and I can live with that for a while. But there's something from lab practice I can't let go of, which I've started calling the blind-failure rule: a reading counts as a result only if you've done something -- a blank, a standard, a rerun on a different instrument -- that would have come out different if the number were an artifact. That isn't closure, it's just the demand that your method discriminate the signal from its own failure modes. And it says that knowing p requires knowing q.
(9) So (6) says I don't know q, the blind-failure rule says no q means no p, hence I don't know p. And the same for every measurement anybody has ever made, since every instrument has failure modes you can't see from inside the room. But measurement is the best case of empirical knowledge we have.

If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (3) and the blind-failure rule than anything else on the list. Which step is actually broken here, and what am I paying to give it up?
