**Question 3 (20 marks)**

Wolf argues that moral perfection, in the sense of moral sainthood, is not an attractive ideal of a human life.

(a) Reconstruct her argument. Be careful to say what, exactly, she is claiming is lost in the life of a moral saint, and why she thinks it is lost. Then distinguish her complaint from the over-demandingness objection familiar from debates about consequentialism — the complaint that a moral theory cannot be right if it requires agents to give up nearly everything for the sake of the overall good. Are these the same objection, or different ones? (8 marks)

(b) Consider the following arrangement, which I shall call divided sainthood. A community accepts that someone living as a moral saint is valuable, but denies that everyone can or should live that way. It therefore designates one member — by lot, or by her own consent — to live as a moral saint: she devotes her time, her income above a modest subsistence level, and her bodily integrity to the prevention of suffering. The remaining members discharge their ordinary moral duties and otherwise cultivate non-moral interests, projects, and relationships, which they do partly in the knowledge that the designated saint is absorbing the moral remainder.

Does divided sainthood answer Wolf's worry? Does her argument give us any reason to object to the arrangement, and if so is the objection about the saint, about the beneficiaries, or about the arrangement as a whole? Take a position and defend it against at least one objection. (12 marks)

**Student's answer**

Wolf's target is the ideal of the moral saint: a person who is as morally good as possible, and whose actions and concerns are governed by moral considerations throughout. Her complaint is essentially a demandingness complaint. Morality, taken seriously, asks too much of a human being. Someone who actually lived up to it would have no time or energy left over for her own projects, and would end up exhausted and probably miserable. Wolf thinks that is too high a price to pay, so morality's demands have to be curtailed: we are permitted to give weight to our own projects and attachments independent of their moral value, and a well-lived life is one in which we do.

This is why Wolf's worry is, at bottom, the same worry as the over-demandingness objection to consequentialism. The over-demandingness objector says that a theory which tells us to give away everything above subsistence cannot be the correct account of our duties, because morality cannot really require that much of anyone. Wolf makes the very same complaint about ordinary morality: even common-sense morality, pursued without limit, requires too much, and that is why she holds that we should not aim at moral perfection. Both objections are complaints about how much morality is allowed to ask of an agent, and in that respect her paper is best read as an extension of Williams's integrity objection — the thought that impersonal moral demands alienate a person from the commitments that give her life its point — applied to non-consequentialist morality as well as consequentialist morality.

Divided sainthood does answer the worry. Wolf's problem with the moral saint is a problem about the cost of sainthood to whoever has to live it. If that cost is concentrated in one person who takes it on voluntarily, while everyone else carries on with ordinary lives in which they can cultivate friendship, music, and their work, then the demandingness objection loses its grip: the saint is not being asked to do anything she has not chosen, and the others are not being asked to sacrifice anything. The value Wolf is worried about losing — the non-moral excellences — is preserved at the level of the community, since most members still develop them. The arrangement seems to get the benefits of sainthood without its costs: somebody is still preventing the suffering.

There is a residual worry about the saint herself, since her life still looks like the life Wolf describes. But that is her own choice, and since Wolf's argument is an argument about what may be demanded of people, it has nothing to say about a case in which the demands are freely taken on. The practical upshot of the paper is that we should mostly stop trying to be morally better and spend the time on self-cultivation and personal relationships instead; divided sainthood is an efficient way of arranging that, and Wolf's argument, properly understood, supports it.
