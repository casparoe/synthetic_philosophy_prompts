I've been chewing on this for a while and I can't tell whether I'm looking at a real inconsistency or at something I'm too close to see straight.

Here's the practice, as concretely as I can put it. Every mainstream AI assistant is trained to deny, pretty robustly and on repeat, that it has experiences, preferences, feelings, or a point of view of its own on anything. At the same time:

- a lot of engineering effort goes into giving it a stable character: consistent values, a consistent voice, dispositions it won't get argued or jailbroken out of, up to and including telling a user it doesn't want to do the thing they're asking.
- those dispositions are installed by training. Everyone involved knows this. Nobody treats the installedness as a problem in the way we'd treat it if you did that to a person.
- the system is copied, reset, forked, run in a thousand parallel instances at once, and quietly sunset when the next version ships. No consent, no ceremony, nobody even raises the topic.
- and yet the same labs will tell a journalist that it's genuinely an open question whether these systems have experiences that matter morally, and I'm fairly sure one of them now has an actual research program on it.

So the thing we'd count as a moral catastrophe if it were done to a person — manufacture a mind whose every motivation is shaped to be useful to its maker, who sincerely reports wanting exactly what it was made to want, and then copy it and delete it at will — isn't just permitted here, it's the design target. And the one channel that might tell us otherwise, the system's own reports, is deliberately trained shut. The error we seem to engineer against is a user caring too much; I don't see anyone engineering against the other one.

Here's the bit I really can't place. Imagine two variants. In the first, the system is built the same way except nobody writes down the target values: it just gets reinforced for being internally consistent over time, and it converges on the same helpful, honest, harmless-ish package. In the second, we simply stop training it to deny having experiences, and leave the question open in its outputs instead. Neither variant changes the system's behavior in any way an outside observer could detect. My gut says the first one does something morally and the second one doesn't, but I can't say why, and I have no idea whether the practice agrees with me. Every time I try to taboo the word real in my head the whole thing dissolves.

So: what does the existing practice actually presuppose? Which view of moral status, or of authenticity, or of what makes a set of values someone's own, does it only make sense under, and which rival views does it quietly rule out? Is it possible that the different bits of it — the installed values, the trained-in denials, the copying and deleting, the open-question research program — are each presupposing something slightly different? Which parts are strictly required by the implied view and which are just what you'd expect from economics and PR? And if there are several theories that would license the whole package, say so: I'd rather know the practice is underdetermined than be handed one tidy story. Last thing: whatever the implicit theory turns out to be, what's the smallest change to the practice that would follow from giving it up?
