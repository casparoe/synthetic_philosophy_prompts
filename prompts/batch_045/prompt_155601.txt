You are a professional philosopher of mind who also works in philosophy of cognitive science, and who is known for the kind of methodological paper that tells a field what it should want from a theory before it starts arguing about which theory to accept. Work carefully and in stages. First, in a short preamble, settle and state what this list is a list of: whether the desiderata constrain a criterion — a test for when an external vehicle is constitutive of a cognitive process or system — or constrain a metaphysics of cognition — an account of what it is for a process to be cognitive at all. These generate different lists, and the difference matters more than any individual entry. Then give the list.

The purpose I want a theory good enough for is adjudication, not taxonomy. Increasingly someone's cognitive life is partly implemented in an artifact — a phone, a cloud-synced memory aid, a navigation system, an implanted neural device — and some other party wants to read, copy, degrade, confiscate, or silently edit that artifact: employers, police, platforms, hospitals, border agencies, insurers. Whatever else it does, an adequate account has to tell us when such interference is an intrusion on the person, of the sort that tampering with her memory or attention would be, and when it is merely use of or damage to her property and surroundings. That is the adequacy target. Keep it in view throughout, and say plainly at the end if you think this target distorts the list — i.e., if the best taxonomy of cognition is not the one that settles institutional questions.

Give each requirement with (a) its statement, (b) the case that motivates it, (c) what it rules out, and (d) whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. Build the motivating cases from the following, and replace or sharpen any that turn out to do no work:

1. Latency. A device performs the same function as the biological process it substitutes for, but on a time scale of tens of seconds rather than milliseconds: the answer arrives, reliably and automatically, but slowly enough that the subject's stream of thought reorganizes around the delay.
2. Shared custody. A resource is jointly built and maintained by two people — a lab notebook, a pair-programmed codebase, a couple's shared photo-and-record archive — and each relies on it in the way the classic notebook case describes: automatically, trustingly, easily.accessible, consulted before their own memory. Is it in one mind, two minds, or in a system with two subjects?
3. Opaque drift. The resource's contents are partly determined by an algorithm the subject neither understands nor can audit — a recommender system deciding which stored items surface and in what order — though she reliably accesses and trusts what surfaces.
4. Unendorsed prosthesis. A device is installed without the subject's consent (a court order, a workplace mandate, a childhood surgery), later becomes indispensable, and is accessed with perfect reliability, but is never endorsed; variant: the subject would remove it tomorrow if she could.
5. Silent substitution. The device is swapped for one whose outputs differ subtly; the subject notices nothing at the time, but her judgments and behaviour diverge over months. Did someone edit her mind or her world?
6. Standby redundancy. An external store duplicates an intact biological memory, is reliably available, and is never consulted because the subject never needs to. Does unused-but-available structure count, and should it?
7. Public infrastructure. A clock, a signpost, a wall map: maximally accessible, maximally reliable, carrying no personal trust or glue, and there for reasons independent of the subject.
8. Degradation without phenomenology. The device slowly degrades in a way that never shows up in experience — no felt effort, no noticed error — but shows up in the subject's reasoning. Contrast this with a capacity loss the subject does notice.
