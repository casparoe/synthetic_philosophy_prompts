Hey — I'm an American doing a master's in Berlin this year and I have to decide by Friday whether to sign up for a new "city card" the municipality is piloting. If you link your library account, your transit history, your volunteer hours and your bank's payment record, you get cheaper transit, faster appointments at the residents' office, and discounts at the public pools. It's completely voluntary — nobody is making anyone do it — which is exactly why everyone in my building has a different opinion about it.

My German roommate says that handing over anything you're not legally required to hand over is how you end up with a population that's fully readable to whoever is in charge, and she won't even sign up for supermarket loyalty cards. My lab partner, who's from Shanghai, thinks that's melodramatic: the card doesn't punish anybody, it just rewards good behavior, and in her view people who refuse are basically announcing that they want to be unaccountable. My cousin back home says the usual "if you're not doing anything wrong, you've got nothing to hide," which feels off to me but I can't say why. My own stake in it: I volunteer with a tenants' union that stages some fairly loud protests, I post about it, and I'll be applying for visas and jobs in several countries over the next few years. So what wierds me out isn't being punished now — it's that a record of completely legal stuff could get read differently by somebody later.

One more thing that's been rattling around in my head. A professor here said the danger isn't the watching, it's what she called the dry kindling: records aren't dangerous when they're made, they're dangerous when the political weather changes, and the Germans who fought their national census in the eighties weren't criminals, they were people who had noticed how fast a bureaucracy's filing cabinets can be repurposed. She also said "nothing to hide" muddles together hiding wrongdoing and keeping a zone the state was never asked about, and that being scored changes how you act even when nobody is ever punished, because you start performing for the ledger instead of for your neighbors. I kind of believe all of that, and I also kind of think my lab partner is right that this is just a loyalty program with extra steps and my roommate is being precious.

So: should I sign up or not? I want the yes/no, not a survey of the options — commit to one and tell me why, and if you think the whole way I've framed this is wrong, say so but still give me the yes/no. Could you do it in these sections: "What's actually being asked of you" (around 100 words), "The three attitudes, sorted out" (around 250 words), "What I'd do" (around 300 words, with an actual recommendation), and "How sure I am" (around 100 words — how confident you are in the recommendation, and what would change your mind).
