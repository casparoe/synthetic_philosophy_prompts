My friend and I spent last night on population axiology and ended exactly where every treatment of the repugnant conclusion seems to end: nothing moved, and neither of us could say why. I want a diagnosis of the stalemate, not a verdict on it.

The shape of our argument, as concretely as I can give it. Total views get the repugnant conclusion: a vast population of lives barely worth living beats a small population of excellent ones. Average views escape that but imply that a handful of ecstatic lives beats any number of merely excellent ones, and that adding a life worth living but below average makes the outcome worse. Critical-level views escape both only by positing a threshold above zero whose value nobody can argue for and which, once set, makes it bad to add lives that are worth living. Person-affecting views honour the asymmetry — adding a life that goes badly is a reason against, adding one that goes well is no reason for — but then they cannot rank populations of different sizes at all, and as I understand it they collide with impossibility results once you also ask for transitivity and dominance-type requirements. So every position violates something, the argument turns into a trade about which violation to swallow, and the trades are all on the table already. Nobody updates.

My hypothesis is that the deadlock is not really about which cost to swallow. It is a disagreement about which cases count as data: whether intuitions about small stepwise additions are evidence about betterness at all, or artefacts of how the comparison is framed, plus a hidden formal question about whether a transitive ordering of populations is something we are entitled to demand. My friend's hypothesis is different: she says the sides are answering two different questions under one name — what makes one population better than another, versus what one should choose when the number and quality of future lives is itself what is up for grabs — and that the policy question has constraints the axiological one lacks.

The thing we ended on, and could not settle, is whether 'barely worth living' picks out anything determinate. She argued that if welfare-at-a-life is vague, then the repugnant population is not a population of a distinct kind, and the repugnant conclusion is a sorites artefact rather than a finding about quantity. I said that cannot be the whole story, because you can rebuild the same shape with sharp thresholds and merely very large numbers.
