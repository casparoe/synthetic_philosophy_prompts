I'm a junior at a large public high school, and I sit as the student member of our school's discipline board. Over the past year I've noticed a pattern in how we argue about who "caused" something bad, and I can't tell whether it is a real thing with a name in philosophy or just a habit of speech that our board has picked up. I'd like to know what it is, or, if it isn't anything established, what to call it and exactly where its edges are.

Three hearings, stripped down:

1. A teacher left a cabinet of exam papers unlocked overnight; four students photographed the paper. Everyone was comfortable saying the teacher's failure to lock the cabinet caused the leak. When another member noted that the same cabinet had been left unlocked twice before with no leak, and that a determined student could have got in anyway, nobody weakened the causal claim. Instead the description of what the teacher owed us got larger: he should have locked it, and also counted the copies, and also not left the exam in the room at all.
2. A hallway fight that a dozen people saw and nobody reported. Asked what caused the injury, we named the two fighters and then, vaguely, the people who walked past. Nobody would name any particular passer-by, but nobody would say that the passers-by had no causal role either. The claim stayed with the group without landing on any member of it.
3. A group project where five people each assumed someone else would email the teacher. Asked who caused the zero, we picked whichever member we were already annoyed with; asked what to change so it doesn't recur, we proposed a rotating reminder system and said nothing about that person's reliability.

What I think I'm seeing is this: a claim that someone's not-doing-something caused an outcome seems to run off whether we can point to whose job it was and how much we already want to blame them, and to be strangely insensitive to how much difference their acting would actually have made. When the difference-making is challenged, the obligation gets restated in a stronger form rather than the causal claim being withdrawn. And when no one was designated, the claim doesn't dissolve — it floats upward to a collective. I've been writing it in my notes as duty-tracking, but that name does no work I can't do in a sentence.

So, concretely: does this have an established name, or does it sit between two or three named things? I have come across the phrase causation by omission, but the cases I've seen discussed are about unwatered plants and promises to water them, and they don't seem to cover the part where the obligation stretches under pressure, or the part where the claim attaches to a group while refusing to attach to its members. Is that one phenomenon or two? Does the same thing happen with things people did rather than failed to do, and if not, what does that asymmetry tell us about what is going on?

Please don't just hand me a label. I want the carving: which nearby concepts this resembles, how it differs from each, which fits best, and one pair of hypothetical cases that would let me tell for myself whether I've found one thing or two. And I'd rather have your own analysis than a list of papers; I have no way to get at journals from here.

Two more things. First, tell me how confident you are in each part of your answer, and specifically which part rests on a contested claim about how ordinary speakers actually use the word cause rather than on something you can settle from the armchair. Second, tell me what would change your mind: if you think duty is doing the work here, what would you expect to hear people say if it weren't; if you think this collapses into something already named, what observation about our arguments would make you revise that; and if you think it's genuinely new, what would convince you it isn't.
