Hi — I'm writing a short essay, around 1500 words, on Aristotle on money and exchange, and I'd really like comments on the rough notes below before I commit to a full draft. I'm not after references or a reading list; I want to know whether the argument actually holds up and whether it's worth writing at all.

The notes:

Thesis: the usual way of reading him — money as medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value — more or less gets him backwards. In book 5 of the Nicomachean Ethics money gets introduced to solve a problem about proportionate return between people who are different and unequal, the builder and the shoemaker. What he actually says is that demand is the real standard, that money is a conventional stand-in for that demand, that it doesn't exist by nature and can be altered and made useless at will, and that it serves as a guarantee that exchange will be possible later when a need turns up. So money isn't a thing with functions, it's a standing relation of mutual pledge inside a group that can compare what it owes one another. Two consequences I want to press.

1. Something can tick all three textbook functions and still not be money by his own test. Take in-app currency, platform credit, or company scrip: the issuer is also the only seller, redemption runs only into the issuer's own goods, and the conversion rate can change whenever the issuer fancies. His remark that money exists by custom and can be rendered useless at will is normally read as a throwaway point about convention. I want to turn it into a diagnostic: the question isn't who issued the thing but who can unilaterally alter the measure, and where that power is one-sided the thing is a levy dressed up as a measure, because the demand it supposedly represents isn't a demand the parties share.

2. Paying with data isn't exchange at all. On his account the house and the shoes have to be made commensurable by a single measure before trade can happen, and exchange only holds a community together when there's reciprocal demand on both sides. Behavioural data has no proportion to the service you get for it, isn't produced by the person the way shoes are, and can't serve as a guarantee of anything. So the familiar complaint that the price is wrong or that it's surveillance understates the case: on his terms no exchange has occurred, and what the user recieves is closer to a share of a common stock or a grant than to a wage — he even has a case of his own along those lines, a city handing someone an export licence when it has the corn he needs but doesn't need his wine. If that's right the critique doesnt have to lean on fairness or privacy, which I think makes it stronger.

3. Where I'm shakiest: book 1 of the Politics is famously hostile to retail trade and to lending at interest, and I don't want the essay to collapse into Aristotle disliked money breeding money, since that's both boring and would make every modern financial asset non-money. I'd rather say the corruption is structural, an asymmetry in who can alter the measure, rather than motivational, what you want the money for. Would he accept that distinction, or am I smuggling a modern worry about power into a text that's really about proportion?

- Is claim 2 genuinely interesting, or only a fancy way of saying what people already say about platforms and data?
