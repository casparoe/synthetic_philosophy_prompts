I'm writing a paper for my normative ethics seminar, and the argument I've built against the possibility of genuine moral dilemmas now seems to me to run off the rails somewhere — but I can't say where.

The case: Ana promised her brother she would be at his wedding, and promised her sister she would drive her to a procedure that cannot be rescheduled. The wedding and the procedure are at the same hour in different cities, and no one else can cover either. Call the two options A and B.

(1) Ought implies can: if Ana cannot do X, then it is not the case that she ought to do X.
(2) Agglomeration: if she ought to do A and she ought to do B, then she ought to do (A and B).
(3) She cannot do (A and B).
(4) So by (1) it is not the case that she ought to do (A and B).
(5) So by (2) it is not the case both that she ought to do A and that she ought to do B. There is no dilemma here; there is only the question of which single act she ought to perform.

That much still looks fine to me. What bothers me is what I seem to have shown next.

(6) If there is exactly one thing she ought to do, then in doing it she has done everything she ought, so there is nothing left over for her to regret, no one she has wronged, and nothing for her to apologize for or to compensate.
(7) And the reasons on the losing side must be defeated outright rather than merely outweighed, since a merely outweighed reason would still be a reason, and then (2) would apply to reasons as well and we would be back in a dilemma.
(8) So there cannot be a tie either: if the reasons were exactly balanced, deliberation could only settle the matter arbitrarily, whereas (5) requires that there be a determinate answer she was in a position to get right.
(9) Hence there is no such thing as a distinctively tragic practical skill — no excellence at choosing well when something must be lost — because on this picture the only thing to get right is a single determinate fact.

So the argument does not merely eliminate dilemmas; it eliminates moral residue, hard choices, and dirty hands along with them. That is the part I cannot accept.

I would rather keep (1) and (3), and I would rather keep the datum that something is genuinely lost whichever way Ana acts, than keep the later steps. But each of (2), (7) and (8) looked as though it followed from what came before. Which step should I reject, why did it look safe, and what does each of the alternatives cost me?
