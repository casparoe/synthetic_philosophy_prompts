I am finishing the second chapter of a dissertation on distributive justice, and the chapter keeps helping itself to interpersonal comparisons of well-being that I have not earned. Before I commit to a comparison metric, I want the specification written out: what a theory of interpersonal comparisons would have to deliver in order to count as adequate for the use I am going to make of it.

The purpose, so that adequacy is relative to something: the metric has to function as the informational input to a distributive principle that a real allocator applies. Concretely, a fixed health budget divided among identified patients in a single year, where the allocator has clinical records, a limited amount of observed choice behaviour, and some self-report; does not have anyone's full preference ordering; will be second-guessed by patients who compare notes; and has to defend the allocation in public. And the metric has to be strong enough to adjudicate between sum-ranking and priority-to-the-worst-off rather than being reverse-engineered from one of them.

What I want from you is a numbered list of requirements, each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, each tagged as non-negotiable, negotiable, or a prejudice of one camp. Organise the list around the grades of comparability — comparisons of order only, of units, of levels including the placement of each person's zero, and of ratios — because what I most need to know is which grade each requirement forces and what it costs to get there. Anchor the requirements to cases, not to slogans. The cases I want handled, at a minimum:

1. Two patients in the same clinical condition, one of whom has recalibrated her expectations downward after years of chronic pain and now reports herself content, the other not.
2. The chain: a committee is confident comparing a mild migraine with a fractured wrist, a fractured wrist with moderate depression, moderate depression with blindness; composing the chain yields a migraine-to-blindness comparison that nobody will endorse.
3. An allocation that moves resources from one patient's final year to another patient's first year — the comparison of one person at two times must not be settled by a different procedure from the comparison of two people at one time.
4. The numeraire test: state everyone's position on patient A's scale, then on patient B's; do the orderings agree, and do the ratios?
5. The zero: where does each person's neutral point sit, and does locating it at a level below which life is not worth continuing smuggle a normative judgment in under a measurement label?
6. Strategic presentation: patients know they are being read and have reason to present as worse off than they are.
7. A decision that changes who exists, where one of the parties to the comparison exists only under one of the options.
8. Whether a benefit to a better-off patient becomes more urgent merely because worse-off patients exist.
9. A very large number of barely perceptible harms set against one severe harm: does the metric have the resolution to say anything here, and if it says nothing, is that a defect?
10. Public defensibility: the allocator has to be able to give each patient a reason, not just a number.

Here are the candidate requirements I have accumulated from reading and from arguments with my committee. Tell me which survive as genuine desiderata, which are negotiable, and which are artifacts of a camp:

- recoverability from observed choice alone, with self-report and clinical judgment treated as illegitimate;
- deference to each person's own testimony about how well off they are;
- completeness over every pair an allocator might face, versus the capacity to declare some pairs incomparable;
- separability of the interpersonal ordering from unaffected parties' positions, versus sensitivity to rank or to the shape of the distribution;
- invariance of all interpersonal comparisons under independent positive-affine rescaling of each person's scale;
- non-manipulability by strategic presentation;
- closure under chaining, so that comparability does not go sorites-fragile across adjacent pairs;
- a single unified treatment of intrapersonal and interpersonal comparison;
- extension to merely possible people;
- reason-expressibility, i.e. that every comparison the metric issues can be stated as a reason to the person who loses out.

Two suspicions I want tested rather than indulged. First, that full invariance under independent rescaling of each person's scale cannot coexist with level comparability, so that any priority-to-the-worst-off view is purchasing its comparisons with something that preference data alone does not supply; if that is right, say what is being imported and whether the import is acceptable. Second, that completeness, refusal to make anyone worse off merely because others are worse off, and transitivity cannot be had together; check this rather than take it from me, and if it fails only given some further assumption, isolate the assumption. More generally: if some subset of the requirements you end up endorsing is jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly, because the unsatisfiability is itself the result I care about most.

Then tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates, and which requirement you would drop from my list above and why.

Only after the criteria: a compact scorecard, a table is fine, rating against your final list the following, briefly and without discussing their provenance — strict ordinalism with no interpersonal comparisons at all; the impartial-observer approach on which one ranks outcomes by imagining oneself in each person's position under an equal chance of being them; partial comparability of the kind that stops short of full level comparisons; a resource or primary-goods proxy used in place of well-being; a fixed health-state tariff of the kind used in cost-effectiveness analysis; and the flat-footed realist claim that there is one true cardinal scale and the problem is merely epistemic. Keep the scorecard short; the criteria are where I want the effort. No literature review and no citations — I have those, and I need the specification, not the bibliography.
