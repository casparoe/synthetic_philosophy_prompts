I record a fortnightly interview podcast on philosophy of physics — one guest, long-form, and I try to get about forty minutes of actual questioning rather than exposition. My next guest is a philosopher of physics who has just published a book defending a position she calls nomological primitivism about the quantum state. Roughly, in her terms: there is no space of ontic states at all — no hidden-variable space for the quantum state to be a probability distribution over — there are only events, localized happenings in ordinary spacetime, together with one law-like object, the quantum state, which is neither a substance nor anyone's information but the rule that assigns probabilities to events. She offers this as a genuine third option, neither ψ-ontic nor ψ-epistemic in the Harrigan–Spekkens sense, because a law is supposedly neither a thing in the world nor a state of knowledge about one, and she treats the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem as a result about models that have a hidden-variable space and assume preparation independence — something her view, on her telling, was never in a position to violate. Audience context: technically literate but listening, not reading, so questions should be intelligible to someone who knows what a qubit is and has heard of Bell's theorem, and should avoid heavy notation. She has fielded PBRObjections a hundred times and is very good at two moves I want to close off: whenever someone says this is just ψ-ontism with the serial numbers filed off, she replies that the ψ-ontic/ψ-epistemic distinction is defined over hidden-variable models and her theory has no hidden variables, so the dilemma has no grip; and whenever someone presses on collapse or nonlocality, she says that laws don't act, they only govern.

What I want from you is eight or nine interview questions for her, arranged so that the first couple let her make her case on her own terms, the middle ones tighten, and the last third makes that first move progressively harder to reach for. For each question give me the line as I would actually say it out loud, then the reply she is most likely to give in a sentence or two, then a follow-up aimed specifically at that reply. The substance I want covered: (1) whether refusing a hidden-variable space is a real commitment or purely protective — in particular, her rule assigns probabilities using amplitudes rather than probabilities, and it assigns them over configurations, so ask in what sense the interference-bearing, configuration-space structure of the rule is merely representational when that structure is exactly what makes the rule predictively adequate, and what her view would have to give up if she conceded it is not; (2) the law/state mismatch — laws are normally general, time-independent and not system-relative, while quantum states evolve and belong to particular systems, so ask what exactly is the law and what is the state, and when she answers that the universal state is the law and the effective states of subsystems are derived by conditioning on their environment, press her on why those derived states don't look epistemic again, leaving her nomological at the cosmic scale and epistemic in the lab; (3) a concrete walk-through case — a single quantum system prepared and later detected in an interferometer with widely separated arms, and what her view says exists in between, which should set up the question of what determinate outcome the rule is a rule for; (4) Bell-type correlations: her rule assigns joint probabilities to spacelike-separated events that depend on settings chosen on both sides, so ask what she has gained over the ψ-ontic view that says the same thing while positing a non-separable physical state, and when she says that laws don't act at a distance but only govern holistically, press her for a difference, in explanatory or observational terms, between holism in a law and realism about a non-separable state; (5) what the view was supposed to buy — the old attraction of ψ-epistemicism was that if the state is information then collapse isn't a physical process and the measurement problem deflates, but she can't call it updating since it's no one's information, so ask what happens on measurement, and then where the Born probabilities and determinate outcomes are supposed to come from, i.e. whether she has relocated the measurement problem rather than solved it; and (6) ask her to state her criterion for what counts as real and then apply it herself to two or three test cases you pick (the electromagnetic potentials, the spacetime metric, an ordinary probability distribution) chosen so that the cases squeeze her criterion. End with one closing question about what evidence or theoretical development could count against the view. Please skip anything generic — no how she got into foundations, no inviting her to restate the view.

Finally, give me a short host's note, around 150 words, in which you commit to a verdict rather than surveying options: say which of your questions you think is the strongest and why, and say plainly whether you think nomological primitivism is a real third option or ψ-ontism in disguise — pick one, give your reason, and then name the single consideration most likely to make you change your mind, so I know where to press hardest if she beats the question I thought was my best.
