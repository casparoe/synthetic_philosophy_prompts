I'm putting together the take-home final for my MA seminar on the theory of conflict (they've read Schelling and some of the second-strike/credibility stuff) and I need one essay question on brinkmanship that a student who's just memorised "the rationality of irrationality" and "a threat that leaves something to chance" still cant answer.

Here's the wrinkle I want it built around: suppose one of the two states has pre-delegated its retaliatory strike to an automated system, no human in the loop, so that its second-strike threat is no longer something it can choose to carry out or refrain from. I want students to work out whether that solves or wrecks the commitment problem: is machine certainty a better deterrent than a leader who might be irrationally stubborn, can an automatic system "leave something to chance" at all (it could randomise, but is that the same thing as being the sort of agent who might not back down?), and does a perfectly credible tripwire at one threshold invite probing just below that threshold — the stability-instability worry in a new shape. Also make them handle the "nobody has used one in anger since 1945, so deterrence obviously works" move: the sample is censored, one failure is irreversible, so say what could even count as evidence here, without letting them lean on historical cases or dates.

Give me the question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme: five must-hit points, marks per point out of 20 total, and for each point the way students typically get it wrong.
