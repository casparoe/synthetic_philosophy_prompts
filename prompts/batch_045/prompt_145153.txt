A friend and I got stuck on Benacerraf's identification problem — he thinks the argument, taken seriously, proves way too much, and I cant find the step where it breaks. His version:

(1) General principle: if everything we require of the A's is captured by the structural role they play, and two different candidates both fill that role perfectly, and nothing about the role favors one over the other, then there is no fact of the matter which candidate the A's are — the A's aren't identical to either.
(2) For the naturals the role is the ω-sequence role: whatever satisfies the Dedekind–Peano axioms. Both the von Neumann progression (0 = ∅, 1 = {∅}, 2 = {∅,{∅}}) and the Zermelo one (0 = ∅, 1 = {∅}, 2 = {{∅}}) fill it, and nothing we actually do with arithmetic decides between them; they differ only on junk questions like whether 0 ∈ 2.
(3) So numbers aren't sets, and "is 0 a member of 2?" has no answer. I can live with that.
(4) But (1) has nothing special to do with numbers. Run it on ordered pairs: Kuratowski's ⟨a,b⟩ = {{a},{a,b}}, plus Wiener's and Hausdorff's definitions, all satisfy the only thing we require of pairs, and the pairing condition picks none of them. So ordered pairs aren't sets either, and "is a ∈ ⟨a,b⟩?" has no answer. Nobody believes that — apparently there's a principled reason to prefer Kuratowski (rank, uniformity, typing).
(5) And run it on sets themselves: any system with the right membership structure could be "the sets," so sets aren't sets. That's not a conclusion, that's a breakdown.
(6) So (1) is false, and then the identification argument gives us nothing and numbers might just be sets after all.

I'd much rather keep (3) than swallow (4) or (5), so where's the slide? My hunch is that "nothing about the role favors one" is doing different work in (2) and (4): for pairs the constraints that settle it seem to come from outside the theory of pairs — which sets exist, ranks, the reduction having to be carried out inside one background set theory — while in (2) we only allow constraints internal to arithmetic. But I don't see why those outside constraints don't apply in the number case too, and I also don't see why (5) isn't just another instance of the same move. Which step do you give up, and what does each option cost me?
