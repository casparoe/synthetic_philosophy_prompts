I'm auditing a graduate seminar on population ethics, and I keep circling back to a position I've started calling lexical threshold negative utilitarianism (LTNU). The rough statement is this: suffering matters, and it matters in a way that is not just a matter of degree; there is some threshold intensity of suffering such that below the threshold pleasures and pains may be traded off against one another in the ordinary aggregative way, while at or above the threshold the suffering takes strict priority over any quantity of pleasure, however large and however widely distributed. My seminar friends and I got stuck on the second half of the picture, the question of commensurability. It is very natural to suppose that pleasures and pains are two directions on a single cardinal scale of hedonic tone — that there is some unit, call it a hedon, in which both the intensity of a pleasure and the intensity of a pain can be measured, and that the entire asymmetry between them is captured by the sign of the quantity plus the lexical rule that kicks in at the threshold. But that combination strikes me as unstable, and I would like to see the instability spelled out.

Please argue for the following thesis: once LTNU grants that pleasures and pains are commensurable on a single cardinal hedonic scale below the threshold, it cannot coherently withhold that commensurability above the threshold; the lexical rule does real normative work only if severe suffering carries some further, non-hedonic property that pleasure lacks — an urgency, a demand not to be produced, a side-constraint-like status — and once that further property is admitted, LTNU is no longer a form of utilitarianism at all but a hybrid of aggregative consequentialism with a constraint, and it inherits the familiar pathologies of lexical or infinite weights rather than the elegance of a scalar theory. As you develop the argument, please say precisely what the single-scale assumption commits you to (cardinality, interpersonal comparability, unboundedness of the scale in the positive direction, and the treatment of duration and number of experiencers as multipliers on intensity), and show which of those commitments the lexical threshold is in tension with.

Two objections I want you to answer head-on, since they are the ones my friends pressed. First, the objection from ordinary lexical orderings: it is perfectly coherent to order goods lexically even when they are comparable on a common measure — one can compare two candidates on a shared dimension and still let a second dimension break ties or dominate, as in familiar lexical-priority structures in political philosophy and in lexicographic preference orderings in decision theory — so lexical priority per se does not entail incommensurability, and the thesis overreaches. Second, what I'll call the saturation reply: perhaps above the threshold the hedonic intensity scale is not linear but compressive or saturating, so that no finite sum of sub-threshold pleasures can ever reach the region where the severe pain sits; on this picture the lexical appearance is explained entirely within a single, merely curved scale, with no appeal to any extra normative property, and the thesis is simply false. Please say why each reply does or does not save the view, and be explicit about what distinguishes a merely steep or curved trade-off function from a genuinely lexical one.

Finally, please address the decision-theoretic wrinkle, because it is where I suspect the argument actually bites. Under uncertainty about where exactly the threshold lies, or about the hedonic intensity of some experience one might bring about, an expected-value maximizer who accepts a lexical structure seems to face a domination or fanaticism problem: an arbitrarily small probability of crossing the threshold appears to swamp arbitrarily large certain quantities of sub-threshold well-being, and choices among options that all involve some threshold-crossing risk become difficult to rank at all. Argue whether this wrinkle supports the thesis or undercuts it, and whether the friendliest version of LTNU should respond by becoming a view about risk, about permissions rather than overall value, or about the structure of reasons rather than about betterness. I'm less interested in a survey of what has been said on these questions than in seeing one line of argument pushed as far as it will go, so please commit to the thesis and defend it rather than hedging between the options.
