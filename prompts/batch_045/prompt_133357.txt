Some background: I'm a hardware engineer, not a philosopher. I got into this after a genuinely annoying argument at work about whether we "knew" a batch of parts was within spec, and I ended up reading a long-ish popular blog post on all this by someone who clearly knows the material and is good at explaining it. Here's roughly what it said. Knowledge, it claimed, used to be understood as true belief plus good evidence, so whether you know depends only on your reasons and nothing else. Then along came epistemic contextualism, which points out that "knows" behaves like words such as "flat" or "tall": the standards it sets shift with the context of the conversation, so I can truly say on Friday that I know the bank will be open on Saturday, and yet later that day, when missing a mortgage payment is on the line, that very claim about me is false. Pragmatic encroachment, the post went on, is just the same idea in different clothes: how much is at stake for the person determines whether they know, so knowledge is partly a practical matter rather than a purely evidential one. The invariantists — the people who hold that the standards for knowledge never shift — are therefore committed to saying that evidence is the only thing that matters, and they're stuck denying the obvious point that you need stronger evidence when more is riding on being right. Both views share an unacceptable consequence, though: if knowledge depends on stakes, then a stranger who secretly places a huge bet on whether your belief is true can destroy your knowledge without your ever finding out, and since that's obviously absurd, the whole family of views is refuted.

I'm inclined to think the stakes change what you should *do*, not what you know — but I definately can't tell whether that's a real position somebody defends or just me being stubborn. And here's the case that's actually bugging me. Last Friday I ran a torque check on a bracket and logged it as within spec; my evidence was good but not perfect, the kind of check that's plenty for an ordinary job. Saturday morning, without telling me, my manager made that single check the only remaining gate before a forty-million-dollar satellite shipment goes out the door. On Monday the client postponed the launch indefinitely and the stakes evaporated again. So: did I know on Friday? Did I stop knowing on Saturday even though nothing about me, my evidence, or the bracket changed? If I did stop, did I start knowing again on Monday, or is knowledge not the sort of thing that can flicker like that? Does it make any difference that the stakes were really the client's and my employer's rather than mine — someone at work muttered that that isn't pragmatic encroachment at all but something called moral encroachment, and I have no idea whether that's right or whether it would change the answer? And one more thing: is the secret-bet objection actually a serious problem for these views, or is there a standard reply the post just skipped?
