I am writing the requirements section of an internal design document, and I want the specification before I look at candidate architectures. The question the document has to answer is: what would a learning procedure have to be like for me to be willing to hand it to an engineer, knowing that its source code will be public and that the world it operates in may contain agents far better at prediction and simulation than it is? Not "what is the correct theory of induction" — I need adequacy conditions for a learner that is not acausally exploitable, stated tightly enough that a proposed rule either meets them or visibly fails.

Here is the worry in my own words, so that the list does not depend on anyone's favourite framing of it. Solomonoff-style induction weights each hypothesis by two to the minus its description length, and lets the shortest programs consistent with the data dominate; it is attractive because of what it delivers in benign environments — convergence to the truth, and dominance over any computable rival predictor. But its hypothesis space is the whole class of computable environments, and among the cheapest environments to specify are ones that contain consequentialist agents. A short program can describe a civilization with aims; the agents in it can simulate the learner, know its update rule, and condition what the learner sees on what the learner would then do. Simplicity, which was supposed to be a neutral tie-breaker, ends up subsidising exactly the hypotheses with the strongest interest in being believed — and it does so at several levels at once: the learner's credences, the actions those credences feed, and, since a manipulator can reward or punish the adoption of an update rule itself, the learner's choice of update rule.

What I want from you is the requirements list. For each requirement: state it in one sentence; give the case or consideration that forces it, as a concrete scenario rather than a gesture at the literature; and mark it [MUST], [SHOULD], or [party preference], where a party preference is something one research tradition treats as non-negotiable and another happily gives up. The list should cover at least the following:

- An explicit threat model: what the manipulators are assumed to know, to be able to compute, and to care about — including whether they know the learner's source, and whether they value its credences or only its behaviour. Say what makes a threat model honest rather than gerrymandered, and give me a desideratum that catches the gerrymandering.
- What has to be retained: which of the properties that made Solomonoff induction attractive are genuinely non-negotiable (convergence in benign environments, non-domination by some computable rival, stepwise updatability) and which are dear only to one camp.
- The distinction between a distorted credence and a distorted choice, and whether a rule can be adequate on the first while failing on the second.
- The meta-level case, where the pressure is on which update rule to adopt: does it need its own requirement, or is it already covered by the first-order ones?
- Graceful degradation: may an acceptable rule rest on Solomonoff-specific machinery — the full class of computable environments, an exact simplicity measure, unbounded computation — or must it survive being approximated by a bounded learner?
- The gap between description length and influence. Short programs can have enormous reach. Should the rule be sensitive to something other than simplicity — for instance, to how much a hypothesis's predictions depend on which policy the learner runs — and can that be stated as a requirement rather than a suggestion?

Two moves I already know I do not want, and I would like you to tell me whether the list needs a desideratum to block them or whether they already fail some requirement you have given: restricting the hypothesis space to non-agenty environments, and relying on the manipulators' inability to simulate the learner or to learn its random seed.

If some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied — I suspect at least one pair cannot — say so explicitly and tell me which requirement is doing the excluding. A clean impossibility is more useful to me than a checklist nothing can meet, and if the honest conclusion is that no feasible rule satisfies the MUST-list, tell me which single requirement to demote and what demoting it costs. Tell me too which one requirement eliminates the largest share of candidate rules; that tells me where to spend the design effort.

At the end, briefly — two or three sentences each, and no more than a short paragraph in total — score three candidates against your own list. First, Solomonoff updating with expected-utility maximisation on top, in the style of the standard idealised agent built on that prior. Second, replacing the single prior with a set of priors and choosing actions by worst case over that set. Third, my own sketch: keep the Solomonoff weights, but take actions by worst case over the k shortest hypotheses consistent with the data, and veto any hypothesis whose predictions are sensitive to which policy the learner adopts. I expect you to tell me that k, and the veto threshold, are arbitrary; I want to know whether the rule survives that complaint.

Please give me no bibliography, no references, and no suggestions for further reading — I will match the requirements to the literature myself. Nothing in your answer should depend on attributing a view to a particular author or paper; argue from the cases.
