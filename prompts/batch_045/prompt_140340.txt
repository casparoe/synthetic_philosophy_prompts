You are a philosopher of mind who has spent your career on questions about what it takes for something to have a view of the world, and about what we are doing when we use words like "she," "believes," and "was deceived" of a thing with no single mouth and no single brain. I am a novelist, and I need you to referee an argument between two characters before I can write the scene honestly.

The narrator keeps a colony of an ant I have invented for the book, which she calls the ledger ant. The details matter, so here they are. The colony is about forty thousand workers and one queen, though the queen turns out to be irrelevant to everything that follows. The nest is a flat carton slab with a central chamber and radiating galleries. A caste of very small workers does nothing at all except walk the galleries. Each one carries two glands: as she walks she lays down a long-lived waxy compound at a steady rate, and whenever her antennae touch another ant she secretes a solvent that dissolves a little of that compound wherever she happens to be. The consequence is that the waxy residue on the walls of the central chamber settles at a level that tracks how many living bodies are in the nest and where they are gathered: many ants means many contacts means more dissolving means a thin residue. The walls therefore carry something like a running tally of how well-staffed the colony is, and it lags reality by several hours.

No single ant ever checks the whole slab. Downstream, two things respond to the local thickness of the residue. Nurses near the brood read it and switch larvae onto or off the forager diet. Foragers at the nest mouth read it and set their range and their caution: when the residue is thick they stay close to home and travel in groups; when it is thin they scatter and go far. So the state of the walls has one content and two quite different uses, and no part of the colony contains or inspects it. The small workers who maintain it live about six weeks and are replaced continuously; the slab itself lasts for more than a decade. My narrator has kept hers for six years, during which the entire workforce has been replaced several times over while the tally and the behaviour tied to it have stayed exactly as they were.

Her difficulty is this. An old friend, a behavioural ecologist, proposes to paint a synthetic version of the waxy compound onto the central chamber for ten days. During those ten days the colony's reading will say, falsely, that it is well-staffed and unharmed, while in fact a drought has cut its food supply and it is losing foragers fast. He expects the colony to under-react and to lose some brood, which he will make good afterwards by feeding it; he has run the procedure on colonies in his laboratory and none have died. His reason for wanting to do it is not cruelty but method: holding the tally at a value that contradicts the facts is, he says, the only way to find out whether the state of the walls actually drives the nurses and the foragers or is merely a by-product of them.

She refuses. Her friend says: there is no her. There are forty thousand insects and a smell on a wall. A smell is not an opinion, it is a setting, and a setting cannot be lied to; the worst he can do is damage a machine that belongs to nobody. She answers that the colony would be relying on its own reading of itself, that he would be exploiting that reliance, and that she would object even if the experiment were completely harmless — even if he held the reading falsely for ten seconds and put it back — because deceiving someone is a wrong to the person deceived, whoever is or is not "home" in there. She cannot say who would be deceived. No individual ant would be. The queen would not be. When two colonies of this species are merged, the two tallies settle to a single intermediate value within a day and the fused colony then behaves as though it had always held that one estimate, which she thinks helps her and he thinks helps him.

So: should she let him do it, and is her refusal anything more than the sentiment of a woman who has spent six years talking to an insect? What would have to be true for her to be right? I am especially interested in whether the fact that the tally can be written from outside tells us anything at all — it occurs to me that one could do something similar to a single animal, and I want to know whether that parallel is real or a trick of the imagination — and in whether persistence through the complete replacement of every part, and the fusion of two colonies, cut any ice in either direction.

Please note what you think my narrator is most likely conflating: the years, the name she has given the colony, the fact that she talks to it. Say plainly if her instinct is sound but her reasons for it are bad, or if his argument is stronger than it sounds and hers is sentiment dressed up.
