I've been arguing for a few years now — in seminars, in a paper that keeps getting desk-rejected, and increasingly in conversation with people who work on this rather than just teach it — that the question "which animals are conscious?" is not a well-posed empirical question with a discoverable answer, and that the field's habit of treating consciousness as the sort of thing that switches on at some point in phylogeny is a leftover from a bad picture.

The view I've ended up with (I've been calling it dimensionalism, mostly for lack of a better word) is that what we bundle together as consciousness in animals is a set of capacities that can in principle come apart: something like nociception-with-valence, something like binding features of a scene into a single unified field, something like the capacity to monitor one's own uncertainty and act on it, something like flexible revaluation of previously learned values, and so on. On this view "is a lobster conscious?" has no answer, and "is a lobster conscious in respect of dimension D?" does. The thing that pushed me there wasn't armchair: it's that every marker anyone proposes seems to dissociate from every other one somewhere in the tree of life, and the dissociations don't line up with any single neural story I know how to tell.

I want someone to try to break it. Specifically:

What would count against it? The one candidate I take most seriously myself is that the dissociations turn out to be noise — that if you actually ran the battery across a decent spread of species, performance on all the markers loads onto a single general factor, and there really is one capacity that rises and falls together and tracks something neurally identifiable. That would hurt. What else is there? And please don't bother with citations or a reading list, I can find those; it's the structure of the exposure I'm after.

Which of these would a committed dimensionalist concede as a defeater, and which would we simply absorb? That's what worries me most, actually. My view has an annoying feature: any apparent dissociation can be filed as "ah, another dimension," which makes it look unfalsifiable. Is it? Has it been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, is that a fatal problem for a claim of this shape, or is it just what framework-level claims look like?

There's also something I don't think I've seen handled: even granting degrees, degrees need a scale and a scale needs a unit. What's the unit for comparing, say, a corvid with a cephalopod, when they share almost none of the relevant machinery? If there is no answer there, does that count against degrees of consciousness or only against my ambition for the view?

And one more, which I'd personally find most persuasive of anything: I'd genuinely move if someone showed me that the view eats its own motivation. Almost everyone who cares about this cares because they want to say something about how we treat animals — law, farming, research policy, all of which want yes/no verdicts. If dimensionalism makes those applications impossible, or collapses into either trivial panpsychism ("everything is a bit conscious") or into a skepticism that no one working on welfare could use, that's a cost I'd weigh heavily.

So: rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a defender would concede and which we'd dismiss, tell me whether the view predicts anything a threshold view doesn't (I suspect it does, but I'm not sure I like what), and flag any that have arguably already partly happened. Its fine to tell me the view is better insulated than I think — I'd just want to know how much of that insulation is principled and how much is me moving the goalposts.
