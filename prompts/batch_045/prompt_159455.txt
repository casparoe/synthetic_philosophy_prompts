I sit on the eleven-member programme committee of a national research council. At Friday's session we allocate the last uncommitted block of a five-year budget, and two applications are competing for it. I have been asked to draft the recommendation memo the committee will vote on, and I would like you to write it.

The first application is a resubmission: a five-year programme grant from a group (call them the Halvorsen group) working on the neurobiology of memory. The field's dominant view is that a memory is stored as altered synaptic weights within an identifiable neuronal ensemble, and that the standard demonstration of this is to identify the ensemble, measure its reactivation, and show that manipulating it alters recall. The Halvorsen group denies that memories are stored in any substrate at all: on their view a memory is a stable pattern of an animal's structured engagement with its environment, and synapses are relays, not records. Four years ago we rejected them 7–4, with the minute recording that "proposals must be assessable against current standards of evidence in their field." Two of this round's four external reviews repeat that objection almost verbatim. The fourth reviewer — the only one who has published in both vocabularies — says it is the most interesting application she has read in ten years and that the field's inability to adjudicate it is not the same thing as its being weak.

The dispute turns on a published result the applicants consider an anomaly: animals trained on a two-stage task, given a manipulation that by the standard assay abolishes ensemble reactivation, perform at chance on the standard test but recover to pre-manipulation levels within a few trials when returned to the original apparatus with its original distal cues, and then degrade again when a single cue is altered. The mainstream reads this as context-dependent retrieval plus savings from spared traces — a puzzle the standard model already accommodates. The applicants read it as something the standard model should not have predicted. The disagreement bottoms out in what the manipulation is taken to have done, and each side can only describe that in its own theoretical vocabulary.

Two further facts matter. The applicants decline to pre-state their hypotheses in the field's measurement vocabulary, on the grounds that those quantities presuppose what they deny; instead they propose an "interface experiment," jointly designed and pre-registered with the mainstream group, whose outcome they say both camps can agree in advance would count against them. The mainstream PI has agreed in writing to co-design it but not to accept its outcome as decisive for his own theory, since he thinks their manipulation also affects attention and arousal. And funding the Halvorsen application at full scale means declining a renewal for the mainstream group, which produces steady, reliable, unsurprising work; funding at reduced scale for two years is also possible, as is deferral.

Write the memo we could actually adopt: start with a single "Recommendation" line stating the motion, then the reasons, then the conditions and dates, then an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side and why it does not carry. Two things I specifically want handled rather than gestured at. First, I do not want the memo resting on the thought that paradigms sometimes shift and are later vindicated: we have to decide ex ante, we have no track record of picking winners, and treating "unassessable by current standards" as a mark of profundity is its own failure mode. Second, the memo has to say what we would need to see at a 24-month review to conclude the money was well spent even if the applicants' interpretation of their results is wrong, and, symmetrically, what would show it was wasted even if they are right — criteria that do not themselves presuppose either paradigm. No citations or bibliography; reason from the case as described. Maximum 700 words.
