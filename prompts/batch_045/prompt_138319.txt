I want an independent second opinion on one of them before I finalise the marks. I have deliberately not recorded any view of my own below, because I do not want to anchor you: assume the marker's stance, whatever it is, is not available to you.

The question, as set, was this.

**Question 4 (25 marks). Answer all parts.**

An eccentric billionaire places before you a vial of toxin. Drinking it will make you painfully ill for a day, but it will not threaten your life and will have no lasting effects. The billionaire will pay you one million dollars tomorrow morning if, at the stroke of midnight tonight, you intend to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon. Assume that what triggers the payment is a reliable device that reads, at midnight, the intention you actually have at that moment: it forecasts nothing, and the reading of that device is the sole trigger of the transfer. The billionaire stipulates that you need not drink; the money will be in your account hours before the time for drinking arrives, and you are free to change your mind.

**(a)** Set out, as numbered premises, the argument for the conclusion that you cannot form the intention to drink the toxin. State whether its conclusion is best read as a claim about psychological possibility, about rationality, or about reasons, and say what turns on the difference. *(7 marks)*

**(b)** Call the thesis that one can intend to φ only if one believes that one will φ the Belief Constraint. Suppose the Belief Constraint is true. Someone argues that the puzzle is thereby settled: you cannot intend, because you know you will not drink, and since the interesting question was supposed to be what it is rational to do, what remains is a decision-theoretic question of the Newcomb sort, about whether to perform the mental act that the machine rewards. Assess this line of thought: does accepting the Belief Constraint settle the puzzle, merely relocate it, or leave it where it was? *(8 marks)*

**(c) Variant: the counterfactual scanner.** Everything is as before, except that the billionaire now pays if and only if, at midnight, it is true of you that you would drink the toxin the following afternoon, were the money already in your account and the vial in front of you. (i) Does this variant make the agent's task easier? (ii) An objector says: in this variant you can secure the money by swallowing, tonight, a pill that will make you violently ill all morning unless you drink at noon; and since that pill was equally available in the original version, the original puzzle was never about intending at all, only about rearranging your own future incentives. Assess the objection. *(6 marks)*

**(d)** The puzzle grew out of reflection on nuclear deterrence: can a state intend to retaliate when, once deterrence has failed, retaliating would serve no further purpose? Say whether the toxin case is a good model of that question, and identify one respect in which the analogy may mislead. *(4 marks)*

Here is the student's answer, transcribed without correction.

**(a)** The argument is: (1) By Monday afternoon the million is already in the bank, and drinking then adds a day of pain while gaining nothing. (2) So on Monday afternoon I will have no reason to drink, and being rational I will not drink. (3) I know this about myself now, on Sunday night. (4) Intending to do something requires believing that one will do it. (5) So I cannot believe that I will drink, and therefore I cannot intend to drink.

Two readings of the conclusion should be kept apart. On the psychological reading, the conclusion is that no agent in my situation could form the intention at all; on the rational reading, it is only that forming it would be irrational. The argument as I have given it supports the psychological reading, because premise (4) is a conceptual claim about what intending is, not a normative claim about what it is sensible to do. The rational reading is a separate question, and Kavka's own interest is in the way reasons to intend and reasons to act come apart: I have an excellent reason to intend to drink, namely the million, but no reason at all to drink. Since the argument shows the intention is psychologically unavailable to me, there is no reason for me to try to form it, and the sensible thing is simply not to bother.

**(b)** If the Belief Constraint is true, then the puzzle is settled, and settled in a way that reveals what kind of puzzle it always was. It is Newcomb's problem with a mental state substituted for the boxes. In both cases the payoff is fixed by a mechanism that anticipates what is in the agent's head and does its work independently of anything the agent later does; in both cases the agent must decide whether to produce the state the mechanism rewards. So the question reduces to the familiar dispute between causal and evidential decision theory. I take the causal theory to be correct, and it gives the answer that the agent cannot collect: no mental act of his at the moment of choice can affect what the mechanism has already registered, so aiming at the intention is pointless. It is worth adding that the constraint is a fairly modest requirement — all it really asks is that you not regard it as settled that you will fail — and that is exactly what I cannot do here, since I know I will not drink.

**(c)** (i) The counterfactual variant is easier. The agent no longer needs to be in any particular mental state at midnight; he needs only that a certain counterfactual about himself be true, and one can act so as to make counterfactuals about oneself true by changing one's situation rather than one's mind.

(ii) The objector is right, and the point generalises. Swallow the pill tonight and it becomes true that you would drink at noon. The same pill was available in the original version, and there it would have produced the intention as well, because once you have taken it you have a perfectly good reason to drink. So the original puzzle was never really about intending; it was about whether you can rig your own future incentives. Nothing in it turns on the concept of intention, and the argument in part (a) was unsound all along, since the pill shows that the intention was available to me after all.

**(d)** The case is a good model of the deterrence question, and the parallel is exact. Once deterrence has failed and the cities have been hit, a retaliatory strike cannot undo the damage and can only invite a second strike, so the state, like the agent with the vial, will have no reason to do the very thing it must intend to do. If the intention cannot be formed, then the deterrent threat is a bluff, and the entire practice of nuclear deterrence rests on a threat that no rational state can actually mean. The only escape is collective self-deception about what the state would do.
