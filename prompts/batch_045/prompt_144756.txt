Hey — I'm running a half-day workshop next month for our press office and the comms leads, and the thing everyone keeps dancing around is our official line that "we never lie to the public." About half the room reads that as: fine, so as long as every sentence I utter is literally true, whatever people walk away believing is on them. I want to break that open with a diagnostic quiz rather than a lecture, so I need you to build me the instrument.

Here's what I need:

Ten questions. Every one a concrete case from this job, and please draw from at least these: the non-denial denial; "no comment" issued in a tone everyone reads as confirmation; answering the question you wish you'd been asked; a true statistic picked because of the frame it implies; dropping a decision at 6pm on the Friday before a holiday; briefing one friendly reporter knowing she'll print the thing you're not allowed to say; using a technical term like "no confirmed cases" that your team and the public read two different ways; answering in language or jargon you know the audience won't parse; letting a reporter's wrong story run and correcting it only after publication; and one case where you tell the plain truth and aren't believed because your office has cried wolf too often. Also throw in the classic doorstep case — someone comes to your house asking where your friend is, and you're pretty sure they mean to kill him — as a calibration question, because it separates people who think context never matters from people who think it always can.

Hard constraint: no question stem may use the words lie, lying, liar, deceive, deception, mislead, or misleading, and neither may the answer options. Otherwise people just sort themselves by which label they like. Options should be things like "totally fine," "a bit off but no big deal," "just as bad as saying the false thing outright," "worse than that."

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of isms: first the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space. Two branch nodes I insist on, because they're the ones my team never thinks about: (1) who supplies the false content — does the speaker put the false claim into the world, or does the hearer assemble the false belief themselves out of true materials the speaker arranged; and (2) traceability — whether afterwards there's a false sentence in the record the hearer can throw back at you, or only an impression you can disown. Argue in the tree about whether traceability makes engineered literal truth better (the record stays clean) or worse (you keep deniability and the hearer is made to co-author their own mistake).

Seven leaves, named and one-sentence-each on their most awkward commitment: strong assertionism (only asserting what you believe false is intrinsically wrong, everything else is just ordinary disclosure duties); the assurance-and-role view (saying "we never lie" already promised more than literal truth); the no-difference view (mechanism is irrelevant, both manipulate the hearer's reasoning); the nothing-is-even-pro-tanto-wrong view; the view that deliberately engineered literal truth can be worse than an outright false statement; practice-relative conventionalism (the local rules of the activity draw the line and there's no deeper fact); and the eliminativist option that the lying/misleading distinction is a bad proxy and the tree should be rebuilt around something else — breach of assurance, manipulation, whatever you think.

Then: a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the leaves, with a "your pattern matches no leaf" outcome; flag any pair of leaves your questions genuinely can't separate instead of pretending they can; and for each empty combination say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just undefended — and please call out specifically any cell that's empty in theory but is more or less our actual house style, since that's the bit the workshop has to land.

Assume the room has had no philosophy, so define any term of art in-line as you go.
