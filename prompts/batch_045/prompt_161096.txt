You are a professional philosopher who works on the philosophy of mind and the emotions, and who is unusually good at reverse-engineering the implicit rule behind somebody's run of verdicts on cases. I need your help before a seminar.

My supervisor is running a seminar series on the question of what emotions are, and I have tabulated every verdict she has given so far, because next week she is ruling on a new case and I want to walk in having already worked out the rule she is running, the other rules that fit her track record just as well, and one further case that would force her to choose between them. Please work offline: no bibliography, no reading list, no citations, no names of papers — I want your own analysis in your own words. She uses a three-valued scale — "emotion", "not an emotion", and "emotion, but only in a derivative or attenuated sense" — and here is her record, with the terse reasons she gave: being startled by a car backfiring, not an emotion ("a reflex; nothing is taken to matter"); hunger pangs and the urge to eat, not an emotion ("a drive, not a take on what matters"); the itch and irritation under a plaster cast, not an emotion; pain, not an emotion ("it hurts and you want it to stop, but nothing is presented as mattering"); déjà vu, not an emotion; the uncanny sense that someone is standing behind you in an empty house, not an emotion ("a seeming about what is there, not about what matters"); the bodily rush and exhilaration after a winter swim, not an emotion ("all feeling, no object"); the mounting sense that you are about to solve a crossword clue, not an emotion ("it is about whether you will be right, not about what matters"); a standing conviction, held for years and acted on daily, that smoking is foolhardy, not an emotion ("not an episode, just something you think"); free-floating anxiety with no identifiable object, emotion; an irritable mood that lasts a whole morning, emotion ("it still colours how everything strikes you"); grief at the death of a fictional character, emotion; schadenfreude at a stranger's small mishap, emotion; amusement at a pun, emotion; awe at a night sky, emotion; moral outrage at an injustice read about in a distant newspaper, emotion; disgust at the smell of soured milk, emotion; curiosity about how a lock works, emotion ("something is presented as worth finding out"); fear of a spider you fully and sincerely judge to be harmless, emotion ("what counts is how it strikes you, not what you believe" — and she adds in a footnote that this is why she rejects the judgement theory, on which emotions are evaluative judgements or beliefs); and being moved by a piece of wordless music, emotion but only derivatively, "because there is no object whose welfare is at stake".

Next week's case is this. Mr H. is a retired actuary with a hypothetical condition I will call interoceptive silence: from birth he has received no interoceptive signal from his body at all — no racing heart, no churning stomach, no flush, no lump in the throat, no urge to cry or flee — and he reports never having felt anything of that kind, while his voice, face and posture are entirely ordinary and observers read him without difficulty. What he does report is that situations strike him. An offhand remark from his brother strikes him as an insult and keeps him writing letters about it for weeks; a loose handrail strikes him as dangerous and he takes the other staircase; a colleague's small favour strikes him as generous and he sends a note of thanks. He quits committees, avoids streets, and says of a neighbour's harmless terrier, "I know it cannot hurt me, but it still strikes me as dangerous." In the laboratory, however, when masked images of spiders are flashed below the threshold of awareness, his pupils dilate, his skin conductance jumps, and afterwards he chooses the other room — while reporting no experience of anything striking him as dangerous and no bodily feeling whatsoever.

What I want, thinking carefully and in this order: first, brainstorm the candidate rules — every rule you can construct that is consistent with the whole of her track record, stated in canonical "a state is an emotion if and only if…" form, each one checked against every precedent above and not merely against the easy paradigms; then narrow to the two or three that genuinely fit and tell me which one you think she is running and why, weighting the phrasing of her reasons as evidence. Then predict her verdict, on her three-valued scale, on Mr H.'s everyday states and separately on the masked-spider episode, saying for each which precedent you take to be the closest analogue and whether the rules disagree about which emotion he is in (anger, fear, gratitude) as opposed to merely about whether he is in one at all. If her precedents really do not settle it, say so plainly and say what principle she would be committing herself to with each choice. Finally, give me the single further case you would put to her in the following session — described concretely enough to be run — that best separates the surviving rules, with each rule's prediction recorded before the fact. Format your answer as four numbered sections matching those four requests.
