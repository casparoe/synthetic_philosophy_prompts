You are a philosopher of science who has spent most of your career working alongside field archaeologists, and who has also sat through a fair number of heritage-permit hearings, so you know how these decisions actually get made. Please think carefully about this one and reason it out before you write: I'd like you to start with two or three paragraphs of brainstorming where you say what you take the real problem to be, flag anywhere you think I've framed it badly, and note what you'd need to know that I haven't told you. Then give me the memo itself, and keep the memo separate from the brainstorming.

Some background. I've ben on this county's historic preservation commision for six years, five voting members, I'm the one who is not a professional anything (retired civil engineer, I do roads and drainage). We have to vote in three weeks on an application to build 42 houses on a low ridge above the river. Its a pre-Roman farming landscape, no writing from the people who lived there and nothing much from anybody else about them, so everything anybody says about the site has to come out of the ground.

Our ordinance is what makes this hard. If a site shows evidence of ceremony or of institutional practice, we can require the applicant to either redesign around it or pay for a full excavation first. If it's just a domestic site, the only thing required is what they call record-and-release: an archaeologist watches the machine strip the topsoil for a week and writes up whatever shows up, and the houses go in. So the entire decision rides on how much you can get out of some pits.

Here's what the trial trenching in April found. There are eight pits in a rough arc, plus one small square building, about four metres across, set off to one side. In the pits: three pots that were nearly complete but had been broken before they went in, all the pieces present and refittable; animal bone that is very mostly skulls and right forelimbs; two quern stones, one sitting upside down at the bottom of a pit and one smashed with the upper stone lying on the lower; a dog buried on its side in one pit with a small heap of burnt grain beside it; very little charcoal. No human burials, no figurines, no imported material, nothing that looks like an altar or a temple.

The developer's consultant says in his report that "the assemblage is consistent with the disposal of domestic refuse" and that none of it is strange: pots break all the time, the skull-and-limb pattern is what you get when dogs drag carcasses around and the small bones wash out, the dog is a pet burial, the burnt grain is a hearth cleaned out. He recommends no further work. Our county archaeologist says the opposite, that these are what he calls "structured deposits" (his phrase, or whatever the term of art is now, meaning deliberately placed rather than thrown away), that the repetition and the selection of parts and the inverted quern are a grammar you don't get from a midden, and that this is a small ceremonial site.

Two things have come up that I did not expect and that I think are the actual crux. First, there's a descendant-community group with formal consulting status with us, and they say the ridge is a place their tradition describes as where things were given back to the ground, and that the objects confirm it. But one of our staff, a grad student, pointed out that the tradition was first written down in 1974, which was after an amateur society dug part of the same ridge, and that the members of that society were from the same community and their notes argued the site was a temple. So the tradition and the objects may not be independent of each other at all. One of our commissioners says that destroys the corroboration; the group's representative says the tradition is far older than any excavation and 1974 is just when somebody wrote it down.

Second, and this is the one I keep going around on: our ordinance says institutional practice. One of our commissioners, an economist, says that even if the county archaeologist is right about ritual, one household doing something odd is not an institution, and that unless we can point to a repeated pattern across more than one site we have no business calling it one. The county archaeologist says there has been only one partial dig on comparable ground, about twenty miles away, never properly published, and that he'd rather not lean on it anyway.

I should also say the economist's wider worry, which I half share: that "ritual" is the category everything falls into when we can't explain it otherwise, that no possible find could ever get rejected as non-ritual once you're in that frame, and that five unelected people would then be able to stop housing on the basis of an interpretation nobody outside the room can audit.

What I want from you is the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. About 900 words, addressed to the commission, and it needs to do four things.

One, give us a bottom line: approve as applied for, approve with conditions, require redesign, or continue for more information. If it's conditions or more information, say exactly what happens, who does it, what would count as a stop and what would count as a go, and by what date — I need something I can put in a motion, not a general recommendation to be careful.

Two, tell us what would actually count as evidence that these things were put there as part of a practice with a shared meaning, as opposed to being one household's rubbish, and — this is the part I expect you'll be useful on — what would in principle make each side change its mind. If neither reading can be ruled out by any evidence anybody could collect now, say that plainly, explain why, and then tell us what a commission is supposed to do with a situation like that.

Three, take the developer's side and the economist's side as seriously as you can — the housing need, the cost of delay, the auditability worry, the institution-versus-one-off point — and only then say which arguments win and why. I don't want the losing side flattened; some of us are going to vote that way and they deserve a real answer.

Four, tell us how to treat the oral tradition given the contamination worry, in a way that is neither brushing it aside nor treating it as beyond question.

And please: no citations, no references, no bibliography, no reading list. I can't check any of it, half the commission won't read it, and the county attorney says anything we rely on has to be in the record anyway. Just the argument. Write for a smart non-specialist, and if you use a technical term, define it in a clause rather than assuming I know it.
