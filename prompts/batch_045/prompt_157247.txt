We read (as I understood it) that a historian named Fischer argued back in the 60s that Germany's leadership bore the main share of responsibility for how July 1914 went down, and that this was a big deal partly because the Versailles treaty had pinned a kind of legal war-guilt on Germany. I said something like "so on that reading Germany is the one to blame," and he shut it down with this line he repeats like a mantra: everyone was to blame, so no one was. He uses it for two things at once — first, to say that singling out Germany is just victors' propaganda, and second, to say that no German alive today "owes" anything at all, not an apology, not commemoration, not money. It feels like that sentence is doing about six different jobs and none of them are spelled out, and I can't tell whether I'm disagreeing with a real thesis or with a shrug.

Can you take that one-liner apart for me? I'd like a kind of menu of the precise claims it could be making, and for each one a verdict: is it a triviality nobody denies, a substantive claim that's actually defensible, or something false? The distinctions I keep half-forming are things like: causal contribution vs. blameworthiness vs. mere answerability (having to explain or repair something you didn't culpably cause); whether blame is a fixed budget that has to be split up among contributors, so that adding culprits necessarily reduces each one's share, or whether several parties can each be fully blameworthy for the same outcome; the "asymmetric leverage" point, that even when lots of governments contributed, one of them may have been the one that could have stopped the whole chain unilaterally while the others couldn't, which would make equal-sharing readings collapse; whether a state or a nation can be a bearer of blame at all or only the individual officeholders can; and I vaguely remember reading about a four-way split between criminal, political, moral and some fourth kind of guilt that isn't about wrongdoing at all, which might be the bit that survives across generations. Please also say, reading by reading, which ones would actually get my roommate his two conclusions (Germany wasn't specially responsible, and nobody today inherits anything), which ones only get him the first, and which ones are consistent with Fischer-style blame still being true. And tell me which reading you'd bet he actually means when he says it, since that's the one I should argue with. Oh, and one more wrinkle: if blame can't be inherited but some forward-looking duty to repair can, I'd like to know what exactly has to be true for that duty to land on people who personally did nothing — please write the answer in American English, and keep it in list form so I can take it to office hours.
