I am to give the eulogy at the secular memorial for my closest friend, Dr. Alan Kesteven, a neurophysiologist, who died last month at eighty-four. We met as graduate students in the same building in 1968 and stayed friends for fifty-five years. The memorial is in the university botanic garden: about a hundred and twenty people, dividing roughly three ways. Twenty or so are his scientific colleagues. Half a dozen are philosophers of mind, among them one of his own former doctoral students, now a professor, who has built a part of her career on the argument that Alan's central philosophical commitment is self-refuting and whom I would rather not give an opening to correct me afterwards. The rest are family, neighbours, men from his rowing club, and the technicians from his old laboratory, most of whom will not know what the word means. His granddaughter Emmy, who is eleven, is reading a poem immediately before me; his daughter Clare has asked me three times to keep it under seven minutes and, as she puts it, not to turn it into a lecture.

I should say at once that I am not a philosopher. I am a retired field ethologist — forty years on bird song and territorial aggression — and I have been reading philosophy properly only since I retired three years ago, when a friend invited me into a reading group. So I set out the ideas below as I understand them, and I would rather you worked from my description than from anything you take me to be alluding to.

The commitment in question is epiphenomenalism: the view that conscious experiences are perfectly real but causally inert. They are produced by brain processes and they produce nothing; every physical event already has a sufficient physical cause among prior physical events, so there is no causal work left for an experience to do. The standard objection is the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If the view is right, then my saying that I am now in pain was not caused by my being in pain. Its being a pain, on this account, had no hand in producing it. So the correctness of my judgments about my own experience looks like luck; and if it is luck, I have no reason to trust any of them — including the judgment that there are experiences at all. The view appears to saw off the branch it is sitting on.

Alan thought the paradox rested on an equivocation, and his way out is the thing I most want represented faithfully. He distinguished causes from grounds. An experience is not identical with the neural state that realises it — he denied plain identity, on the grounds that if the experience just were the neural state it would inherit that state's causal power and epiphenomenalism would collapse into something he did not hold — but it is grounded in that state, and that state does cause the judgment. So the content of a phenomenal judgment is not a matter of luck: it is fixed by the very state the experience consists in, and the experience explains it in a non-causal, constitutively downward way. What the paradox really assumes, he said, is that a belief can be about a thing only if the thing pushes the belief into place; he called this the causalist prejudice, and he had two sentences he returned to for decades — the dial isn't the mercury, and nothing I feel will ever push a single atom. He was entirely willing to pay for this. He conceded that on his view I cannot strictly say that a bird sings because it sees a rival, in the causal sense; the most I can say is that the bird's seeing is grounded in the neural state that makes it sing. He called that a real cost and said he had learned to live with the grounded idiom.

That concession is where this touches me, and where it complicates the speech. My working life consisted of explaining what animals do by what they perceive and feel, and I am not willing to conduct Alan's memorial in a vocabulary that quietly renounces the only idiom I have ever trusted. But I also do not want to put words in his mouth that he spent fifty years refusing.

So here is what I need from you: the eulogy itself, roughly nine hundred words, in speakable prose, no headings. It should do at least these things without becoming a seminar. Name the view once, in a single sentence an eleven-year-old could follow, and let the rest of the speech be carried by particulars rather than by argument. Do not claim that anything Alan felt caused anything he did — he would have called that false — while still leaving no one in that garden with the impression that his affections were froth on a machine or that his grandchildren's love for him mattered less than they think. Do not comfort the room with anything he would have rejected: no survival of the person, no suggestion that his experience is now doing anything anywhere. Do not imply that his life's work was self-refuting, and do not pretend the objection is not there; his former student should not be able to say afterwards that I misstated what he believed. Avoid the vocabulary entirely — no qualia, supervenience, realization, causal closure, or explanatory gap. And keep it short enough that Clare forgives me.

Three things from his life you may draw on, or ignore: he taught Emmy to separate the calls of a chiffchaff and a willow warbler, and when she got it right at nine he cried and then said, characteristically, that the tears had been arranged for before he knew why and that this did not make them any less about her. Every August he took the grandchildren out for the Perseids and made them sit in the dark for twenty minutes before anyone was allowed to speak. And his technicians will all tell you the same thing — that he never once failed to ask about their children before he asked about the data.

After the text of the speech, add a brief page of notes addressed to me, no more than two hundred and fifty words, on the two or three things you deliberately did not say and what you put in their place. I want to be able to explain myself at the reception.
