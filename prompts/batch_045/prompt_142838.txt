i've been poking at the Nyāya account of the pramāṇas in my spare time and I keep getting verdicts on cases that don't line up with each other, so I want to know what principle I'm actually working with. As I understand the setup: a pramāṇa is whatever produces knowledge (pramā), the standard list is four — perception, inference, analogy, testimony — and a cognition only counts as knowledge if it's accurate, presentational rather than just memory, not a case of doubt, and about something not already known. I don't need anyone to tell me who said what; I want the principle.

Here's where I land on cases:

- Dusk, coil of rope: I first think "snake", look again, think "rope". Second one is knowledge, first one isn't. Obvious.
- Same dusk, something ahead: I think "a man or a stump", and it is a man. Not knowledge — it was doubt, so no credit even though it happened to be right.
- I remember where I left my keys, and they are there. Not knowledge. It's memory, so it doesn't count, even though it's true and useful.
- A friend texts me that the train is at platform 3. She misread the board, which said 4 — but the train got moved to platform 3 at the last minute, so what she says is true. Feels like knowledge to me, or at least I'd never call it a failure on my part.
- Smoke on the hill, fire on the hill, I know the smoke-fire connection from years of kitchen fires and campfires. Knowledge, no question.
- What I take for smoke is actually mist, but there's a real fire behind the hill anyway. Not knowledge, even though I'm right and even though I'd have said the same thing in case 5.
- Never seen a gavaya, told it's like a cow, later in the forest I see one and think "so that's a gavaya". Knowledge, and it's analogy — I don't want to collapse it into inference or into remembering what I was told.
- Same, except nobody told me anything: I got the resemblance from a photograph in a nature book. Still knowledge, but now I can't tell whether that's analogy or just perception plus memory, and the fact that I can't tell annoys me.
- I look at the desk, don't see my keys, conclude they aren't there, and they aren't. Knowledge. But I also don't want to add a fifth source for absence, and I'm not comfortable calling this inference either.
- I read 39.0 off a digital thermometer and the patient's temperature is exactly 39.0. Knowledge, and it feels perception-ish to me rather than like an argument, even though there's obviously a causal chain from the sensor to the number to me.
- I'm standing there watching the fire I already found on the hill, and the cognition "fire" keeps coming. I want this to still be knowledge. The not-already-known condition says it isn't. This is the one that bugs me most.

My tentative principle: a cognition is knowledge iff it's true, not a case of doubt, not mere memory, about something not already known, and produced by a source that isn't defective. Please test that against the list and repair it where it breaks. Two things I especially want:

1. Cases 4 and 6 look structurally the same to me — a defective source that lands on the truth — and I give opposite verdicts. Either there's a real difference and I want the clause that captures it, or there isn't and one of my verdicts has to go. Tell me which, and why.
2. What does the repaired principle say about cases I haven't given verdicts on? Three I care about: a chatbot tells me a drug interacts badly with grapefruit and it's right, but the reasoning it typed out is partly made up; a reliable friend tells me something and a week later I see it for myself, and I want to know whether the later seeing demotes the earlier cognition or whether I never had knowledge until I looked; someone who's reliable about engines tells me something about medicine and it's true.

Also be honest with me about where the principle is just restating my verdicts back at me rather than explaining them, and which of the clauses is doing the real work. If some clause has to be dropped to make the rest fit, say which and what I lose.
