I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on the institutional preconditions of desert claims, and I want to stage an exchange between two colleagues of mine who keep talking past each other. Please write a focused dialogue (American English, under 900 words) between them.

Setting: a university is considering replacing its market-based salary schedule with a Circumstance-Adjusted Merit Audit. The audit scores each faculty member against a predicted output distribution for a synthetic peer group matched on observed background variables — school funding where they grew up, parental education, health history, caregiving years — and pays people on the residual: the gap between their actual output and what was predicted for someone from their starting point. Both characters accept that brute luck shouldn't affect anyone's distributive share; they disagree about whether circumstance-adjusted effort can ground desert-based pay at all.

Dr. Vance defends the audit: the residual is the closest operationalization we have of what the person herself contributed through responsible choice, and desert is the only consideration that makes unequal pay something other than a bargain struck from a position of advantage. Dr. Okonkwo objects: the residual captures the portion of luck the matching model can't see (temperamental fit to a task, lucky collaborations, noise in the model itself) rather than responsibility; a thorough audit requires measurement intrusive enough to be demeaning whatever it finds; and desert, even when genuine, fixes only an ordering and not the size of the gaps, so the real work in any schedule is still done by the university's aims.

Have them work through three cruxes: (1) whether the residual tracks responsibility or merely the unmodeled part of luck, and whether the modeling error is random or systematically correlated with advantage; (2) whether desert can supply a metric — how much more the more deserving person should receive — or only a ranking, and what an ordinal desert claim can and cannot justify about a cardinal differential; (3) whether the audit's costs in privacy and expressive meaning count against desert-based pay as such or only as side constraints on how it's implemented.

Keep both equally competent and make them answer each other: Vance owes a real response to the metric problem rather than a restatement that desert matters, and Okonkwo owes an account of what would make a differential genuinely deserved rather than merely acceptable. Let them isolate at least one disagreement that turns out to be merely verbal, and let each concede one substantive point to the other.
