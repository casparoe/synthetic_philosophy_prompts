Hi — I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I've got a piece due next week on whether white lies are actually wrong. I think I've worked out a clean way to settle it and I'd like someone to poke at it before I file.

The argument goes like this. Politeness conventions are obviously local and learned: the Dutch are famously direct, the Japanese famously indirect, both systems hang together internally, and neither is wrong — they're just different local solutions to the same problem of not shredding each other's face all day. I know those national labels are caricatures at the edges, but the underlying contrast in norms feels real enough to lean on, and it came out of my own reporting too: I got told to my face in Amsterdam that my questions were lazy and it was weirdly refreshing, whereas in Tokyo I could never work out whether a host actually wanted me to stay for another drink.

But then, if politeness is conventional all the way down, isn't there also no culture-independent fact of the matter about whether a white lie is wrong? Once you know the local script, the question of whether a given white lie was ok looks fully answered, and asking whether it is really wrong on top of that seems like asking whether you should drive on the left or the right in the abstract. Which would mean the moral philosophers who write about lying — the absolutists who say you should never lie even to someone at the door asking where your friend is, and the consequentialists who try to add up the pleasure — are arguing about something that isn't there. The only real question is fit with local convention. That's my landing.

Two gadgets I want to build the piece around:

1. A split I've been calling lubrication lies versus steering lies. Lubrication lies are the tiny formulaic falsehoods that keep an interaction running and don't change what anyone will do — telling a host the food was lovely, telling a colleague their talk was great, the I'm-fine kind of thing. I want to argue these aren't really lies at all, because nothing anyone does turns on them. Steering lies actually redirect the hearer's practical reasoning, and those are the genuine moral cases. There's also an odd third version where the person you're lying to, or someone else at the table, definately knows you're lying, and I never know what to do with that one.

2. Cashing out the cost of a lie as inflation rather than harm: every lie slightly devalues your future assertions, and the cost is paid by whoever has to interpret you later, not by the person you lied to, who usually feels fine. I like it because it explains how a white lie can be harmless to its target and still not be free.

The objection I keep getting is the doctor who lies to a patient about a diagnosis. I want to use that as my concession case — the one place where I'd admit local convention doesn't settle it.

So: does this hang together, and where's the hole if not? What's the strongest version of the conclusion I'd actually want to defend in print, and how do I handle the diagnosis case and the never-lie-no-matter-what crowd without giving up the main claim? Im on deadline, so be blunt if I'm about to embarrass myself.
