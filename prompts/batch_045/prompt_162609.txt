Hey — I'm TAing discussion sections for an intro aesthetics course this term, and my own seminar just went over Hume's essay on the standard of taste, and there's a tangle in it that's gotten stuck in my head and that I'd like to be able to explain to undergrads without just shrugging and saying "well, its all subjective, sort of."

Here's what I keep circling. Hume seems to want both things at once: that beauty isn't a quality sitting in the object but a sentiment we feel, *and* that some verdicts are genuinely going wrong — that the person who insists some mediocre epic poet is better than Milton isn't merely reporting a different preference but is somehow mistaken, and that the standard is located by looking at what qualified critics converge on once you've stripped out self-interest, prejudice, laziness, lack of practice and comparison. Two bits of that confuse me. First: what am I actually supposed to *do* when my own felt response reliably diverges from the joint verdict of the true judges — am I meant to train my sentiment until it comes around, or just to say "this is beautiful even though it does nothing for me," and if it's the second, in what sense is beauty a sentiment anymore? Second, and this is the part I really want your take on: imagine two critics who both satisfy every item on Hume's checklist — decades of practice, enormous range of comparison, no axe to grind, sharp good sense — but who differ in something purely constitutional rather than in prejudice or ignorance. Say one of them has that condition where the two ears disagree about pitch (diplacusis? something like that), so a bunch of chords that sound blended and warm to everybody else beat and come out rough and sour to her, and this reliably makes her dislike passages everyone else loves. Is she disqualified as a true judge? It looks like there are only two ways to go and each costs Hume something: if she's disqualified *because* she's the one who'll come out disagreeing with the convergent verdict, then "true judge" collapses into "person who agrees with what the canon already converges on," and the standard isn't being discovered by the judges so much as restated by them. But if she isn't disqualified, then you have two fully qualified judges with discordant verdicts and the standard has no answer at all, which means the machinery only ever settles the easy cases where someone was obviously biased or lazy.

So: could you walk me through what's really at stake here at a gut level? Concretely — is the true-judge test best read as a way of *finding* an independent standard, or as a way of *constituting* one, and does Hume have to pick, or is there a reading where he doesn't? I don't need a walkthrough of the essay's argument in order, I've read it, and please don't hand me a reading list or cite anyone by name — I just want the conceptual shape of the problem, plus a sense of which horn someone sympathetic to Hume should grab and what they lose by grabbing it. Also feel free to push back on my setup if you think I've set the options up wrong or smuggled in an assumption that Hume wouldn't grant.
