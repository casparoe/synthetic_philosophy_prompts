I'm putting together a short position paper for a decision-theory reading group — the idea is that each of us circulates a "profile" of our verdicts on the Newcomb-style cases and the principles we think generate them, and the others try to find the joints. I've written mine down and I'd like an audit before I send it out, because reading it back I can't tell which part is load-bearing. Here it is.

**Verdicts on cases**

1. In the standard Newcomb problem (one opaque box, a predictor with a long公开 track record, take one or take both) I take one box. More than the verdict, I want the *rationale*: my reaching for the one box is strong evidence that the million is there, and I reject the demand that a rational choice must make its outcome more likely by causing it. Call this the evidential gloss.

2. In the counterfactual mugging — a predictor flips a fair coin out of my sight; if it comes up heads it pays $10,000 to anyone it predicts would have handed over $100 had it come up tails; if it comes up tails it asks for the $100 and pays nothing — I'm told it came up tails, I'm asked for the $100, and I hand it over.

3. In the hitchhiker case — a driver rescues me and drives me into town having read in advance whether I'd pay once I was safe; I'm safe now — I pay.

4. In the XOR blackmail case — a predictor sends me a demand for $1,000 exactly when one and only one of the following holds: (a) I would pay $1,000 on receiving the demand, (b) the damaging secret would become public anyway; and nothing I do affects whether the secret comes out — the demand arrives and I don't pay.

**Principles I take myself to be relying on**

5. "It's already settled whether the money is in the box / the coin has landed / the secret will come out, so nothing I do now can change that" is never by itself a reason to refuse. Settledness does no normative work on its own.

6. The correct verdict on a case can't depend on the order in which I find things out, or on whether I've already looked at the coin or opened the boxes. If a verdict flips depending on when I evaluate it, something has gone wrong.

7. Self-binding devices and precommitment don't make an action rational that wasn't already rational; they only alter what I can manage to bring off. So "I should have bound myself in advance" can't be what makes (2) and (3) right.

8. The counterfactuals that matter are counterfactuals about the decision procedure I actually instantiate, not about some procedure I could redesign myself into at will. That I could, given effort, rewrite myself into a person who pays isn't yet a reason to pay.

**Robustness claims**

9. In the transparent version of Newcomb's problem, where I can see what's in the boxes before choosing, I still take only the one box, and I don't think whether I can see the contents is normatively relevant either.

10. None of the verdicts in (1)–(4) should flip if the predictor is 99% accurate rather than perfect. A view that gets the perfect-predictor cases right only by exploiting the limit case is not a view I want.

What I'd like from you is an audit, not a survey. Which of these are genuinely in tension with which, and how badly — ranked by severity rather than listed as if they were all equally bad? For each real tension, tell me what would have to be given up and what giving it up costs me elsewhere in the set. Where a tension is only apparent, say why it dissolves rather than flagging it alongside the others; in particular I want to know whether (5) really does undercut my refusal in (4), and whether (7) is compatible with (2) and (3) or quietly contradicts them. If one revision cleans up more than the rest, name it. And if some of these can be held together only by precisifying a principle I've stated loosely — (6) and (8) both feel loose to me — tell me which precisification you'd commit to and which one you'd reject, and why.

Please take a definite position: tell me which package you think I should actually defend, and which commitments you'd drop, with reasons rather than a menu of options to choose from. Where you're relying on a contested claim about what some decision theory implies, say so and say how much of your verdict rests on it. I have no stake in which of the ten survives; I'd rather find the crack now than have the group find it for me.
