I've been going to the same rationalist-adjacent meetup for years and some version of Goodhart's law comes up every few months, always in the one-liner form — "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — and always deployed as a conversation-stopper. Last week it got used against me. We were arguing about whether our little local giving group should score grant applications on a few explicit numbers (cost per unit of outcome, some rough reach estimate) instead of the current setup, where two people read the narrative applications and go with their gut. Someone said "Goodhart's law" in that tone that clearly means "explicit metrics always get gamed, therefore holistic judgement," and everyone sort of nodded. I pushed back with something like "judgement is a measurement channel too and it gets Goodharted just as easily, people learn to write the kind of narrative our reviewers respond to," but I dont think I said it well and I'm not fully sure it's right. What complicates it for me is that the one-liner isn't Goodhart's own phrasing — I gather the wording comes from Marilyn Strathern quoting Keith Hoskin in a 90s paper on accountability stuff — and the version actually attributed to Goodhart himself, from a 1975 article on UK monetary policy, is "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes," which reads to me like a much narrower econometrics claim than what my meetup person needed it to be.

The problem is I can't keep straight which thing is being claimed. Here are the distinctions I can half-see, and I'd like them sharpened: (a) it's just the intervention-versus-conditioning point — any predictor that isn't causal breaks once you intervene on it, i.e. correlation-isn't-causation with extra words — which is true but seems to apply to human judgement exactly as much as to a spreadsheet; (b) something like a regressional story where the proxy is target-plus-noise, so hard selection over-selects positive residuals and the shortfall grows with how hard you select relative to how noisy the proxy is, which sounds substantive and quantitative and would tell you "don't optimize too hard on a noisy proxy" rather than "don't use proxies"; (c) a strategic story that requires actual agents who know the metric and have incentives, and which splits into redirecting effort from unmeasured to measured dimensions (teaching to the test) versus corrupting the measurement itself (faking the numbers) — note that in both of these the measure may still be measuring its thing perfectly, so "ceases to be a good measure" looks like the wrong diagnosis; (d) a thing I keep coming back to that I can't find a name for, call it shadow dimensions: the target is high-dimensional, the metric is a low-dimensional projection, so even a noiseless causally-valid metric leaves all the unmeasured coordinates unconstrained and optimization pressure drives them somewhere extreme — is that a separate failure mode or just (a)-(c) in a costume, and does it need an intentional agent at all, or does gradient descent / natural selection do it too; (e) a constitutive or transformative reading where instituting the metric changes the practice being measured, so the metric doesn't fail to track the target, the target just stops being the thing you wanted.

So: can you lay out for me the menu of precise claims that line could be making, ordered roughly trivial-to-radical, and give me verdicts on each — platitude, substantive-and-defensible, only-true-with-caveats, or just false — plus, for each one, whether it would actually get my meetup person to "so drop the numbers and trust the reviewers' gut"? I particularly want to know which reading has to be doing the work if publicity or explicitness of the metric is supposed to be the active ingredient, because that's the only place I can see his conclusion living, and even then it seems like the conclusion you'd draw is "hide or rotate your metrics, use several independent channels, cap the optimization pressure, audit for corruption" rather than "use vibes, vibes aren't a metric." Bonus points if you can tell me where (d) fits, whether there's a reading of the line that's straightforwardly false (e.g. that any proxy degrades without bound under any amount of optimization), and whether the one-liner and Goodhart's 1975 sentence are even claims about the same phenomenon. I want something I can bring to the next meetup without sounding like I'm just name-dropping a law.
