so I've been defending a version of the "channel criterion" for units of selection for a while now, and I've started to worry that I've patched it into something that can't lose. I want you to tell me what would actually count against it.

Rough statement: a level L is a genuine unit of selection only if there is an inheritance channel at L — parent units at L give rise to offspring units at L with correlated traits, so that fitness differences at L can accumulate over generations. Genes pass trivially. Organisms pass via development. Whole groups pass only when the life cycle includes a group-level bottleneck: founding by one or a very few propagules, or genuine group fission. Aggregation groups, where the members just come together each generation, never pass, on my view — you can get enormous group-level fitness differences, but with no channel at the group level there's nothing there for selection to accumulate on, and the lower-level bookkeeping predicts the outcome on its own.

The patch I'm suspicious of: I've been playing with a made-up organism to test the bottleneck requirement. It secretes a persistent extracellular scaffold, the group's composition changes the scaffold's chemistry, the scaffold outlives every individual in the group by many generations, and its chemistry biases which genotypes later recolonize it. So a group-level trait has effects on the composition of *later* groups, through a structure that persists — group "offspring" without any bottleneck. I decided this counts: heritability via a persisting scaffold is still a channel, because the scaffold is part of the group's life cycle rather than a generic background condition.

What I want, sorted roughly fatal-to-awkward:
- What would show the channel criterion itself is wrong — not just that it's one useful convention among others, but that it misclassifies real cases? Is there a case with a textbook bottleneck where the group level is nonetheless doing no causal work, or vice versa?
- Whether the scaffold move has quietly made the view unfalsifiable. If any persisting environmental modification counts as a channel, then beaver dams and soil and nest sites are inheritance channels too, "group selection" becomes nearly ubiquitous, and my view collapses into the deflationism I was trying to avoid — the position that there's just one process and the levels are bookkeeping that the Price partition lets you take or leave. Is that collapse a defeater or a vindication, and how would I tell the difference?
- Where the two descriptions (group-level vs. lower-level) actually come apart in what they predict about the direction and rate of trait change under identical demographic parameters. That's the thing that would move me most, more than anything about what we "should" call selection.
- Which of these a committed channel theorist should concede as counting against the view, and which they get to dismiss as changing the subject. My lab mate says the whole channel business is metaphysical decoration bolted onto an accounting identity, and I don't have a great reply.

Please don't just tell me it depends what you mean by selection — I know that move. Tell me which way of meaning it costs me the most. No references needed, I want the arguments.
