I am designing one session of my upper-level seminar on formal epistemology and would like you to design the whole thing for me, as a runnable activity rather than a lesson plan.

The setting: 16 students, one 75-minute session, one instructor and no assistant. No laptops for the students; I have a projector, a whiteboard, index cards, markers, dice, and my phone. The students can compute Bayes' theorem with a probability tree and are at ease with a three-variable joint distribution; almost none of them have met a proper scoring rule.

The topic is Jeffrey conditionalization and uncertain evidence. Not the formula as a piece of machinery to be presented, but three things I want them to run into and only later be able to name. First: that there is a common kind of learning that does not fit the mould of "take some proposition E to be certain and set your new credence in H equal to your old P(H|E)" — cases where nothing is settled, your confidence in E merely moves to some value short of 1, and you nonetheless have to change your mind about H. Second: that the natural generalization — spread your new credence in H across the cells of the partition as a weighted average of P(H|E) and P(H|¬E), weighted by your new credences in E and ¬E — gives the right answer in a wide class of cases, and that this is surprising given how little information it uses. Third, and this is the one I care most about: that the rule's key assumption is a substantial condition and not a formality. I want them to see that two evidence-channels can move their confidence in E to exactly the same number while requiring quite different final credences in H, because one of the two channels also carries information about how strongly E bears on H. The update is not a function of the new marginal alone.

Constraints. No lecture and no handout of the formalism up front. The words "Jeffrey," "probability kinematics," and "rigidity" must not appear in any student-facing material or in anything I say before the debrief, and ideally the weighted-average rule should not be written on the board until after the last round. No real money: points, a leaderboard, and something trivial as a prize. Whatever they write down must be written down individually and before any discussion in each round, because I want genuine scatter in the room, and I want at least one round in which the room predictably splits into two camps that disagree with each other by a margin nobody would call noise — and where the split is visible on the board before anyone explains it.

My own half-formed idea, which you should feel free to discard: a hidden state, a sensor that reports on it, a report the students never see directly, and an "impression" of the report delivered through a channel whose character varies — sometimes the channel is merely noisy about what the report said, sometimes the way it is noisy also tells you something about how trustworthy the sensor was. I could not get the incentives to work, and I could not stop the students from either rounding their confidence in the report up to certainty or simply not moving at all.

What I need from you:

1. Props and procedure: exactly what is prepared in advance, how the hidden state and each channel are randomized (a facilitator's table of draws against dice or a percentiles table, not a phone app I have to trust), and how I can show the students afterwards that the draws were honest rather than engineered to make a point.

2. The rounds, with a timeline in minutes: what is publicly known at each stage, what each student sees, and what each student commits to in writing. I want a control round in which the evidence really is hard and plain conditionalization is available; a round in which the evidence is soft and the weighted-average rule is exactly right; and a final round in which the same numerical shift in confidence about the evidence proposition is produced by two different channels in two halves of the room, with the two halves needing to end up far apart.

3. The scoring scheme in full: the formula for the proper scoring rule you recommend, why you picked it over the alternatives, why I should elicit two numbers from each student rather than one (their credence in the hypothesis and their credence in the evidence proposition), what stops a student from gaming one number at the expense of the other, and how scores are aggregated across rounds so that one lucky trial does not decide the session.

4. The reveal and the board artifact. I have in mind a scatter plot with the evidence-credence on one axis and the hypothesis-credence on the other, with each student as a dot, and a reference line drawn only after the dots are up — the line being the locus of points consistent with the weighted-average rule using the old conditional credences. Tell me how to run the reveal so that the students notice on their own that the two camps in the last round sit on opposite sides of that line, and what I should say, and not say, at that moment.

5. The debrief: a mapping from specific moments in the session to specific formal claims, in the order I should take them, with the terminology introduced only here. Include which predictable student errors I should treat as reasonable and take seriously rather than correct.

6. An honest account of what will go wrong the first time I run it — the failures of comprehension, of arithmetic, of attention, and of staging — and what to do about each in the moment.

7. An honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by lecture: in particular, say plainly where the setup misrepresents the real epistemological situation, since in my version the students are always handed a clean partition, a known joint distribution, and a determinate number for their own confidence in the evidence proposition, none of which real agents have.

8. A complete answer key: every correct number for every round, computed, including the numbers for the two channels in the final round, and a note on how sensitive the effect is to the parameters so I can tune it if the gap between the camps comes out too small or too large.

9. If it can be made to work, a variant of the final round in which two pieces of evidence are presented in opposite orders to the two halves of the room, arranged so that anyone who has been updating with stale conditional credences ends up in a different place depending on the order while a full Bayesian does not. If it cannot be made to work, say so and tell me why.
