I am revising the lecture in which I introduce P. F. Strawson's essay "Freedom and Resentment" to second-year undergraduates, and by the time we reach his distinction between considerations that excuse an agent and considerations that exempt him altogether, the room tends to have gone rather flat. I should like a short joke — three sentences at most — suitable for a lecture slide, and I should prefer it not to be the sort of thing one could equally tell about any view whatsoever.

Strawson was writing well before pharmacological moral enhancement became a live topic, so I have begun using an invented case in seminars, and I should like the joke to be set against it. Suppose a drug, marketed as Equanimity, that reliably and reversibly suspends a person's capacity for the participant reactive attitudes — resentment, moral indignation, gratitude, guilt, the whole range — for some hours at a time, while leaving her theoretical moral judgements, her prudential reasoning and her command of moral vocabulary entirely intact. Someone who has taken it will still tell you, in the coolest and most articulate way, that she has been wronged; she simply cannot resent it, and cannot feel gratitude when matters go her way.

The jest must turn on the gap between those two things: on a person's discovering, rather to her cost, that what she has secured by suspending the attitudes is not release from blame alone but release from a good deal else besides — from the goodwill of others, and from a standing that only participation in those attitudes confers. A reader who has genuinely absorbed Strawson should laugh; a reader who has only skimmed a summary of him should have to have it explained. No puns, no exclamation marks, nothing that trades on the mere strangeness of the drug, and nothing that would embarrass me if it appeared, unattributed, in front of two hundred undergraduates.
