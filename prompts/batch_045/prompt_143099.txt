I write about science and ideas for a living, and last month I was at a small invitation-only workshop on decision theory and AI where this argument broke out at dinner. I wasn't a participant and I don't have a horse in this race, but I might want to use some of it in a piece and I'd like to know who's actually right before I do. Names changed, and I quoted neither of them.

The talk that evening was about the updateless decision theories that came out of the rationalist / AI-alignment world: the rough idea that you should choose a whole policy from behind a veil of ignorance about which situation you'll end up in, rather than deciding case by case on causal grounds. The speaker used the famous Omega case, the one where a near-perfect predictor flips a fair coin and, if it comes up tails, asks you for a hundred dollars — while if it had come up heads it would have handed you ten thousand, but only if it predicted you'd have paid in the tails branch. Updateless decision theory says pay. The speaker said this was obviously right, the room was visibly split, and then Ravi (an ML researcher) and Nina (a philosopher) went at it for about forty minutes over the bread basket. Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Ravi: The standard case makes updatelessness look exotic, but it isn't. Try my variant instead. Omega makes a perfect simulation of you, puts it in a room subjectively identical to yours, asks both of you for a hundred dollars at the same time, and hands the real you ten thousand iff the simulation says yes. Neither of you can tell which one you are. Now a plain causal decision theorist who updates on nothing more than self-locating uncertainty gets the same answer: if I might be the simulation, then my saying yes causes the real one to get the ten thousand. That's an ordinary causal lever. So the appearance of acausal reasoning in the original case is an artifact of how that story is told. Updatelessness is just causal reasoning plus not knowing which copy you are.

(2) Nina: You've built a different problem. Your case contains two things the original doesn't: a decision-maker whose act is causally efficacious, and a beneficiary who might be her. That's a two-person coordination problem with a lever attached. The original case has no lever at all — Omega's prediction is already in the bank, and nothing you do now changes the heads branch. Your reconstruction doesn't touch the hard case, it relocates it. And here's the test: if Omega labels the rooms, so the simulation knows she's the simulation, then on your own account there's no reason for her to pay. She gets nothing either way and the ten thousand goes to somebody else. Your reduction collapses exactly where the original difficulty lives, unless you stipulate that the copies care about each other. In which case what's really doing the work is cross-copy concern, not causality.

(3) Ravi: Fine, but that's not a retreat, it's the point. The original case also requires you to care about the version of you in the heads branch. Nobody thinks paying is justified if you only count dollars that land in the branch you're actually standing in. So UDT always involves weighting outcomes for versions of you that don't exist, and your "collapse" is just that same commitment showing up somewhere it's easier to see. And once you've granted it, the simulation case shows the mechanism is entirely ordinary: causal leverage plus self-locating uncertainty.

(4) Nina: Then the puzzle was never a puzzle about decision theory. It's a question in moral psychology about how much to discount your merely possible selves, wearing a formalism as a disguise. And the formalism hides a second, worse problem: how much weight each branch gets. Take the original case but make the coin heavily biased — heads one time in ten thousand. UDT now says don't pay, because the ten-thousand branch is weighted by its probability and it isn't worth a hundred dollars in the other nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine. But nothing about my concern for the heads-branch version of me changed. So all the action is in the weights, and I want to know why the prior probability of a branch is the right weight for concern about it. Why not weight by similarity to me? Why not weight every version equally?

(5) Ravi: The prior is forced. You evaluate a policy by its expected return across the situations it might land you in; that just is expected utility. If you weight branches equally regardless of how unlikely they are, you'll pay a hundred dollars for a one-in-ten-thousand shot at ten thousand, which is a losing trade, and in a world with predictors you get cleaned out. Every other weighting is exploitable.

(6) Nina: Exploitable relative to what? I'm not playing a repeated game against an ensemble of myself. The only money that changes hands is in the branch I'm in. You keep adding up branches I know didn't happen, announcing that the total is negative, and treating that as a reason for me — but I never agreed to maximize a total. I agreed to be a person in a room being asked for a hundred dollars.

(7) Ravi: But the person in the room benefits from having been an adder-upper, because Omega conditions on the policy. Even the purest this-branch-only self-interester has reason to want to have been built as someone who pays. That's precisely why the answer is pay and not "oh, then it's a free choice."

(8) Nina: Has reason to want to have been is a reason to wish you'd been manufactured differently, not a reason to act. You can only convert it into a reason to act if deciding now is somehow evidence about, or partly constitutive of, the algorithm Omega predicted. And if that's your move, you're doing evidential decision theory, and you owe me an answer about the smoking lesion, where the correlation between the act and the outcome is perfectly real but there's no reason at all to think the act makes a difference.

(9) Ravi: Not the same. UDT runs counterfactuals on the output of your decision algorithm while holding the causal and logical facts fixed. In the lesion case the correlation runs through a common cause, the gene, so varying your output doesn't move your cancer risk and UDT says smoke if you enjoy it. In the mugging the correlation runs through the prediction itself, so varying your output does move the payout. That's exactly the distinction evidential decision theory can't see.

Then the check came and they dropped it. I definately can't tell who won, and there's one thing neither of them addressed that I noticed on the walk back to my hotel: in Ravi's variant, once Omega tells you which room you're in, the right answers come apart — the simulation should pay and the real one shouldn't — and that felt like it broke something in his argument, though I couldn't say what.

Please go through the nine moves and tell me who's right about what at each step, not just who wins overall: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say briefly why, and then tell me what both of them are missing and whether the room-labels point I stumbled onto is the real objection or just a confusion.
