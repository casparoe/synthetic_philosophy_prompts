I've been invited to give the closing talk at our city's monthly rationality meetup — the room will be maybe sixty people: mostly software engineers and a handful of physicists who read LessWrong and ACX, two professional historians who came once out of curiosity and now come back because they enjoy arguing, an archaeologist who works on Aegean prehistory, and the usual effective-altruism-adjacent contingent who will want to know whether any of this changes what anyone should do with money. I have forty minutes plus fifteen for questions, and I want to spend it arguing one thesis rather than surveying a topic.

The thesis is about demarcation, and here is the version I've converged on after too many arguments in blog comments. Pseudohistory — Atlantis, ancient astronauts, Fomenko's New Chronology — is not demarcated from history by being false (bits of it are true by accident, and plenty of respectable history is false), not by being unfalsifiable (Fomenko makes any number of checkable claims), and not by being hostile to evidence (its practitioners cite constantly). What marks it, I want to say, is a structural feature I've started calling the ratchet: the program is built so that no observation lowers its posterior. Every find is a confirmation, and in particular the observations that on any prior view ought to have hurt it get laundered into support — a gap in the record becomes evidence of suppression, a failed prediction becomes evidence of how deep the conspiracy runs, a dating that contradicts the scheme becomes one more instance of the scheme's own claim that the record has been tampered with. Nothing is ever allowed to be a down-coins event. I want to keep this distinct from two things people will want to collapse it into: Popperian unfalsifiability (a program can be falsifiable in principle and still ratcheted in practice, because its auxiliary hypotheses are recycled as confirmations instead of ever being allowed to fail) and Lakatos's progressive-versus-degenerating distinction (mine is supposed to be visible in a single episode of reasoning rather than only in retrospect over decades, and to be about the sign of the update rather than about whether novel predictions keep coming). My two worked cases are the ancient-astronaut literature and Fomenko; my hardest case is something like continental drift, which had a lot of the sociological marks of crankery and turned out to be right.

What I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room — not from a philosophy-of-science seminar, not from a skeptics' convention — ordered by how much damage they'd do if I fumbled them, each phrased the way that kind of person would phrase it (I want the archaeologist's objection to sound like an archaeologist's and the physicists' to sound like a physicist's, e.g. something about the priors doing all the real work), each with a reply I could actually deliver in about two minutes, and each with an honest note on where that reply is soft, evasive, or question-begging. I expect at least the following pressures and want your judgement on what I'm missing: that the ratchet is just Lakatos in worse vocabulary; that demarcation was declared dead decades ago (the Laudan line) and I am reanimating a corpse; that working historians protect their own cores just as fiercely, so my criterion would convict half the profession; that Wegener shows the criterion condemns exactly the sort of speculative program that sometimes turns out to be right; that I've produced a sociological marker of low status and am laundering credentialism as epistemology; that my Bayesian framing quietly assumes the very priors that encode mainstream consensus; and — from the people who won't care about any of this unless I give them a reason — why the label does any work at all, i.e. what decision-relevant job that's pseudohistory does that that's false or that's unsupported doesn't already do. Please also give me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. And please don't give me citations, further reading, or a bibliography: I know the literature, and what I need is the dialectic rather than the sources.

Finally, end the sheet with one short paragraph addressed to me rather than to the room: say which single objection you think ought to be able to change my mind about the ratchet criterion and why — what I'd have to be shown, or what kind of case I'd have to encounter, for me to revise or drop it — and where, in your view, my reasoning is most likely failing in a way I haven't noticed.
