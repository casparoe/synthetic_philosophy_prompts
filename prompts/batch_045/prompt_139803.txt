I am trying to locate, precisely, where a disagreement I have with a colleague about the dark forest hypothesis actually lives, and I would like your own credence together with its derivation rather than a survey of what has been argued on either side.

Define the proposition as follows. Let C be the number of civilizations that have ever arisen in the Milky Way and reached a level of technology at which they could in principle have become detectable across interstellar distances — high-power beacons, the waste heat of large-scale engineering, self-replicating probes, or anything of that kind — counting ourselves if we belong to that class. The proposition D says that at least half of the members of C either were destroyed by another civilization before they could become detectable, or were capable of detectability but deliberately stayed quiet or actively concealed themselves, and did so mainly because of the risk posed by other civilizations rather than because of cost, indifference, or a simple lack of interest in expansion. If C turns out to contain only us, treat D as false. Give me P(D).

Decompose it. At minimum I want:

- P(C > 1): the probability that the Great Filter is not, for the most part, already behind us.
- P(galactic reach | C > 1): that self-replicating probes or comparable technology make it cheap — cheap relative to one civilization's resources and achievable within some tens of millions of years — to visit, seed or affect essentially every stellar system in the Galaxy.
- P(the asymmetries favour striking | reach): that locating a target and destroying or pre-empting it is cheaper, faster and safer than communicating with it, and that the possibility of a sudden technological explosion makes waiting genuinely costly rather than costly only in some abstract game-theoretic sense.
- P(restraint fails | the asymmetries favour striking): that neither a collective-action structure in which everyone prefers someone else to bear the cost and risk of a strike, nor the risk of self-disclosure that a strike carries, nor a stable quietist equilibrium, leaves the forest unexploited — and that whatever silencing occurs is not simply the work of one early hegemon enforcing stillness on everyone else.
- P(chains of suspicion | restraint fails): that unverifiable intentions, large distances and effectively non-repeating encounters really do defeat attempts to signal benignity, rather than being soluble by cheap signalling, delegation, or verifiable commitments.
- And whatever probability you assign to the rivals: that the silence is mostly rarity, mostly settlement-without-loudness for reasons unconnected to fear, mostly brief windows of detectability that we have simply missed, or mostly an artefact of our having searched a very small volume of parameter space for a very short time.

Two wrinkles I want handled explicitly, because I think they carry most of the real argumentative weight and I do not see them much discussed.

First: the target of pre-emption need not be a peer. In the usual telling, a civilization waits for radio leakage and then strikes the emerging competitor. But a strike becomes far more attractive if it can be aimed at biosignatures instead. A chemical disequilibrium of the kind produced by biological activity is in principle detectable at interstellar distances, and it advertises a promising world hundreds of millions of years before anything on it could pose a threat to anyone. If cheap sterilisation of merely biological worlds is possible, then the dark forest does not merely silence civilizations; it should long ago have cleared the Galaxy of biospheres.

Second, and this is the point I want you to weigh rather than note in passing: that very implication is also the hypothesis's most acute vulnerability, because it is directly testable from where we sit. The Galaxy is old by any current estimate, the Earth has hosted life for a substantial fraction of that span and has carried an oxygen-bearing atmosphere for roughly the last two billion years, and our own existence is a sample drawn from whatever process governs biospheres. Conditional on cheap biosphere-striking being available to any civilization that arose early, we should expect to find ourselves in a Galaxy where such strikes have been carried out — and we do not appear to be such a sample. Tell me how much this consideration alone drags P(D) down, and whether the available escape hatches are strong enough to restore it: that strikers target technospheres rather than biospheres, that strikes are expensive enough to be worthwhile only against nearby systems, that no one has yet reached us, that we sit inside some region protected or overlooked, or that we are in an early epoch before the forest closes.

Give me a number for each term and one line of justification for each, then the combination; say explicitly how the terms bear on one another, since I assume several are not independent. Then tell me which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and what sort of evidence — astronomical, game-theoretic, or conceptual — would move it most.

For calibration of the disagreement I am trying to place: my credence is around 0.35 and my colleague's is around 0.7, and I doubt we are disagreeing about physics or astronomy. Given your decomposition, identify which sub-question most plausibly carries that gap, and say what each of us would have to concede to close it.
