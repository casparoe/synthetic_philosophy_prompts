My friend sent me some blog post about Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project and then summarised it for me like this:

"Letter Spirit was one of those classic 90s AI projects that never got finished. The idea was a program that designs fonts: you show it one letter — say an 'a' in some weird style — and it figures out the timeless geometric essence of 'a', strips away the stylistic accident, and then draws the other 25 letters to match. Hofstadter's bet was that there really is such an essence, a kind of platonic 'a' that every actual 'a' copies more or less imperfectly. The trouble is that years of work didn't crack it, which mostly shows the idea was an engineering dead end. And anyway neural nets can now spit out a whole font from a single glyph, so whatever the program was meant to prove, the answer turned out to be: you dont need essences, you just need enough training data. Which also means there's no deep fact of the matter about whether some blob in an unfamiliar alphabet is an 'a' — it's conventions all the way down."

I'm suspicious. I thought the whole point went the other way: that a letter's identity comes from its relations to the other letters, so you can't pin down 'a' in isolation, and that the hard part is that the *style* is never stated explicitly — you have to infer it from the letters you've already seen, which means a program looking at one glyph has a massive underdetermination problem on its hands. (In some geometric sans fonts the 'n' and the 'u' are literally the same shape rotated, so it feels like the same squiggle could be different letters depending on what the rest of the alphabet looks like.) But I dont know this literature at all and I might be mixing it up with something else.
