i'm giving one of the short 'fun' talks at the end of our consciousness seminar dinner next week — five minutes max — and i want to close with an actual joke instead of a slide of thankyous. The room will be roughly half global workspace people (the global neuronal workspace version: ignition, broadcast to consumer systems, availability for report and memory and motor planning all coming along together) and half philosophers, plus a couple of illusionists who think the whole access-vs-phenomenal distinction is a mess we should stop making.

So: write me one joke, setup and punchline, short enough to read out loud, about the gap between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness (Block's distinction, yes). It has to turn on the actual content of the dispute rather than on philosophy-of-mind branding — no bats, no zombies, no Mary, and no 'it's just what it's like' doing the work as a punchline on its own. Ideally it turns on what global workspace theory is really committed to: on that picture something becomes conscious exactly when it gets globally broadcast, which is precisely why people say it's a theory of access only, with a seperate leftover question about the feel.

There's one hinge I'd especially like it to land on if you can make it funny. My own hobbyhorse (I've been calling it the bootstrapping problem in my notes — as far as I know it isn't an established thing in the literature) is this: the no-report paradigms that are supposed to get at phenomenal consciousness without routing through report still have to be calibrated, and you calibrate them using trials where subjects *did* report what they saw — you pick your report-free marker, pupil dilation or microsaccades or whatever, on the basis of trials where the subject said 'yes, I saw it'. So access gets smuggled back in at the calibration step, and the experiment never actually settles the overflow question either way. If the joke can turn on that — the marker, the calibration, the supposedly report-free measure quietly leaning on a report — great. If you can't make that funny, pick some other real hinge in the dispute and go with it.

Don't hand me three options and leave me to choose: commit to one joke, then give me two or three sentences on why it works, and a note on where it takes liberties — which contested reading of the distinction, or of what global workspace theory is claiming, the joke leans on, and whether it's unfair to the illusionists in the room.

British English please, the department is weirdly fussy about it in anything that ends up on a slide.
