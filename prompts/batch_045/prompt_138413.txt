Got into a proper argument with my mate Priya last night after a work thing — started out about whether our team's incident review process is fair and ended up being about knowledge and power and whether those are even two different things. The bit we couldn't get past: at the review, our colleague Sam (most junior person there, quiet, only one in the room without a degree from somewere fancy) flagged a failure mode in the design. Nobody engaged with it. Twenty minutes later Tom says basically the same thing and everyone goes oh yes, good catch. Priya's line: that's just status and office politics in a philosophy costume, there's nothing distinctively epistemic about it, it's the same story as who gets interrupted in meetings and who gets promoted. My line: no — something else is taken from Sam there. He isn't just ignored, he's failed *as a source of knowledge*, and there's a particular flavour of insult in that you don't get from being merely disliked. Then we went round in circles for an hour on whether that extra thing is even real.

So I want to hear the argument done properly by two people who both know this literature. Write me a dialogue.

Two characters, both sharp, neither a strawman, and I want them actually answering each other's points rather than each stating a position. One holds that what happened to Sam is a genuinely epistemic wrong — testimonial injustice, being undermined specifically in the capacity of a knower — and that this matters over and above the practical or status harm. The other holds that this is a misdescription: what's really going on is status subordination and unfair distribution of goods, and calling it epistemic neither explains anything new nor tells you what to do about it.

Two wrinkles I want them to chew on, because they're where I think Priya's position is weakest and I'd of loved to have had them last night:

- The corroboration bind: speakers who expect to be doubted tend to do the careful thing — bring the data, bring a second person along, hedge precisely, pre-empt objections. And that very preparation gets read as insecurity, or as having an agenda, and their credibility drops further. So the obvious advice is self-defeating. Whoever thinks any of the fix sits on the speaker's side has to deal with this.
- Credibility escrow: a proposal we half-invented at the end. Claims get logged and assessed with the author's name stripped out; identity is revealed only after the assessment is locked. I think that obviously helps Sam. But Priya said something I can't shake: if you have to hide who's speaking to get the claim a fair hearing, you've got the outcome without ever giving Sam the thing he was owed, wich is being believed by someone, as him. I want the skeptic to press this hard and the other one to have an actual answer rather than just conceding it.
