I'm in a small graduate reading group in philosophy of biology, and for our last meeting we've set ourselves an exercise that I first thought was frivolous and now think is genuinely hard, so I'd like your help with it. The question is what Charles Darwin himself — not "Darwinism," not what a modern textbook asserts about what selection acts on — would have said about the units-of-selection dispute, given that he died before Mendel's work was generally read, before the word gene existed, and before anyone had separated the question of what selection acts on from the question of what is reliably transmitted across generations. Two things in his own writing make this non-trivial for me. First, in the Origin, when he confronts the sterile worker castes of social insects — the case he treats as the most serious special difficulty for his theory — he seems to me to say that the difficulty is lessened or removed once you allow that selection can bear on the family or the community and not only on the individual organism. Second, in the Descent of Man, in his discussion of the moral faculties, he appears to reason that a high standard of morality gives little or no advantage to an individual man and his children over the other men of the same tribe, yet gives an immense advantage to one tribe over another. My supervisor's view is that these are isolated lapses into group-thinking in an otherwise thoroughly individual-level theorist; another person in the group says they show Darwin was a multi-level selectionist avant la lettre, and that only the modern synthesis made everybody forget it. I don't want to settle that by appeal to labels.

So here is the case we invented for the session, which I'd like you to apply him to. Suppose a long-running evolution experiment with microbes — or, if you prefer, digital organisms — in which two levels of selection are deliberately pitted against each other: within each local group, a hoarder type that keeps a public resource to itself outreproduces a sharer type that releases the resource, but groups containing more sharers grow faster and are more often the founders of new groups. The results, suppose, are these three. With frequent mixing between groups, the hoarder type fixes in every replicate and the population then collapses. With rare mixing, sharers are stable, and over the whole collection of groups the sharer type ends up more common than it began. And when the experimenters delete the group-level founding events entirely while leaving everything else, including the supply of new mutations, untouched, the hoarder fixes anyway — but the total population is smaller at the end than at the start. Suppose also that these organisms swap small functional modules sideways between living individuals, so that there is no tidy lineage of particles to track: heritable variation is transmitted, but not by anything that looks like a genealogy of genes.

What I want from you is a reconstruction, not a verdict on who is right. Tell me how Darwin, working with the materials he actually had, would describe what is going on in that experiment. Would he count the competition between groups as natural selection in the same sense as the competition within them, or as something else — a distinct principle, or a mere consequence of individual-level processes — and what in his texts and commitments would push him each way? Would he locate the thing that is selected at the individual, at the group, at the family, or at the trait; and would he even have the apparatus to hear that as a question, lacking particulate inheritance and lacking any distinction between the entity selection acts on and the entity that carries the change forward? Does the sideways-transfer wrinkle matter to him at all, or is it invisible given how little he could say about the mechanism of inheritance? And where does he end up committed to something he would have found unwelcome — for instance, that the kind of reasoning about tribes in the Descent of Man is doing real explanatory work in a case that has nothing to do with human beings, or that his handling of the sterile castes commits him to more group-level structure than he elsewhere allows? Please keep three things clearly separated as you go: what you take Darwin actually to have written or been committed to, paraphrased in your own words and indicating which of his arguments you are leaning on (I don't need citations or a reading list — my library access is patchy); what is a reasonable extrapolation from those commitments to a case he never saw; and what is anachronism that he could not have said even in principle. Where the same evidence supports both my supervisor's reading and the other student's, say so, and say what further consideration would break the tie. I would rather have an answer that shows me where the reconstruction is forced and where it is a guess than one that confidently places Darwin on a side.
