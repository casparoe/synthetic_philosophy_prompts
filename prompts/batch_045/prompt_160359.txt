I'm putting together a seminar on indirect normativity and I need a case that isn't one of the usual suspects. Two views to work with: (R) what someone ought to do is fixed by the reasons there are, and to deliberate correctly is to try to track them; (D) what someone ought to do is what they'd settle on after deliberation that's been idealised — more information, more inferential power, full coherence among their desires, a properly vivid grasp of everything they already know.

Build me a one-paragraph, thoroughly ordinary case — a hospital, a school, a small firm, no sci-fi, no thought-experiment machinery — where R and D come apart, and where the gap is not generated by (i) the agent being ignorant of some relevant non-normative fact, (ii) any ordinary practical irrationality (no akrasia, no incoherent preferences, no failing means–ends), or (iii) the operative reasons being self-effacing. For (iii) I want the outcome motive-invariant: nothing downstream may change depending on what she believes about her reasons or what she's motivated by.

I've been useing two bits of jargon for this. An *enriching* idealisation only adds to the agent and never takes anything away — no deleting or damping of her existing desires and attachments; an *amputating* one does that. The case has to work under a purely enriching idealisation, so the standard complaint that idealisation strips away what matters to her can't do any of the work.

After the scenario: state what each view says and why, tell me which single structural feature is generating the divergence, what happens to the case if you remove that feature, and how a defender of whichever view comes out worse would attack it as cheating. It's for a second-year seminar, so the disagreement has to fall out of the two views rather than be built into the description.

Open straight with the scenario, no preamble, then the verdicts and the rest.
