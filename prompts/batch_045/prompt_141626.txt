im giving the closing talk at a two-day workshop on risk communication next week — my slot is 20 minutes plus maybe ten for questions, so realistically four or five exchanges — and i want to walk in with a real prep sheet rather than vibes.

The thesis is a style-guidance claim with a philosophy-of-probability argument underneath it. Practical version: when an official source publishes a probability, newsrooms should print it as a bare number, and every hedge we currently bolt onto it — "likely," "cannot be ruled out," "scientists warn of a risk of," "models suggest" — should instead be broken out into a separate sentence about the model's track record. Not deleted, just moved. My three examples are a hurricane-landfall percentage, an epidemic projection band, and a central-bank-ish "roughly one-in-three chance of a downturn."

The argument underneath: (1) there's a deference norm — if you learn that the chance of A is x, and the rest of what you know is admissible (roughly, it doesn't route around the chance to tell you about A directly), then your credence in A should be x; (2) deference only does its coordinating work if the reader can pick out the chance claim as such and treat everything else as separate evidence; (3) a hedge stapled to the number is read as second-order information about the number, so the reader ends up deferring to something that isn't the chance at all. I've been calling that gap "attenuation drift" — the distance between the number as issued and the number as received — and my fix is the split presentation: one first-order line, one second-order line, so the drift is at least visible.

The room is about forty and it's not my usual crowd. A dozen-ish operational forecasters (broadcast meteorologists and ensemble people) who will say their 30 percent is a frequency over perturbed initial conditions and not a chance in my sense at all. Two or three epidemic modelers who work on exactly the forecasts that change the thing they're forecasting. A block of science-communication and numeracy researchers who study how real readers mangle percentages and who think my "bare number" is a worse intervention than any hedge. A couple of editors whose actual worry is legal and reputational — the hedge is the insurance policy. And one philosopher of probability who was on the morning panel, is a Humean best-system person about chance, has written on self-undermining chances, and reportedly asks the first question in every session she attends.

What i need: the eight-ish objections i'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do rather than by how clever they are, each phrased the way that group would phrase it (the forecasters and the numeracy people and the philosopher should not sound like each other), each with a roughly 90-second reply, an explicit note on what i should concede up front vs. what i have to hold, and a flag on where my reply is soft. Tell me which one i should just concede in the talk itself instead of waiting to be asked, and which one is the weakest point in my own position that i should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices. And give me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
