hi — I'm in a political theory seminar on territory and migration this term and my final paper is due in about two weeks. I want to write it on District 9 (the Blomkamp film: the prawns stranded over Johannesburg, MNU moving them out to District 10, Wikus getting sprayed with the fluid and slowly turning into one of them), read through the open borders debate. Specifically the freedom-of-association case for a right to exclude — a political community gets to pick who it associates with, so it can refuse entry — and the reply I keep running into, that border control is coercion exercised over outsiders and so it needs a justification addressed to them, not just to the people already inside.

What I'm stuck on is that almost every version of this argument seems to picture immigration as people who want to move and a state that wants to stop them, with the country they came from still sitting there. In District 9 the prawns can't go anywhere. Their ship is dead above the city, home is unreachable, so "excluding" them doesn't amount to refusing entry — it amounts to holding them in a settlement indefinitely with nowhere to be removed to. Is the right to exclude still the same right in that case, or is a right to refuse entry quietly doing the work for a right to intern, which nobody argued for? And they didn't choose to come, their ship broke down. That's a lot closer to shipwrecked sailors than to economic migrants, and most people accept a duty to rescue. So what happens to that duty when the rescue lasts twenty years? Does it turn into a permission to stay, or does it stay a rescue and just never end?

Second thing: MNU is a company. The camp is run by a contractor, Wikus's father-in-law is the one profiting, and the state mostly just wants the problem gone. Does the association argument survive that? It's meant to be about a self-determining people deciding who to be joined with, which is a pretty different thing from a private firm doing removals for profit. And on the other side of it, the prawns don't seem to have anything like a government — there's no one to negotiate with and no institutions that could reciprocate anything — so the version of the argument that's about a people's collective self-determination seems to have nobody on the other side to be about.

Third: Wikus never crosses a border. He becomes a prawn and that's it, he's out — and the only reason MNU wants him back is that he can fire their weapons. I think the film is saying something about membership actually being conditional on usefulness rather than on consent or a shared life, but I can't tell if that's a real point about the right to exclude or just a plot device.
