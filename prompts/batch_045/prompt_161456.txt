I work in operations at a mid-sized bank, and for the past year or so I've been reading about AI alignment on my own — no formal background, mostly blog posts and the occasional paper I only half follow. The distinction I keep chewing on is the one between outer and inner alignment, and inside that, the idea of goal misgeneralization: the claim that you can specify the objective correctly, end up with a system whose internal aim really does score well on that specification across the situations it was shaped in, and still have it competently pursue the wrong thing once the environment changes out from under it. A friend of mine who actually does ML says that when I start applying this to organizations I'm just rediscovering Goodhart's law and dressing it up in machine-learning vocabulary, and I don't think he's entirely right, but I can't say exactly why yet.

So I'd like a case analysis, using a case I know from the inside of my own industry: Wells Fargo's fake-accounts scandal, which came to a head in 2016. As I remember the broad outlines — please correct me gently if I have a detail wrong, but don't let the analysis turn on the exact figures — the bank pushed very hard on cross-selling, branch staff were given steep daily or quarterly sales goals, employees under pressure opened deposit and credit-card accounts and signed customers up for products without their knowledge or consent (on the order of a million or two accounts, I think, with the estimate revised upward later), there were large regulatory fines, the CEO left, and there were reports that staff who flagged the practice through internal channels were themselves pushed out. Almost nobody involved appears to have thought they were serving customers.

Here is my own half-formed contribution, which I want you to test rather than flatter. I've started calling it the leash: what matters for inner alignment, I want to say, is not whether the internal goal matches the intended goal on the situations that shaped it, but whether the channel that let the principal shape and correct that goal still reaches into the new environment — whether there is any path by which information about the real objective can get back to the thing doing the optimizing once conditions change. A taut leash carrying the wrong signal is worse than a slack one, because it produces confident, competent behavior that nobody at the top has any remaining way to notice is off-target. I also want a three-way sorting: a case can go wrong because the internal aim was wrong from the start though it scored well on the proxy; because the internal aim was fine and the world moved; or because there was never a single internal aim at all, only habits and procedures that frayed at the edges. Where does Wells Fargo fall, and where does the leash idea actually do work versus where is it muddy?

Please approach this as someone equally at home in machine-learning alignment and in organizational design, and think it through carefully before you answer. Start by laying out the mapping explicitly and committing to it: who or what plays the base optimizer, what the base objective was, what the internal or mesa-objective was if there was one, what counts as the training situation and what counts as deployment, and what would have counted as evidence of divergence — and say plainly if any of those slots has no candidate. Then run it in both directions: what the goal-misgeneralization story predicts about what happened and what should have been done; which features of the case it explains, which it distorts, and which it simply cannot reach; and what the case gives back to the concept — a clean instance, an anomaly, a case the concept was never built for, or a case that only resembles one for superficial reasons. Please be especially hard on the sharpest version of my friend's objection: these were human beings with their own aims — bonuses, keeping a job in a brutal sales culture — who often knew the account-opening was wrong. That looks like a textbook story about perverse incentives, and it may be that the inner/outer distinction is doing no work here beyond restating principal–agent theory. If there is a reading on which the bank as a system, or its incentive machinery, is the thing that got optimized and misgeneralized, lay it out; if there isn't, say so. Finally, tell me what the minimal structure of a genuine organizational case of goal misgeneralization would have to be, so I can tell the difference next time.

End with a single sentence summarizing your overall judgment, and then, on the very last line, a fixed machine-readable verdict of exactly this form: \answer{INNER} if this is best understood as a genuine case of inner misalignment or goal misgeneralization, \answer{OUTER} if it is only a badly specified objective with ordinary strategic behavior under it, \answer{BOTH} if it is genuinely both at different levels of description, or \answer{NEITHER} if the inner/outer framing does not really apply to organizations and the case only looks like it does.
