hi — I want you to stress-test an idea I've been chewing on about transitional justice, and I'd rather you poke holes in it than agree with me.

The standard way of thinking about truth commissions and amnesties — the amnesty-for-truth trade, roughly: the successor state doesn't know what happened, the perpetrators do, punishment is the only leverage the state has, so waive punishment and buy disclosure, with full-disclosure conditions and maybe a political-motive test to stop people claiming amnesty for ordinary crimes — seems to me to treat truth as one single kind of object. Call it *archive-truth*: a stock of facts that accumulates, isn't used up by being known, and doesn't much care who is in the room when it's stated. On that picture the only design questions are the price (how much leniency for how much disclosure) and verification (does the account check out).

What I think that picture misses is a second object: *neighbour-truth* — an account given in public by someone who will go on living on the same hill as the people he names and the people he names them to. That kind of truth is rival and local: it's expensive to produce in roughly the proportion that it's useful to the people nearby, it can make cohabitation harder rather than easier, and its value depends almost entirely on the audience and on what happens the morning after the hearing, not on whether it lands in a final report. If that's right then amnesty is the wrong currency. You don't want to wipe the slate; you want to return the speaker to the community with a visible mark on him and a shared script for what he did — which looks much less like amnesty and much more like a sentence discount, public local proceedings, and compulsory attendance.

The case I want it run against is Rwanda's gacaca courts. The facts as I have them (correct me if I've got something badly wrong, and don't invent numbers): after the 1994 genocide there were far too many suspects for the ordinary courts or the ICTR, so from 2001 the government revived a customary form of neighbourhood assembly into a national system of lay courts — on the order of twelve thousand of them, several hundred thousand accused (I want to say around 850,000, but don't hold me to it), wrapping up around 2012. Judges were locally elected lay people; hearings were public and held where the crimes happened; confessing, pleading guilty and repenting bought a substantially reduced sentence, often served as community service in the same community; the alleged planners, leaders and notorious killers were reserved to the ordinary courts and the ICTR; crimes by the RPF side were outside the mandate.

What I'd like:

1. Set the mechanism out carefully — who's the principal, who's the agent, what's the currency, what counts as disclosure, what's the verification problem — and then say what the archive-truth reading predicts gacaca should have looked like and produced.
2. Which features of gacaca does that reading explain, and which does it misdescribe? I'm thinking especially of: leniency rather than amnesty; the local public audience and lay judges; the sheer volume and the correspondingly thin procedure; the exclusion of the organisers; the official narrative the process is often said to have installed (Tutsi as the only victims, Hutu as the only perpetrators).
3. Does excluding the planners break the trade, or is it what makes the trade possible? Arguably the thing amnesty-for-truth is supposed to buy is exactly the command structure, and gacaca structurally could not buy it.
4. Where is the theory silent rather than merely incomplete? Can it say anything about who bears the costs of truth-telling (witnesses threatened, survivors attacked after testifying), about the pace-versus-quality trade-off, about the state being itself a party to the violence, or about whether any of it actually improved cohabitation?
5. Run it the other way: does gacaca confirm the amnesty-for-truth model, is it an anomaly, or does it show that amnesty was never the load-bearing part? And be brutal about my archive-truth / neighbour-truth distinction — is it doing any real work, or just relabelling? Tell me what would falsify it.

Please answer in British English. And where you're not confident about a fact, just say so plainly instead of guessing — I'd much rather have the gap marked than a figure made up. If you think the whole framing is confused, say that too, I can take it.
