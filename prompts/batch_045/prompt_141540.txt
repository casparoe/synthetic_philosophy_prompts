Hi — I'm a PhD student in linguistics (well, linguistic anthropology, sort of), and I've got a pattern in my own data that I can't find a name for. It's possible somebody has named it and I just don't read the right literature; it's also possible nobody has and I need to coin something for chapter 3 before my committee asks. Either way, I don't want just a label. I want to know what the thing actually is, what nearby things it isn't, and where its edges are.

Setup, briefly. I've been working on the fishing-gear vocabulary of a small island community (I'd rather not name it, ethics stuff). The microdomain is terms for gill nets. There are four, which I'll call pila, taru, méska and won — pseudonyms; in my fieldnotes they're glossed as 'fine', 'middle', 'coarse' and 'very coarse'. The underlying dimension is close to one-dimensional: mesh size, with target species riding along on top of it.

The thing I keep tripping over:

(1) In a sorting task with twelve nets spanning 20–60 mm mesh, one consultant puts the pila/taru cut at about 32 mm and the taru/méska cut at about 47. Two months later, same consultant, a different twelve nets spanning 40–95 mm: the cuts land around 58 and 79. Nothing about their fishing changed in between. Show them a 45 mm net in the second session and they call it pila without hesitating; remind them they called a 45 mm net méska last time and they aren't bothered — I get something like 'that one was with the small ones'. So the boundary isn't a threshold on mesh size. But they talk as if it is: 'a pila is the sardine net, it's the fine one'.

(2) It's asymmetric, which is the part I really can't place. When a much finer monofilament net showed up a few years ago, pila did not stretch down to cover it. People built a phrase instead — 'the new kind of pila', 'the thin-thin one'. But when a much coarser net came in, won just absorbed it, nobody said anything, and the word's upper end effectively vanished. My hunch is that the fine end is pinned to a function (a target species, a way of fishing) and the coarse end isn't pinned to anything, but I don't know what to do with that hunch or even whether it's the same phenomenon as (1).

(3) Two fishermen whose boats carry different ranges of nets will apply different words to the very same net, fast, and without treating the difference as a disagreement. 'For us that's a taru.' Which sounds contextualist, except it isn't like 'tall' or 'local', where there's at least sometimes something in the sentence, or something obvious in the situation, that the comparison class goes into. Here the comparison class is just whatever nets happen to be in play, and if I ask a consultant what the comparison class is they can't tell me — they insist the word picks out a kind of net.

What I think it isn't, so you don't waste time: it isn't the usual prototype / typicality-gradient / fuzzy-boundary story, because there aren't any typicality effects here, judgments are instant and categorical, and nobody hedges. It isn't ordinary vagueness or polysemy either, or not only that: the shifts are systematic and directional, not a sprinkle of borderline cases. And it isn't the plain comparative-adjective case, because there's no slot anywhere for the comparison class to go.

What keeps going round in my head is whether the right description is that these words don't encode thresholds at all. Maybe what they encode is a rank in a locally constructed ordering — roughly 'the finest of the nets currently in play' — so the content gets filled in by the situation rather than shifted by it. On that reading there's no stable core and no moving boundary, just an ordering plus an anchor, and the apparent stability of everyday usage is an artifact of the anchor rarely moving, because the set of gear a boat actually carries is pretty fixed. It also feels adjacent to (but not the same as) what people say about concepts that seem to get their content by being hooked up to something in the context, rather than by describing anything intrinsic to the thing itself.

So: does this configuration have a name of its own? If it does, which of the neighbors is it really, and how exactly does it differ from the ones I gestured at above? If it doesn't, I need to name it and define it, including what would and wouldn't count as an instance — and in particular whether (1), (2) and (3) are one phenomenon or three. And I'd really like a way to tell the 'rank in a local ordering' story apart from a 'fixed threshold plus a moving anchor' story: what would distinguish them, and is there any case, actual or constructable, where they give different predictions? I can definately build a follow-up elicitation around whatever the discriminating case is, so be as concrete as you can.
