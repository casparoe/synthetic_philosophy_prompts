I spent forty-one years as a population ecologist — salmon and seabird monitoring at first, then multi-model inference and risk assessment for a state agency — and in retirement I have joined an evening reading group that has, by a route I could not now reconstruct, arrived at moral uncertainty. I do not want the debate explained to me again. I want it restated in the vocabulary I actually think in, and I want the restatement to be honest, which in my experience means being explicit about where it fails rather than quietly papering over the gap with a plausible-sounding sentence.

The vocabulary I am fluent in and would like used as the target language: multi-model inference and model averaging (AIC weights, posterior model weights, ensembles); the distinction between parameter uncertainty, structural uncertainty and model discrepancy; risk versus Knightian or deep uncertainty, together with the tools that go with the latter — robust satisficing, minimax regret, info-gap robustness; sensitivity analysis and elasticity of a recommendation to an input I cannot measure; bet-hedging and diversified strategies under environmental variability; value of information, expected value of perfect versus partial information, and adaptive management as a way of buying information with action; and the ordinary machinery of decision trees with probabilities attached to the branches. I can read a likelihood, a posterior and an elasticity table without help. My philosophy is three months of one evening a week and nothing before that, so please do not assume I know any term of art, but do assume I will notice if a piece of arithmetic is doing work that has not been justified.

The case I want each translation tested on, since a translation that has not been made to compute something has not been made at all. My wife and I have a modest estate and no children. Three candidate dispositions: fund the restoration of a coastal wetland the two of us have worked on for thirty years; give the same sum to an effective global health charity; leave it to my niece, who will in all likelihood spend it retraining as a pilot. The theories my group is treating as live disagree about every pairwise ranking here, and they disagree in kind and not merely in magnitude: one ranks by total welfare produced, one by priority to the worse off, one by special obligations and permissible partiality towards one's own, one treats the wetland's non-anthropogenic value as not commensurable with welfare at all, and one is not a first-order theory at all but a theory about what one ought to do when one is unsure which first-order theory is true. I want that last one handled rather than set aside.

What I would like delivered.

One. Restate, in my vocabulary, the candidate answers the reading group has labels for — the my-favourite-theory rule, maximise expected choice-worthiness, and the variance-averse or hedging variants of the latter — together with at least one candidate built for deep uncertainty rather than risk, since it is not obvious to me that uncertainty about which moral theory is true is the kind of uncertainty to which probabilities can honestly be attached. For each, say what the analogue of the quantity being maximised or satisfied is, where the probabilities come from, and which of my existing tools it is genuinely the same argument as.

Two. Work the estate case through in that vocabulary, and in doing so confront the problem I have not seen handled: the theories produce choice-worthiness on scales that are not obviously comparable, so any weighted sum needs a normalisation rule. Two rules suggest themselves to me from my own work. Under the unit-range rule, each theory's scale is stretched so that the best and worst available acts differ by one. Under the unit-variance rule, each theory's scale is rescaled to unit variance across the feasible acts. State which rule the aggregation needs, whether the two rules can disagree about the ranking of my three options, and — this is the part I care about — whether the recommendation can change when an option I would never choose is added to or removed from the menu. In ecology a properly specified decision analysis is not sensitive to the inclusion of options one will never take, and if the moral case is sensitive in that way I want to know whether that is a defect in my normalisation proposals, in the very idea of aggregating across theories, or in the expectation that there is a single ranking to be had.

Three. Tell me what plays the role of data. In my work, model weights are posteriors: they come from out-of-sample predictive success against a system that is really there, and they move when evidence moves them. Say what the moral analogue of an out-of-sample test is, what observation would shift a second-order probability from 0.4 to 0.6, and whether there is anything in the philosophers' discussion that corresponds to updating at all. If there is not, say plainly whether that makes the model-averaging framing decorative — an idiom borrowed from a case where it earns its keep — or whether the framing survives because the weights are doing some other job, such as expressing how seriously I take a view rather than how likely I take it to be true.

Four. Handle the fixed point. If one of the live theories already prescribes how to act under moral uncertainty, then aggregating across the set that contains it appears to require knowing the answer before one computes it, which in my field would make me look for a fixed point and then ask whether one exists and whether it is unique. Say whether this is a real structural feature of the problem or a confusion my group has manufactured by putting a first-order and a second-order theory in the same set, and tell me what my vocabulary has to say about the difference.

Five. The residue report, in three grades. Which claims of the moral-uncertainty debate translate into my vocabulary cleanly, which translate only with distortion that you should identify precisely rather than hide, and which cannot be expressed in it at all. I have my own suspicion about the last category: multi-model inference in ecology is licensed by there being a real system that the models approximate, and it is not obvious to me what the averaging is for if there is no truth of the matter about which moral theory is correct. Tell me whether that suspicion is on target, and more generally whether the residue indicts my vocabulary or shows that something in the original debate was less clear than it looked — or whether it shows something else, which I would rather hear than a tidy verdict.

Two constraints. Please do not give me citations, reading lists or names to look up; I have no library access worth speaking of and would rather have the argument. And where your target vocabulary forces an assumption that the moral debate does not grant — that values are commensurable, that expectations are the right thing to take, that theorising aims at a truth, that the action set is fixed in advance — mark it as an import rather than using it silently. I would rather have three unresolvable gaps labelled than one smooth paragraph.
