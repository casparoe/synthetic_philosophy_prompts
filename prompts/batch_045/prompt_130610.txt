I teach a second-year module on philosophy of science, and the last lecture of term is on reduction and emergence. I want to close it with a poem I can read aloud, something that will land on first hearing with no handout. My idea is a sestina, because its machinery already makes the point I have been making all term, and I would like the form rather than the vocabulary to carry the argument.

The six end-words should each name a higher-level or functional kind — the sort of thing a special science, or ordinary life, picks out — that is genuinely multiply realizable: one and the same kind across physically heterogeneous cases. Pain, money, a knot, a wave, a game, life itself would do if you can make them work (they need not rhyme), but choose a better set if one occurs to you. The requirements are that each of the six can be said truly of things with nothing interesting in common at the level of particles, and that each stanza supplies a different physical basis for at least some of them: a nervous system, and then something with no nervous system at all; something made of cells, and then something made of none; gold, then paper, then entries in a ledger. Keep the standard rotation, so that the word closing one stanza opens the next and so on, and let the rotation be felt as the argument: what I want the students to experience is the same word arriving again in a new position while every physical thing around it has been replaced — the word is the only thing that survives the turn.

Please keep the honest version of that argument. What multiple realizability tells against is reduction by bridge laws or type-identities, not the claim that each particular event is a physical one, and the poem should not blur the two.

Then I want the envoi to do something harder. Across its three lines the six words have to come back together, and there the worry I end on should surface: if every physical event already has a sufficient physical cause, what causal work is left for the words that keep returning? That is the exclusion problem, Jaegwon Kim's version of it, and I would rather the lines posed it than answered it — the lecture is supposed to end with the students arguing, so no smuggled resolution in the tone, and no rescue by a metaphor about levels.

Two last constraints. There should be no technical vocabulary in the poem beyond the six words themselves and no philosopher's name in it, so that it works for someone who has only just heard the lecture. And it should be right as well as good: a colleague in the field should find the philosophy correct rather than gestured at, even where the poem is being playful.
