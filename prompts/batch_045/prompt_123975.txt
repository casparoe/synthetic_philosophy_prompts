You are advising the research director of a technical AI safety group that has committed, for the next two years, to debate-style scalable oversight as one of its two main research lines, where a weaker judge adjudicates between two systems that argue for competing answers. The budget under discussion is small: about eight researcher-years, plus enough compute to run experiments with current open-weight models, but no access to frontier closed models and no ability to test protocols with systems substantially more capable than the judge. The point of the agenda you produce is a go/no-go decision: at the end of the two years, leadership will use it to decide whether to keep the line, shrink it, or shut it down and move the people to some other oversight approach. So a question is worth working on partly insofar as its answer would still be informative if the group ends up switching methods.

Before you begin, three distinctions I want you to keep separate, because they are not standard in the way this topic is usually framed and I do not want them collapsed into each other:

1. Verification-depth gap. For a given question distribution, compare the checking effort a fixed judge needs in order to reach some confidence level in a directly submitted answer against the checking effort the judge needs to reach that same confidence by working through a debate tree. Call the ratio the verification-depth gap. Debate is only doing genuine amplifying work where that gap is large; where it is near one, the protocol is mostly overhead.

2. Credibility-matching obfuscation. The usual worry is that a dishonest debater hides an error inside a long or technical-looking argument. There is a second failure mode: the honest debater is driven to make a simple correct answer look complicated, padding it with steps, caveats, or formalism, because a judge unable to distinguish depth from mere laboriousness rewards whichever argument looks more worked-through. Here the debate fails even though no error is hidden: simplicity has stopped being credible.

3. Deferred adjudication. Suppose a judge's verdict is not scored at the time of the debate but settled much later, either by eventual empirical outcomes or by a more capable successor system auditing the transcript. Obfuscation that cannot survive later audit may be a very different problem from obfuscation that can, and the two may deserve different amounts of the group's effort.

Given all that: which three open questions about obfuscated arguments and the limits of debate should this group work on, and in what order? I want the ordering defended rather than asserted. For each question, say what else it is upstream of, how tractable it is with eight researcher-years and no access to systems smarter than the judge, and what specific empirical or theoretical result over the next eighteen months would move it up or down the list. Then name one or two lines of work in this area that you would explicitly deprioritize, and explain why their answers would change neither the go/no-go decision nor anything downstream of it.

Work directly from the distinctions above instead of from how the problem is conventionally presented, and do not cite, survey, or recommend readings. Aim for roughly a thousand words, in American English.
