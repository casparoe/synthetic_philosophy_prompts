I work in the evaluation unit of a public agency that funds and rates several hundred independent providers of a service whose quality nobody knows how to measure directly — take it to be vocational retraining for displaced workers. Our rating regime has grown by accretion over about fifteen years, and each layer was added as a repair after some gaming episode:

1. A dashboard of fifteen indicators — completion, placement at six months, earnings at twelve months, learner satisfaction, employer satisfaction, and a set of process measures — published in full, with no thresholds attached to any single one;
2. Risk adjustment of each outcome indicator for the characteristics of the intake a provider takes on, with the adjustment model published, intended to remove the incentive to select easy cases;
3. Short-notice inspections covering matters that are not on the dashboard, whose findings are written up in narrative form and not given a score;
4. A single overall grade, A through D, computed from a published weighted formula over the dashboard indicators together with a summary of the inspection findings, and attached both to funding and to reputational consequences.

What puzzles me is that the bundle appears to contain machinery for defeating Goodhart's law and, sitting on top of it, a device for recreating exactly the thing the machinery was built to defeat. The dashboard replaced a single placement target that had produced obvious abuse. The composite grade was then added because no minister, user, or journalist will ever read fifteen indicators. But the composite is one published specification with one set of weights, and my tentative argument is this: fifteen indicators can in principle constrain a provider in fifteen directions, whereas a weighted average of them constrains it in one, and one constraint is precisely what can be gamed. I also suspect the point is worse than that — the effective number of constraints is the number of *independent* directions of variation among the indicators, and most of ours are correlated, so it may have been three or four all along. The same pattern appears elsewhere: risk adjustment was introduced to stop selection, and what it has produced is a great deal of work on how cases get classified, a disputes process, and an audit function to police the classification. Each repair seems to relocate the gaming rather than reduce it.

The distinctions I have been working with, which I would like you to use and to criticise, are three failure modes. Channel gaming: the agent acts on the measurement channel rather than on the measured quantity, by reclassifying, recoding, or removing a case from the denominator. Effort reallocation: a genuine improvement along the measured axis bought by degradation along axes nobody measures. Construct erosion: optimising the proxy damages the very quantity the proxy was tracking, so the number keeps rising while the thing itself declines. It seems to me that the third is invisible in principle from inside any fixed set of indicators, and that our inspections — the only component that could in principle detect it — may be unable to, because inspectors work to a known framework and providers can maintain permanent compliance with it.

I would like the implicit theory reconstructed. Specifically:

What theory of measurement does the bundle as a whole presuppose — about what a proxy is, about whether a proxy's validity has a domain within which it holds and outside which it fails, about whether the thing being rated is a magnitude, a cluster of partly independent goods, or something essentially contested? And what theory of the providers does it presuppose: what must the designers believe about how agents respond to a published rule for any of this to be worth doing at all? Is the bundle best read as resting on a claim about measurement or on a claim about agency, and do its parts rest on the same one?

Then the sorting. Which of these commitments does the regime strictly require, and which does it merely sit well with? Does it require that the fifteen indicators jointly exhaust what matters about a provider, or only that they cover the dimensions most at risk? Does the risk-adjustment step require that provider quality and case mix be genuinely separable — and if so, does the weighting in the composite grade require the same decomposability, or a different one? Where do the components pull against each other, and which single component would a theorist who wanted the rest to be coherent have to give up?

Finally, the counterfactual. Suppose we accepted the skeptical view: that for a concept as thick as a good retraining provider there is no proxy stable under sustained optimisation and no fact of the matter about the relative weights. What would the agency concretely have to stop doing? Would it have to abandon the published grade, the published indicators, or the consequences attached to them — and if the honest answer is that it must go on publishing numbers it knows are being degraded while it continues to fund providers, what is the justification for that? Do not just tell me what the sceptic believes; tell me what this institution would have to look like if the sceptic were right, and where it would lose something real.

Please also say plainly whether you read this regime as an answer to Goodhart's law or as an accommodation with it.
