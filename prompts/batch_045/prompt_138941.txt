I've been reading a long-form piece in a science-and-ideas magazine about whether moral status admits of degrees, and I can't tell how much of its summary of the position to trust. The piece is sympathetic, and it reads as though it were written by someone who knows the material, but a couple of sentences strike me as doing more work than they should, and I don't have the background to say which. Here is the passage, condensed but close to the original wording:

> The old picture drew a bright line: a being was either a person, with full moral status, or a thing, with none. The dimensional view rejects that. Moral status is a matter of degree, and the degree is set by how much experience there is — by three quantities that researchers now think can be measured independently: *bandwidth*, how much of the world can show up for a creature as good or bad at a single time; *intensity*, how much any one of those states matters to the creature, how hard it grabs the creature's attention and motivation; and *temporal depth*, how far a state reaches into the creature's past and future, whether a pain is merely present-tense or is taken up into a self extended in time. A hoverfly scores low but not zero on all three, a mouse higher, a human higher still, so the three of us differ in moral status in the way a small heap differs from a large one. Nor is any of this controversial among people who work on the science. A decade of work has settled that insects have valenced states: bees that have been shaken go on to treat ambiguous cues as though they expect the worst, and injury responses in flies are modulated by the same neurotransmitter systems that modulate pain in us. Once you grant that, the gradations follow automatically. The hard cases people like to raise turn out not to be hard. A thermostat has a target and corrects its own error, so strictly speaking it has a little of what it takes, though so little that the difference never matters in practice; a fetus or a patient in a persistent vegetative state simply sits at some point or other on the same scale. And the view doesn't leave us helpless, because on its own arithmetic the losses add: a million mosquito deaths are a million small wrongs and come to the same as one large one. What the view does entail — and this is where most readers balk — is that there is no defensible stopping point between the hoverfly and the thermostat. Anyone who accepts graded status is on a slope that ends either in worrying about the feelings of their coffee machine or in drawing an arbitrary line that is no better than the bright line they started with.
