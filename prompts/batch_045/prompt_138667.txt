I run a fortnightly lunchtime "logic club" at the software company I work at — mostly backend engineers, totally fine with induction and recursion, very allergic to anything that smells like a word game. We get 40 minutes and a whiteboard, and somebody always brings a laptop and tries to code the problem up. Last month we did the two-envelope thing and it went badly, so I want next session to be tighter. I've got the surprise exam paradox in mind and I don't need it explained to me — I need a plan for explaining it to them, built around the fact that they will absolutely try to brute-force it.

First structural question: should I actually perform the announcement? My idea is to open the session by telling them, straight-faced, that I'm going to spring a short quiz on them at one of the next five club meetings and that they won't be able to work out which one in advance, then let them do the backwards induction out loud, watch it appear to rule out all five dates, and then spring the quiz at meeting three anyway. Is that worth 20 of my 40 minutes, or does staging it confuse the issue — the puzzle is about whether that announcement can be true and known, not about whether I'm a clever riddler? If I do stage it, what do I have to say immediately afterwards so the lesson doesn't turn into "haha, got you"?

Second: I want to split "surprise" into two things before anybody touches the induction — not-feeling-startled versus not-deducible-from-the-announcement-plus-the-days-already-known-to-be-clear. My instinct is that if I don't make them pick one out loud first, half the room runs the argument on the psychological notion and concludes nothing is ever surprising, the other half runs it on the deducibility notion and concludes no exam can happen, and then they talk past each other for the rest of the session. Is that the right order, and which of the two should be the one carrying the session?

Third, the crack. I think the argument breaks at the very first step, eliminating the last day: to deduce "it must be Friday" on Thursday evening you still have to be entitled to trust the announcement, and that's precisely what goes wobbly once Friday is the only date left. But I'm not confident and I don't trust myself to say it cleanly. Where in the sequence should the diagnosis go, and what should I pointedly refuse to open up at this level (I want to keep the Moore's-paradox / knowability can of worms shut or I'll lose the room entirely)?

Misconceptions I'm expecting: that it's just a self-reference trick like the liar and there's nothing to see; that the resolution is simply that the teacher was lying or the announcement was false; and — the one I'm most worried about — someone enumerates the five dates, writes a predicate for surprise, gets an answer and thinks the thing is settled. I think what to say there is that surprise isn't a property of a date but of a date relative to an information state, so their enumeration has nowhere to put the information state, but I want that in under two minutes with no epistemic logic. What else would engineers in particular bring that I haven't listed?

Last thing: the carrying case. I was thinking of shrinking to a two-day week first, where the induction is one step and the twist is visible immediately, then expanding to five — but I could also run five first and shrink later. Which order and why? And tell me what that example has to have and, more importantly, not have: my worry is that if I let them model it with a die roll or coin flip the surprise condition quietly collapses into low probability and the whole puzzle evaporates. Finally, give me one question for the last three minutes whose wrong answers are informative, so I can tell whether they've actually got the puzzle rather than just learned a bar trick.
