I have no formal training in any of this — I work as a court reporter, and I got pulled into the subject after our housing co-op's last election produced a result that almost everybody disliked. Since then I have been reading about it on my own, and I have arrived at something that I suspect is too easy, which is why I am writing to you.

Everything I have read puts Arrow's result as a claim about systems in which each voter puts the options in order: no such system can satisfy all four of the conditions — unrestricted domain, the Pareto condition, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship — at the same time. If the impossibility only gets a grip on systems that use rankings, then it looks to me as though the way out is simply not to use rankings. The conclusion that there is no perfect voting system would then be a fact about ranked ballots rather than a fact about voting.

So I drafted a rule for the co-op. Each member scores every option from 0 to 10 instead of ordering them. The option with the highest average score wins, except that any option scored 0 by more than one third of the members is struck off the ballot before the averages are taken. My thought is that because these ballots carry intensity and not merely order, Arrow's theorem has nothing to say about this rule at all: it is not the sort of thing the theorem is about, so it is not caught by the impossibility, and I should be able to tune its parameters so that it violates none of the four conditions.

When I posted the idea, one reply said I had not escaped anything and had only pushed the difficulty into the numbers, which I could not follow; another said the rule simply invites people to exaggerate their scores, but that strikes me as a separate complaint about strategy rather than about Arrow's conditions. I would be grateful for help with three things:

1. Which of the four conditions, exactly, does my rule fail? I would like to see the actual pattern of ballots that makes the failure visible, not just be told that one of them must fail.
2. What would I need to change so that it fails none of them? Is there a version of a scored rule that really does satisfy unrestricted domain, the Pareto condition, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship together?
3. If, as the first reply suggests, my rule is not outside the result at all, what is it about replacing ranks with numbers that fails to get me out? That is: what would a scored ballot have to contain, over and above what a ranked ballot contains, for the impossibility genuinely to lapse?

I am not asking for a survey of alternative voting methods or for anything I would need to look up — I want the reasoning about my rule in particular. Please finish with a single final line in exactly this form, with nothing after it:

\answer{...}

where the blank contains a phrase of eight words or fewer naming the extra assumption my system has to make in order to be outside Arrow's result — the assumption that is not already present in the ballots themselves. If you think no such assumption is needed, write \answer{none}.
