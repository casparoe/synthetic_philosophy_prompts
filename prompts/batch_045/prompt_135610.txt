I've been useing this argument in my metaethics seminar for years and a colleague just poked a hole in it I can't close.

(1) A belief is justified only if the process that produced it is reliable, i.e. it tracks the truth about the domain it's about.
(2) Our moral beliefs come from a faculty shaped by natural selection, and selection tracks fitness, not moral truth: it would happily have installed whatever moral judgments got our ancestors more copies of their genes, whatever the moral facts were.
(3) The moral judgments we actually have are pretty much the ones the fitness-only story predicts, and unlike perception we have no way of checking them against the facts independently of the faculty that produces them.
(4) So our moral faculty isn't reliable, so no moral belief is justified.

Here's the hole. She says: run (1)–(4) on logic and induction instead. Selection shaped our inferential habits for the same fitness-only reasons; the habits we have are the ones you'd predict; and we can't check modus ponens against the logical facts except by using modus ponens. So:

(5) The reliability requirement in (1) is either a general requirement on any belief-forming process, or an extra burden we're piling on morality alone.
(6) If it's general, it takes out logic, memory and induction too — and then (4) can't be asserted either, since asserting it is an inference.
(7) If it's an extra burden, then (1)–(4) just assumes what it was supposed to show.
(8) So evolutionary debunking either collapses into global skepticism or never had a target.

I want to keep (2) and (3) — that's the interesting empirical bit — and I'd rather not loose logic to save morality. So: which step goes, and what does giving it up cost me? Two things I especially want your read on. First, (4) is itself a claim about what we have reason to believe, so does the argument saw off the normative branch it's sitting on, or is that just rhetoric? Second, a student of mine added a case I can't sort out: imagine we found out that the judgments selection actually favored were ones we now reject outright, say ruthless nepotism toward full siblings and indifference to everyone else. Does that divergence make our moral faculty look more trustworthy, because it isn't marching to fitness's drum — or less, because it's now producing beliefs selection had no interest in getting right? I get the feeling "reliable" is doing more than one job across (1), (4) and (6), but I can't nail the shift.
