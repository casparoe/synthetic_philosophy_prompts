**Question (10 marks, target length about 400 words)**

Goodman's *grue* is defined relative to a fixed time t: a thing is grue iff it is observed before t and green, or is not observed before t and blue. Since every emerald observed so far has been green, and every one of them is therefore grue as well, the evidence supports "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" alike; the two hypotheses diverge only in what they predict about emeralds first observed after t.

Now consider the same construction with a dataset cut instead of a time cut. Let D be the finite set of emeralds that have been examined so far; all of them were found to be green. Define: x is *trune* iff (x is in D and green) or (x is not in D and blue); x is *drune* iff (x is in D and blue) or (x is not in D and green).

(a) Explain why the evidence in D supports "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are trune" equally, and state what each hypothesis predicts about an emerald not in D. (3 marks)

(b) Suppose D is not a random sample: a collector assembled it by picking out stones that looked green to her. Does this selection bias make trune more projectible than grue, less projectible, or neither? Is the bias relevant to the riddle at all? Explain. (3 marks)

(c) A critic objects that trune raises no riddle, because trune is defined by a relation to a particular finite set and so is not a genuine qualitative property of the emerald, whereas green is. Reconstruct Goodman's reply, using the symmetry between the green/blue vocabulary and the trune/drune vocabulary, and say what he would appeal to instead in order to rule trune out. (4 marks)

**Student's answer**

The point of the grue construction is that any finite body of evidence is compatible with endlessly many hypotheses that agree on what has been observed and disagree about everything else, so the evidence alone never selects a hypothesis to project.

(a) Every emerald examined so far is in D and green, and each of them is therefore trune as well. On the straightforward enumerative view of confirmation that Goodman is attacking — a generalization is supported by its positive instances and unsupported by nothing in particular — the emeralds in D are equally positive instances of "all emeralds are green" and of "all emeralds are trune," so the data support both to the same degree and neither is contradicted. The two hypotheses agree on every emerald in D and disagree only outside it: the first predicts that an emerald not in D is green, the second that it is blue. The riddle depends on the cut falling at a boundary we have not yet crossed. If emeralds on the far side of the cut had already been examined and found green, the trune hypothesis would have been refuted outright, in the same way that a grue hypothesis whose time t lies in the past is refuted by the green emeralds observed since.

(b) The bias changes the quality of the evidence but not the structure of the riddle, and it makes trune neither more nor less projectible than grue. Because the collector chose stones that already looked green, the greenness of the stones in D is guaranteed by the procedure that produced the sample rather than by any regularity among emeralds; the fit between the data and "all emeralds are green" is therefore not surprising, and the data are weak support for both hypotheses, since "all emeralds are trune" predicts greenness within D just as well. But this is a separate defect from unprojectibility, and it is one the two hypotheses share. Had D been a perfectly random sample of emeralds, every examined emerald would still have been both green and trune, and we would face exactly the same choice. Cutting on membership in a set rather than on a date makes no relevant difference: the two predicates are gerrymandered in precisely the same way.

(c) The critic is mistaken, and Goodman's reply is that the symmetry runs in both directions. In the trune/drune vocabulary, green is itself the disjunctive, positional-looking predicate: a thing is green iff it is in D and trune, or not in D and drune. So trune can be defined in terms of green and blue, and green can equally be defined in terms of trune and drune, which means that the disjunctive or relational look of a predicate is an artifact of which vocabulary is treated as primitive rather than a fact about the property. What actually separates them, on Goodman's account, is entrenchment: green and blue are entrenched because they are familiar words that speakers and scientists have used an enormous number of times, whereas trune and drune are invented jargon that no one had used before this exercise, so unlike green they have no track record at all. That is what entitles us to project green and not trune. But since entrenchment is the only thing that separates the two hypotheses, and since entrenchment is merely a fact about what we happen to have said in the past, there is no principled reason to prefer green; the choice of what to project rests on nothing more than linguistic habit, and the new riddle accordingly collapses back into Hume's problem of justifying induction.
