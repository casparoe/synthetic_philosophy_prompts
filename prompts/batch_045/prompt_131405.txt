Before retiring I spent most of a career in experimental atmospheric physics, and I have lately been sitting in on — and helping to mark the written work for — a colleague's advanced undergraduate course on choice, games, and fairness. The mathematics I am comfortable with; what I want is a philosopher's ear for arguments that sound right but do not quite close. One problem sheet asked the students to compare Nash's bargaining solution with the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution on a pair of constructed examples. I would like an independent, point-by-point assessment of the answer transcribed below, written as if you were the grader of record.

The handout defines things as follows. A (two-person) bargaining problem is a pair (S, d): a closed, convex, comprehensive feasible set S of utility pairs (payoffs being von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities, so any lottery over agreements is itself an agreement), and a disagreement point d. The ideal point is a(S) = (a₁, a₂), where aᵢ is the largest utility player i could get in S while the other player receives at least their disagreement payoff. The Nash solution N(S) maximises (x₁ − d₁)(x₂ − d₂) over S. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution KS(S) is the maximal point of S lying on the segment that joins d to a(S). The axioms discussed are Pareto efficiency (PO), symmetry (SYM), invariance under positive affine transformations of either player's utility scale (INV), independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA: if S ⊆ T and the point selected for T lies in S, then the same point is selected for S), and monotonicity (M), which the handout states precisely. For a problem (S, d) and any utility level u that player j might receive, let h⁽ˢ⁾ᵢ(u) be the greatest utility player i can obtain in S while player j receives exactly u, with h⁽ˢ⁾ᵢ(u) = +∞ if player j cannot receive u anywhere in S. Then M says: if h⁽ᵀ⁾₂(u) ≥ h⁽ˢ⁾₂(u) for every u, then the solution must give player 2 at least as much in T as in S; and symmetrically, if h⁽ᵀ⁾₁(u) ≥ h⁽ˢ⁾₁(u) for every u, then it must give player 1 at least as much in T as in S. The handout also states the two characterisation theorems: PO + SYM + INV + IIA single out the Nash solution, and PO + SYM + INV + M single out the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution.

THE QUESTION

Two research groups are negotiating over joint use of an instrument. Utilities are as above and the disagreement point is d = (0, 0). Compare the two situations

S = convex hull of {(0, 0), (120, 0), (0, 120), (90, 75)}
T = convex hull of {(0, 0), (120, 0), (0, 120), (90, 75), (84, 84)}

(a) [10 marks] Compute the ideal point of each problem, and compute the Nash and Kalai–Smorodinsky outcomes for S. Show your working.
(b) [10 marks] Do the same for T.
(c) [8 marks] Does the passage from S to T test axiom M? State exactly whether each solution violates M on this pair of problems, and justify the answer from your computed numbers. Does the passage also test IIA?
(d) [6 marks] Which of the two solutions satisfies M in general? Give a reason, not merely a citation of the characterisation theorem.
(e) [6 marks] A member of the seminar objected that the verdict in part (c) is "merely an artifact of the way utility has been measured, and would vanish if the same situation were described with different utility functions." Assess this objection in three or four sentences.

Total: 40 marks.

THE ANSWER AS SUBMITTED

(a) In both problems each player can receive 120 while the other receives 0, so the ideal point is a = (120, 120) and d = (0, 0).

For S, the Pareto frontier consists of the segment from (0, 120) to (90, 75), which lies on y = 120 − x/2, and the segment from (90, 75) to (120, 0), which lies on y = 300 − 2.5x. The Nash solution maximises the product x·y. On the first segment x(120 − x/2) has its stationary point at x = 120, so the product increases throughout [0, 90] and is largest at (90, 75), where it equals 6750. On the second segment x(300 − 2.5x) has its stationary point at x = 60 and is therefore decreasing on [90, 120], so it too is largest at (90, 75). Hence N(S) = (90, 75).

For the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution I draw the segment from d to a, which here is the diagonal y = x, and take the point where it leaves S: x = 120 − x/2 gives x = y = 80, and for x > 80 the frontier lies below the diagonal. So KS(S) = (80, 80).

(b) T is the convex hull of S together with the new vertex (84, 84), so T properly contains S, and the ideal point is again (120, 120). On the frontier of T the new vertex is a kink. The product x·y there is 84·84 = 7056, which exceeds 6750 at (90, 75). To the left of the kink the frontier lies on y = 120 − (3/7)x, and x(120 − (3/7)x) increases up to x = 140, so on [0, 84] it is largest at the kink. To the right the frontier lies on y = 210 − 1.5x, and the product decreases on [84, 90]. Hence N(T) = (84, 84). The diagonal y = x leaves T exactly at the new vertex, since for x slightly above 84 the frontier is 210 − 1.5x, which lies below x. So KS(T) = (84, 84) as well.

(c) For each player i and each utility level u of the other player, the best payoff i can obtain while the other receives u is at least as large in T as in S; and no level u in [0, 120] is attainable for either player in one problem but not the other, so the convention about +∞ does not make the hypothesis fail. The hypothesis of M is therefore satisfied for both players, and M requires both players to be weakly better off in T than in S. For KS: (80, 80) → (84, 84), which satisfies M. For Nash: (90, 75) → (84, 84), so player 1 loses six units and Nash violates M on this pair. This is consistent with the handout's remark that Nash satisfies IIA: the point (84, 84) is not in S, since the frontier of S at x = 84 is y = 120 − 42 = 78, so IIA has nothing to say about the move from S to T. What the example illustrates instead is the handout's further remark that no solution can satisfy IIA and M together, given PO, SYM and INV, since the two axioms characterise two different solutions.

(d) This is not special to the example. By the characterisation theorem, KS satisfies M in general. The reason can be read off the construction: the KS outcome is the point at which the segment from d to the ideal point a leaves the feasible set. When the feasible set grows, a can only move outwards, and the point at which that segment leaves the set can only move outwards with it, so neither player's payoff can fall — which is precisely what M demands.

(e) The seminar objection should be rejected. Neither solution depends on how utility is scaled: both are invariant under any increasing recalibration of either player's utility function, because all they use is the ordering of the feasible agreements together with the position of d and of the ideal point relative to the frontier. The numbers above are therefore doing no work, and the verdict in (c) is not an artifact of the choice of utility scale: it would survive any redescription of the situation.
