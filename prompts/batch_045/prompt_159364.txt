I'm drafting a paper on the semantic paradoxes for a graduate seminar, and before I build a whole argument on top of it I want somebody to stress-test the package of commitments I've drifted into. My advisor says the set sounds "Kripkean with minimalist sympathies," but I can't tell whether that's a coherent description or a polite way of saying I'm relying on something I've officially disavowed. Here, as carefully as I can state it, is where I've landed:

(1) Semantic closure. There is one natural language, containing one truth predicate that applies to the sentences of that very language. I reject any solution that stratifies the language into object-language and metalanguage levels.

(2) Gaps. Paradoxical sentences such as the Liar are neither true nor false. The gapped ones are, roughly, the sentences whose evaluation never bottoms out in non-semantic facts, and I take the minimal fixed point to be the intended model — I want that to be a correct answer about how the language actually works, not a convenient pick among equally good fixed points.

(3) Ascent neutrality (my own label; I haven't seen it defended anywhere, I just keep finding myself assuming it). For any declarative sentence S, moving back and forth between S and "S is true" never changes what a speaker is committed to, never changes assertability conditions, and never turns an unproblematic sentence into a problematic one.

(4) Deflationism. Truth has no hidden nature and does no explanatory work; its whole content is its logical and expressive role. I want to say this uniformly, including about sentences that themselves talk about truth.

(5) Classical logic everywhere except where paradox forces otherwise. Full bivalence and classical semantics for all non-semantic discourse, including claims we will never settle — about the deep past, or about what is happening now in distant galaxies — and I want to keep the ordinary classical rules of inference, including modus ponens, conditional proof, and unrestricted truth-introduction and truth-elimination.

(6) Two case verdicts. The Truth-Teller ("This very sentence is true") is neither true nor false. So is the sentence "This sentence is either false or neither true nor false," and I'm prepared to say the same of any revenge sentence built on that pattern: I don't think iterating the gap response ever generates a genuine contradiction.

What I'd like is an audit rather than a summary. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which only look like it? For each real tension, tell me what would have to give, what that would cost me given the rest of the set, and — importantly — rank the tensions by severity instead of listing them as equally serious; then tell me the single revision that would clean up the most. I have a vague sense that (3) is doing more work in my thinking than I've noticed, but I can't locate the trouble and I'd rather not build on a bad foundation. One thing to watch for: I take Curry's paradox seriously as a separate problem from the Liar, and I haven't decided whether the gap treatment even touches it, since it doesn't seem to need negation the way the Liar does — if that's where the worst problem actually sits, say so. Please don't spend the answer telling me which named position in the literature my package resembles or what I should read; I want the audit itself.
