The question (20 marks). A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time. If the first heads falls on toss n, you receive (−1)^{n+1}·2^n/n dollars: a gain of $2 if n = 1, a loss of $2 if n = 2, a gain of $8/3 if n = 3, and so on. This is the Pasadena game.
(a) [6 marks] Evaluate the sum over n ≥ 1 of P(n)·payoff(n), and the corresponding sum with |payoff(n)| in place of payoff(n).
(b) [8 marks] Does the game have a well-defined expected value? Say how your diagnosis compares with that of the St Petersburg game, in which you receive 2^n dollars if the first heads falls on toss n.
(c) [6 marks] One response holds that the expectation is simply ln 2: the outcomes come with a natural order, by toss number, and the sum taken in that order is ln 2. Assess this response, and say whether the difficulty generalises to every gamble with infinitely many possible outcomes.

The student's answer.
(a) The probability that the first heads falls on toss n is 2^{-n}, so the probability-weighted sum is the alternating harmonic series, Σ (−1)^{n+1}/n, which converges to ln 2. The probability-weighted sum of absolute payoffs is Σ 1/n, which diverges.
(b) Since Σ 1/n diverges, the expected value is not well-defined: the positive part and the negative part of the series are both infinite, so there is no finite number the expectation could be. In that sense the expectation is infinite. This is exactly the situation in the St Petersburg game, where payoffs also grow as fast as the probabilities shrink, so that game's expectation is likewise not well-defined and is infinite. The two paradoxes therefore share a single moral: unbounded payoffs make expected value useless, and a rational agent should set expected value aside in both cases.
(c) The natural-ordering response is, I think, correct. The outcomes are given to us indexed by toss number, so that ordering is not an arbitrary choice of ours, and taken in that order the series sums to ln 2. The Riemann rearrangement theorem only shows that other orderings yield other answers; it does not show that the game lacks a value. So the expected value is ln 2 after all. The problem is also quite general: whenever a gamble has infinitely many possible outcomes, its expectation is an infinite sum, and rearrangement is always available, so no such gamble has a well-defined expected value. That includes the St Petersburg game, where the sum merely happens to come out positive and infinite.
Finally, none of this touches expected utility theory: if the utility of money is bounded, the relevant series converges absolutely and the puzzle disappears.
