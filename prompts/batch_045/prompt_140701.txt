Hey — I do the science-and-ideas beat for a magazine, and once a year I help put on a small end-of-year night (70ish people, half ML folks, half poets, one very patient bartender) where instead of panels we do short readings built around a single idea. This year's idea is the symbol grounding problem, and I want to close the night with a poem instead of a quote from some panelist, so what I need is a sestina. Yes, a sestina: six stanzas of six, three-line envoy, standard rotation, and I'd like the six end-words to be red, hand, ground, token, meaning, cause. The rotation should be doing the real philosophical work — each stanza tries to cash one of the words in for something that isn't a word (red as something actually seen, hand as something that can push, cause as something that makes things happen), and each time the attempt slides off into another one of the six before the stanza ends, so the reader gets the feeling of being forwarded from entry to entry in a dictionary. Please also keep the six end-words out of the rest of the poem entirely: they may only ever appear at the end of a line, so that hand and ground are always the last thing reached and never actually used. Light inflections like hands or grounding are fine.

The bit I keep tying to explain in prose, and the reason I want a poem rather than a paragraph: I've started calling it a grounding debt in my notes — every time you define a word with another word you take out a small loan that somebody eventually has to repay by actually bumping into the world — and the live question is whether that debt ever has to be repaid, or whether a system that has never bumped into anything but has mapped, very exactly, the shape of how every word leans on every other word already has everything it needs. I really dont want the poem sneaking in a cheap payoff: no love, no child's hand, no the body knows. A poem made entirely out of words cant point by claiming to point, and if the closing tercet dissolves into warm humanism I'll have to cut the whole thing.

So leave the envoy genuinely undecided. The two-end-words-per-line convention means hand and ground will land next to each other for the first time in the poem, and I want it to be unclear whether that's the debt finally being settled or just one more shuffle of the same six tokens. The robotics crowd should recognize the specific failure mode rather than a name-check of the Chinese room, and the poets should be able to follow it with none of that background. Under a page, and no lo!-style archaic filler — I'd rather it sound like something someone could actually read out loud in a bar with a band setting up behind them. If it helps: I'd imagined the first stanza trying to ground red in some object on a table and the second trying to get cause to do genuine work, but go wherever the form wants.
