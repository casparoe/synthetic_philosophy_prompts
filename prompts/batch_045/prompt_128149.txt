I'm part of a small reading group — mostly cognitive-science graduate students, plus two type designers — that has been working through the Letter Spirit material, the project of building a program that can look at a font and invent new letterforms in that font's style. The question we keep circling is deceptively simple: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? Last week a friend of mine, a jazz musician who sat in on the session, claimed that the whole problem dissolves under the right analogy. A letterform, he said, is exactly like a tune. A melody stays the same melody whether it is played fast or slow, in C or in F-sharp, on a piano or on a kazoo; what survives every performance is a pattern of relations — the intervals and the rhythmic skeleton — rather than any particular pitch, timbre or dynamic. Likewise, he argued, every 'a' is a single abstract structure that survives every change of weight, contrast, serif treatment and ductus, and a font is simply a particular performance or arrangement of that structure. The conclusion he wanted to license is fairly strong: because a competent listener hears the same tune through wildly different arrangements, the essence of a letter must be equally real and equally detachable from its surfaces, so it ought to be possible in principle to extract that essence and to improvise new letters in a given style the way a player improvises a new chorus over a standard — the remaining difficulty is engineering, not conceptual. Several of us found this persuasive in the moment and suspicious afterwards, and we would like an audit rather than a verdict.

Could you lay the analogy out explicitly — a table of correspondences would be ideal — saying what plays the role of the notes, the intervals, the key, the instrumentation, the ornamentation and the performer's interpretation, and then what, on the letterform side, is supposed to be the score, the performance and the listener's recognition? Then I want the places where the mapping breaks, with some discipline about which disanalogies actually bear on the conclusion he wants and which are merely incidental noise, together with an account of which features of music the analogy quietly imports into the target — a determinate work, a notation, a clean work-versus-performance split — that letterforms may simply not have. Some of the worries we kicked around, so you can see where we are: a tune unfolds in time and is perceived sequentially, whereas a letterform is taken in all at once as a spatial Gestalt; there is no pre-existing score for 'a' that is independent of the performances, since the only data we have are other fonts, which are themselves performances, so the skeleton has to be inferred rather than read off; the parts of an 'a' — bowl, stem, terminal, aperture — do not seem to be individuated in advance of the whole letter in the way notes and intervals are individuated in advance of an arrangement; a melody tolerates an extraordinary amount of surface change and still counts as the same tune, whereas an 'a' stops being an 'a' after a fairly small amount of deformation, and the same stroke-skeleton can turn up in letters that are plainly different letters; and most tellingly, style in music looks like interpretation laid on top of a fixed structure, while in letterforms style seems to reach all the way down into the skeleton — a bold 'a' and a light 'a' from the same family differ in proportion and construction, not merely in instrumentation. If the analogy is shallower than he thinks, what would be a better comparison: is the identity of a letter more like the identity of a phoneme within a language, a word within an idiolect, or a gesture within someone's handwriting — something whose essence is settled by its contrasts with the other members of the system and by a generative disposition rather than by an intrinsic shape? And does the strong conclusion — that the essence is out there to be extracted, and that generating a new letter is just improvisation on a known theme — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? Please don't point us at anything to read; assume that no one in the room can look anything up, and give us the conceptual audit itself.
