I'm in a philosophy of mind course, we just did Mary's room and the knowledge argument, and I got into an arguement with a friend who's a third-year med student that I can't settle. Here's the thing I keep coming back to: medical training is built around the idea that some things just can't be learned out of a book. You can memorize every written description of what a heart murmur sounds like, or what an inflamed appendix feels like under your hand, or the exact colour a particular rash has, and the profession still will not let you near patients until you've actually heard, felt and seen enough of them — clinical rotations are mandatory, procedures get logged by count, and there's that old saying about see one, do one, teach one. At the same time the very same institutions treat text and recorded data as perfectly adequate for transmission elsewhere: you're expected to manage conditions you've never seen a case of, written guidelines are treated as enough to standardize care, recorded heart sounds and image atlases get used as substitutes for live patients, and there's a lot of enthusiasm for diagnostic systems that have never had a body or a sense organ at all.

So what does the hands-on requirement actually presuppose? My friend says it's basically the knowledge argument in institutional form — there are facts about what a murmur sounds like that you can only get by having the experience, and rotations exist to hand you those facts. I think that's too quick, and that the practice fits just as well with the boring reply that Mary doesn't learn a new fact at all, she just acquires abilities: recognizing, imagining, remembering. On that story what rotations build is a similarity space — knowing that this is the same murmur as that one, on a different stethoscope, in a noisy room, in a patient with slightly odd anatomy — which is a point about generalizing to new cases and not obviously about consciousness at all. My TA floated a third thing that I can't tell is deep or a total cheat: maybe rotations are really for calibration, i.e. learning when you don't know, and that would be a purely epistemic point with no metaphysics in it.

Can you reconstruct the implicit epistemology as cleanly as you can, and then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with — does the hands-on requirement really need there to be experiential facts that no amount of text could convey, or would an ability-first story, or a straight physicalist one, license exactly the same training regime? Is the practice even consistent with itself, given that the same institutions let written protocols, recordings, and body-less diagnostic systems stand in for experience in other places? And be concrete about the redesign: if we dropped the Mary-style reading and took the rival view seriously, what in medical training would you actually get rid of, and what would you keep for reasons that have nothing to do with consciousness? At the end, tell me what would change your mind about your own answer — what kind of argument or evidence would convince you the training requirement is (or isn't) real evidence for phenomenal facts — and where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
