I'm a retired general practitioner who has been sitting in on a fortnightly reading group on philosophy of mind at the local university, and we've spent the last two months on the cluster of ideas that get called predictive processing and the free energy principle. I've come away with an impression I can't quite put into words, and I'd like help locating it — or naming it, if it has no name.

The impression is this. Again and again the framework seems able to accommodate opposite outcomes by adjusting a single dial, and the dial's setting is read off the very behaviour that was supposed to be explained. First case: perception is described as a guess that the senses then correct. A subject fails to notice something obvious in front of them, and the account says that their guess was confident, so the senses got little say. Another subject reports something that isn't there at all, and the account says that their guess was confident, so it overrode the senses. Same machinery, opposite outcomes, one dial. Second case: one person changes their mind readily when the evidence shifts, another clings to a belief in the teeth of the evidence. The first is described as trusting the senses too much, the second as trusting them too little — but as far as I can make out, the only way anyone discovers how much someone 'trusts the senses' is by watching how readily they change their mind, so the description is fixed after the fact. Third case: I raised the old worry that if the whole point is to keep mismatch with the world to a minimum, the creature should curl up somewhere dark and unchanging — and the answer I got was that the creature's guess already contains the guess that it will be up and about and looking, so staying still would itself be a mismatch. That struck me as the same shape of move once more: whatever the creature ends up doing gets written back into what it was supposedly expecting. A friend in the group added that on this reading anything that manages to hold itself together — a thermostat, a puddle — counts as doing it too, and that this was offered to us as a feature rather than a problem.

One member said it's simply that the theory is unfalsifiable, and I don't think that is quite my complaint. I'm not worried, in the abstract, that no conceivable observation could refute it; I'm worried about a particular habit — redescribing whatever happened in terms of a quantity that is itself only ever measured by the happening — and about why that habit is so hard to police in this framework, compared with somewhere like evolutionary psychology or psychoanalysis, where people spot it quickly. So: is this one phenomenon or several? Does it have an established name in philosophy of science? A philosopher in the group muttered something about Duhem and Quine, but as I understand that idea it concerns saving a hypothesis by adjusting separate auxiliary assumptions, whereas here the adjustment lives inside the framework's own vocabulary and is available before any prediction has failed. Please tell me which of the neighbouring notions actually fits — unfalsifiability, rescue by auxiliary hypotheses, epicycle-adding, a degenerating research programme, a just-so story, or something I haven't heard of — and if none of them fits, coin a term, define it, and give me boundary cases. Most of all, I want to know what would distinguish a legitimate deployment of the move from an empty one: what sort of independent measurement, or what constraint on the dial, would make me stop worrying in a particular case?

Please answer in American English, in continuous prose with no bullet lists or headings, and in no more than 600 words. Lead with a one-sentence verdict on whether the phenomenon has an established name, and prioritise the carving over the labelling: say how it differs from its nearest neighbours and what test separates a good deployment from a vacuous one, rather than listing every candidate label you can think of.
