I'm writing a feature on how real institutions decide when their members disagree about which moral framework is correct: a foundation board, say, splitting its giving between global health and animal welfare while its members split their credences between utilitarianism, prioritarianism, and a view with agent-relative constraints. Everywhere I look in this area I find the same picture doing the work: treat moral theories as parties in a parliament, give each theory seats proportional to your credence in it, and let the assembly decide. I have watched people use it to conclude that hedging across theories is plainly rational and that anyone who simply acts on their favourite theory is being dogmatic, and I want the audit rather than the pitch.

Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the voter, of seat share, of a coalition, of vote-trading, of the legislation that comes out the other end — and then the places the mapping fails. The ones I keep tripping over: that parties have interests while theories have criteria of correctness; that assigning seat shares already presupposes comparisons of degrees of choice-worthiness across theories, which is most of what the exercise was supposed to deliver; that a parliament exists to accommodate persons fairly whereas here the question is supposed to be which act is correct; and that parliamentary output is binding on everyone in a way a decision is not.

One wrinkle I would like handled directly: an interviewee argued the picture only earns its keep when an institution faces a long sequence of decisions rather than a single one, because only then does a theory's seat share have anything to trade across. Does the analogy improve under repetition or fall apart faster? Finally: does the anti-dogmatism conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the parliament carrying it the whole time, and is there a comparison that does the job better — disagreement among experts, or diversification of a portfolio?
