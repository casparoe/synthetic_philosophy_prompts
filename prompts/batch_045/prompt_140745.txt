I am a journalist who has spent the last two years reporting a book on the science of well-being, and I have agreed to give the closing keynote at next month's annual meeting of a well-being research consortium. The room will be about sixty people: roughly twenty psychologists and psychometricians who measure experienced well-being by experience sampling and day reconstruction, a dozen happiness economists and policy staff who live off life-satisfaction scales and cost-benefit analysis, five or six philosophers of well-being, four or five contemplative-science researchers who study meditation, three addiction clinicians, and a scattering of programme officers from the foundations that fund all of it. These are people who have built instruments, defended them in public, and staked grants on the assumption that well-being is something you can put a number on and then improve. They will be polite. They will also be defending decades of work.

My thesis has two halves. The first is descriptive: the pattern this field keeps producing — big, reliable movement in global life-satisfaction reports with almost no corresponding movement in how people's days actually feel; the rapid return to affective baseline after windfalls and after bereavements; the difficulty of producing durable gains from positive-affect interventions; the flat relation between income and affect above a modest threshold — is not a set of puzzles awaiting better measurement. It is the pattern a certain view predicts, a view almost nobody in the field has taken seriously. Call it tranquilism, and let me stipulate the version I mean: the only things that make a life go better are the absence of craving and the absence of suffering, and positive pleasure, meaning, achievement and satisfied preference contribute nothing of their own. On that view the hedonic treadmill stops being a tragedy, the null results stop being failures, and the income plateau stops being a paradox. The second half is prescriptive: if that is right, the field is measuring and funding the wrong thing. What should be measured is the frequency, intensity and duration of craving and of suffering; on this view the flagship instrument — a ladder on which respondents rate their lives — is close to irrelevant to well-being, and the most valuable technology available to the field would be something that reliably quietens craving. To blunt the obvious reductios I plan to draw a distinction between craving proper and what I will call appetitive engagement: desire that is pleasant in the having, as in absorbed work, curiosity, erotic longing or ambition. My claim will be that tranquilism condemns only the former. I expect that distinction to be where the philosophers and the contemplative-science people aim. Several people in the room also know that the book comes out in the spring and that I have been privately urging a reallocation of foundation money toward craving reduction.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defence of the view. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get in that room — the ones this audience would raise, not the ones a philosophy seminar would raise — ordered by how much damage they would do if I handled them badly. For each one: the name or role of the person most likely to ask it, the question phrased the way that person would phrase it, in their vocabulary rather than mine; a reply I could deliver in about two minutes; and an honest note on where that reply is soft. I also want, separately: what I should concede in the first two slides rather than be forced to concede from the floor; which single reply has to land if the prescriptive half of the talk is to survive at all; the one objection that has real force and that I should probably concede outright rather than parry; and the question I am quietly hoping nobody asks — I have my own candidate for it, and I would like to see whether you arrive at it independently.

Two constraints. I do not want citations, a bibliography or a reading list; I need objections and replies, not sources, and I could not check them before the talk anyway. And please do not give me the generic objection that this is just Buddhism or Epicureanism with the metaphysics stripped out, unless you first convert it into the form the contemplative-science researcher in that room would actually put it — in her terms, against her own data, in a way I could not wave off with a sentence.
