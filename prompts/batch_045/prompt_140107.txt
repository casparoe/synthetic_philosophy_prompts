I've been reading a pop-sci book on quantum mechanics — one of those "everything you know is wrong" ones, I won't name it because I'd rather not get into whether the author is any good — and there's a section on the many-worlds interpretation that I cant tell its fair or not. Here's more or less what it says, compressed:

"Everett's many-worlds interpretation drops the collapse postulate: the wave function never collapses, so every possible outcome of a measurement actually happens, each in its own branch. This creates a problem with probability. If all the outcomes occur, the natural way to get probabilities is to count branches — but counting branches gives the wrong numbers, because an experiment with a 90/10 split still produces just two branches, so counting says 50/50, not 90/10. That is the Born rule problem, and it means many-worlds is in flat contradiction with experiment: unless Everettians can explain why probabilities track the squared amplitude rather than the number of branches, the theory is dead. The usual fix is a decision-theoretic argument: a rational agent who knows the universe is branching will place bets at odds given by the squared amplitude. But this is circular — betting odds are already probabilities, so the argument assumes exactly what it was supposed to derive. The more honest version just bites the bullet and says the squared amplitude is a 'measure of existence,' so a branch with twice the amplitude really does contain twice as many worlds, which makes branch counting come out right after all."

The branch-counting-gives-50/50 bit feels like it has to be a parody — surely nobody thinks Everettians just forgot about that — but I also can't locate where the real difficulty is supposed to be. The decision theory bit does sound like a dodge to me, though I'm prejudiced because I always find those arguments slippery. A friend who took a philosophy of physics class says the whole thing is really about self-locating uncertainty before a measurement and that the book never even mentions it, which makes me more suspicious, not less.
