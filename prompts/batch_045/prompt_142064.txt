The bit that bugs me is that nobody in the seminar has said what *kind* of counterfactual a "counterfactual of creaturely freedom" is supposed to be. We did similarity-ordering accounts briefly, but in my causation course last term we did the structural-equation / interventionist picture, where you evaluate a counterfactual by intervening on an exogenous variable and letting the model's equations churn. If I try to model a free agent that way, the agent's decision looks like a stochastic node — reasons-responsive but not determined — so what the model spits out for "what would S do in C" is a probability distribution over outcomes, not a single one.

So here's the thing I want to make into a position. Call it *distributional middle knowledge*: God knows the exact probability distribution over what S would do in C, but not which single thing S would do. My hunch is that distributional middle knowledge buys you most of what providence is supposed to need — you can stack circumstances, run risks knowingly, get broad outcomes to arbitrary confidence — but not meticulous control over individual free choices, and that Molinists quietly slide between the two versions because the strong single-outcome version only makes sense if something in the circumstances settles the stochastic element, which is determinism wearing a hat.
