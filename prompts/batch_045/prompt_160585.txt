Me and a friend had a three-hour argument last night about Bertrand's paradox and I've come away not knowing what I actually think. Not about the maths — we agree that the three standard setups give 1/2, 1/3 and 1/4 for the chance that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle — but about what follows from that. So I want to lay out my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and ask what principle, if any, I'm implicitly using, because right now I think I'm being inconsistent.

1. The chord question as stated, with no mechanism given. Verdict: no answer. Not 1/3, not an average of the three, nothing. The question is incomplete, and anyone who gives a number has quietly added information.

2. But: a straw of fixed length thrown onto a floor with a circle painted on it, from far enough away that I have no idea where it lands. Now I'm comfortable, and I definately want to say 1/2. My friend said this is what some Bayesian — Jaynes, he thought — argued from an invariance principle, and that it shows 1/2 is the answer to the original question too. I don't buy the second half, and I can't say why.

3. A cube from a factory. I'm told its edge length is somewhere between 1 and 2 cm and nothing else. Chance its volume is above 3.375? I want to say 1/2, and I feel fine about it, even though I know this is the same problem in a hat: spread your indifference over volume instead of over length and you get about 0.66. And my friend rubbed in the wierd part — if I'd been told the volume is between 1 and 8 instead of the edge between 1 and 2, my own reasoning hands me 0.66 for the very same machine. Same object, different sentence, different number. I don't know what to do with that.

4. I made up a family to check myself. Pick a radius uniformly, then put the chord's midpoint at distance r along it with density proportional to r^k. As k moves continuously, the answer slides continuously: 1/2 at k = 0, 1/4 at k = 1, and everything in between and beyond. This makes me more sure of verdict 1 — but I can't see why it doesn't also kill verdict 3, where the same trick slides things around too.

5. Coin of unknown bias, never tossed. Chance of heads on the first toss: 1/2, and I'm happy. Ten heads in a row, then chance the next is heads is 11/12: I was also happy, until my friend pointed out that other sensible symmetric priors give other numbers. Now I don't know which of those two verdicts I'd keep if I could only keep one.

6. Three points picked independently and uniformly on a circumference; chance the triangle is acute. I'm confident it's 1/4, and that this is a real question with a real answer — even though pick independently and uniformly is exactly the sort of stipulation that in the chord case I treat as one option among many.

7. Random point inside a circle; chance it's closer to the centre than to the rim. 1/4, no hesitation, and no feeling that I was handed a mechanism.

So sometimes indifference over a space feels like it gives me knowledge (3, 5, 6, 7) and sometimes like it gives me nothing (1, 4), and the difference can't just be that a mechanism was specified, because in 7 none was and in 4 I specified a whole family of them.

My stab, such as it is: indifference is licensed when the space of possibilities has a unique measure invariant under the symmetries the problem leaves open, and the chord case is one where it doesn't — once the circle is fixed in place you've only got rotations left, whereas a point in a region inherits something canonical from the plane. I can half see it, but I can't get it to deliver 3, and I can't tell whether it rescues 5's second verdict or kills it.

What I want: a principle, or two live candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it cold to a new case, with a clause-by-clause note of which case motivated each bit; its verdict on at least one case I haven't listed, ideally one where it says something I'd resist; and a straight answer about which of my verdicts have to go. If the honest answer is that 3 and both halves of 5 die while 1 survives, say that, and tell me what it's like to live with it. And argue it out rather than pointing me at a literature — I can't look anything up from here, and I want the principle, not a reading list.
