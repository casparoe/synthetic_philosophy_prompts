Several large philanthropic funders, and the cause-selection advice written for donors in that circle, rank candidate causes with a three-factor rubric. Each cause is scored for importance (roughly: how much value would be created, or how much disvalue averted, by solving the whole problem), tractability (roughly: what fraction of the problem a marginal dollar, or a marginal researcher-year, can be expected to solve), and neglectedness (roughly: how little funding and talent is currently going into it). The three scores, usually on coarse logarithmic or 1-to-5 scales, are combined multiplicatively, and the product is used to rank causes: money goes to the top-ranked cause until it is judged saturated, then to the next. Within a cause, the same funders switch to a different-looking metric: expected good per dollar in a common unit such as a disability-adjusted life year, with an explicit deduction for the share of the outcome that would have happened anyway without the grant.

What puzzles me is that the two halves seem to rest on different ideas. Neglectedness is normally justified by diminishing returns: the best opportunities in a crowded field are already taken, so your marginal dollar buys less there. But tractability as usually defined is itself a marginal-per-dollar quantity, and importance is a total, not a marginal, quantity; a product of the two already looks like value per dollar at the current margin, which would make neglectedness either redundant or a correction of a different kind. Meanwhile the funders say they care only about outcomes and not about who gets credit, yet they treat heavily funded causes as unattractive in their own right; and they defend concentration in one top-ranked cause, rather than diversification, using the same diminishing-returns reasoning that made neglectedness matter. Leadership also carves out an exception for what it calls hits-based causes, where nearly all the value comes from a small number of large successes and the average grant accomplishes nothing.

I would like the implicit theory reconstructed. What view of value, and of what a funder ought to do, does this practice presuppose — maximization of impartial impersonal value, commensurability across very different kinds of good, cause-neutrality, a specific story about diminishing returns and about how other funders respond to your spending? Which of these does the rubric strictly require, which does it merely fit comfortably with, and where is it philosophically underdetermined, so that a satisficer, or someone who thinks a funder may properly favor a cause for reasons of relationship or obligation, could still use it? Is the multiplicative form doing real work or is it a heuristic that misrepresents the quantity it approximates, and are importance, tractability and neglectedness even separable enough to multiply?

Finally, suppose a trustee proposed replacing the rubric with a straight estimate of the expected counterfactual value of the marginal dollar, computed explicitly against a model of the whole funding landscape including crowding out and crowding in, and with allocation decided over a portfolio rather than by ranking. What exactly would that change about which causes get funded and how, and would anything that the current rubric tracks well be lost in the translation?
