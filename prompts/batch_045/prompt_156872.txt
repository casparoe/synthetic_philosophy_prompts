I run a weekly voluntary reading group at a small liberal-arts college — its not a class, 14 people, mostly third-year philosophy and psychology majors, 90 minutes, room with a projector and a whiteboard but no reliable wifi. In two weeks its my turn to pick the topic and I want to do illusionism about phenomenal consciousness: the Frankish/Dennett-flavoured line that introspection doesn't reveal intrinsic, non-representational qualitative properties of our experiences but actively misrepresents its own states as having them. Not the crude version where nothing is conscious and we're all secretly zombies — the version where something is definitely going on, and the mistake is in how that something gets presented to the subject.

The problem is that every time I've tried this informally it collapses. I show some visual illusion (Mach bands, or two identical grey squares that look different against different backgrounds), say "qualia are like that," and someone immediately points out that the illusion is itself a conscious experience with a distinctive character, so all I've done is hand them one more datum. They're right, and I don't have an answer in the moment. So: design me an actual activity — a game with payoffs, a role-play, a staged case with forced choices, whatever fits — where the group runs into the problem before I name it, so that by the end they can state the illusionist claim in their own words and also say precisely what it does *not* deny. No lecture up front, and the word shouldn't appear until afterwards. My rough idea was a round where everyone writes a careful private description of what a stimulus is like, I secretly re-present the same stimulus under a new label (or swap in a near-neighbour), and they have to defend or retract their earlier description — maybe with credence chips staked on what their own future reports will say. But I can't see how to get from "your reports are contaminated and contestable" to "introspection misrepresents," or from "one attributed property is misrepresented" to "there are no phenomenal properties," so feel free to scrap it.

Two more things. One of the regulars is a committed realist who will say: fine, my description was influenced by the label, but there is still something it is like, and that was the whole question — I want the design to make that move genuinely uncomfortable rather than brushed aside, and the debrief should be honest about where it survives untouched. And I want them to come away seeing illusionism as a view with a *debt* attached — somebody owes an account of the illusion in non-phenomenal terms — not as a denial. Give me the timeline, roles or rules, the debrief mapping particular moments onto particular parts of the view, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (including the ways it will collapse into eliminativism), and a frank note on which feature of illusionism the activity simply cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply myself. Practical notes: printouts and index cards are fine, food isn't (one member has a severe allergy), so if you think an ingestible version is worth the hassle, say so and I'll check with her.
