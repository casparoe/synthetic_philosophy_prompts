I'm in a seminar this term on probability and induction (phil dept, cross-listed with stats, mostly upper-level undergrads) and I've built myself an argument I cant find the exit to. We did the reference class problem a few weeks back — the thing were any particular case sits in indefinitely many classes whose frequencies don't agree: my car starting tomorrow is in the class of cars, of Hondas, of Hondas that already failed once this month, of cars owned by people who check their oil, whatever. The rule from the reading is: use the narrowest class you've actually got decent statistics for.

Our TA likes inventing machinery, so he gave us what he calls the settling rule, which I think is just the reading's rule made mechanical. Start with a class K you have stats for. If you can find a property your case is known to have such that the frequency of the outcome inside the subclass differs from the frequency in K, move to the subclass — a more homogeneous class is a better guide. Keep going. When nothing you can think of moves the frequency anymore, that number is your credence. If it never settles, you don't get a number.

Here's what I built out of it:

(1) The credence you should have that a particular case has the outcome is the frequency of the outcome in the most homogeneous class containing that case that you have statistics for. (That's just what makes a frequency a stand-in for the case's own chance.)
(2) If K′ is a subclass of K picked out by a property you already know the case to have, and the frequency in K′ differs from the frequency in K, then K′ is more homogeneous than K, so the frequency in K′ is the better estimate.
(3) Properties aren't in short supply. For any class with more than one member you can find a subclass whose frequency is different, and usually several pointing in opposite directions. Toy version: six known cases, three with the outcome, so K gives one-half. Narrow to the three that fit some property my case also has and you get two-thirds; narrow with a different property and you get one-third; keep going and you get one-half again; at the very bottom you get a single case and the frequency is one or zero.
(4) So the settling rule never settles above the level of single cases, and at that level the number is always one or zero. Which means: for any particular case, either there is no number you should have, or the number is one or zero.
(5) That's nuts. Nobody thinks my confidence that my car starts tomorrow should be one, or zero, or nonexistent. Insurance, weather forecasts, and the statistics this very argument is made of would all be nonsense. It also seems to eat itself: the frequencies I set the classes up with are themselves frequencies in some class, so by (4) I shouldn't trust the numbers I started from either.
(6) So one of (1)–(4) is false, and (1) is the only one that looks like a real thesis rather than bookkeeping — so (1) is false, which would be depressing, because the whole frequency-bridge idea was the reason I took the course.

My friend from the stats side says (4) is completely fine and the right move is to bite the bullet: single-case probabilities are all zero or one, the middling numbers are just bookkeeping about your ignorance, and the reference class problem dissolves because there was never anything to be uncertain about. I think that's cheating but I can't say why.

So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine when I wrote it down, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd rather keep (1) than (5) if I have to pick, but I want to know what I'm paying. And if it's (3) that breaks, what exactly is supposed to rule out the junk properties — and is that a principle a view that says probabilities are frequencies is even allowed to help itself to?
