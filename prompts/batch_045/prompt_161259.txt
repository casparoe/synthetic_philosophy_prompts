I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and I have just been commissioned to produce a year-long series of roughly six long pieces on the theme of competence without comprehension: Darwin's strange inversion of reasoning, in which purposive-looking design is explained by a process that had no purposes, and the Turing-style inversion, in which intelligent-looking performance is explained by a mechanism that need not understand anything. The terms of the commission matter for the advice I need. I have reporting time and a travel budget, not a laboratory: I can interview researchers, reconstruct historical episodes, commission a few small analyses, and work through arguments on the page. I cannot settle anything that requires a new experiment or a new system. So the real question is what the series should try to establish, and in what order, given that most of it will be synthesis rather than discovery.

The provisional framing I have been working with goes like this. Call it the displacement thesis: comprehension is never really eliminated by an inversion, only relocated — it belongs to whoever or whatever stands upstream (the programmer, the training signal, the selecting environment, the scientific community that checked the output). If that is right, then the interesting questions are about where comprehension sits, how far it can be displaced, and what happens when the environment shifts and the debt comes due; if it is wrong, or merely tautological, the series should say so early rather than build on it. A related thought I keep returning to: comprehension may be required at acquisition time but not at execution time, so a system can be both genuinely unintelligent in operation and the product of a great deal of intelligence — which would make a lot of the public argument about whether a machine understands rest on a failure to say which moment is in question. And a third: understanding might be attributable to communities rather than persons, so that a field or an institution can be said to grasp something no individual member grasps, which is either a useful extension of the displacement thesis or a sign that it has become unfalsifiable.

My candidate list, which I do not trust, is roughly: whether the competence/comprehension distinction survives being spelled out, given that comprehension might just name a cluster of more demanding and more portable competences — explaining, handling counterfactuals, transferring to a new setup, degrading gracefully; whether the two inversions stand or fall together, or whether the Darwinian case succeeds on grounds that the Turing case does not; what evidence could actually discriminate flexible competence from comprehension, and whether that is a principled test or only a heap of proxies; whether the inversion can be turned on ourselves, since much of human expertise is tacit and performed before it can be articulated; and what any of this implies for how a science writer should cover claims about what machines can and cannot do, for education, for deployment and trust, and for our sense of what human understanding is worth.

What I want from you is an agenda, not a survey. Given six pieces, a year, interviews rather than experiments, and an audience of intelligent non-specialists: which three or four questions should the series be built around, and in what order? Defend the ordering against alternatives. I want to know the criteria you are using and the trade-offs between them — how much other work depends on the answer, whether it is tractable by reporting and argument rather than by experiment, whether it is already well covered, and whether resolving it would actually change what a reader believes or does. Tell me what kind of evidence, if it appeared in the next eighteen months, would move an item up or down your list, and what kind of source I would have to go and see to get it. And tell me which of the alleged implications I listed are overrated or simply do not follow, since I would rather cut two pieces than pad them.

One last thing about register. I need to publish claims that will survive being read by people who work on this. So throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the literature, mark clearly where a claim is a minority or live-disputed view rather than the working consensus, and tell me where my own framing — the displacement thesis especially — quietly assumes something contested. If you think the framing is the problem, say so and give me a better one.
