I retired six years ago from a national geological survey, where I spent most of my career in field mapping and stratigraphy, and for the last four years I have sat on the small nomenclature and classification panel that maintains the controlled vocabulary our survey and the companies reporting under the national reporting code are required to use. The vocabulary runs to something over two thousand defined terms. Each year perhaps a dozen of them are contested: a mapping team wants a term stretched to cover rocks the definition seems to exclude, an industry user wants an exception, a data manager points out that the same word is doing two different jobs in two modules of the database. Each item gets roughly forty minutes of discussion, there is no philosopher on the panel (there is me, a sedimentologist, a data architect, and a lawyer seconded from the ministry), every decision goes into a public register in a page or less, and the register is binding — it has twice been quoted back at us in commercial disputes, so an entry that looks arbitrary is worse than no entry at all.

In the last two years I have been reading philosophy in the evenings, and at our most recent meeting I made the mistake of saying out loud that our whole practice seems to rest on a distinction that Quine denies. My colleague read the 1951 paper "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" and took from it the following: that we cannot draw a clean line between statements true by virtue of meaning and statements true by virtue of fact; that attempts to explain the first sort of truth in terms of synonymy or definitional interchangeability run in a circle; that our commitments hang together as a web or field of force in which experience presses only at the edges, so that any single statement can in principle be held true come what may if we are willing to make drastic enough adjustments elsewhere; and that what people call a definition is really just a statement we are, at present, extremely unwilling to give up. A younger member of the panel concluded from this that definitions are therefore nothing but policy, and that we should resolve every contested term by asking which reading is most convenient for our users. That conclusion seems to me to prove far too much — used that way it would license us to settle a genuine disagreement about the rocks by changing the words — but I cannot put my finger on the mistake, and I have to live with this panel, not win an argument in it.

So I would like you to build me the procedure that the web picture actually supports, as opposed to the procedure it is said to support by someone who has half understood it. What I want is something the panel can run, in order, in forty minutes, by four people with no philosophical training and no library access: the questions to ask in sequence; what counts as a sufficient answer to each, stated tightly enough that two panels would give the same verdict; the points at which the procedure returns a definite no; and the points at which it must stop and hand the decision to a named person because the remaining question is one of values, cost, or statutory authority rather than of fact or meaning. I have a candidate device of my own that I would like you to test, repair, or replace. Call it the two-keys rule: the panel may treat a statement as definitional — that is, as something to be settled by fiat rather than by evidence — only if it can (a) write down in advance the kind of observation or argument that would reopen it, and (b) name at least one other statement somewhere in the vocabulary that we would prefer to revise first if that observation arrived. If we can supply neither key, then what we have is not a definition but a substantive theoretical commitment wearing a definition's clothes, and the panel should be required to say so in the register. Please tell me whether that device captures what Quine was after or quietly reintroduces the very distinction he rejected, and be explicit about which of the distinctions in his argument survive being turned into a checklist and which we should simply drop.

I would like the procedure applied, in full, to three cases from a docket like ours. First: a lithological term whose definition includes a depositional process, so that calling a three-hundred-metre succession by that name already asserts a mechanism; two competent sedimentologists disagree about the mechanism, and the mapping team needs an answer before the field season. Our instinct is to say this is a factual question and to go and look at more outcrop, and I suspect the procedure should tell us that the instinct is naive in a specific way I want spelled out. Second: a proposal to widen the definition of a common term in the vocabulary so that it covers crystalline solids produced by organisms and by industrial processes as well as by geological ones, purely so that a health-and-safety dataset can reuse our identifiers. Here the panel's strong preference is to treat the matter as a free choice of convention and to vote on grounds of convenience; if your procedure says instead that we are not entitled to settle it that way, say so plainly, because that is the answer I will like least and most need to hear. Third: our rules fix each stage boundary by a marker horizon in one designated reference section, and the marker in one such section has turned out to be diachronous — it does not record the same instant in other basins — so that keeping the boundary where it is will quietly detach the unit from the event the unit was created to track, while moving it will invalidate years of published correlations and require thousands of database records to be re-keyed. I take this to be the sort of case where the procedure should run to a stop and declare a value judgment, and I want to know exactly where it stops, whose signature the statute requires on that line, and what should be written in the register so that a future panel can see what was traded for what. Finally, please say plainly what the procedure cannot do: the contexts in which it will misfire, whether our lawyer's demand for a hard yes-or-no in a regulatory setting can be met at all, and how it should handle a vocabulary that is maintained in two languages at once. I can get to no university library from where I live and I do not trust popular summaries, so please argue from the ideas themselves rather than pointing me at a reading list, and do not cite anything.
