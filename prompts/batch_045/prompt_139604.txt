I hold a fairly specific version of the Epicurean position about death, and I would like a map of what it risks rather than a defense of it. Here is the version. Nothing is intrinsically bad for a person unless there is a time at which that person exists and at which it is bad for them; I will call this the existence requirement. Death, on this view, leaves no such time: once someone is dead there is no subject left for the harm to attach to, and while they are still alive their death is still future and, so I claim, not yet intrinsically bad for them, since the familiar dread is a matter of attitude rather than of a pending harm. Two things distinguish my version from cruder ones. First, I am not committed to an experience requirement: I happily grant that a person can be harmed, at a time when they exist, by things they never experience, such as the collapse of their life's work during a dreamless coma, a betrayal they will never learn of, or the quiet destruction of their reputation. So the work is being done entirely by the subject's existence, not by their consciousness. Second, I reject merely counterfactual losses: that you would have had forty more good years is not by itself a harm unless there is a time at which it is had. I also accept the consequences in the cases my reading group keeps raising: annihilation followed by exact reassembly a year later is not bad for the traveler even though no time seems to pass from their own point of view; the symmetry with the time before birth holds; and whatever badness death has is exhausted by the process of dying and by its effects on others.

What I want is an account of what should move me off this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate the defeaters a defender of the position would themselves concede from the ones they would dismiss as question-begging. Three lines of pressure seem worth testing. First: does the existence requirement have independent support, or is it a restatement of the conclusion dressed up as a principle? Consider whether other domains give us harms or wrongs to beings that do not exist at the time in question, such as harms to not-yet-conceived people, to future generations, to a corpse or a reputation, or to someone who is wrongly brought into existence, and say for each whether it is a close enough analogue to be decisive or whether it is disanalogous in a way I would be entitled to point at. Second, the timing problem: a rival says the harm of dying young is had before death, as a truncated life or a frustrated desire, which would make my view collapse into that rival's by another route. Is that a stable position or a bullet I would have to bite, and what would distinguish the two? Third, there is an asymmetry I find harder to explain than the standard one about prenatal nonexistence: even someone who grants me the argument is normally upset by a terminal diagnosis in a way that is not just fear of dying or concern for survivors, and they accept life-extending treatment; if I reinterpret all of that as expressive rather than belief-like, am I insulating the view rather than supporting it, and is insulation of that kind a problem for a claim about what we have reason to do rather than a metaphysical posit?

I would also like you to take seriously one argument a friend pressed on me that I have not seen discussed: prudential rationality under uncertainty. Even a modest credence in the deprivation account makes an early death an enormous expected loss, and my confidence in the existence requirement is not the kind of confidence that should be cashed out at full value under that multiplication; so even if my argument is sound, it looks as though I should act as if death were bad for me, and a view that I must believe while behaving as though its negation were true seems to have something wrong with it. Tell me whether that is a real defeater or a confusion about what first-order normative claims are for, and whether a defender would grant it. What would move me most, I think, is a case where the existence requirement yields a verdict I would independently regard as wrong in an area where I have no stake in the outcome; what would move me least is a bare report that people find early death tragic, since I already know that and have an account of it. Please do not cite anything or point me toward further reading: I am after the arguments themselves, and I am asking a system that cannot look anything up. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, meaning the most serious defeater or the verdict that there is not one, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. American English, and please be blunt.
