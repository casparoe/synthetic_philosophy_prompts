I'm retired now — thirty-odd years as an experimental biophysicist, mostly electrophysiology, so I'm use to trusting instruments and also to catching them lying — and I've drifted into a small philosophy of mind reading group. They've asked each of us to say in a page or two where we stand on how a science of consciousness ought to be set up, and before I write mine up I want to know whether the position I've ended up with actually hangs together. Roughly where I've landed: (1) heterophenomenology is the right method — take what subjects say about their experience as data, don't take it as authoritative, and build the theory out of whatever structure you can get at from the third-person side; (2) I'm a physicalist, and a large part of why I like the method is that it looks like it will get us a science of consciousness without postulating any extra ingredients; (3) I want the method to work where there's no language — I think a baby, a chimp, and maybe someday some machine can be conscious, and I'd like the science to be able to say which, at least in principle; (4) I think the method is metaphysically neutral, it isn't smuggling physicalism in, it just fixes the evidential base properly, so someone who isn't a physicalist could sign up to it too; (5) I still think the explanatory gap is real, that a complete heterophenomenological plus neuroscientific story would leave something that feels unexplained, and I don't think saying so is cheating; (6) I want to treat verbal reports the way I'd treat an instrument reading — data with error bars, not gospel — including using them to calibrate any proposed neural or behavioural marker of consciousness. Go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely fight and which only look like they do, then rank what you find by severity, say for each what would have to give, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I can live with dropping one of them; I just don't want to walk in with a bundle that only looks tidy.
