I work on the copy desk at a mid-sized newspaper in the States — mostly city council coverage, plus a weekly "we checked this" column where we stamp claims TRUE, FALSE or UNVERIFIABLE — and about four months ago I fell down a youtube rabbit hole about Bertrand Russell's theory of descriptions and ended up buying a used logic textbook, and now I'm definately the annoying one in the group chat. What hooked me isn't really the theory, it's what the theory does to an institution like ours. Russell's whole point, as I half-understand it, is that the little word the carries an existence claim and a uniqueness claim, that the phrase the present King of France doesn't stand for anything at all on its own (it's what he calls an incomplete symbol, it only contributes to the meaning of a whole sentence), and that therefore the famous example sentence about whether the present King of France is bald isn't gappy or unratable or kind of up in the air — it's just plain false. My desk's gut says the opposite: that you can't rate a claim about somebody who isn't there. I keep trying to explain this to my colleagues and it lands as a joke, so I'd like to see the joke written properly.

So: write me a parody of our internal house style guide. Call it something like "House Rules for Rating Claims Containing Definite Descriptions," issued by the standards desk of a newspaper you can invent, 700–900 words, American English, numbered rules with a short worked example verdict under each one (the verdicts are the funny part — a claim, a ruling, a one-line note on why). The voice should be deadpan bureaucratic: calm, confident, mean without ever raising its voice, and nobody should ever step out of character to explain the philosophy or wink at the reader.

The technical stuff is where the humor has to come from, so please actually get it right and let each bit generate its own absurdity. Here's what I want covered, roughly in this order:

1. existence and uniqueness both have to hold, so the fails if there's no satisfier OR more than one — give me a rule about ties, ideally with something mundane like the tallest building downtown, where a second tower built to exactly the same height flips a previously TRUE claim to FALSE overnight;
2. the negation scope problem: the sentence saying the King of France is not bald splits into two different verdicts, one TRUE and one FALSE, and the guide has to decide which one to print, probably by inventing some hideous compound rating and then forbidding it in headlines;
3. the word present is time-sensitive, so the desk needs a timestamp rule about when a claim is evaluated, and something about who counts as present at press time;
4. the negative existential — the sentence saying there is no such person as the present King of France is TRUE, and I want the guide to treat this as its proudest achievement, since it's the one case where a perfectly true sentence looks like it's about something that isn't there;
5. the incomplete-symbol bit — no meaning in isolation, meaning only for the whole sentence — so the guide should ban the phrase anywhere except inside a complete rated sentence (no headlines, no photo captions, no transitions), and should require reporters to rewrite everything in the there-is-exactly-one-thing-such-that form, which the news editor keeps rejecting as unreadable.

Where I'd like it to bite: the desk's rules keep working perfectly and the paper keeps getting worse. Somewhere near the end I want one quiet rule that applies all of this to an utterly ordinary sentence — the reason the levy failed, the mayor's biggest donor, whatever you pick — and rates it FALSE for want of a unique satisfier, like it's the most natural thing in the world. No footnotes, no citations, and please don't quote Russell or anyone else directly (I can't check sources and I don't want to print anything I'd have to verify) — paraphrase anything you need. Somebody who knows the material should be able to tell you know it, and somebody who doesn't should just enjoy a genuinely nasty style guide.
