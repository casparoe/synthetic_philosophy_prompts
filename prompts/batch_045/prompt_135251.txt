You are a professional philosopher with a second specialism in the historical study of nationalism — someone who has taught both Hobsbawm and Ranger's The Invention of Tradition (1983) and Benedict Anderson's Imagined Communities (1983) at graduate level, and who is equally comfortable with the conceptual questions here (what a tradition is, what social construction amounts to, what authenticity could consist in) and with the empirical ones. Please think carefully before answering, and begin with a few paragraphs of unstructured brainstorming: note which distinctions from this literature would actually do argumentative work in a case like mine, which are merely decorative, and what would have to be true for each of the positions on my committee to be the correct one. Then diagnose my framing before you advise me: I would rather have my confusions identified and ranked than receive a smooth answer that grants them.

The context is personal and practical. Last spring I joined the small committee organising my town's quadricentennial — the four hundredth anniversary of the borough charter of 1624. We are producing both a season of events and a short interpretive booklet, and we have split into two factions over how honestly to present the age of the practices we are reviving: the beating of the bounds, the mayoral procession in regalia, a civic anthem whose words were written by a local organist in the 1880s, and a founders' day dinner. One faction argues that the practices are documented continuously back to the charter, and that the invention-of-tradition literature therefore simply does not apply to us: on their reading, Hobsbawm's thesis bites only on practices that are in fact recent but are dressed up as ancient, so the whole question is an empirical one about origins, to be settled in the archive. The other faction argues that all of it is essentially a Victorian and Edwardian fabrication, that presenting it as four centuries old would be a kind of lie, and that we should either rebrand the festival as heritage or drop the continuity claims altogether.

I have been asked to draft a position paper, and I have provisionally settled on a decision procedure. For each practice we would establish, from the documentary record, whether the current form is genuinely continuous with the 1624 form, using a rule of thumb proposed by our chair: call it the sixty-fifty rule, according to which a practice counts as the same practice across any fifty-year window only if at least sixty per cent of its descriptively observable elements — route, words, music, costume, officiants, and declared purpose — are unchanged. We apply this window by window from 1624 to the present; wherever the chain breaks, we classify the practice as an invented tradition and treat its age-claims as false; wherever it holds, the critique is empirically refuted and we may describe the practice as genuinely four hundred years old.

Several things worry me, and I would like you to address them in order of how much damage they do to my procedure. First, the obvious one: is inventedness a property of a practice's provenance at all, or is it a matter of what the practice is doing now — ritualisation, repetition, the claim of continuity with a suitable past, the legitimation of institutions? Our mayoral procession was essentially created in 1888; it has now been performed for longer than many things everyone unhesitatingly calls ancient, and I do not know whether an invented tradition ages out of the category, and if so after how long. Second, I am unsure whether the imagined in imagined communities is doing the same work as the invented in invented traditions; if it is, one of my two literatures is redundant, and if it is not, I do not know how to combine them. Third, one committee member has cited writers who claim to have found genuine nations or national sentiment before the modern period, and says this refutes the modernist position outright, which would let us ignore the whole framework — but Anderson opens, as I recall, by treating the objective modernity of nations alongside their experienced antiquity as something to be explained rather than a contradiction to be resolved, and I cannot tell whether that makes the pre-modern evidence irrelevant or merely in need of a different description. Fourth, and most important for the booklet: suppose we conclude that several of these practices are invented in the relevant sense. Does that settle anything at all about whether we should keep them, fund them, or teach children to take part in them — or does the historical finding leave the practical question completely open?

Please tell me which of my assumptions are false, which are defensible but under-specified, and which questions I have not thought to ask; then tell me, in about a page, what the position paper should actually say.
