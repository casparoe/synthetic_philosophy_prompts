i'm writing the framing section for an internal report — our little interdisciplinary subfield (I won't say which, it's small) has been accused of being an echo chamber, by people inside it about as often as by people outside, and before I let myself type "echo chamber" into an official document I want to know what I'd be committing to. so give me the spec. what does a theory of echo chambers / epistemic environments have to deliver to count as adequate for the two jobs I need it for: (1) letting a member of a community tell, from roughly where they already stand, whether their community is one, and (2) telling an institution whether something it built — a feed, a hiring practice, a conference and citation circuit — is producing or sustaining one. different jobs, possibly different theories, say if so.

here's my half-baked candidate list, cut it up however you like. it should distinguish communities that merely don't hear the other side from ones that teach their members to discount anyone outside. it should handle the case where someone is soaked in opposing content but only ever in degraded, strawman versions, so exposure makes them more confident rather than less. it should handle a person with no community at all, just a feed tuned to them by something that doesn't itself believe anything. it should say whether the verdict changes when the community's central belief is in fact true and its outside critics are in fact unreliable or lying — and likewise for the group that pools testimony the mainstream dismisses and turns out to be right twenty years later, which surely can't come out as straightforwardly bad. it should handle the asymmetric community: genuinely excellent inside its niche, systematically wrong about everything one domain over. it should say whether the cheap first-personal markers ("I read the other side constantly", "I've changed my mind about things") are evidence of anything. it should give an exit story that the person inside could in principle accept, given that the reason they'd take your word for it is exactly what's supposed to have been removed. and it should be applicable to the theory's own scene — our citation graph is tight too, and whatever you hand me is going to come out of somebody's tight citation graph.

for each one: the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and whether it's non-negotiable or just something one camp happens to want. be straight with me about which two or three on my list are prejudices of a camp dressed up as desiderata, because I suspect at least that many are. then the part I actually care about: which subset of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied? my own guesses are the pair "diagnosable from the inside using resources you already have" with "insulation is thorough", and "symmetric — not a partisan weapon" with "pointy enough to pick out real cases", but tell me where the real inconsistency is. and then which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the accounts people actually reach for.

skip the full scorecard; a one-line gesture per requirement at which family of account it's aimed at is plenty. table is fine, prose is fine.

last thing: mark the two requirements you're least sure are genuine and tell me what would make you drop them — which argument, or which empirical finding about how people actually update, would change your mind about the list itself, and where you think the list is most likely to be wrong.
