I want a number, and I want to see how you arrive at it.

Consider a sealed comparison. A community will exist for exactly three centuries and then come to an end: no emigration, no contact with any other population, no descendants beyond the final generation, and no effects that outlast the community itself. Two policies are available. Under A, the population holds steady at 20,000. Under B, it holds steady at 2,000,000. Under either policy, every person who lives has a life at the same high positive level of well-being, and no one is worse off than they would have been under the other policy.

There are two variants of the case.

In the bare variant, the two communities are stipulated to sustain exactly the same range of collective goods — the same library, clinic, orchestra, and research programme — so the only difference between A and B is that B contains an additional 1,980,000 people, each of them with a life well worth living, none of them displacing anyone.

In the threshold variant, that stipulation is lifted. A community of 20,000 can, over three centuries, sustain a library, a clinic, and a school, but not a university, a full symphony orchestra, a high-energy industrial base, or a large-scale scientific programme. A community of two million can sustain all of these, and each of its members lives with access to them.

The question, for each variant: is the larger community better, is the smaller community better, or are the two not comparable — neither better nor worse? Give me your credences across those three possibilities, as percentages summing to 100, for each variant separately.

Then decompose. State the sub-questions your numbers depend on with a rough probability and a one-line justification for each: whether additional worthwhile lives as such make an outcome better, or whether only the average level matters; whether there is some threshold of welfare below which an added life contributes nothing, or less than nothing; whether the bare variant is destabilised by the style of argument in which one repeatedly trades population size against welfare level and arrives somewhere intuitively unacceptable; whether goods that are irreducibly collective, or that come into existence only above a certain scale, carry moral weight that is not simply the weight of the welfare they produce; and whatever else you think belongs. Say how these combine into the headline figures.

Finally, three things. Which sub-question are you least confident about? Which single term, if it were settled one way or the other, would move your numbers most, and what would count as settling it? And how much of the gap between your two variants is carried by scale-dependent collective goods rather than by the value of extra lives as such — I want to know whether my attraction to the larger population is really an attraction to there being more people, or only to what a large number of people can do together.
