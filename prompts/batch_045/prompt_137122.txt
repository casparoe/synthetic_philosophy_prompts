I'm in the first year of a cognitive science PhD, and last term I sat in on a graduate seminar on consciousness taught by a visiting philosopher who defends a higher-order theory. She was unusually good at producing verdicts and unusually reluctant to state her criterion in a single sentence, so I wrote down what she said about every case we discussed and I have since been trying to reverse-engineer the rule. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it: in dreamless sleep there are no conscious states at all, despite plenty of neural activity. Ordinary seeing while awake is conscious, with the caveat she repeated all term, that creature consciousness and state consciousness must be handled separately and that the interesting question is always about the state. Blindsight: the patient's above-chance forced-choice discrimination in the blind field is not conscious vision, even though the state carries the information and shapes behaviour. The taxi driver absorbed in conversation who brakes for a pedestrian, later says without hesitation that he saw a pedestrian, and reports no occurrent thought about seeing anything: yes, she said, he was conscious of the pedestrian. She used a piece of jargon here about being conscious of something transitively without being conscious of it intransitively, and I did not fully follow it at the time. Thought insertion in schizophrenia: the inserted thought is conscious, she said, even though the patient denies it is his — the sense of ownership is a separate question from whether the state is conscious. A chimpanzee and a raven: conscious. A lizard and a fish: not. A three-month-old human infant: probably not yet, adding that she would rather be wrong in one direction than the other. A densely amnesic patient with no memory of the stimulus a second after it vanishes: conscious at the time, she said, since memory and report have nothing to do with it.

The reason this is now my problem rather than just a puzzle is that our lab is designing a study and I have to defend the design. Assume for the sake of argument that we can decouple the metacognitive channel from the first-order states it normally reads out. In condition A we suppress the readout signal during a brief window while leaving forced-choice discrimination accuracy high and priming and pupil responses intact, so that afterwards the subject says they saw nothing and were guessing. In condition B we drive the metacognitive system directly on noise-only trials, so that the subject has a vivid and insistent sense of having just seen a face, with no stimulus presented and no above-chance discrimination at all. There is a third condition, C, that I would also like to run, in which the readout is intact but systematically misreports: the first-order state is as of red and the higher-order signal is as of green. What I want is her verdict on A, B and C, and more importantly the rule she is running. As far as I can tell, at least four rules fit everything I recorded: that a state is conscious only if there is actually occurring a higher-order state representing the subject as being in it; that what matters is merely a working self-monitoring channel, a standing capacity, whether or not it fires on this occasion; that the higher-order state must be produced by the first-order state in the right way, so that an independently generated one does not count; and — this is the one my lab-mate keeps pressing on me — that none of the higher-order machinery does any real work, and a state is conscious just when it is rich and available to the rest of the system, in which case the professor's verdicts turn entirely on independent empirical claims about blindsight, infants and lizards.

These rules come apart on A, B and C, though possibly not in the way I think they do, and I may be missing a rule that fits her track record better than any of them. So: tell me what she would say about each condition and why, tell me which rule best explains the verdicts I have, and be explicit about where her track record is simply silent rather than committed. Tell me also which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analogue to each new condition, since I expect she will appeal to one if she is asked. Finally, I have one slot left in the protocol: describe a single further condition that would best separate the rules that survive your reconstruction, say what each surviving rule predicts about it, and say whether running it is really the best use of that slot or whether some other case would discriminate more cleanly. I am much less interested in the individual yes-or-no answers than in the shape of the rule and in where the rule and her verdicts leave her free to choose.
