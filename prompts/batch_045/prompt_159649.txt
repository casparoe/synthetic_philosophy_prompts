I'm a retired molecular biologist — spent most of my career on signalling networks — and I've only been reading philosophy properly for about three years, so go easy on me. Next month I'm giving a short talk (25 minutes plus questions) at a pragmatism reading group in a philosophy department, and what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet.

My thesis, in brief: the standard charge against Peirce — that his long-run convergence account of truth is just James saying truth is what pays, with extra steps — is wrong, and the way to see why is to read convergence as a claim about the fixed point of an idealised inquiry process. But that same reading generates a seperate worry that Peirce people mostly ignore: a dynamical process can have several stable fixed points, and which one you land in depends on where you start, so convergence by itself doesn't pick out a single final opinion. I then argue this hurts James much more than Peirce: if truth is just expedience for some inquirer at some time, nothing in the view pushes locally useful beliefs to fit together into a consistent set, so you don't even get the pressure toward one attractor that Peirce gets for free.

The room: about a dozen people. Half are James people who think his version is the humane one and that Peirce is just a colder version of the same thing. Two or three are Rorty-flavoured and think truth-talk is only a compliment we pay to beliefs we like, so the realism question is a pseudo-problem. There's one Peirce specialist who will say I've turned Peirce into a philosopher of science and lost the point about the reality of the object. And there are two philosophers of science, both antirealist-leaning, who'll want to know what any of this has to do with how science actually works.

So: the objections I'll actually get from this crowd, in the order they'll come, phrased the way they'd phrase them (not the way an analytic metaphysician or a decision theorist would). For each one, how much damage it does, a two-minute reply, and where my reply is soft. Tell me which objections I should just concede up front instead of fighting. And give me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks, with a note on what I'd say if they do. Assume I know the dynamics side cold and the scholarship side barely at all — flag anywhere my argument leans on a reading of Peirce or James that a specialist would reject out of hand, since I dont want to find that out live.

Keep the whole thing under 800 words and to no more than eight objections; I'll print it as two pages of notes and that's all I get.
