You are a moral philosopher who works at the intersection of normative ethics and comparative moral philosophy, and who has taught graduate seminars on both consequentialism and Buddhist ethics. I would like you to take this seriously and think it through before writing: start by sketching out, for yourself, the candidate correspondences and the ways each of them might fail, note where you are genuinely uncertain, and only then commit to an answer. Do not try to look anything up — everything you need is below, and I want you to reason from the structure of the views rather than from any authority. Please do not supply citations, reading lists, or textual references; I want the analysis itself, in about 1,200 words, using tables wherever a table is clearer than prose.

The situation: I run a small reading group — six people, mostly secular, four of them with an effective-altruism background — that is working on Buddhist ethics with one question in view: is it, structurally, a form of consequentialism? Two analogies have come to do all the argumentative work, one on each side, and I want them audited rather than repeated.

The first belongs to a member I will call M. He puts it like this: karma is a natural law, and the model for a natural law is gravity. Impersonal, automatic, exceptionless, purely causal, operating whether or not anyone recognizes it. On that picture, the Buddha's claims about which actions are skillful are empirical claims about which actions produce which outcomes, and the machinery is impersonal in the way physics is. From this M draws a conclusion he actually uses: Buddhist ethics is act consequentialism in exotic dress. The bodhisattva vow is aggregate-welfare maximization across a longer time horizon and a wider causal net; the distinctive vocabulary — merit, rebirth, liberation — is ornament on a familiar normative structure; and someone who has already absorbed modern consequentialism gains nothing normatively by reading the tradition, only a longer causal story about where the consequences land.

The second belongs to R., who does Buddhist studies. Her counter-analogy is that karma is not physics but training: what an action does is not produce a separable outcome but shape the agent who performs it, the way a repeated physical exercise reshapes a body or a repeated practice reshapes a character. Read that way, she says, Buddhist ethics is Aristotle in a different key — an aretaic theory about formation and flourishing, with consequences imported into it by modern readers who want it to be familiar. Her conclusion, also put to use: consequentialism has no purchase here, and the debate M is having is a category mistake.

Two things came up in the texts that neither analogy seemed to handle, and I want them treated as data rather than as curiosities.

The first is what I will call the field-of-merit structure. The tradition does not treat the karmic yield of an act of giving as a function of the act and its effects alone. It holds that the yield also depends on the quality of the recipient: an otherwise identical gift — same donor, same intention, same object, same material effect — produces more merit when given to someone far along the path than when given to someone with no attainment at all, even holding fixed every consequence we can independently measure. The recipient is described with an agricultural metaphor, as the ground in which the gift is planted. I am stipulating this as part of the view under discussion; I am not asking whether it is defensible. I am asking what it does to the two analogies.

The second is the practice of dedicating merit. A practitioner performs a good action and then formally directs its merit toward others — toward deceased relatives, or toward all beings — and the tradition also holds that a person can acquire merit simply by rejoicing in a good deed someone else has done. So merit appears to be at once something that accrues to a particular causal stream and something that can be directed or shared. I want you to notice that this is the point at which the two analogies seem to pull hardest in opposite directions: a quantity of good that can be moved around looks consequentialist, while a modification of one's own stream that is only mysteriously called transfer looks aretaic.

Two further features of the target are worth having on the table explicitly, because I think one of the analogies quietly depends on denying them: the goal of the path is described as unconditioned, not produced by any cause, and the path is in part a matter of bringing karma to cessation rather than of accumulating better karma; and there is no enduring self, so whatever accrues the fruit of an action accrues to a causal continuum rather than to a substance.

Here is what I want from you.

1. Two correspondence tables, one for each analogy, filling in at least: what plays the role of the act, of the outcome, of the causal mechanism, of the temporal horizon, of the currency in which outcomes are measured, of the person or stream to which results accrue, and of the stopping point — the state of affairs at which the practice aims and beyond which no further improvement is sought. Where a cell cannot be filled, say so rather than inventing a filler.

2. For each analogy, the disanalogies — and a separation between the ones that matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support and the ones that are merely decorative. A map can be loose in some respects and still carry an argument; I want to know which looseness is load-bearing.

3. What each analogy smuggles in from its source domain that the target lacks. Gravity, for instance, is quantitative, additive, ratio-invariant, and indifferent to the mental states of the bodies involved; training has no analogue of a fruit that can be dedicated to a third party. Say what the target would have to possess for the import to be legitimate, and whether it does.

4. Run the two pieces of data through each analogy. What would a gravity reading have to say about the field-of-merit structure, and about dedication and rejoicing? What would a training reading have to say? If answering them requires an ad hoc amendment to the analogy, say so plainly and estimate how much the amendment costs.

5. Which conclusions survive. Does M get his stronger claim — nothing normatively distinctive, the tradition can be skipped by anyone who knows modern consequentialism — or only something weaker? Does R get hers — consequentialism has no purchase — or only something weaker? If they both get something, say how much of the disagreement is verbal.

6. If neither analogy can carry what its user wants, propose a better one — a third source domain, with its own mapping table — and state explicitly what it would support and what it would still not support. If you think the honest answer is that no single analogy will do the work, say that instead and say what would replace the analogy as the way of framing the question.

Finally: if any part of my description of the doctrine above is wrong or misleading in a way your answer depends on, flag it in one or two sentences at the end rather than silently correcting it.
