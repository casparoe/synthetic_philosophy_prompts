I write about science and ideas for a national newspaper, and I am finishing a piece on whether "left" and "right" still pick out anything real, or whether we go on using the words out of habit. After a conference panel on polarization last month I ended up at dinner with two colleagues — Hal, a political demographer, and Ruth, a historian of political thought — and they argued about it for most of the meal while I mostly listened. I have to make a house-style decision at the end of this: do we go on describing people, parties, and movements as left and right, or do we write issue by issue? I honestly could not tell who was ahead, and they asked me to adjudicate, so I would like you to do it instead.

Their exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Hal: the axis is exhausted. On immigration, trade, surveillance, drug policy, and speech you now find self-described leftists and rightists on both sides; the vocabulary survives from a seating arrangement in an eighteenth-century assembly and does no explanatory work any more. What actually organizes politics now, he said, is educational stratification and something like a cosmopolitan/national divide, which cuts across the old axis. (2) Ruth: test it rather than assert it. Give her a stranger's position on unionization, she said, and she will predict that stranger's position on school funding, climate policy, and sentencing well above chance — and so will any attentive reader. The labels are historically arbitrary; the clustering they track is not. Moreover, political actors coordinate using the words, which is evidence that participants can see the structure too. (3) Hal: the clustering is real but manufactured. Parties bundle issues and voters take the bundle; among the politically disengaged the constraint between issues is much weaker, and when elites re-sort — redistribution plus immigration restriction, say — followers re-sort with them within a cycle or two. So it is a sociology of coalitions, not a principle anyone holds. (4) Ruth: then explain why re-sorting is so uneven. Her own formulation, which she called "the asymmetry of default": on the right, the existing arrangement is the baseline and departures from it owe justification; on the left, a durable pattern of advantage is itself the thing that owes justification. She claimed this predicts not just positions but which arguments move people, and that it predicts friction inside re-sorted coalitions rather than the clean re-bundling Hal's story implies. (I don't know whether this is a known thesis with a name or something she invented at the table; if you recognize it, name it, and if not, judge it on its merits.) (5) Hal: that is the correlation re-described at a higher level of abstraction, and it is elastic — any position can be rewritten in either frame, so it cannot fail. It also mispredicts: moral conservatives who want to ban a practice are treating the status quo as unjustified, and libertarians and traditionalists disagree about the baseline constantly while continuing to vote together. And the whole dispute, he added, ignores that most of the interesting variance now sits on a second axis orthogonal to the first. (6) Ruth: dimensionality is not the question — of course there is a second axis; the question is whether one of them does enough work to deserve the old name. Top-down bundling, she said, cannot explain why some bundles assemble easily and recur across countries and periods while others keep failing: something is constraining the space. Nor can we simply drop the terms, because issue-by-issue description quietly reimports the axis — readers infer the rest of a person's positions from one of them anyway. (7) Hal: that is precisely the damage the labels do; they license inference, so people end up with positions they never took, and journalists should describe positions rather than brand them. (8) Ruth: and in the process lose any way of saying why the same coalitions keep reassembling.

Please go through the exchange move by move: for each of the eight moves say whether it is clearly right, mostly right, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, and why, rather than just declaring a winner. Then say what both of them are missing — the framing, distinction, or piece of evidence that would restructure the argument — and finally commit: who is more right overall, and what should I actually do with the words in my copy? I don't want a survey of positions or a both-sides summary. Take a definite stand, give your reasons, tell me how confident you are in each part of your verdict, and tell me what single finding would most quickly make you change your mind.
