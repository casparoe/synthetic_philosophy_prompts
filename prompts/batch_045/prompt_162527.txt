After retiring from a career in field ecology, I have been reading philosophy of history. Much of my working life went into estimating occupancy for cryptic species, so I am well practiced at refusing to read absence as absence: one lays out repeated visits, estimates a detection probability, and corrects the naive occupancy estimate for the events one missed. The silences in historical archives look to me like the same problem in another vocabulary. The archive is a sample of what happened, drawn very unevenly — towards the literate, the propertied, the urban, the male — so the response should be to model that selection process and correct for it, recovering the underlying reality.

What puzzles me is that historians of subaltern groups treat the silences as themselves the object of study rather than as a nuisance parameter to be estimated away. I grant that an archive offers no repeated visits, and that its categories are not fixed independently of what was recorded; but those seem to me to make the correction harder, not to make it the wrong thing to want. Why, then, is the ambition to correct for detection regarded as a category mistake rather than as a badly under-determined estimation problem?
