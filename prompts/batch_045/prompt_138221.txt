I'm in this reading group — me (I'm the philosopher, I mostly take notes and bring the biscuits), two CS PhD students, and an economist — and two of them have been going at it for about four months over whether Löb's theorem is a real obstacle to an agent trusting its own successors, or whether the whole thing is an artifact of writing "trust" down as provability in the first place. I finally got them to agree to an adversarial collaboration and volunteered to produce a first draft of the joint statement, so: can you write it?

Here's the setup as I understand it, and half of what I want from the document is for it to make the formulations precise rather than repeat slogans. Löb's theorem, as I've got it: for a formal system S of the usual strength, if S can prove "if S proves P, then P", then S already proves P — so a system can't in general help itself to the reliability of its own proofs as a premise. The alleged obstacle: take an agent that builds a modified version of itself (call it a successor) and will only switch it on if it has justified to itself that the successor is safe. If the successor reasons in the same S, the parent can't establish the successor's reliability from inside S, so either each generation has to be a bit weaker than the last (fewer theorems, or shorter proofs, or less search) or the agent takes a leap it can't justify — and there's a version of the worry where the agent keeps deferring to a successor it never actually builds. One camp says this is structural and bites on real systems: you either bleed capability per generation or you end up trusting something you have no grounds for, and the fact that the loss is small at each step doesn't help if you need many steps. The other camp says it's an artifact: no real agent justifies its actions by producing proofs, trust is calibrated confidence in another system's outputs, degrees of belief about your own successor are not the sort of thing Löb's theorem constrains, and "you lose a step of strength per generation" only sounds alarming if you've confused proof-theoretic strength with actual capability.

Two things our group generated that I want built into the document, wich as far as we can tell nobody has said anywhere: First, distinguish a successor that has the same theorems but far more search — say 1000x the compute in the same proof system, so it finds proofs the parent couldn't find in its time budget — from a successor with a strictly stronger proof system, and ask whether the obstacle is really about which sentences are provable or about which ones actually get output, since on one reading the extra-search successor proves nothing new and the parent has no proof-theoretic complaint about it, while on the other reading it plainly out-reasons the parent and is exactly the thing you wanted to trust. Second, consider a "track-record" architecture with no proofs anywhere: the parent runs candidate successors on a battery of tasks it can itself score, forms calibrated predictions about what they'd output on held-out tasks, and delegates on that basis. Does the Löbian problem come back through the back door once a successor is good enough to model the parent's scoring procedure and the parent's predictions about it? Both camps should record what they predict here in advance.

Format: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims both would sign — including what Löb's theorem does and doesn't say, what a "successor" has to be like for the question to bite at all, and whether this is genuinely about self-modification or would arise just as much between two agents reasoning about each other (is the "self" doing any work?); (2) a table of the residual disagreements, each side's position phrased the way that side would actually phrase it, with the strongest version each is willing to defend and a note on where each suspects the other of attacking a weaker claim; (3) the cruxes — sub-questions where settling one would move at least one of them — and for each, a concrete case, toy experiment, or argument both accept as bearing on it, with each camp's prediction written down before the fact (one crux I suspect is whether the per-generation loss is compatible with unbounded capability growth in practice; another might be whether a successor that models the parent can adversarially degrade the parent's calibration — the shallow-search-engine-judging-a-deeper-engine analogy is floating around our group, use it only if it's really load-bearing); (4) a short section on which parts of their four-month argument were merely verbal.
