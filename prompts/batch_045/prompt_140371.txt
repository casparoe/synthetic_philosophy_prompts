I'm assembling the final take-home essay for an upper-division seminar on personal identity, and I want to pair a film with a reading. The reading is Hofstadter on the distributed "I": the claim, developed across Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979) and I Am a Strange Loop (2007), that a self is not a thing but a strange loop — a pattern of self-reference realized in a substrate — and that a pattern of that kind can be realized, to varying degrees and with varying fidelity, in more than one brain at once. Hofstadter's own application of this is the one I care about: after his wife's death, the version of her that went on existing was the partial, diluted, causally efficacious copy of her pattern still running in his own brain (and, more faintly, in other people's, and in the habits and turns of phrase they had picked up from her without remembering where they came from). On that picture, surviving is a matter of degree, has nothing essentially to do with being consciously remembered, and is measured by how much fine-grained structure getsinstantiated elsewhere rather than by whether anyone keeps a picture of you.

The film is Pixar's Coco (2017). I want you to read it as a fable about exactly this. Miguel's great-great-grandfather Héctor is fading out of the Land of the Dead because his daughter Coco — very old now — is forgetting him; the film's rule is that the dead persist only as long as some living person remembers them, and that being wholly forgotten is a second and final death. Please work from the plot as common knowledge, and don't quote dialogue.

A reading that is faithful in both directions, and that doesn't just reduce the film to an illustration, would need to handle at least these things:

1. The counterfeit-memory problem. Héctor's songs are famous all over Mexico; an enormous amount of his pattern — melody, phrasing, taste — is instantiated in millions of living brains and in the recorded performance of Ernesto de la Cruz, who murdered him and stole the songs. Yet Héctor is nearly gone. So the film plainly thinks the wrong kind of instantiation doesn't save you. What condition is it appealing to? My suggestion: patternism quietly needs a causal-history constraint, not just a similarity constraint, or else the richest possible survival will be a fabrication. Say whether you agree, and what exactly the constraint should be.
2. Unremembered instantiation. Héctor also survives where nobody would call it memory: in Miguel's hands on a guitar, in the family's ban on music, in a shoemaking business founded in reaction to a betrayal. The film's visible mechanics, by contrast, are strikingly external and name-centred — a photograph on an ofrenda, a marigold bridge, a blessing. So: is the film's criterion of survival too narrow by Hofstadter's own lights, or is the photograph standing in for something structural that the plot can't show?
3. Degree and threshold. Hofstadter's survival is continuous; Coco needs "final death" to be a cliff. Where does the film cheat here, and does it have to?
4. Dilution. What survives in Coco is a pattern of Héctor; what will survive in Miguel is a pattern of Coco's pattern of Héctor. Does second-order realization count as survival of the original at all, and should we care less about it?

Then say where the reading breaks down honestly, and I'd rather you said so than forced it: the film is committed to glowing skeletons who have experiences, can be harmed, and can look forward to annihilation, which is not a pattern in anyone's brain — and strictly speaking, on the Hofstadter picture, the annihilation of a pattern once no one is left to run it is not a misfortune for the pattern. Judge whether the film's horror at being forgotten survives that, or whether its real subject has quietly become the loss to the living.
