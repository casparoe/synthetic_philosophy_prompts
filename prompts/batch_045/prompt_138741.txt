**Who's speaking? (notes)**

Nearly everyone in this argument is answering one of two questions. Either: does he still deserve to be honoured? Call that the verdict view. Or: does keeping the statue do more good than harm? Call that the consequences view. I want to suggest both are asking the wrong *kind* of question, because both treat the statue as something that says something about the past. A statue isn't really a claim. It's an act done in the present, and the interesting question about it isn't whether it's true or whether it's kind, but whether it's in order.

The verdict view has a problem it never quite faces. If monuments track desert, then every monument is provisional on our continuing moral confidence, and the honest conclusions are要么 either take them all down or admit we were never honouring virtue in the first place. We honour founders, generals, artists, benefactors — people who were often not admirable, and we mostly know it. Worse, the verdict view can't explain the cases where the answer is obviously "keep it, but not *there*": the battlefield marker, the grave, the museum case. In those cases nothing about the verdict on the man has changed, and yet everything about the monument has.

The consequences view has different problems. It's unresolvable in practice — it turns on facts nobody can get, like how much a passer-by actually suffers — and, more importantly, it treats the people who object as places where effects land rather than as participants in something. There's a real difference between being hurt by a thing and having a thing done in your name without your say. That difference is close to what the fight is about, and both standard framings delete it.

Here's the reframing. Putting up a monument is an act with felicity conditions: it has to be done by the right party, in the right circumstances, with the right kind of uptake. We're entirely used to this with promises and bets — a bet you had no authority to make isn't a lost bet, it's no bet. Monuments have the same shape. A monument purports to be *the community* honouring someone; the "we" is load-bearing. And a "we" can fail in a way that isn't a mistake about history at all. Who paid, who voted, who was in the room, who objected and was ignored — those are facts about whether the act came off, not facts about how it turned out later. Call it the standing condition. A monument put up by an administration over a population it ruled didn't merely honour badly; in a sense it never managed to do the thing it was attempting, because the speaker it claimed to be was never a speaker — just the loudest party in the room.

Three things follow. First, infelicity can be retroactive: standing can be withdrawn. A monument can go out of order without its subject getting any worse and without anyone being newly wounded. Second, that's why "he was a man of his time" misses the point — nobody is claiming he's deteriorated. Third, it explains something that puzzles people on the other side of this: why recontextualising so often doesn't work. Adding a plaque doesn't repair standing. A plaque saying "by the way, he also did X" is just a longer utterance by the same unauthorised speaker: more content, same speaker. Plaques fix a failure of testimony, and testimony isn't primarily what's being complained about.

Now a bit I only half believe: venue is part of the act, not an optional extra. Honour performed in the town square, with the council there and the school children lined up, is an act by the whole town. The same object in a museum is an act by curators *about* the town. So relocating isn't a compromise between keeping and removing — it's substituting a different speaker. That's why "move it to the far corner of the park" tends to satisfy nobody while a museum transfer tends to actually end the argument.

Street names are different again, and I don't think the standing account handles them the same way. You can walk past a statue. You can't avoid a street name, because a street name isn't something you look at, it's something you *say* — every time you give directions, write a return address, call an ambulance. So a renaming dispute isn't about one act performed once by the wrong speaker; it's about being conscripted into performing a tiny act of honour a thousand times a year. The complaint there is closer to forced speech than to unauthorised speech, and the remedy should have a different logic: with a statue you ask who is speaking, with a street name you ask who is being made to speak. This is the bit I find most interesting and have thought about least.

Where I know I'm shaky: (i) Why does standing matter? If the answer is "because unauthorised speech harms people," then I've just pushed the consequences view back one level and given it a fancy name, and the whole detour was pointless. (ii) Speaker identity over time — if the speaker of a monument is just whoever maintains it, then every monument is permanently unsettled and I've handed the decision to whoever shouts loudest in March. (iii) I've said nothing about the honoured person's own standing: if we honour someone whose values we now reject, aren't we misrepresenting *him*, and does that count for anything?
