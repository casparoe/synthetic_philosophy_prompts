Been reading about Athens lately and there's a pattern in how their institutions actually operated that I can't stop poking at. Four cases.

One: the assembly voted to kill every adult male in Mytilene and enslave everybody else, sent a trireme out with that order, slept on it, and at a fresh session the next day reopened the whole question and voted the opposite — a second ship raced after the first and got there just barely in time. So a decision of that size could be undone completely, with no special justification, as long as you caught the ship.

Two: on Sicily, Nicias tried to talk them out of the expedition by spelling out how enormous a force it would really take. The assembly just voted him the enormous force. As far as I can tell there was no procedual slot for 'yes, but smaller' — you took or left the package somebody had actually drafted and put his name to.

Three: in a trial where the law didn't already fix the punishment, the jury didn't get to pick a sentence. They had to vote for one of two whole packages: what the prosecutor asked for, or what the defendant named for himself. Socrates' prosecutors asked for death; he had to name his own alternative, and famously named something so high-handed that it near enough handed them the result.

Four, which I think is the weirdest: a decree could be attacked afterwards by going after the man who proposed it, even though thousands of people had voted for it.

Putting it together: the demos can throw a decision away entirely but can't adjust it by degrees, and when it backs out, one named individual eats the blame. I've started calling it the second-ship problem — the only way to moderate a decision is to annihilate it and then race the consequences.

So: what does a setup like that presuppose about what a collective decision is? My guess is that it only really makes sense if the assembly isn't an author with a view of its own that gets refined over time, but something more like a judge picking between complete bids that other people drafted — which would also explain why the proposer is the one who's liable afterwards, and why the defendant's counter-penalty has to be a genuine bid he can lose. But I want to know how much of the pattern that story actually forces versus how much it merely fits: could a view of the assembly as aggregating everybody's preferences, or as reasoning together toward the common good, license the same rules just as well? Where exactly is the practice underdetermined? What has to be true for the binary-choice rule at the penalty stage to be a good idea rather than a bad one — does it need punishment to be the kind of thing that gets claimed and counter-claimed rather than measured on a scale? And if I 'fixed' the second-ship problem by letting the assembly amend motions, or letting a jury split the difference between the two penalties, what stops working that I probably wanted to keep?
