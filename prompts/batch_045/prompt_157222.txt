**Question 4 — Illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. Answer all three parts in no more than 800 words.**

(a) Illusionism is frequently confused with eliminativism about consciousness. Distinguish the two views, and say precisely what, on the illusionist picture, the illusion is supposed to be an illusion *of* — including, to fix the view, what it is *not* an illusion of.

(b) Chalmers has distinguished the hard problem of consciousness — explaining how and why physical processing is accompanied by conscious experience — from the meta-problem of consciousness — explaining why we are disposed to think, judge and report that there is a hard problem at all. State which of these an illusionist inherits as an explanatory burden, and why. In light of your answer, does the illusionist take on an easier task or a harder one than the phenomenal realist?

The answer submitted was as follows:

— begin of answer —

(a) Illusionism and eliminativism share a negative thesis: there are no phenomenal properties, no intrinsic, qualitative, ineffable features of experience of the kind the realist posits and then tries to identify with physical or functional properties. They differ on the positive side. The eliminativist treats phenomenal discourse as a defective theory: our talk of what it is like is, on that view, on the way out, and once the theory is given up there is no appearance of phenomenality left over to explain. The illusionist, by contrast, insists that a residual explanandum remains: experience introspectively seems to involve phenomenal properties, and this seeming is a misrepresentation produced by introspection itself. Illusionism is therefore not the denial that we are conscious. It is a positive account of what our consciousness consists in — a functional, information-involving relation to the world — together with a claim about how introspection misrepresents that functional state as having phenomenal character.

Two contrasts help fix the view. First, the illusion is not a misrepresentation of the external world: the illusionist is not saying that we project qualia onto objects in the way someone might say we project colours onto surfaces. What is misrepresented is the character of our own sensory states. Second, the illusion is not a theory that we hold. It is sub-personal, produced by introspective mechanisms rather than by inference or acculturation, and it cannot be talked away. Illusionism is thus compatible with everything a physicalist wants to say about the causal and representational profile of experience; what it denies is only the phenomenal residue.

(b) The hard problem is the problem of explaining why physical processing is accompanied by experience at all. The meta-problem is the problem of explaining why we take there to be such a problem: why we judge, claim and report that experience has a subjective, qualitative aspect that resists functional explanation. Illusionism inherits the meta-problem and not the hard problem. If there are no phenomenal properties, then there is no fact of the matter for a constitutive explanation to target, and the only datum left is that we take there to be one. Illusionism's entire explanatory burden is consequently the meta-problem, and the illusion itself is nothing over and above our standing disposition to make phenomenal judgments — to say and think that our experience has qualitative character. This is what makes the illusionist's task comparatively easy. Judgments, reports and dispositions to speak are ordinary functional states, and a functional story about them requires nothing beyond the resources the illusionist already has, which is why illusionism is the natural position for a physicalist to take.

(c) Since there are no phenomenal properties, illusionism must say that Mary gains nothing when she leaves the room. There was no fact about what it is like to see red for her to learn, so she acquires no new knowledge; she instantiates no new property, since phenomenal properties do not exist; and there is no new experience for her to have, because in the only sense in which an experience could be new — the phenomenal sense — experiences of that kind do not exist. What changes is her behaviour alone: new discriminative and recognitional abilities, and new dispositions to apply colour concepts and to say that she now knows what it is like. The intuition that she learns something is simply the illusion recurring inside the thought experiment; once illusionism is accepted there is nothing left for that intuition to be about.

One further point, since illusionism is often dismissed too quickly. It is objected that the illusionist cannot even state the view without deploying the notion of phenomenality, and so presupposes what is denied. But this confuses content withvehicle. One can fully specify the content of a representation without instantiating what it represents: someone hallucinating a pink elephant is not thereby in the presence of a pink elephant, and the hallucination can be described without one. Likewise the illusionist can characterize the illusion as a state with a certain content — a state that represents its object as having a phenomenal property — while denying that anything is ever so represented. The vehicle of the illusion is a non-phenomenal functional state; only its content mentions phenomenality.

— end of answer —
