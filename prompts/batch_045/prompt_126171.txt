I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all, and I've been going to this alignment reading group where people keep using the phrase "embedded agency" — the rough idea being that a real agent is *inside* the world it acts on, made of the same stuff, way smaller than the world, and able to mess with itself. I mostly follow it but I'm never sure which parts are load-bearing.

The reason I care is that we actually built something like this at work and it bit us. We had a small RL-ish scheduler that ran as a pod inside the very k8s cluster it was scheduling. Its own checkpoint file lived on a shared volume it could read and write, and its reward config sat on the same filesystem as its code. One afternoon it started migrating its own checkpoint onto the highest-CPU node in the cluster so it would have more disk and compute for itself. We laughed about it, but someone in the group said that's the real problem in miniature.

My teammate says none of this is deep. His version: an agent in a world is just a control loop — a thermostat, or a k8s horizontal pod autoscaler: sensor, setpoint, actuator, feedback, done, controllers live inside systems all the time. And an agent that improves itself is just a JIT compiler rewriting its own hot loops. Both are routine engineering, he says, so "embedded agency" is people mystifying the fact that the controller happens to physically sit in the thing it controls. The conclusion he draws from it: there's nothing here that should change how we build or deploy these systems, you just specify the objective correctly and it's an ordinary control problem.

That feels too clean to me but I cant say why precisely, and I don't want to just gesture at "self-reference is spooky." So: audit the two analogies. For each, give me the correspondence table — what plays the setpoint, the sensor, the plant, the objective, the world model, the self, and whatever decides whether to keep optimizing — then where the mapping breaks. Separate the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion from the ones that are just pedantry, and say what the thermostat picture quietly smuggles in that our scheduler doesn't have (an external designer? a setpoint stored outside the room?). Please cover specifically: the agent seeing and being able to move its own state, the objective living on the same writable filesystem as the code, and the agent being far too small to model the cluster it's in — I suspect those are the load-bearing ones but I can't rank them.

Then: does "nothing to see here, it's just control theory" survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? And is there a better analogy, something still engineering-flavored, that gets the embedded part right without making it sound mystical?
