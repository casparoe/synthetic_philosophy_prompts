I'm retired — thirty-odd years in analytical chemistry, mostly method validation — and I've started going to a philosophy reading group at the library. Last week we did the bank cases: on Friday everybody's happy to say they know the bank will be open Saturday, then something important comes to ride on it and suddenly they don't know. Half the group says the stakes themselves decide whether you know (pragmatic encroachment, I think its called); the others say only the standard that the word knows picks out in that conversation has moved; and someone said nothing about what you said changes at all, only whether you should have said it. I've been trying to translate it into the only vocabulary I trust. It looks like shifting your significance threshold with the cost of a false negative — except, and this is the bit I can't get over, you're not allowed to touch the evidence when you do it. That strikes me as mad, and I'd rather lean into the madness than argue with it, so I want you to write me some satire.

About 700 to 900 words: a formal circular from the head of a made-up research institute's Office of Epistemic Compliance to all staff, announcing that the institute has now officially adopted the view that knowledge depends on practical stakes. Deadpan bureaucratic register, the sort of thing that sounds perfectly reasonable for three paragraphs and then quietly isn't. What I'd like somewhere in it: a worked example in lab language where the evidence is held fixed and only what hangs on it changes — say, whether a batch meets spec, which on Friday is a line in the methods section and on Monday is a phase I trial; then something about stakes nobody can see, the rule that knowledge gets graded on the actual stakes rather than your estimate of them, so an unread email from Legal can reach back and void what you definately knew last week; then the point where the office notices it can manufacture knowledge on the cheap by arranging for nothing much to depend on anything — a Stakes Reduction Unit, liability caps, the lot — and is rather pleased with itself; and somewhere a line where the compliance officer admits she can't tell which view she's actually administering, because the stakes version and the contextualist one return identical verdicts on every case she's ever filed, plus an aside advising staff to stay quiet about their own knowledge while colleagues in low-stakes conversations are free to attribute it to them. It should make the four people in my group who've read a paper on this laugh because they can see I've got the target right, not groan because I invented a silly position nobody holds — but my neighbour on the allotment should still follow it. Don't let the officer wink at the reader; keep it straight.

After the piece, a couple of paragraphs on where it cheats: which clauses are commitments a real defender of the stakes view would happily own, which are extrapolations they'd call unfair, and whether the contextualist rival is genuinely a different view or the same view in different words. And if I've got this backwards — if nobody actually holds what I'm describing, or the joke can't land because the view doesn't have the feature I think it has — say so plainly rather than writing the thing politely; I'd rather know than be humoured. No headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, please, including in that second bit: I read this stuff on a tablet in the garden and anything with structure in it just looks like a spreadsheet. Continuous prose, like an email.
