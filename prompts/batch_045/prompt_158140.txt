I need a thought experiment built to order, for a short teaching module on suffering-focused ethics. The module's thesis is that "suffering-focused" names a family of positions rather than one position, and that the deepest split inside that family — between summing suffering and giving priority to whoever suffers most — is the same split that divides mainstream welfarist views; it does not track the negative/positive distinction at all. The case is meant to make that visible, not to refute anyone, so the verdicts have to fall out of the theories plus the numbers, never be stipulated.

Setting and scope. One ordinary institution — a district hospital's last box of a long-acting opioid, an understaffed hospice's weekend rota, a shelter's veterinary budget. Realistic, single-shot, one budget cycle, one resource to allocate. Fewer than a hundred individuals affected; no wild animals, no future generations, no extinction risk, no exotic stipulations, no uncertainty, no knock-on or long-run effects, nothing deontic — no rights, duties, consent or fairness questions doing any work. Exactly two options, and the only difference between them is who gets how much analgesia: same resource, same clinical effectiveness per unit, same people existing either way. One patient is in severe, prolonged pain; several dozen are in moderate pain. The single patient must be worse off than any of the others on every dimension you specify; the others must be genuinely helpable; and the total suffering averted by treating the many must exceed that averted by treating the one, but not by so much that sheer numerosity is doing the work.

The verdicts I want, in the reverse of the usual direction. The aggregating suffering-focused view — minimize the sum of suffering, with happiness admitted only as a tie-breaker — must join classical total utilitarianism (maximize the sum of welfare) in choosing the many, while both a negative prioritarian (priority to those suffering most, whether you formulate that lexically or merely as a convex weighting of suffering) and an ordinary prioritarianism over welfare generally, with no special negative focus, must choose the single patient. So the two views usually cast as hard-nosed aggregators abandon the person in agony, and the prioritarians, negative or not, defend him. If your four verdicts don't line up that way, redesign the case rather than adjusting the theories.

What to hand back. The scenario in a single paragraph. Then: (1) an explicit cardinal scale for suffering — intensities, durations, counts — with explicit numbers for both options, including whatever the options differ by on the pleasure side, so nothing is hand-waved; (2) the arithmetic under each of the four views, checkable line by line; (3) a robustness pass showing the divergence is structural and not carried by the pleasure differences — set every pleasure gain to zero and re-run all four, then double them and re-run all four, and report which verdicts move; (4) an ablation isolating the feature that does the work: vary the single patient's severity, the size of the group, and lexical versus merely convex priority one at a time, and say where the two camps reconverge and which parameter is the pivot; (5) an honest note on which view is forced into something its own adherents would find uncomfortable here — the aggregators writing off an identifiable agonized patient, or the prioritarians spending the whole stock on one person — and the strongest available reply, whether that is rejecting interpersonally comparable cardinal suffering, biting the bullet, or charging that my specification has stacked the scale; (6) a closing verdict on what the case actually establishes: is it an objection to aggregating suffering as such, or only to the package of pure summation plus a fixed interpersonally comparable cardinal scale, and would it still have force against a prioritarian who accepted that scale?

Please do not cite anything or supply references; I need the case, the numbers, and the reasoning, not the literature.
