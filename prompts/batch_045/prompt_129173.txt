I'm presenting a paper at an epistemology workshop next month and want a Q&A prep sheet for it. The paper argues that the standard way of drawing the inside view / outside view distinction misdescribes what the outside view is for. Three claims: (1) the "leakage problem" — choosing a reference class always involves inside-view judgments about which features are causally relevant, so the outside view is never a clean alternative to the inside view but only a partially insulated one, and its apparent purity comes from hiding those judgments inside the choice of class; (2) "conditional deference" — deferring to a base rate is a remedial norm whose stringency should track how contaminated the agent's inside-view information is likely to be, not how broad the reference class is, so an agent with mechanistically validated, non-overfitted inside-view evidence owes the base rate nothing; (3) "the modesty ratchet" — epistemic modesty comes in three varieties that can come apart (modesty about one's evidence, about one's own competence, about one's reference class), and in some domains deferring to the base rate is a failure of the first variety dressed up as an exercise of the third. My recurring example is a lab group estimating how long a project will take when it has carried out nine projects of that kind before.

The room will be about fifteen people: mostly social epistemologists who work on peer disagreement and conciliationism, two philosophers of science who do historically detailed case studies of scientific prediction, and one professional forecaster with an actual track record. Nobody there is a decision theorist, and several are suspicious of the forecasting-and-rationality literature's habit of treating calibration data as normatively self-interpreting.

Give me the objections I should expect, in roughly the order they will arrive, phrased the way these people would actually phrase them — the conciliationist's objection will not sound like a decision theorist's. For each: how much damage it does, what I should concede, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and where that reply is softest. Tell me which of the three claims is in genuine trouble and which is merely provocative, what I should concede in the first two minutes to take the sting out of the room, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks, with an honest note on whether I have an answer to it.

Please write the prep sheet in American English.
