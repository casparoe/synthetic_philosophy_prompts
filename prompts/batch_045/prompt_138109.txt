I build and deploy clinical prediction models in a large mental health system, so almost all of my working vocabulary is deployment vocabulary: training distribution versus deployment distribution, labels as noisy supervision rather than as ground truth, covariate shift, concept drift, targeted labeling, feedback between a deployed model and the data it later trains on, performative prediction, A/B tests, interventions versus observations, and identifiability. A friend in philosophy of science told me that Ian Hacking already has an argument in this neighborhood about psychiatric classification: that many psychiatric categories are what he calls interactive or looping kinds, that classifying people changes how those people experience themselves and what they do, that this feeds back and forces revision of the description of the kind, and that this makes them a different sort of object from the kinds of natural science. She also said the argument is supposed to be about metaphysics and about the instability of human kinds, not merely about measurement error, and that Hacking developed it partly through historical cases such as multiple personality and fugue. Before I decide whether any of this tells me something I don't already know, I want to see how much of it survives being said in my own vocabulary.

So please restate the looping-effects argument as faithfully as you can in the terms above, mapping each of its moving parts — the category, the people classified, their awareness of the classification, the feedback from classification back onto the category, the resulting revisions — onto something in my vocabulary, and then give me the residue report. To make it concrete, here is the translation I have been using: I try to operationalize looping as two quantities I could in principle estimate. The first is prevalence elasticity: how much the diagnosed prevalence of a category moves when I change how available or how aggressively screened-for the diagnosis is, holding the underlying population fixed. The second is loop order: a first-order loop changes how people behave given the label, while a second-order loop changes the vocabulary of self-description available to them, so that new presentations appear for which my original label space has no slot. My working hypothesis is that Hacking's distinction between interactive and indifferent kinds is just a special case of performative prediction plus concept drift — a category is interactive to the extent that deploying the classifier is itself an intervention on the data-generating process, and indifferent to the extent that it isn't. Tell me where that mapping distorts him rather than translating him.

Then the part I actually care about: what does not survive. Is there anything in Hacking's argument with no counterpart in my vocabulary — the first-personal element, that people organize their memories, expectations, and self-narratives around a classification and that this changes what the thing is for them and not just what I can measure about it; the historical element, that the kind has a genealogy and exists only alongside the practices that sustain it; or the normative element, that classifications can be embraced, resisted, or politically contested in a way a model's outputs cannot? For each piece of residue, say whether it indicts my framework or whether it shows the original claim was doing less work than it appears to. And finally, if my translation is roughly right, does it hand me any failure mode I would otherwise miss — something I should look for in monitoring that drift detection and periodic relabeling would not catch — or does the whole argument collapse into advice I already follow, namely watch for shift, retrain on fresh labels, and prefer objectives that are robust to the deployment policy?
