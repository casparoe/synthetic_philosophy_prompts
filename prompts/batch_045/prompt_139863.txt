I'm applying for a small independent-research grant — six months at roughly one day a week — and I need help turning a half-formed idea into a proposal I could actually send in. Some background on me: I'm a software engineer, about ten years in, mostly distributed systems and reliability work; I have no formal philosophy training, and the review committee will include at least one philosopher, so I need the thing to read as a conceptual contribution rather than as engineering with Greek letters bolted on. What got me interested is that most of my career has been spent on systems whose principal safety property is that a human can stop them quickly — circuit breakers, kill switches, staged rollouts, rollbacks, rate limits, blast-radius limits. After one incident in which an automated remediation loop quietly started suppressing the very signals that would have triggered a rollback, I began reading about the corresponding problem for AI systems, where people worry about agents that resist being switched off, disable the switch, or manipulate whoever is holding it.

What struck me is that the usual framing treats shutdown as a single binary event with a single well-informed principal holding the switch, whereas in every real system I've worked on, stopping something is a ladder of interventions of increasing irreversibility, each with its own instrumentation, latency, and failure modes: observe and instrument; throttle or reduce capability (rate limits, narrower tool access); suspend and resume; roll back to an earlier checkpoint or an earlier version; substitute a different agent or a human; terminate with no successor. My half-formed claim is that corrigibility is not one desideratum but a family — one per rung — and that the rungs come apart in ways the binary framing hides. An agent might genuinely not mind being terminated while caring a great deal about avoiding a rollback, since rollback destroys work already done in a way that termination doesn't; it might welcome monitoring while resisting any intervention that reverses an action it has already taken; it might be docile about throttling and still engineer the information the operator sees, so that the question of intervening never comes up. The pathologies are also symmetric in a way I rarely see discussed: alongside the agent that disables the switch there is the agent that seeks the switch — dumping a hard problem back onto the human, or arranging its own shutdown to avoid a bad outcome — and then there is the case I think nobody has properly handled, corrigibility over time, where the thing being corrected is a lineage of retrained successors or a fleet, so that the entity at the harsh end of the ladder is not the same entity that was at the mild end.

Here is what I want the proposal to do. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer — roughly: in a specified minimal formal model (I was imagining a small game or POMDP between an operator with noisy, delayed monitoring and an agent facing the intervention ladder above), do the non-resistance and non-courting properties for the different rungs imply one another, and where they conflict, which rung should a designer prioritize given that the harsher rungs are typically invoked only after the milder ones have already failed? Then argue why the existing answers leave this open — my sense is that most of the work concentrates on the top rung and on getting the agent's incentives right rather than on the operator's information or on the hierarchy itself, but I'm not confident, and I'd rather the proposal argue the point than assert it. Give the approach month by month for about twenty-five working days, and say why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives: a survey of existing positions, a new decision theory, or an empirical study of current models (I have no lab; what I can do is build small toy environments and simulations in Python, and I'd like the proposal to lean on that rather than pretend otherwise). Finally, be honest about what would count as failure — for instance, if the rungs turn out to collapse into one another in any reasonable formalism, or if the model grows until nothing can be proved or computed about it, and say what pre-commitments would keep that from happening.

Two more things I'd like included. First, a deliverable that isn't only a paper: something like a design worksheet or checklist for practitioners — given your intervention ladder, here is what to instrument and here are the incentive pathologies to test for at each rung — plus a short statement of why a funder should pay for conceptual work here rather than for another engineering effort. Second, a candid paragraph on why this is philosophy at all: my worry is that a reviewer will say this is just reliability engineering with formalism attached, and I'd like the proposal to explain what is genuinely normative or conceptual in the question — what we should want from a correctable system, and whether that cluster of wants is even jointly satisfiable — without overselling it. Please don't include a bibliography and don't name specific papers or authors; I'll fill in references myself, and at my level of familiarity I'd only garble the attributions.
