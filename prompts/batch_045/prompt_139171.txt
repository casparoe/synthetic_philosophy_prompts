Hi — I need a second opinion on a piece I'm thinking of assigning in my upper-level consciousness seminar, and I'd rather not embarass myself in front of thirty undergrads by handing out something with a howler buried in it.

It's a long post by a science writer whose stuff is normally careful (I got it from a colleague, no idea whether anyone's vetted it), describing the access/phenomenal distinction and then global workspace theory. The relevant stretch goes roughly like this:

Start with the distinction. Phenomenal consciousness is the what-it's-like: there is something it is like for you to be in a state. Access consciousness is a different thing — a state is access-conscious when its content is available to the rest of the mind, available for you to report, to reason with, and to use in guiding what you do. Block introduced the pair, and his point was that these are two properties that happen to travel together in creatures like us, and that the access one is the tractable half: it's the one where you can go into the lab and find a mechanism.

That mechanism, on the currently dominant story, is the global workspace. Specialised perceptual and memory systems compete for entry into a capacity-limited workspace in prefrontal and parietal cortex; when one of them wins, its content is broadcast to every other system at once in an all-or-none "ignition." Being broadcast just is being conscious — that's the whole theory. So the difference between a stimulus you consciously see and one you don't is simply whether it got far enough up the competition to ignite, and once it's in, it's in: everything in the workspace is conscious in exactly the same way, and a representation that fails to ignite isn't conscious at all. The theory is therefore committed to there being no phenomenal consciousness without access. A state that isn't broadcast to the consumer systems doesn't merely fail to get used — there is nothing it is like to be in it either.

People who worry about the hard problem object that all of this explains what can be reported and acted on, not why any of it feels like anything from the inside. But the workspace people have a reply ready: the workspace is where the feeling itself happens, so identifying it is identifying the physical basis of phenomenality, which is what the hard problem was asking for in the first place.

My instinct is that it's fine through the first paragraph and comes off the rails somewhere in the last three sentences, but I can't put my finger on exactly where, and one bit of it I've now seen said in about five different places, so maybe I'm wrong that it's wrong.
