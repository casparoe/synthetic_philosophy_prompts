Here's the half-formed thing. I've become convinced the knowing-how debate has been arguing about the wrong unit of analysis. Almost every case in it — the pianist, the bike rider, the safecracker, the chess player who knows how to get out of a mate she'll never actually face — is a one-shot task where success is immediate and easy to identify. The intellectualist says such knowledge is propositional knowledge of a way, grasped under some practical mode of presentation; the ability theorist says it's a capacity, and Ryle's regress argument is supposed to block the propositional reading. Fine. But a huge share of the know-how we actually attribute is for extended, open-ended, feedback-dependent stuff: knowing how to run a lab meeting, how to keep a friendship alive through a bad year, how to raise a kid, how to steer a department through a budget crisis, how to calm a panicking colleague. Call it regulative or managerial know-how. My suspicion is that the two families of account each win on their home ground — the propositional analysis handles the one-shot cases better, the ability analysis handles the open-ended ones — which would mean "knows how" has no single unified account and the interesting question isn't which side is right but where the split falls and what explains it.

Two more distinctions I want forced into the proposal, because I think both are doing uncredited work in the standard cases:
(i) executability versus guidance — can you know how to do something you cannot do at all (the pianist who's lost her hands, a task whose opportunity never arises), and does the answer differ between one-shot and open-ended tasks?
(ii) the modal profile of an attribution — how much variation in circumstances the agent can absorb and still count as knowing how. Neither side seems to have a way to represent this, and I suspect the classic disagreement cases are partly disagreements about how demanding the profile is.
