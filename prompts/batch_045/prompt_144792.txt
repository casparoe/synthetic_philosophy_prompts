I'm writing a seminar paper on material constitution — the statue and the lump of clay, coincident objects, and the modal argument about their differing persistence conditions — and I want to use the Pinocchio story as an extended case rather than a decorative aside. Give me a reading that takes both the story and the metaphysics seriously; say which version you're working from (Collodi's novel or the Disney film) and where the two would support different answers.

The scenes I want it to turn on: the piece of wood that already whimpers and complains before Geppetto shapes it; the episodes in which Pinocchio's matter changes while he continues — the nose that lengthens with each lie, the donkey ears and tail he acquires in the Land of Toys (Pleasure Island in the film); and the final transformation, in which he wakes as a flesh-and-blood boy, Collodi leaving the puppet behind as a limp marionette propped against a chair where Disney simply has the puppet turn into the boy.

So: is the puppet, before the change, a person constituted by wood but not identical with it, or only an artifact that no one should count as a person? What becomes of the standard modal argument when it is run in reverse — the lump survives squashing, the statue does not, while Pinocchio survives reshaping, immersion and partial transformation and the puppet-body arguably does not survive being replaced by a boy? And is there anything in the idea I want to try out, which I have not seen argued: that "real boy" has a normative application condition in this story, since it is conferred for being good, which makes it less like a substance sortal and more like "statue" on intentionalist or artefact-history accounts — a status that a single persisting object acquires? If that holds, the clay is precisely what cannot acquire a status even in principle, and the constitution debate may have been arguing about sortals where it should have been arguing about statuses. Tell me if that collapses, where the story resists the reading — it is, after all, a fable about growing up and being good, not an ontological puzzle — and what, if anything, the story shows about constitution that the standard cases do not.
