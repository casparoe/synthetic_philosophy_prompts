I'm writing a seminar paper on Bayesian confirmation theory, and my reading group has backed me into an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. I would like you to locate the step I should reject, explain why that step looks compulsory when read quickly, and tell me what rejecting it costs me elsewhere.

The argument, in steps:

(1) On the Bayesian account, a body of evidence E confirms a hypothesis H for an agent just in case learning E would raise her credence in H: P(H|E) > P(H), or equivalently P(E|H) > P(E), with every term evaluated on her credence function before she learns E.

(2) When the general theory of relativity was completed, the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion — roughly 43 seconds of arc per century, unexplained within Newtonian mechanics — had been on the books for decades. Every competent physicist of the day assigned credence 1 to the datum E.

(3) If P(E) = 1 and P(H) > 0, then P(E|H) = 1 = P(E). So by (1), Mercury did not confirm general relativity: not weakly, not at all. This is the problem of old evidence, and note that nothing in (1) lets the degree of confirmation depend on how precise the datum was, how surprising it was when first measured, or how central it was to the theory's reception.

(4) The repair I want to defend changes the object of learning. What was new in 1915 was not E but the entailment-fact N: that the field equations of general relativity entail E. N was unknown until someone actually did the derivation, so P(N) < 1, and N is learnable. Confirmation of the theory by old evidence is really confirmation by the newly discovered fact that the theory predicts it.

(5) For learning N to raise credence in general relativity, we need P(N|H) > P(N), and this is plausible in the historical case. Conditional on the theory's being true, it is likely that a correct theory of gravitation has the resources to yield the observed anomaly; unconditionally, an arbitrary candidate theory need not. So the boost is real, and it is inherited by H.

(6) But the size of the boost — indeed its existence — is entirely a function of the agent's prior uncertainty about N. An agent who could tell instantly and infallibly whether any hypothesis entails any datum would have P(N) = 1 whenever N holds, and would get no boost whatsoever. An agent who is very bad at derivations, who can never tell whether H entails E, gets the largest boost. The repair therefore makes the degree to which Mercury confirmed general relativity a decreasing function of the reasoner's logical competence.

(7) Call the following principle Competence Independence: how strongly a body of evidence supports a hypothesis cannot depend on how good the agent is at logic or calculation. The principle looks compulsory because a normative theory of reasoning is supposed to tell us how to reason well, and a measure of evidential support that goes up when the agent reasons badly has the sign reversed.

(8) By (6) and (7), the entailment repair fails. And the failure generalizes. The other main repair measures confirmation by the credence the agent would have assigned to E before she learned it — a counterfactual or ur-prior. But the agent's actual credences do not single out a unique ur-prior, since many different priors converge on her current function once each is conditionalized on everything she has learned since. So confirmation again floats free of her evidence and is fixed instead by a hypothetical feature of her psychology.

(9) So no Bayesian account explains how data already in hand can support a theory. But accommodating data already in hand is what theory construction is: Newton against the planetary motions, Darwin against the distribution of species, Einstein against Mercury. The Bayesian is thus committed to saying that ordinary scientific practice systematically destroys evidential support, and that a theory is least supported by precisely the data it was built to save.

(10) That cannot be right. So one of the steps above is wrong.

If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) — the Bayesian core — and the historiographical claim in (9) than keep Competence Independence; but I want to know the bill either way. Which step do you reject, and why did it look fine? If the flaw is in the move from (5) to (6), I would like to know what the agent's uncertainty about N is really tracking, and whether describing it that way saves the repair or quietly relocates the problem. Does the counterfactual repair in (8) fail for the same reason as the entailment repair, or for a different one? And once the best step is removed, is anything left of the claim that Bayesianism cannot accommodate old evidence, or does the problem dissolve into an artefact of idealization? Please do not simply tell me the conclusion of (9) is acceptable; if you think it is, say what it costs elsewhere in the theory.
