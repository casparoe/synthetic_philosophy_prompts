I spent three hours after dinner arguing with a friend about the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, and she left me with a reconstruction of it that I cannot find the exit from. I have written it out as charitably as I can, and I would like to know which step to give up.

(1) The objection rests on an asymmetry: it is one thing for me to accept a burden now for the sake of a greater benefit to myself later, and quite another for you to impose a burden on me for the sake of a greater benefit to someone else. Asymmetries need grounds.

(2) There seem to be only two candidate grounds: (a) numerical identity, the bearer and the beneficiary are literally the same person; or (b) the psychological relations that normally hold within a life, memory, continuity of intention and project, similarity of character, call this the continuity relation.

(3) In the standard fission case, one subject's two cerebral hemispheres are transplanted into two different bodies; the continuity relation holds at full strength between the original and each successor, but numerical identity cannot hold between the original and both. So (b) can hold, fully, without (a).

(4) Here is the case she invented, and the part I trust least: call it coalescence. Two subjects, A and B, are gradually merged; over some months their streams of experience are fused into a single stream C, whose memories are drawn equally from both and whose later experience contains no fact of the matter about which of them is the one enjoying it. Identity plausibly holds between A and C, but the continuity relation between A and C is only partial, C inherits roughly half of what A did and expected. So (a) can hold without (b) at full strength.

(5) The continuity relation comes in degrees, and the parameters can be tuned: set them so that the continuity between A and C is stronger than the continuity between an ordinary person at twenty and that same person at eighty.

(6) If the ground is (a), then a burden imposed on A for C's benefit is impermissible while the very same burden imposed on C for C's benefit is permissible, and nothing about anyone's experience, memory, or desire differs between the two situations. So (a) makes the permissibility of a sacrifice depend on a bare numerical fact that makes no experiential difference.

(7) So the ground is (b). But (b) is degreed, and at the strength it has between A and C it is no weaker than it is across long stretches of a single ordinary life. So a burden imposed on A for C's benefit is as permissible as an ordinary prudential burden.

(8) Compare two situations that differ only in whether the fusion is ever performed. In the first, A bears a burden for B's benefit and no fusion follows; identity says this is impermissible. In the second, the same burden falls, with the same experiences on both sides, and then A and B are fused; identity now says the burden was a prudential sacrifice inside C's life all along, and so permissible. Whether the sacrifice is permitted turns on whether a neurosurgeon later merges the two subjects, as though a surgical fact could convert a violation of someone into an act of prudence.

(9) And because (b) admits of degrees with no non-arbitrary cutoff, the separateness objection is either empty, ruling out nothing the utilitarian wants to permit, or it proves too much: any formulation strong enough to forbid interpersonal aggregation between A and B also forbids the intrapersonal sacrifices it was designed to leave untouched, since coalescence makes the two come to the same thing.

I would rather hold on to (1) and to the claim that the objection is getting at something real than to anything in the metaphysics of (3) through (5), but I cannot name the move that is doing the damage, and I want to know what my preference costs me.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: first, name the step you reject and say in one clause what is wrong with it; second, explain why that step looked compelling enough to pass; third, say what rejecting it commits me to; fourth, price the most tempting alternative step to reject instead; fifth, describe the further case that would best tell these two diagnoses apart.
