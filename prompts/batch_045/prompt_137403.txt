I teach a graduate seminar in decision theory, and last week I put the following on the board as a five-minute warm-up, expecting to knock it down in the same five minutes. I did not knock it down, and neither did the room. I would be grateful for a diagnosis from someone with no stake in my answer.

Set-up. Fix an agent with a prior P over a finite set of states S, a finite set of acts A, and a utility function u on act-state pairs. For a cost-free signal with realizations e in E and likelihoods P(e|s), write

U* = max over a in A of the sum over s of P(s)u(a,s)

and

U*(E) = the sum over e of P(e) times [max over a of the sum over s of P(s|e)u(a,s)],

and say that the value of the signal is V(E) = U*(E) − U*.

(1) Good's theorem: V(E) is non-negative for every signal, and equals zero only if some act that is optimal before the signal remains optimal under every posterior P(s|e) with P(e) > 0.

(2) The proof is one line, and it seems to leave no room for exceptions. Among the signal-contingent policies open to the agent is the constant policy: perform the ex ante optimal act a* whatever e comes up. That policy is worth exactly U*. Maximizing over a set that contains a policy worth U* cannot yield less than U*. So the most that information can do is let you do better where doing better matters, and it cannot make you worse off.

(3) The standpoint of the comparison in (1) is the ex ante standpoint, the standpoint of the agent deciding whether to acquire the signal. But that is the only standpoint from which a decision can be assessed: to assess a choice afterwards, by the lights of the agent one has become, would ratify every self-transformation whatever, simply because the transformed agent approves of it. So if information is non-negatively valuable ex ante, it is valuable, full stop, and the agent who has it has no complaint.

(4) Hence it is never rational to decline cost-free information. And the agent who declines it, or who later wishes she had not learned what she learned, is making an error — not a tragic or forgivable one, but the same error as refusing a free improvement. There is no coherent 'right not to know'; there is only a right to be protected from people who act on information one does not have.

(5) The obvious replies to (4) fail, because the two things they point at are not changes in the decision problem at all; they are consequences inside it. Consider Ada. A letter is on its way, containing the result of a screening she can no longer prevent; she can only open it or burn it unread. The test is perfectly reliable, there is no treatment, and her prior that it is positive is 0.4. (i) If she learns she is positive she will, as a matter of psychological fact that she now knows, lose the capacity to take pleasure in anything for the rest of her life; the grief is caused by the knowledge, not by the condition, which is asymptomatic. (ii) She is under a legal duty to disclose a positive result to her insurer, who will then cancel her policy. If she is positive she can also rearrange her affairs — work, money, family — in ways that are better by her own ex ante lights conditional on being positive. Now fix u as of the ex ante standpoint: the utility function she has now, which ranks being-a-grief-stricken-person-who-knows, and being-a-person-whose-insurance-was-cancelled, exactly where she now ranks them. Fix A as her present act set. Then (i) and (ii) are consequences attaching to states, already priced in u; the probability that the letter says 'positive' is 0.4 and is not affected by anything Ada does; the grief and the cancellation follow from what the letter says. So nothing in the decision problem changes when the envelope is opened, which is exactly what (1) requires. Ada should open it, and she should open it for the reason the theorem gives: the flexibility is worth something and the rest is already in the price.

(6) And (5) generalizes. Whatever learning does to you — your moods, your options, what other people then do to you — it does inside a decision problem whose utility function and act set are fixed at the time of the decision whether to learn. So the more that learning changes you, the more decision-relevant it is, and by (1) and (2) the more valuable it is. The apparent counterexamples to (4) are the cases where the theorem bites hardest.

(7) So: Ada is irrational if she burns the letter; her physician should urge her not to; anyone who says 'I wish I had never found out' is confused; the reader who avoids spoilers is making a mistake; and the institutional practice of honouring refusals of information in medicine and genetics is not a protection of the patient's interests at all, and must be justified, if at all, by something else entirely.

I do not believe (7). But I do not want to give up much to escape it. I want to keep (1): it is a theorem, and the one-line proof in (2) is the clearest thing in the subject. I want to keep (3), at least for the ordinary run of cases. And I want to keep that Ada's refusal can be rational in the way a competent refusal of treatment is rational, not merely excusable.

That leaves (4), (5) and (6). Tell me which step is doing work it is not entitled to do, why the step looked sound when I wrote it on the board, and what each of the remaining ways out would cost me. Three things in particular. First: if (5) is where it breaks, I need to be told what the objects of Ada's utility are supposed to be, since that is where my colleague will press hardest, and whether the right response is to generalize Good's theorem to signals whose payoffs depend on the signal, or to concede that its scope is narrower than it is usually advertised to be. Second: I suspect the grief case and the disclosure case do not break in the same way — one seems to remove the constant policy by changing payoffs, the other by removing an act — and if they differ, I would like that spelled out and priced separately. Third: if the only real flaw is in (3) or (4), then say so plainly, and tell me what I am left able to say about the right not to know.
