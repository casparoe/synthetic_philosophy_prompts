I've been reading arguments about moral deference in the rationalist blog space for something like fifteen years, and this term I'm the TA for a colleague's upper-division seminar on moral epistemology — she writes the questions, I mark the essays. I've got one I've now graded twice and come out with different marks, and I'd like an independent read before I finalize.

The exam question, reproduced in full:

> Ben is a junior member of a research group. He has discovered that a senior colleague and mentor, on whose recommendation his own job applications depend, fabricated part of a dataset that several other labs have since built on. Ben is unsure what he owes to the colleague, to the funders, to the labs relying on the result, and to his own career. He has very little confidence in his own judgment about cases like this, and he proceeds as follows. First he asks a postdoc which of the senior people in the field is worth trusting on questions of research conduct; the postdoc names Mara. Mara tells Ben that he is morally required to report the fabrication to the funders. She also tells him which features she takes to be reason-giving — the reliance of other labs, the colleague's mentorship and his dependence on Ben's silence, the damage to Ben's own prospects, the integrity of the literature — and how she weights them. Ben follows her reasoning while she lays it out, agrees with each step, and cannot reconstruct any of it the next morning; he nonetheless believes that he is required to report, and he reports, citing Mara when asked why. Separately, Ben queries a fine-tuned system that predicts the verdicts of a large and demographically broad panel of respondents on scenarios like his; it returns a 0.78 probability that the panel would judge reporting obligatory, with no reasons attached. He treats this as independent confirmation of Mara's verdict.
>
> Answer all of the following. (1) Distinguish verdict-deference, reason-deference and weighting-deference, and say for each whether Ben's reliance on it is defective and why. (2) Ben defers on the meta-question of whom to defer to. Does using testimony to select a testifier make his position better or worse, and can it be done without already exercising the moral judgment he claims to lack? (3) Consider the swamping-style objection: if the point of a moral belief is to get the agent to act rightly, and the act is right, what is lost by the belief's provenance — or, if something is lost, does that loss disappear once we notice that an aggregate predictor can be retrained on new cases whereas Ben's recollection of Mara's reasons cannot be? (4) Say which of the three views discussed in the seminar best explains whatever you take to be defective here, and why the other two do not.

The three views were stipulated in the lectures, and essays were told to rely on the stipulations rather than on the historical literature:

> **The Grasp View.** Testimony can give you knowledge that an act is wrong, but not an understanding of why it is wrong. Moral understanding requires seeing the reasons for yourself, and it is distinct from — and not supervenient on — knowledge. Pessimism about moral deference is therefore a claim about what the recipient lacks, not about whether her belief is justified or true.
>
> **The Standing View.** The defect, if there is one, lies in the agent's posture rather than in her epistemic position: she acts on another person's judgment while remaining fully answerable for the act, and cannot answer for it in her own terms. This complaint applies even when the deferred belief is true, justified and reliably formed, and it applies to an agent who does grasp the reasons but hands the decision off anyway.
>
> **The No-Transfer View.** Moral knowledge is not the kind of thing that testimony can transmit. Testimony gives evidence about what others believe, not moral knowledge; so there are no moral experts to defer to, and the epistemic status of a deferred moral belief is the same whatever the source — a person, a panel, or a model.

The student's answer, verbatim apart from formatting:

> Ben does nothing wrong in relying on Mara and something wrong in relying on the model, and the difference between them is not one of degree.
>
> On the grades of deference: what Ben actually performs is verdict-deference, since what survives the night is the verdict and not the reasons. Reason-deference and weighting-deference are both categorically less problematic, because in each the recipient still does part of the work — in reason-deference she takes the reason-set on trust but does her own weighing, and in weighting-deference she has the reasons in front of her and takes only the balance on trust. Ben's case is therefore the hardest one. I argue that it is nonetheless fine.
>
> Set aside first the question of justification. Mara is a reliable and well-calibrated judge of cases of this kind, Ben has no undefeated defeaters, and testimonial warrant transmits in the moral case exactly as it does elsewhere. So Ben knows that he is required to report. The interesting objections therefore cannot be about warrant; and this is where the Grasp View fails. The Grasp View is standardly presented as the claim that deference leaves the recipient without something she needs in order to count as knowing. But that is the warrant claim in different vocabulary. Once we grant that testimony transmits warrant, there is nothing left for the Grasp View to say: understanding, in any sense that could do epistemological work, supervenes on having a justified true belief together with a reliable connection to the reasons, and Ben has that connection at one remove, through Mara. So I set the Grasp View aside.
>
> The Standing View is the serious version of the pessimist case, and I think it is at bottom the same objection as the Grasp View. Both say that Ben lacks something he ought to have. If he could answer for his action he would have to be able to cite the reasons, and if he grasped the reasons he could answer for it; there is no gap between the two complaints. Stated once, the objection is that moral judgment is not the sort of thing you can hand off, because handing it off leaves the agent estranged from an act for which she remains answerable. That is a real cost. But it is not a cost Ben incurs. He is not in the posture of someone who declines to think about the decision: he thought about it, with Mara, step by step, and he accepts what she concluded.
>
> The genuine failure is the model, and here the No-Transfer View is the strongest of the three, because it is the only one that predicts the asymmetry. Mara can produce reasons on demand; the model transmits nothing at all; and only a view that denies transmission outright explains why the two sources differ in status. Add to this an accountability point: there is no one to whom Ben, or anyone else, can address a complaint about the model's verdict, and no one who stands behind it.
>
> On the meta-question: deferring about whom to defer to is not a further vice, because identifying a reliable judge is an empirical question about track record and calibration, and Ben can settle it by consulting the consensus of people he already has reason to trust. No moral judgment of his own is needed at that level.
>
> On swamping: the objection has force only if the value of a moral belief is exhausted by its producing right action, and it is not. A belief Ben cannot redeploy in the next case, or defend to the colleague he is reporting, is worth less to him than one he can, even when both issue in the same act. But this point does not indict the model as such, since a predictor can be retrained on new cases and its verdicts may generalize over neighbouring cases better than Ben's recollection of Mara's reasons does. So the redeployability argument gives the pessimist nothing here; what does is the accountability gap, together with the fact that Ben has no way of telling whether the panel is tracking anything at all.
>
> Conclusion: Ben's reliance on Mara is in order. His use of the model as independent confirmation is not, and the No-Transfer View is the best explanation of why.
