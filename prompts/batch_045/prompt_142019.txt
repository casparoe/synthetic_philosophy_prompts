Hey — hoping you can help me design one class session. I've got a 75-minute slot in a first-year writing-and-rhetoric course, 19 students, mostly nursing/business/engineering majors, and they arrive already sick of online fights about pronouns and job titles: roughly half think the whole issue was settled years ago, half think it was always silly, and both halves are certain they already know what I'm about to say. So I don't want to just show them Hofstadter's Person Paper move (taking a screed that reads like a segregationist rant and re-pointing the same arguments at gender-neutral language, so they suddenly sound deranged) — I want them to build the trap and then walk into it. The name Hofstadter and the word satire don't get said until the debrief.

The shape I keep circling: instead of handing them somebody else's complacent paragraph, have each small group write one — but on an axis that isn't gender or race, so their stored opinions don't fire immediately. Something invented, maybe: a professional guild in a made-up trade whose honorific and job titles quietly assume one kind of person, or a fictional two-category world (Sunborn/Moonborn, or whatever you'd rather use) where the unmarked term is the generic one. Then groups swap memos, run what they recieved through a transplant table that re-points the whole thing at gender, read them out loud, and a third group has to guess which version was the original.

What I'm most worried about is the debrief, because I think there are at least three different jobs a marker does and my students will mush them together: (1) picking out who is even being talked about — whether the word is doing generic, quantificational work or pointing at a particular individual; (2) defaulting, where the unmarked form quietly imports a picture of the typical member (the thing where supposedly neutral words still make people visualise one specific kind of person); and (3) the social load the marked form carries on its own — condescension, hypervisibility, or just being the version that sounds diminutive. My sense is that a lot of arguments stall because fixing (1) by minting a neutral word doesn't touch (2) or (3) at all, and I'd like the debrief to make that visible using things that actually happened in the room, not a mini-lecture. Please also be straight with me about what the activity can't teach — e.g. that the transplant fools people partly because the new words are clumsy and funny, and that two axes can share a surface form without sharing a mechanism — plus the usual disasters (someone picks an axis just to be edgy, someone refuses to read aloud, someone says so you're calling my grandfather a bigot).

Give me the runnable thing: materials and handout text (including the transplant table with the category pairs and the replacement rules), a timeline in minutes, the exact wording of the few sentences I'm allowed to say before it starts, the debrief mapped onto moments from the activity, what predictably goes wrong and how to handle it, and a stripped-down 30-minute version in case only 6 people show.
