I work on the policy team at a good-government nonprofit. Last month our state legislature held a hearing on a bill that would create a small-donor public financing system for state legislative races. Candidates who opt in, accept a low per-donor contribution limit, and agree to a spending cap would receive public funds matching their small in-state donations at a fixed multiple; candidates who decline can keep their contribution limit high and spend without a cap. Opponents testified against the bill, and one of them kept returning to a single sentence: money is speech. He put it to two different jobs. First, he argued that because the spending cap is a condition of receiving the matching funds, the program makes candidates surrender their own speech in exchange for cash, and a voluntary program that purchases silence with the candidate's own tax money is coercive enough to count as a penalty. Second, he argued that the matching funds are themselves objectionable on the same principle: the state handing a megaphone to some speakers necessarily turns the volume down for those who declined or were ineligible, and if money is speech, then a speaker's ability to be heard cannot be made to depend on the government's judgment about how much speech a race deserves.

I have since realized that I cannot say what the sentence is actually claiming. It seems to oscillate between a banal empirical point, a causal point about what it takes to reach an audience, an expressive-conduct point about spending as such, an equality point about unequal amplification, and something much stronger and, I suspect, false. And the two uses he made of it seem to require different readings, possibly incompatible ones, since the first treats the cap as a burden on speech while the second treats an increase in someone else's volume as a burden on speech.

One complication I keep tripping over, which I have started calling the conversion problem: money is not speech but a claim on other people's labor and on a market whose prices are set by third parties. The exchange rate between dollars and audiences varies by medium, by year, by whether a race is already saturated, and by who happens to own the local airwaves or mailing lists. So a cap on dollars and a cap on speech can come apart in either direction, and it looks to me as though the sentence has to say something about that conversion step before it can do any work at all.

I do not need the case law; I can read the doctrine myself, and I know it is contested. What I want is the conceptual map. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses the sentence could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical. For each, give the claim stated sharply enough that it could be false, say what would have to be true for it to hold, classify it as trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and say whether it would actually support the first objection, the second objection, both, or neither. Please also mark any reading that cuts the other way and would support the matching-funds program it was being used to attack. Two distinctions I would like handled explicitly rather than left implicit: whether the reading covers contributions to someone else's speech as well as spending on one's own, and whether the reading leaves room for disclosure requirements, given that disclosure regulates the flow of money without limiting it. Finally, tell me which reading you take to be the one people are usually reaching for when they say this sentence out loud, and whether that reading survives being stated precisely.

Please write the answer in American English.
