I teach a mid-level undergraduate seminar on value theory — 18 students, one 75-minute session, a room with movable chairs and a whiteboard, no projector I want to depend on. They have worked through expected-utility theory and are comfortable multiplying probabilities; they have heard the words incommensurable and hard choice in passing but have been given no view about them. I want a runnable activity — a game with rules, roles, materials and small stakes — that makes them feel, before anyone names anything, the difference between three things students routinely run together: I don't know which is better, they are equally good, and neither is better and there is no single scale on which they could be.

Constraints on the design.

Materials are limited to what I can carry: index cards, pens, sticky notes, ten poker chips per student, a timer, one double-sided handout, and sealed envelopes. No money, no prizes, no devices, no pre-class preparation beyond printing. The activity must be runnable by me alone.

The subject matter is a fixed fictional agent — Dana, twenty-two, choosing among four lives — described on four cards. Each life is rated on four values only: financial security, artistic achievement, love and friendship, and moral impact. Design the four options so that each peaks on a different value, none dominates any other, and within-value comparisons are easy while cross-value comparisons are exactly where the trouble is. Ratings should be ordinal levels within a dimension and explicitly not comparable across dimensions.

The word incommensurable, and every other technical term, must be kept off the handout entirely. The handout uses only ordinary phrasing: better than, worse than, equally good, too close to tell, no common measure. Nothing may be attributed to a named philosopher or paper anywhere in what you write; I supply the reading list myself.

Mechanics I want built in, though you should revise them if you see a better version.

A five-way verdict form. Every comparison is recorded as exactly one of: A better, B better, equally good, can't tell but there is a fact of the matter, or no common measure. The last two options should be phrased so that students can tell them apart without theory, and the tally of how the class distributes across them is the raw material for the debrief.

Local verdicts before global ones. Students first give verdicts on adjacent pairs only, in pairs, and may not appeal to mere preference — they must give a reason in terms of the values on the cards. Only afterwards are they required to assemble those verdicts into one ranked list of all four lives. The collision between the two exercises is the point, so design the pairs so that most local verdicts come back as no common measure while a few come back determinate, and so that chaining the determinate ones through the indeterminate ones forces a choice the group does not want to make.

Mandated yardsticks. Each small group receives a sealed envelope assigning it a different required unit of account — money, hours with people you love, a named measure of artistic achievement, lives improved — and must produce the one correct ranking in that unit. Groups then compare. Same four lives, same internal coherence, different orderings, and no way to adjudicate between the scales without already having one.

A refusal that costs something. A group may return the verdict no ranking is possible, but the bequest must be disbursed by the end of the session, and refusal routes the money to a default option that nobody in the room wants. Abstention has to be a real move with a real consequence, not a way out.

Chips as conviction, not currency. Students spend chips to make their private verdict count in the group's verdict, and unspent chips are wasted, so spending is worth something and spending twice on the same pair is visibly pumpable. I would like the chip mechanic to separate students who treat the pair as tied from students who treat it as genuinely incomparable, but I also want you to be honest about which cases it cannot adjudicate and how I should present those cases so that I do not overclaim.

Spread small improvements. For one pair a group has declared incomparable, I hold a stack of improvement chits, each adding a tiny specified improvement to one life, and I add them one at a time, spreading them across different values so that no dominance is ever reached and the cumulative total is eventually large. The group must say after each chit whether the verdict has changed, and when it flips they must identify the exact chit that did it and explain what changed between that chit and the previous one.

Give me a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, including what I do while groups are working, and what to do with the groups that finish early.

Then the debrief, in three parts.

First, map the moments onto the distinctions: which minute of the activity produced which of the three confusions I opened with, what the envelope exercise showed about a common scale being an artifact rather than a discovery, what the chained verdicts showed about transitivity, and what the chits showed about whether incomparability can survive arbitrarily small improvements. Say which two ways out of that last argument the group is likely to reach for and what each costs.

Second, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I expect at least these, but add your own: a student collapses everything into preference and says the exercise is pointless; a group quietly uses money as the scale even when its envelope says otherwise; one confident student's verdict becomes the group's verdict; someone says it depends on the person, which Dana is meant to block but will not fully block; someone treats the chits as a measurement problem; the groups finish at wildly different speeds. For each, tell me what I should say in the moment rather than in the debrief.

Third, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in discussion. My own guesses, which I want you to correct or extend: the exercise produces the phenomenology and the structural pressure, but it cannot settle whether the inability to compare lies in the values or in us; it cannot reproduce the stakes of a real hard choice, which is lived through over time, partly constitutes the person who makes it, and carries a kind of retrospective regret that a twenty-minute proxy cannot generate; and it cannot reproduce the view that some values must not be put on a common scale at all, since the exercise asks students to do precisely what that view forbids. If that last view is right, tell me how to convert the discomfort students feel while filling in the exchange rates into evidence for it rather than against it.
