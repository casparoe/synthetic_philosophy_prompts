Construct a thought experiment that separates the selected-effect (etiological) account of biological function from the causal-role (systemic-contribution) account. It should be a single realistic biological case — a trait in a plausible wild population, describable in one paragraph and no longer than 150 words — engineered so that the two accounts give different answers about what that very trait is *for*, and engineered so that the difference is not an artifact of anyone being misdescribed or of anything being stipulated by fiat.

What the case must deliver:

1. The trait T has a genuine selection history for some effect F, and F is not what T currently does. T now makes a robust, systematic contribution G to some real capacity of a real containing system.
2. The selected-effect account's verdict about T is F (and it should treat G as a useful effect rather than a function), while the causal-role account's verdict is G. Both theorists are handed the same complete description of T: its full history of selection and its full current causal profile, including counterfactual behaviour under perturbation. The disagreement must therefore be doctrinal rather than an empirical gap that more fieldwork would close.

Constraints that block the standard ways of dissolving the disagreement:

- T must be fixed in the population — no heritable variation currently available for selection to act on — so the selected-effect theorist cannot absorb G by appealing to ongoing or very recent selection for it. T is nonetheless not a fluke or a random accretion; it has the real history required by (1).
- T must be capable of malfunctioning with respect to G, with a non-question-begging distinction between working and failing states (damage, developmental perturbation, or a specified degradation), so that the case cannot be settled by pointing out that only selected-effect accounts make sense of malfunction.
- The verdicts may not be built in by stipulation: nothing in the scenario may say that G is or is not a function, and the selected-effect verdict must be one a sophisticated defender of that account would actually endorse rather than an absurdity the account has been trapped into.

In your answer, after the scenario:

- State precisely which version of each account you are using, and fix the system-capacity pair that the causal-role function is relativized to, saying why that pair is the natural one here.
- Give each account's verdict and its reasoning in two or three sentences.
- Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed — for instance, if heritable variation in T is restored, or if the population has occupied the relevant environment for many hundreds of generations, or if a different containing system or capacity is chosen.
- Give the strongest available reply from the side that comes out worse, including the conciliatory options: a weakened or generalized selected-effect account that counts differential retention rather than selection proper, a move that grants "derived" or "secondary" functions once maintenance sets in, and the charge that causal-role functions are merely contributions and not functions at all. Say which of these your case resists and which it does not.
- Close with one honest sentence: does the case show the two accounts coming apart extensionally, or does it mainly show that they were answering different questions?

The case is for a seminar on whether the two accounts can diverge anywhere in the actual living world, or whether every apparent divergence is produced by relativizing the causal-role verdict to an uncharitably chosen system-capacity pair. Please keep the whole response under 800 words.
