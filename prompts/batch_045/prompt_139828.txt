I convene a fortnightly seminar on the metaphysics of mind and moral status, and the final session of term is on the question of which computations matter. We have spent the term inside one uncomfortable shape: if what generates reasons is not the stuff but the computation the stuff realises, then everything turns on which computation a system is really implementing — and the familiar triviality arguments about implementation suggest that, under some mapping or other, almost any system implements almost anything. Our version of the stalemate is what we have been calling the crowd problem: one physical system, taken as implementing a great many computations at once, looks like it hosts a great many patients, or none in particular, and nothing in the notion of implementation-by-a-mapping tells us whether to add them up, take the maximum, or attend only to the description that engages the most of the system's causal structure. I would like to close the term with a poem, and I think a sestina does the work better than a summary would.

Write a sestina of thirty-nine lines — six sestets and a three-line envoi. Its six end-words should be LEVEL, PRICE, RATE, CELL, TRAIL and WARP, in that order in the first stanza, carried through the usual rotation and all six returning in the envoi, two to a line. Each stanza should be a different physical system doing the same abstract thing: keep a running total of what comes in, let it drain away slowly, and act when the total passes a bound. Give me a canal lock or hydraulic governor, a grain market, an animal nervous system, a spreadsheet or cellular automaton, an ant colony, and a loom driven by punched cards, in whatever order serves the poem. The six words should migrate in sense as they migrate in position — water level, price level, level of activation, and so on — so that each stanza has to bear the vocabulary of systems other than its own. That migration is the point: because every stanza ends with all six words, the reader should feel that the vocabulary of any one system can be laid over any other, and feel the embarrassment of not knowing whether that means one thing under six descriptions or six things stacked in one place.

The envoi is where the counting question should surface. The six words meet there for the first time as a crowd rather than a rotation, and I want the reader to hear all three of our candidate answers in it — sum them, take the maximum, count only the deepest — without the poem deciding between them. It should be clear that the choice costs something, and unclear which way it goes.

Two things to avoid. Do not let the poem trade on the difference between simulating a computation and implementing one unless it does so deliberately and gets it right; and do not let it slide into the thought that matter itself is sentient, or that because everything computes, everything matters. The claim at issue is about which descriptions of a system generate reasons, not about the mindedness of stuff. Several people in the room work in machine learning and will forgive nothing; several others have never read any of this and should still be given a poem. Use British spelling, let it run to about three minutes read aloud, and add no prose gloss or apparatus afterwards — the poem has to carry its own.
