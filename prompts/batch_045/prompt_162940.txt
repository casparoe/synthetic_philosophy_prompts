I work in logistics and have no philosophy training, but I've been going on long silent retreats for about six years, and somewhere along the way I talked myself into a view about well-being that I can't shake. Stated as carefully as I can: a moment of conscious experience is as good for the subject as it can be if and only if the subject has no consciously experienced need for anything to be different — no craving, no restlessness, no felt lack — and the only thing that makes a moment go worse is the presence of such a need (with suffering as the severe end of it). Pleasure, on this view, isn't good in its own right; it's good only instrumentally, when it quiets a craving that was there. I'm not trying to build a moral theory out of it, only a theory of what makes a stretch of experience go well for the person living it. A friend I argue with — she's the kind of person who says the point of everything is to increase happiness and reduce suffering, and treats those as roughly symmetrical — puts her credence in my view at about 0.15. Mine is about 0.55, and I can feel that the gap between us isn't where we think it is. So: give me your own number, and more importantly decompose it. I want the sub-questions your credence actually rests on, a rough probability and one line of justification for each, and then the combination — and I know that just multiplying conditional probabilities will assume independences that probably aren't there, so tell me where that distorts the result and how you're handling it.

Three things I want folded into the decomposition, because they're where my own confidence moves. First, my friend's central objection is that I've thrown away half the value scale: hedonism has a neutral midpoint with room above it, while my view has only a one-sided distance-from-optimal measure, so it can't say that a moment of contentment plus a great meal is better than a moment of plain contentment. I don't have a reply to that beyond a shrug, and I'd like to know how much of your number rides on it. Second, I've been trying to rescue the view from the objection that it recommends doing nothing at all, by distinguishing craving — a felt pull toward something not yet had — from what I call aiming: being absorbed in a task, having goals, moving toward an outcome, but without the felt lack. A chess player in the middle of a hard game seems to me to be aiming without craving, and that seems compatible with my view; my friend says that distinction is ad hoc and that if it works at all it sneaks something positive back in through the side door. Third, there's a case I can't get past: someone who has habituated to chronic low-grade pain and honestly reports no desire for anything to be different, versus someone in a mild state of absorbed calm. My view seems committed to calling the first person's moment perfect, which feels wrong to me, but I can't say why without helping myself to the very idea of a neutral point that I rejected.
