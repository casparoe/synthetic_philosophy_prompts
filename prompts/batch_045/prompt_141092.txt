I write novels, and the last few submission forms I've filled in for magazines and contests have all grown the same new box: tick here to certify that the work was not created using generative AI. Nothing else on the form has changed. Nobody asks about my editor, who rewrote about a third of my last book, or about the researcher I paid to dig up details about 1970s Cardiff for me, or about the fact that I dictate my first drafts and my partner turns the录音 into paragraphs I then take apart. And nobody cares that I run spellcheck, that my email finishes my sentences, or that I use a thesaurus which regularly hands me the exact word I was groping for.

Here's where I'm stuck, and I want the argument rather than a reading list, because I'll only get lost in citations anyway. What does that tickbox actually presuppose? About authorship, or about understanding, or whatever the right word is. It can't be a straight claim about who produced the sentences: ghostwritten celebrity memoirs get published under someone's name constantly and readers mostly shrug once they find out, and my editor's sentences are legally and morally mine. So there must be some more specific theory in there that makes the model different from the ghost and the thesaurus. Is it that the model does some of the *thinking* the book expresses? Is it about credit, or is it about whether I actually understand the thing with my name on it?

The half-formed idea I keep coming back to is what I call the interview test: a book is really by you if you could sit for two hours of questions about it and not get caught out — why that scene is there, what the character wants, what would have happened if she'd said yes instead. On that test a heavy edit is fine and a ghostwriter is a bit dicey and a model-drafted chapter is hopeless, which sort of matches the practice. But I can't tell whether that's a theory of authorship or just a proxy for something else, and I notice plenty of novelists can't explain their own books in interviews and I don't think anyone wants them banned.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory the box presupposes, and be honest about how much of it the practice strictly requires versus how much it merely sits nicely with. Which rival views does it quietly rule out, and is it even internally consistent — we're fine with the ghostwriter and freaked out by the model, and I want to know whether that's a principled line or just squeamishness. And what would the form have to look like if we swapped in one of the rival views: do we end up with a disclosure of *degree and role* rather than a binary, or blind reading with no question asked, or nothing on the form at all and everything resting on whether I can defend the book in public?

One last thing, because it's the bit that actually worries me and I can't tell if it's coherent. Friends of mine who've messed around with these tools say the real loss isn't that readers get fooled, it's that *they* stop understanding their own work — that writing is how you find out what the book is about, and if something hands you the scene before you've found that out, you end up loosing the thing. If that's the worry, is the tickbox even aimed at the right party? Can a rule about provenance protect an author's own understanding, or is that just not the sort of thing a submission form can do anything about?
