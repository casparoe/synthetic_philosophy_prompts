I'm giving a toast at the wedding of two close friends, and I've realized too late that the genre works against me. Both of them are committed tranquilists: they hold that the only thing that finally matters is the absence of craving and suffering, that the *satisfaction* of a craving is not itself good, and that joy, love, and absorbed engagement in work are strictly worth nothing — merely not-bad. They've asked me not to say anything at the reception that they would have to disavow, which is a real constraint, because a wedding toast is built almost entirely out of positive-good talk. *May you always be this happy. May all your dreams come true. What the two of you have is a wonderful thing.* On their accounting the first is empty, and the second is worse than empty: a craving satisfied in a way that renews it nets out to zero.

The room is split. Half of it is meditation-retreat and suffering-focused-ethics people who will hear any fudge the instant I make one; the other half is family who have never encountered the view and will take a plain statement of it as bleak, or as a slight against the marriage.

So: write a four-to-five-minute toast that lands as affection rather than as a seminar, and that doesn't cheat — no smuggling in positive value under a poetic name. If there is something a tranquilist can sincerely wish for a marriage (a lower background craving load, a reliable quiet, fewer hostages to fortune), that's the material I want you working with.

Two complications I want handled rather than smoothed over. First, the gap between quiet-after-satisfaction and quiet-without-wanting: they agree the second is what matters and the first is nearly worthless, so nothing I say about their life together should rest on the first. Second, they disagree with each other about a third case — total absorption in a task, no felt lack and no craving present at all. She counts it as neutral; he counts it as a genuine instance of the good. They met while restoring a sailboat together and their shared life is mostly made of that state, so I can't avoid the topic and I can't let the toast take a side in an argument they are still having.

After the toast, add a short note: which single line leans hardest on a reading of tranquilism you're least confident is right, and which line you're least confident lands with the family half of the room.
