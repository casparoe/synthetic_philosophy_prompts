I write Haskell for a living and lean on category-theory-flavoured thinking: isomorphism, transport of structure, functors and natural transformations, parametricity, quotienting by an equivalence, and the reflex that only isomorphism-invariant predicates are worth stating. I'm fluent enough that you can use those terms bare. I keep bumping into philosophers arguing about the "hole argument" in general relativity and I want it in my vocabulary, plus an honest report on what gets lost.

Heres the setup as I understand it. Take a solution of Einstein's field equations, pick a matter-free region (the hole), and apply a smooth relabeling of the spacetime points that is the identity outside the hole but reshuffles things inside. You get a second solution that agrees with the first everywhere outside the hole and nowhere inside it. If you think the points have identities of their own, independent of the fields sitting on them, then one and the same past has two different futures and determinism is dead. If you think the relabeling is mere redescription, determinism survives, but you've lost the right to say this point has such-and-such curvature and can only assert isomorphism-invariant stuff. I also gather something like this goes back to Leibniz's shift argument against Newtonian absolute space — correct me if that's a bad gloss.

Translate the whole dispute into my vocabulary as precisely as you can, including whatever work "determinism" and "possible physical situation" are doing, and say whether the substantivalist is simply committing the sin of treating non-invariant facts as facts. Then the residue: what doesn't map, and whether that's because my framework is blind to something real (modality? laws? time?) or because the philosophical disagreement is thinner than it sounds.
