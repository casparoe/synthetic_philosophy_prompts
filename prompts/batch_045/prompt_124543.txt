I'm in my third year, taking a seminar called Ethics and Development, and my final paper is supposed to be about whether international anti-corruption norms are a universal moral requirement or a Western moralism with an imperial passport. I realize now that I picked the topic because of a fight at my family's dinner table that I keep losing, so I'd like help with the concrete case as much as with the abstraction. My uncle runs a small road-building firm in the West African country my parents emigrated from. Last year his firm won a municipal resurfacing contract. Two weeks after the award was announced — after, not before, a fact he insists is decisive — he went to the deputy director of the municipal works department and left an envelope of cash and a gold necklace for the man's daughter's wedding. His account of it: his bid was genuinely the lowest and technically the strongest, the official had said so in the meeting in front of two other people, and he would have won anyway; the money was gratitude for not letting his file sit on a desk for six months, which everyone in the industry knows can happen to anyone for nothing; and a gift to a family at a wedding is not a commercial exchange at all — you don't price it, you don't negotiate it, and if you walked in and offered cash for a signature he would have thrown you out. My American roommate says it's a bribe, full stop, and cites the standard NGO definition of corruption as the misuse of an entrusted role for private benefit. My mother says my roommate's own father got his job because a fraternity brother made one phone call, and that the only difference between her brother-in-law and him is that Americans have a better vocabulary for laundering the same thing.

Three things I can't sort out, and I want each taken seriously rather than dissolved. First, the no-difference problem: suppose my uncle is right that the contract would have gone to him anyway, so that nothing about anyone's conduct or any outcome changed. Is there a wrong here with no victim and no effect, and if so, what kind of wrong is it and who is wronged? Second, the wedding-gift problem: my professor has floated what he calls the multi-track test — that a payment is corrosive precisely when it attaches to a good that is supposed to be allocated by a rule treating applicants as interchangeable (a permit, a license, a tender), whereas it is unproblematic when it attaches to a good where personal judgment is legitimately part of the role (choosing a colleague, writing a recommendation for a student you know). I half suspect this test is rigged to convict my uncle while exonerating my roommate's father, but I also can't see what else would distinguish them. Is the gift/bribe line a real moral distinction or just a class- and geography-coded one? Third, the universality problem: even granting that something is wrong, is it the same wrong in a setting where the alternative to a "gift" is that your file never moves at all, and is my roommate in any position to say so?
