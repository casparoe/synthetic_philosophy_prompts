I teach a second-year general-ed course called something like Science, Reason and Society — about 22 students, mostly non-majors, one 90-minute slot a week. In a couple of weeks we have a session on scientific change and I want to run a game rather than lecture: they should walk out having felt two things, that an inherited framework tells you what counts as a puzzle, what counts as an anomaly and what counts as a solution, and that when two frameworks clash there may be no neutral vocabulary left in which to say what the disagreement even is. Most of them have never heard of Kuhn, two or three have, and I dont want to say the words paradigm or incommensurability, or hand out any reading, until the last ten minutes.

Here's the rough idea I've been playing with, which you should use, fix, or replace: a fictional astronomy setup where two teams work from the same evidence but different handbooks. There's a printed catalogue of flare events on a fictional moon, each card giving a sky position, a timestamp, a peak reading and a background reading. Team A's handbook tells them to compute a quantity it calls intensity (peak minus a background estimated from neighbouring cells); Team B's tells them to compute amplitude (peak divided by a slowly varying baseline). I want those two derived columns to be genuinely related but not by any monotone transform, so the teams end up disagreeing about which flares are the strongest and there's nothing internal to either framework that settles it. Then a second batch of cards arrives from a new instrument (call it the night-side array) that breaks whatever regularity each team had settled on, and a chance to convert to a third framework that rederives both quantities from the raw peak and background and in doing so reclassifies a few older events. What I actually need is the game: printable materials (cards, ledgers — no laptops, no money, points on the board plus maybe a sliver of course credit), the rules and the scoring, the exact formulas and a set of 30-40 numbers I can build the deck from, a minute-by-minute plan for the 90 minutes, and something for one or two extra students to do besides being on a team — I was thinking a funding committee that allocates observing time, so the conversion decision has a social dimension and not just an evidential one. Scoring-wise, two hooks I've been toying with and want you to develop or scrap: prediction points for calling new cards correctly in your own vocabulary, and separate translation points you can only earn by predicting what the other team will say or by writing a conversion manual both teams can live with — with the catch that the manual always breaks somewhere specific, and I want the students to find that place themselves rather than be shown it.

Then the debrief. Map particular moments in the game onto the ideas: puzzle-solving under an inherited framework, anomaly, patching under a budget as auxiliary hypotheses, crisis, conversion as something more like a gestalt switch than an inference, the key terms quietly changing meaning, and the pull to rewrite the history so it looks like everyone was always measuring the same thing. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm guessing at least one student digs in and insists you can always just describe the raw readings neutrally, and I'd like a way of handling that person that doesn't simply assume the conclusion — and be honest about what the game can't deliver. My own worry is that I've rigged the data so incommensurability is guaranteed by construction and that nothing in it reproduces whatever is perceptual, or world-sized, about a real shift; tell me if that's right and what else is missing that I'll have to supply out loud. If the astronomy framing is the wrong vehicle, say so and pitch a different one instead.
