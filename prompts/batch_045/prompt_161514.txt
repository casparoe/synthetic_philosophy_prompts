hey — I'm in a seminar on chance this term (philosophy of science dept, mixed grad/undergrads) and our last session is on interpretations of probability. everyone's bored of the survey-paper format, so I volunteered to close with a poem, and I want it to actually do something rather than just gesture at the debate. I keep rewritting the first line in my head and getting nowhere, which is why I'm outsourcing this.

What I have in mind is a Petrarchan sonnet — octave, turn at line 9, sestet, iambic pentameter but please don't slave over the meter. The scene inside it should be a strictly one-shot trial: a single caesium atom in a detector, in a lab that's being decommissioned that evening, nobody will ever run this experiment again, there is no long run for it to be a sample of. The octave builds the ordinary confident pre-decay way of talking — this atom has a one-in-two-ish chance over the next hour, there was a fact of the matter about what that chance was before the click. Then line 9 is where the frequency view walks in and takes it apart: chance lives in series, a single trial isn't one, and when you try to embed the atom in some series the class you pick is gerrymandered (atoms of this isotope, atoms in this building, atoms whose decay somebody bothers to record) and every choice hands you a different number. The sestet is the two rescue attempts, and the bit I care most about is that neither gets to be clean: the disposition view gives this one setup a real tendency but then has to say what that tendency was doing after the click, whether it explains the outcome or only re-describes it; the betting view gives you your number back but only as something about you, and then has to go borrow the world's number anyway (Lewis's rule that your odds should just defer to the chance — "principal principle", I think he called it?). They keep needing each other and never quite merging.

sorry for the list of constraints: no -ism words, no "credence", no "Bayesian", and if you can swing it the word "probability" shouldn't appear at all — do it with chance, odds, half, whatever. my professor offhandedly described a single-case chance as an IOU the world writes you and may or may not honor, which I weirdly love, so feel free to use that figure but don't let it carry the whole poem. I don't want it to pick a winner or turn into an argument for one interpretation; the closing two lines should say, as plainly as you can, what each view has to give up. it's for reading out loud to about fifteen people, half of whom know this literature and half of whom are only there because it's the last class — the ones who know it should be able to tell which lines belong to which position and see the reference-class move happening in the turn, and the rest should just hear a good poem about one unrepeatable thing.
