My setup: I work in the accomodations and medical-leave office of a big employer, about 30,000 staff. Around 40 claims a month land on my desk, and for each one my team — me plus two occupational-health nurses with no philosophy training at all — has to decide whether this person's thing counts as a "medical condition" for our purposes. If yes, they get paid medical leave and mandatory workplace accomodation, and we're not allowed to second-guess the schedule their clinician signed off on. If no, it gets routed to "flexible working," which is discretionary, unpaid, and the line manager can just refuse it. So the classification does real work either way, and people argue with us constantly. Realistically we get twenty minutes per claim, and whatever we decide has to be defensible in a one-page plain-language note, because appeals go to a lay panel of three people, two of whom are union-elected employees who will absolutely read the note out loud at us.

Here's the wrinkle that keeps causing our biggest arguements. Legal handed us one sentence to apply and it doesn't match any of the textbook views I've read about: a condition counts if (a) something in the person is failing to do what it's supposed to do, or is doing it badly, AND (b) the person would rather not have it, and would still rather not have it if the workplace were arranged differently. I've started calling (b) the Regret Clause, and legal is clear that it's meant to be a second independent filter, not just a restatement of (a).

The case type that generates most of the fights I want you to solve is what I've started calling environment-mismatch stuff: nothing in the person is obviously broken, and they'd be more or less fine in a different job, a different building, or a different shift pattern, but they're not fine in this one. Night-shift security guards whose sleep and metabolism have come apart; people who cannot think at all in an open-plan office; a forklift driver whose colour vision is completely adequate for daily life but not for the new colour-coded signalling system we installed last year. That kind of thing.
