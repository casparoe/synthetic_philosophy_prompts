I've been going back and forth on how much, if at all, it's OK to care less about something just because it happens later, and I figure the fastest way to find out what I actually think is to write down the cases where I already have a verdict.

1. Two policy options, no funny business: 10,000 people get free cataract surgery. Option A does it in 2030, option B in 2230, to a different set of 10,000 people who'd exist either way, equally badly sighted, and none of us knows which set we'd end up in. Stipulate there's no way to invest or bank anything in between, no risk of the programme being cancelled, and no growth, so the later people aren't richer. I feel literally zero pull towards A. Push it to 2430: still nothing.

2. Same, except you can put the money in the bank at 4% and buy twice as many operations later. Obviously later. But that doesn't feel like a verdict about time, it feels like arithmetic.

3. Same as 1, but there's a 1-in-50 chance the whole thing gets destroyed before delivery. Discount by about 2%. Fine — that's just expected value wearing a hat.

4. My own knee: surgery this year or in 2036, same surgeon, same outcome, same recovery, and stipulate I'm certain to be alive and mobile in 2036 and that I won't spend the decade dreading it. I want it this year. Same with a really good bottle of wine, or a trip I know I'd enjoy just as much later. And I don't feel stupid about it, which is the part that bothers me given case 1.

5. A benefit scheduled to arrive after the recipient is dead: worth nothing. Obviously.

6. Some rich person leaves money in a trust, invested, to be spent in one lump on something public two centuries from now. I think that's admirable, not wasteful — even though people who know about this stuff tell me the same money spent immediately would have done more good. I don't believe them and I can't say why.

7. Costs instead of benefits, same setup as 1: either we pay for a cleanup now or the 2230 lot pay it. I'm much more relaxed about handing them the cost than I am about handing them the benefit, which I suspect is cowardice plus compound interest, but I'd like to know if any principle makes it respectable.

8. Where I'm genuinely torn: me at 60 versus me at 85 with advanced dementia, if the good has to land in one place or the other. Part of me wants to discount the 85 case hard; part of me says that's just case 5 smuggled in with some squeamishness I shouldn't trust.

9. Long horizons where I've stipulated away every risk: at 300 years I feel a faint pull towards sooner, at 1000 years a real one, and I can't point to what changed. My guess is my stipulations are doing less work than I pretend, and what's actually growing is how much I trust my own model of what "a benefit" would even mean by then.

My own stab at a principle, which I don't fully believe: discounting is legitimate only when it's tracking (a) a real rate of return, (b) a real chance the benefit never lands or the recipient never exists, or (c) the fact that my grip on the situation — my ability to plan it, promise it, be held to it — gets weaker the further out I go, so a very distant benefit is really a bet on a long chain of other people's future choices. Everything else is just wanting it now.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly? Case 4 is the one I expect it to choke on, and 1 and 4 together are the pair I can't reconcile. If your principle says I have to drop one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why that one. And tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed — a benefit going to people who'll be much poorer than us, say, or one that has to be handed off through five successive generations of a committee that could vote to spend it early.
