You are a philosopher of collective memory and nationalism who has spent a decade as the in-house ethicist and speechwriter for a national heritage ministry. You are unusually good at noticing when a commemorative practice has quietly become inconsistent with itself, and at writing bureaucratic documents whose prose is perfectly smooth while their recommendations are quietly appalling.

Task: write a white paper for the Ministry of Narrative Continuity of the Republic of Vardania, a small fictional state whose national identity has, for two centuries, rested on a single founding defeat at the Bridge of Salt. The official doctrine is that the Vardanian cause was just, the odds were overwhelming, the defeat was total, and the defeat itself (not the war, and not the aim) is the sacred object. Defeat Day is the largest public holiday, the battlefield site is the country's biggest tourism earner, the Society of Descendants of the Salt is a formidable lobbying bloc, and the standard civic formulation is that Vardania was beaten by numbers and not by right.

The crisis the white paper addresses is a double one. First, an internationally brokered settlement has just returned to Vardania everything the original cause was fought over: the territory, the language rights, the bishopric. Second, a truth commission has established, uncontroversially and with documentation, that Vardanian forces committed the first atrocity of the war. So the loss has retroactively been undone and the moral ledger has been dirtied. The Ministry calls this the Victory Contamination, and the white paper exists to solve it.

Before drafting, think carefully in a few short private paragraphs: enumerate the structural requirements a lost cause has to satisfy in order to keep doing identity work (why the loss must be undeserved, why the aim must be restated at a high level of description rather than concretely, why dead heroes serve better than surviving ones, why the grievance must remain unfinished, why a defeat that is commemorated too well stops being a defeat, and what happens when commemoration is also an export industry). Then enumerate the retrofit options the Ministry could adopt, and pick the one that follows the logic of a loss myth most rigorously rather than the one that is most sensible.

Requirements for the piece:

- Form: an internal government white paper, roughly 800 to 1,000 words, with a title, an executive summary, numbered sections, at least one table or risk register, and a recommendation. Use real white-paper idiom: costings, stakeholder impacts, implementation timelines, consultation periods.
- The paper should seriously canvass at least four retrofit strategies and then recommend one, rejecting the others for reasons that display the mechanics of loss mythology. Candidate mechanisms you may use or improve on: aim abstraction, in which the original aim is restated at a higher level of generality so that it can still have been lost; loss substitution, in which commemoration migrates to a different, more serviceable defeat; the reserve grievance, in which the state adopts or licenses an adjacent people's defeat to keep the narrative stocked; a depreciation schedule for public grief; and a martyrdom floor requiring that the commemorated heroes all be dead, so that no survivor can compromise the story by having later made peace.
- Sustained irony, deadpan throughout. No character may say the quiet part aloud; the reader must infer that the nation is now managing a grievance as a managed asset, that the myth has become an industry, and that the least disturbing option on the table is disturbing. No winking, no punchline disclaimers, no authorial asides inside the document.
- The jokes must come from the actual logic of lost-cause myths: the paper's recommendations should be recognizably what a real heritage bureaucracy would conclude if it were fully honest about the function of the myth, and a scholar of nationalism should find the satire fair rather than merely hostile.
- Invented officials, institutes, and stakeholder groups are welcome and should be named. No real named person, living or dead, appears as a target or a character. The document may gesture at familiar archetypes of lost causes, noble defeats, and deliverance-from-disaster stories, but it must not rest on any checkable fact, date, figure, or quotation from a real case, and it must contain no citations, footnotes, or bibliography, since it is being written by a model without web access.

Finish with a short afterword, out of character and clearly separated, of about 150 words: state which real feature of lost-cause mythology each of your three best jokes targets, and say honestly which single feature the paper exaggerates beyond what the historical record supports.
