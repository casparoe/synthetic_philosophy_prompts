I'm a backend engineer -- mostly Go, zero philosophy training beyond whatever seeps in from podcasts -- and I got completely nerd-sniped by an argument at work that I can't shake. We were writing up a postmortem for an outage where someone noticed a bad config during a routine deploy and just... didn't fix it, and we spent an hour arguing about whether "saw it and left it" is really a different kind of thing from "pushed the bad config in the first place." Everyone on the team had strong gut feelings and not one of us could say what the underlying rule was supposed to be. So here are my own verdicts on a pile of cases, and I want to know if there's a single principle that gets them or whether I'm just holding two incompatible intuitions at the same time. (1) I grab a kid and hold his head under the bathtub water until he drowns: monstrous, and I did it. (2) I walk in, the kid is slipping under, I could grab him with zero risk to myself, and I stand there: still monstrous, but definately less bad than (1), and I can't say why. (3) A doctor unplugs a ventilator from a patient who was never going to recover, at the family's request: fine, maybe even kind. (4) A doctor never hooks that same patient up in the first place, same prognosis, same request: also fine -- and here's what bugs me, it feels *exactly* as fine as (3), so the doing/allowing distinction seems to do no work at all here even though it obviously does work in (1) vs (2). (5) A lifeguard watches a kid drown in the shallow end and doesn't move: to me that's almost as bad as (1). (6) A random guy on the beach does the exact same nothing, same kid, same everything except the job title: way less bad than (5), even though the physical events are identical. (7) I never signed up for the organ donor registry and a neighbor dies needing a kidney: nobody's fault, zero blame. (8) I did sign up, I matched, and I ghost the hospital the morning of the surgery: I feel like that's genuinely bad, not just a little bad -- but it's still technically an omission, so if you tell me doing and allowing are morally different, explain this one too.

Then the tech-flavored ones, which are the ones I actually care about. (9) A monitoring service I wrote suppresses a critical alarm, a patient dies: I feel worse about myself here than in (10), where I inherited the same bug, spotted it during a routine deploy, and didn't push the one-line fix that was already sitting in my branch. (11) I set up a cron job months ago that deletes a life-critical alert rule at midnight; I go on vacation and never disarm it, and the rule dies at midnight while I'm asleep on a beach somewhere. (12) Same system, but the vendor shipped the opposite default: the rule stays on only if I log in every night and re-arm it, and one night I don't. (13) A patient's life-support device runs on software my company owns; the license lapses and I could renew it with one click for twenty bucks, and I don't. My gut says (11) is killing, (12) is letting die, and (13) is somewhere in between -- but (11) vs (12) looks like it might just be about which way the vendor happened to set the default, and hanging blame on that feels totally arbitrary, like the morality would flip if someone had picked a different config value.

So: is there one principle here -- something about who initiates a causal sequence versus who fails to stop one, or about what counts as the normal or default baseline, or about duties and roles and who had the easy option -- that gets all thirteen? I want the clause-by-clause version, with which of my cases motivates each clause, and I want you to be straight with me about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which ones I should just drop. Don't hand me a slogan like "killing is worse than letting die," because (3) vs (4) already breaks that for me and (5) vs (6) breaks it in the other direction. And if your conclusion is that the doing/allowing line isn't actually doing any real moral work and something else is, say that explicitly, tell me what that something else is, and tell me what evidence or case would show me I'm wrong. The two I keep going back and forth on: my team ships a change that removes a safety check because the check was slow and expensive, and a user dies as a result -- I genuinely can't tell whether that's a (1) or a (2); and a hospital moves the only working ventilator off a patient the model says has a week left and onto one who needs it right now, no malice, pure triage -- fine, or not, and why. I'd rather have the principle than the individual answers, but give me both.
