My flatmate Ravi and a friend from his economics course got into it last night about voting, and I kept out of it, so I'm bringing it to you instead. Context: their city is thinking about introducing a scheme where everyone who votes gets entered into a draw for a 5000 dollar prize. Ravi says this "solves" the paradox of voting: your chance of casting the one ballot that decides anything is basically zero, so the expected benefit of voting is about zero while the cost of showing up is real — but a lottery ticket makes the expected payoff positive, so voting becomes rational. (1) His friend says that doesn't solve the paradox, it just bribes people: the puzzle was whether it's rational to vote *in order to change the outcome*, and a prize gives you a reason to turn up but not a reason to care about the result — and the people a prize actually moves are the least informed ones, so you end up with a bigger and worse-informed electorate. (2) Ravi says "solve the paradox" just means give a rational person *a* reason to cast a ballout; and anyway, if the prize drags in irregular voters, the electorate ends up looking more like the population, which is the whole point of aggregating votes; and worrying about *who* turns up is a complaint about democracy, not about rationality. (3) His friend: okay, but here's the version that actually bites. Your vote only matters in the case where it's decisive, and the decisive case is a near-tie. A near-tie means the electorate is split roughly evenly, which is exactly the situation where your own judgment is least likely to be what breaks the tie correctly — a small error of yours decides it. So there's an argument that the rational thing is to abstain precisely when your vote would count, and the lottery doesn't touch that at all. (4) Ravi: but then the conclusion is that you should stay home in every close election and vote only in landslides, where your vote does nothing. An argument whose upshot is "the elections worth voting in are the ones you shouldn't vote in" has gone wrong somewhere. (5) Can you go through the five moves and say who's right about what, and what they're both missing?
