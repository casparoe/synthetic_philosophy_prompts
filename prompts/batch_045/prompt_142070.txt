I'm TA-ing a first-year seminar on Dostoevsky and ethics, and we're doing the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov next week — the bit where Christ turns up in Seville at the height of the Inquisition, gets thrown in a cell, and the old cardinal spends the night explaining to him why the Church was right to correct his mistakes. I've taught it twice and both times the discussion collapsed into the same shape: everyone agrees the Inquisitor is creepy, nobody can actually say what's wrong with his argument, and we end up vaguely gesturing at freedom being sacred. I want to do better this time, so I'd like a real reading of the thing rather than a summary.

The part I keep getting stuck on is that the Inquisitor seems to be running together several genuinely different claims, and my students slide between them without noticing. Roughly: (i) a prediction about human behaviour, that if you actually offer people bread and security on one side and the torment of deciding for themselves on the other, the overwhelming majority take the bread every time and go on taking it; (ii) a welfare claim, that they'll be happier once someone else is carrying the weight of their consciences; (iii) a conceptual claim, that freedom and happiness aren't just contingently hard to combine but pull in opposite directions, because any freedom worth wanting has to include the freedom to ruin your own life, so an arrangement that guarantees happiness has by that very fact removed the freedom; and (iv) something that looks like an internal critique, i.e. that judged by Christ's own aim — the salvation of everyone, not just a handful of the strong — the method he picked was self-defeating, and the Church's correction of it is what fidelity to his project really required. I'd like you to separate those, tell me which ones the text actually commits the Inquisitor to, which he could drop without losing anything, and where the real pressure point is. There's also a wrinkle I can't shake about (ii): the Inquisitor doesn't get to be happy himself. He and his handful of elect take on the suffering of knowing, and Ivan's final remark is that the old man keeps his idea anyway, which Ivan clearly thinks matters. So the bargain isn't freedom traded for happiness tout court; it's a redistribution, where the many get tranquility and the few get the agony plus the guilt of having chosen it for everyone. Does that make him a hypocrite, a tragic figure, or does his own life become a counterexample to his claim that freedom is unendurable?

Two more things I'd love folded in if you can. First: a student last year made a comparison I rolled my eyes at and now think is on to something. She said the Inquisitor is basically a recommendation algorithm in a cassock — a system that knows you better than you know yourself, quietly removes the anxiety of choosing, keeps you fed and mildly content while you outsource judgement to it, and unlike his Church doesn't even need to burn anybody. I want to know where that analogy genuinely holds (is the thing being given up the same thing?) and where it quietly distorts the poem, because I don't want to let the class get away with a clever anachronism. Second, the ending: Christ says nothing all night and only kisses the old man on the lips, the Inquisitor lets him go and tells him never to come back, and then in the frame Ivan asks Alyosha whether he renounces him and Alyosha just kisses him, and Ivan laughs and calls it plagiarism. I've never been sure whether to present that as Dostoevsky answering the argument or dodging it: a kiss is not a reason, and it's suspiciously convenient that this poem's Christ stays silent exactly where an answer is needed. But maybe the silence is the point, or maybe the doubling of the kiss — Christ to the Inquisitor, Alyosha to Ivan — is doing philosophical work about what kind of reply a person even is. So: is the kiss an answer, a refusal to answer, or a claim about what could never be an answer? And what does the chapter as a whole add to the freedom-and-happiness debate that the bare arguments don't? Please tell me if the reading I sketched above is wrong or if I'm missing a distinction that does work — I'd rather be corrected now than in front of forty undergrads, and I defintely don't want to end up gesturing at the sacredness of freedom a third time.
