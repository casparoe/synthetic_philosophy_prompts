A friend and I keep going in circles about whether satisficing is a genuinely distinct kind of rationality or just optimizing in a cheap disguise. Here's the claim I want a number on: there are realistic choice situations in which setting an aspiration level and taking the first option that clears it is rationally better than anything that can be represented as maximizing expected utility once every cost of searching, thinking and computing has already been priced into the utility function — i.e. the collapse argument fails somewhere. Count it true if there's at least one defensible normative story on which satisficing rules are mandated for some agents even after all those costs are internalized; count it false if every honest case turns out to be optimization over a bigger option space.

Two wrinkles I want you to actually deal with rather than skate past. First, the cases I find strongest arent ones where you face a huge fixed menu (apartments on a listing site) but ones where the options dont pre-exist your search and get assembled as you go — putting together a research programme, hiring a team, designing a house — so there may be no well-defined option set to maximize over in the first place. Second, my own aspiration levels clearly drift upward when searching is easy and downward when its hard, and I cant tell whether that's a bug or the whole point.

Give me your credence, decomposed: (a) that the search-cost collapse argument really does cover all the fixed-menu cases; (b) that the assembled-options cases are genuinely outside the reach of expected-utility maximization rather than just a modeling inconvenience; (c) that aspiration-level drift is rationally defensible rather than weakness of will or a biased heuristic; (d) that "rational" is determinate enough here for the question to have an answer at all. Number plus one line of justification for each, then say how you'd combine them. Finally, tell me which term the final number is least robust to and what argument or evidence would shift it most. I'm at about 0.6 and he's at about 0.15 — tell me where you think that gap most likely lives.
