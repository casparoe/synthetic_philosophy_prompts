I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a hospital with an active epilepsy surgery programme, and it is my turn to draft the memo for Thursday's meeting. I've read a fair amount of blog-level material on split-brain cases, so please don't spend the memo establishing why the case is philosophically interesting; two of our members already know enough to use the words "person" and "subject of experience" at each other. What I need is a bottom line and the argument for it.

The patient is 44 and had a complete corpus callosotomy eleven years ago for drug-resistant epilepsy. He now has a slow-growing left frontal tumour; his surgeon wants to resect within about three weeks and puts the cost of a further month's delay at a few additional points of risk of permanent expressive-language deficit. Verbally he consents clearly and repeatedly, and he passes our ordinary capacity interview. But when neuropsychology presented the choice separately to each hemisphere — options flashed briefly to one visual field while he fixated, answered with the hand on that side — the split was stable across three sessions: right field, right hand, and voice say operate; left field and left hand say do not. Shown recordings of himself answering both ways, he said the left hand "just does that sometimes," then, writing with his left hand, asked us not to let the surgeons cut. Our neuropsychologist cautions that left-hand responding in these patients is noisy: intermanual conflict and alien-hand phenomena can produce behaviour that carries no preference at all.

Two further facts. Three years ago, before any of this was foreseeable, he signed a directive saying that if his left hand ever appeared to contradict him, the hand should be disregarded rather than treated as a second opinion. His wife says the opposite, and cites an episode two years ago in which his left hand refused a medication he had verbally accepted and he later, by left hand, thanked her for having it stopped.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course — operate on schedule, operate only after some further protocol, or transfer the decision to a surrogate — with a time frame and conditions; it should say plainly what we would be overriding if we proceed on his verbal consent alone; and it should say what evidence would justify treating the left-hand responses as the patient's own refusal rather than as motor noise, and how much weight his earlier directive deserves given that he wrote it without envisaging this situation.
