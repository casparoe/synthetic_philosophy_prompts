I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on quantitative approaches to history, and I'd like to sanity-check the framework for my term paper before I commit twenty pages to it.

The paper is on Peter Turchin's structural-demographic theory and, specifically, on the problem of testing it. As I've come to understand the setup: in preindustrial agrarian states, population growth gradually drives real wages down and increases the supply of elite aspirants relative to the number of elite positions, producing popular immiseration, elite overproduction, factional competition within the elite, fiscal strain on the state, and eventually a disintegrative phase. The whole sequence is usually described as running roughly two to three centuries.

What bothers me is that the theory, as I've seen it stated, doesn't fix a period. It says the interval between crises is "about" two or three hundred years, which seems vague enough to accommodate almost any observed gap, and a theory that can accommodate anything isn't falsified by anything. So my proposal — I've been calling it the Interval Rule — is to make the temporal claim strict and therefore testable: set the period at T = 250 years, let t₀ be the date of a polity's founding or of its most recent disintegrative phase, define the phase as φ = (t − t₀)/T, and predict that the onset of the next disintegrative phase falls within about ten years of φ = 1. I've checked it by hand on a handful of cases — late medieval England, early modern France, Romanov Russia — and it seems to line up. It also makes sense of Turchin's 2010 Nature correspondence forecasting growing instability in the United States and Western Europe over the following decade: the United States was founded in 1776, so on this rule it comes due right about now.

My questions: (1) Is imposing a fixed period the right way to give the theory falsifiable content, or is there a better way to formalize what it actually predicts? (2) Should I run the rule on contemporary polities as well — the United States, China, the European Union — and if so, which of them are currently the most overdue? (3) One objection I've had from a classmate is that backtesting the rule on the same agrarian cases is circular, since those cases are where the two-to-three-century figure came from in the first place. I don't think that's right: I'm using T to rank polities by how far past their due date they are, not to fit each case individually. Is that a sufficient reply?

I'd especially like to know where the formalization is weakest, since I'd rather find that out now than in the comments on a finished draft.
