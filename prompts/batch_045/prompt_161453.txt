I lead a small reliability-modelling team at a water utility. Each week we update failure probabilities for a few hundred pumps, using both sensor summaries and short written reports from inspectors, and an engineer signs off on anything that crosses a maintenance threshold. Two years ago we chose Jeffrey conditionalization as our update rule for exactly one reason, which is also the reason we cannot easily abandon it: we were told, and it seemed to us to follow from the formula, that it lets you update on uncertain evidence without committing to a likelihood. Our reports are noisy and we have no defensible model of how a report is generated by the state of the equipment, so a rule that asks only for the new probability of the evidence and then interpolates across the hypothesis looked like the answer to our problem. We apply it in a spreadsheet, in whatever order the reports arrive, and we had assumed that the order of application is harmless.

I am now unsure that we understand what we are doing, and I would like a rule my team can actually run. Here is the case that made the problem concrete.

Take a single pump and let H be the proposition that it is degraded. Our prior is P(H) = 0.30. Our report taxonomy is binary: R means the report suggests degradation, and its negation means it does not. Historically P(R) = 0.40, P(H | R) = 0.60, and P(H | not-R) = 0.10, which is consistent with the prior. This week's inspection moves our confidence in R from 0.40 to 0.70 without settling it — precisely the uncertain-evidence case the rule is meant for. Applying the rule on the binary partition gives P'(H) = 0.70 × 0.60 + 0.30 × 0.10 = 0.45, which sits exactly on our maintenance threshold, so the difference matters to us.

One of our engineers then pointed out that R is a category in a taxonomy with three leaves: a strong indication, a weak indication, and no indication. Historically these have probabilities 0.10, 0.30 and 0.60, with P(H | strong) = 0.90, P(H | weak) = 0.50 and P(H | none) = 0.10 — consistent with both the prior and the binary figures. Asked to split this week's 0.70, she gives 0.30 on strong, 0.40 on weak, 0.30 on none. Applying the rule on the three-cell partition gives 0.30 × 0.90 + 0.40 × 0.50 + 0.30 × 0.10 = 0.50, five points above the threshold. So the same report, the same elicited probability that the report suggests degradation, and two different numbers. When we back out what the finer treatment implies about the coarse conditional, we get 0.47 ÷ 0.70 ≈ 0.67 rather than the 0.60 we started with, which looks to us like a contradiction rather than a discovery.

My questions are these. First, which partition is the correct one to update on, and by what test should my team choose it, given that in practice our inspectors can usually give us only the coarse 0.70 and cannot reliably split it? I had been assuming there is a canonical choice — the finest partition our report taxonomy supports, or perhaps the one the report is most naturally described in — and that in the absence of a split we should simply use the coarsest partition we can elicit. Second, and what I actually want to build: we have drafted an internal rule we call refinement-invariant updating, which requires that the posterior be the same whether we update on a partition or on any refinement of it, so that the coarse and fine treatments must agree by construction. Can such a requirement be satisfied in general, and is it compatible with the invariance condition the rule needs? Specifically, if we demand it at every level of resolution, do we end up having to specify, cell by cell, the probability of the hypothesis given each possible report? Because if so, we are back to writing down the likelihood we chose this framework to avoid, and I would rather know that now than after another year of thresholds.

Third, one of my colleagues argues that none of this threatens us, because our evidence is noisy and a noisy report does not itself depend on the state of the pump, so the condition the rule requires holds automatically in our setting and the choice of partition is merely a bookkeeping convenience. That argument sounds wrong to me but I cannot say precisely why. Please tell me whether it is wrong, and if so what the invariance condition is actually asserting in a case like ours.

What I need from you is not a survey of the literature but an operational answer: the test we should apply to choose a partition, the circumstances in which the coarse treatment is legitimate and those in which it is not, whether our refinement-invariance draft can be made to work or should be abandoned, what to do about the order in which reports arrive, and an explicit statement of which parts of this remain judgement calls that an engineer must sign off on rather than outputs of the rule. If the honest answer is that the appeal which led us to adopt this framework — that it updates on uncertain evidence without a likelihood — does not survive, please say so plainly and tell us what we would have to supply instead.
