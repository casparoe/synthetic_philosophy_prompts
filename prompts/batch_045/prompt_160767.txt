I teach a utilitarianism-and-its-critics seminar every spring, and the one item that reliably splits the room and never resolves is Williams's integrity objection: the complaint that consequentialism alienates an agent from his own actions because it counts everything he merely fails to prevent as equally his business (negative responsibility), and because it makes him deliberate from thoughts he couldn't act on without embarrassment — the one-thought-too-many case, where the man who can rescue either his wife or a stranger is supposed to need the further thought that rescuing his wife is impersonally licensed. I've also been chewing on this for about three years in a work-in-progress group with two colleagues, and it has stalled in a way that now interests me more than the original question does. Nobody denies the phenomenology: the consequentialist in the group happily grants that acting on the impersonal calculus in the Jim-and-the-Indians case, or in the George case where the job is the only way to support his family, feels like a betrayal of who you are. She just says the feeling is explained by something else — the cost to your motives, the demandingness, your epistemic limits — without conceding that the action is wrong or that integrity is a moral consideration at all. And the Williamsian says no, the feeling is the datum and integrity is precisely what consequentialism cannot represent. We have gone around this maybe a dozen times.

My hunch, which I want treated as a hunch: the dispute isn't really about morality at all, it's three disputes stacked under one heading. One is about the metaphysics of reasons — whether there is any genuinely agent-relative reason, or whether everything that looks like one is just a strong attachment plus a first-personal mode of presentation. One is about which property of a project does the work: a grad student of mine suggested we separate mere depth of attachment from identity-conferral (how central the project is to who the agent takes himself to be), and claim that consequentialists can perfectly well model the first and have no room for the second, so the whole objection turns on whether identification has normative force over and above strength of attachment. I don't think that's quite right, but it's the most interesting thing anyone in the group has said. A third, which a colleague in political philosophy keeps pushing, is that the argument is a proxy war about the division of moral labour and about what alienation from one's own agency is. I also introduced something I called the outsourcing test, to see if it would break the staleness: suppose the impersonal demand could be discharged institutionally — you pay the levy, fund the effective charity, vote for the regulator — and then act from your own projects with a clear conscience about the rest. My consequentialist colleague says the objection loses its target there; the Williamsian says it doesn't, because the objection was never about what you produce, it's about what you deliberate from and what you can say about yourself.

So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict and not a literature survey — I've read the literature, I can recite it. Is this a substantive disagreement about whether there are agent-relative reasons, a verbal one about the word integrity, a clash over which cases count as data (does the outsourcing case count as a genuine instance of the objection, or as a changed subject), or people answering different questions under one name — one about right action, one about the standpoint of deliberation, one about the metaphysics of the self? Say which, with reasons drawn from what each side actually commits itself to, and tell me honestly whether each strand is arguable or bottoms out in intuitions neither side can argue for. Then the part I actually care about: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here? A distinction, a formal result about how agent-relativity behaves in a decision-theoretic framework, a different kind of case, a change of question? Or should I conclude the question as posed is ill-posed and that three years is exactly what I should have expected to spend on it?
