I write historical fiction, and in three weeks I'm giving the closing keynote at a conference of historians of the early modern Atlantic world. I'm using the slot to defend a rule I've worked out in practice rather than in theory, and I want a Q&A prep sheet for it.

The position has two parts. First, the Availability Condition: in a historical novel the narrator is licensed to judge the past in the moral idiom of the present, but a character's inner life — what she notices, what she can name to herself, the shape of her conscience — may only be rendered in concepts that were actually available to someone in her situation at the time. Second, the seam must show: the two registers have to be kept visibly apart, usually as a break in voice or tense, so that judgment is never smuggled in as psychology. When a novelist collapses them, she commits what I call narrative laundering — making the past readable in our terms and flattering us with the sense that we, in that room, would have seen through it.

My example throughout is my own novel in progress, set in Charleston in 1803 and written in close third person inside the head of a slave-trading merchant's wife; the narrator uses a present-day vocabulary — racism, complicity, asset — and the character never does.

The room is about a hundred and fifty people. Roughly half are contextualist intellectual historians who hold that a text's meaning is fixed by the linguistic conventions available to its author; a substantial group are social historians of slavery, committed to presentist moral clarity, for whom any constraint on judgment smells like an aesthetic excuse for soft-pedaling; there are two archivists, a museum curator currently reworking an exhibit, and a handful of novelists who will want to say that all close third person is already doubled and my seam is an illusion.

Give me the seven or so objections I'll actually get, in the order they'll arrive, each phrased the way that subgroup would phrase it rather than the way I'd phrase it, with a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, a list of what I should concede before anyone raises it, and a verdict on which single objection has to be answered for the rule to survive the reception. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — I think it's about the character register having been written now too, but tell me if the dangerous one lies elsewhere.
