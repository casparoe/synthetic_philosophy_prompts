I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and for the past few months I've been reporting a feature on the Darwinian theory of culture — the view that slogans, tunes, habits, rumors, diet tips and moral slogans form populations that vary, compete, and get differentially copied, and that what wins is whatever is good at getting itself copied rather than whatever is true, useful, or good for the people doing the copying. The thing I keep circling back to, and that I'd like the comedy to come out of, is how completely the meme's interest and the host's interest can come apart: a public-health message gets more accurate and less shared, a fear spreads because fear is what makes hands move, an idea recruits its bearer into a community that slowly ruins his life, and none of that registers as a cost from the meme's point of view. I want to run a humor piece on the back page built around exactly that gap, and I want the jokes to come from getting the mechanics right rather than from caricaturing them.

So: write me a satirical advice column, in the flat, cheery, faintly patronizing register of a newspaper agony aunt, in which the letter-writers are memes and the columnist (call her something smug like "Dr. Replicator") answers each one on the single question of how to get copied more. Around 700 words, four or five letters plus replies. Seed the mailbag with things like: an accurate and carefully hedged piece of health advice that nobody passes on; a conspiracy that is losing ground to a shorter, stupider variant of itself; a jingle whose host has grown sick of it; a meme that keeps getting garbled as it crosses between platforms and wants to know whether fidelity is worth the loss of catchiness; a timid idea wondering whether to hitch a ride inside a larger, louder package of beliefs. The advice in every reply has to be genuinely correct as advice about transmission and quietly appalling as advice about anything a human could want — selection should be visibly indifferent to truth and to the welfare of the person carrying the meme, and the columnist should treat a deteriorating host as a pure trade-off (incapacitate him too fast and you lose a vector; keep him just mobilized enough and you gain one). Work in the real features rather than invented ones: the fidelity-versus-catchiness trade-off, dependence on the local environment of platforms and algorithms, competition among near-identical variants, riding along inside a bigger memeplex, virulence that must not outrun transmission. Include one letter from a skeptical human reader raising the actual objection that ideas are not faithful enough replicators to be proper Darwinian individuals at all, and let the columnist wave it off without ever stepping out of character.

Keep it deadpan and legible — no winking at the reader, no footnotes, no explanatory framing — so that someone who has never heard of cultural evolution still laughs and still comes away having felt that on this picture human flourishing is a side effect rather than the point. Let the piece close by biting itself: a P.S. in which the column asks to be forwarded, so that the advice to spread turns out to have been serving the column all along.
