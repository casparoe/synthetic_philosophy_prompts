Our little town is doing a plaque for its 150th anniversary and the committee has completely deadlocked, and there's one argument that keeps winning the room and I can't tell if it's actually good or just sounds good. Hoping you can take it apart for me.

Background: the town's founding story is that during the big flood the mill owner opened the sluice gates and deliberately flooded his own best field to take the water off the houses downstream, and every spring since then there's been a walk out to that field. A couple of years ago somebody went through the county records and turned up a court deposition from a lawsuit a few years after the flood that suggests the mill owner was actually at the tavern in the next town that night and the gates were opened by two of his hired hands, one of whom drowned. So: story vs. record, and the plaque has to say something.

The argument that keeps winning comes from one committee member. She says a town's memory is like a person's memory. You don't go around telling people their memories are wrong because some piece of paper says something else, and you especially don't do that to people who've been through something hard. The memory does something for us that the document can't do, and making us trade the one for the other is like telling someone who survived a bad thing that they're misremembering it. She uses it to say the plaque should just keep the story the way everybody in town tells it.

So: how far does that analogy actually hold? I'd love the correspondence table first — who or what is the remembering subject here, what plays the role of the thing the person actually lived through, what plays the role of the therapist, or the recovered memory, or the document that contradicts it. I suspect some of those slots don't have anything in them and that matters.

Then where it breaks, and — this is the part I care about — which breaks actually matter for what she's using it for. Some things I noticed on my own but can't sort: nobody alive was there, so it's not clear the town has the kind of first-person authority a person has about their own past; a town isn't really one subject doing the remembering, it's a bunch of people telling each other a story for 150 years; the version we have was pushed hard by the mill owner's family, who still own a good chunk of the land around that field and sell the anniversary merch; and if a person's memory is wrong the main person it hurts is them, whereas here the question is who gets to be the hero and who gets left out of the story entirely.

Also I want to know whether the analogy cuts the other way, because the counter-argument someone else made is that the archive is just the paperwork the people with power kept, and the reason the town's version is the only record of the hired hand is that nobody wrote anything down for people like him. If the analogy runs both directions, what does that do to her conclusion?

And last thing: somebody proposed a compromise where the plaque has two short columns, the story and what the record says, side by side, and I can't tell whether the memory analogy supports that or quietly kills it. My gut is that in the therapy case you can't really tell someone their memory is fine, it's just one of two accounts, and have that be a comfort. But I don't trust my gut here since I clearly have a side.

I don't need sources or reading recommendations, I just want to know which parts of the comparison are actually carrying the weight and which are decoration.
