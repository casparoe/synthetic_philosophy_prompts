I got into a long argument with my friend last night — he does RL theory, I'm the philosophy side of a reading group we both help with — and I need to settle it by building something. His position: embedded agency is basically just self-reference, and it more or less dissolves the moment you let an agent point at its own policy instead of copying it. Mine: that's the easy part. The hard part is that the thing doing the modelling is physically smaller than the system it's inside of, so it can't carry a complete model of it, and it also can't fix that by simulating itself simulating itself simulating itself. He conceded the second half and gave it a name — he calls it "last-look": whatever rule you write down, the environment already contains it, because your rule is a physical thing in the world, so the environment always gets the final look at it. Then he said you couldn't build an exercise around last-look because it isn't a decision problem. So now I want to build one.

The setting: week four of a six-week reading group on agency, 14 people, half CS grad students and half philosphy MAs, one 90-minute session. Seminar room, whiteboard, terrible wifi, so nothing that needs laptops. Two weeks ago they did Newcomb and a bit of causal vs evidential decision theory, so I don't want something that just re-runs that — and I really don't want it to collapse into a Keynesian beauty contest where everyone just guesses two-thirds of the average and someone says "levels of reasoning" and we go home. I want the payoff function to literally read their own submission back to them: the thing they write on their card has to be part of the world they're predicting, not just a guess about it.

What they should walk out having felt, before anyone names anything: (1) the target moves because they are inside the system that produces it; (2) any rule they can write on a card is already legible to the rest of the room, and the room gets the last look; (3) the obvious fix — write a self-referential rule, something like "I do whatever I'm about to do" — doesn't escape, it just hands the problem to whoever has to resolve it. Nobody says "embedded agency" until after the last round.

So: give me the runnable thing. Materials (I have index cards, a box, dice, a whiteboard), rules, how to split 14 people up or whether to keep them as individuals, whether anyone gets a secret role, a payoff scheme in points toward one coffee voucher with no real money, a timeline in minutes including what goes on the whiteboard and when, and at least one round where each person's own card gets fed back into the scoring twice in different roles. Then the debrief: which moment in the game is supposed to produce which of the three reactions, what I say when it does, and what I say when it doesn't.

Two things I know will happen and want handled rather than suppressed: the CS half will immediately start hunting for a fixed point or writing a randomizing rule, and the philosophy half will say the setup is incoherent or question-begging. Both of those should be usable moves in the activity, not derailments. And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it that I haven't thought of.

Finally, be honest about what the game can't do. My guess is that it can show the room out-guessing a single agent in one shot, but can't show the agent being *changed* by the environment over time, which I think is the part where embedded agency actually bites — the agent's own update procedure is part of what's getting optimized, and no single-round game reproduces that. If I'm wrong about which bit is unreproducible, say so and tell me what I'll have to just state out loud at the end.
