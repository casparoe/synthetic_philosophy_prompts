Hey — I TA a third-year epistemology seminar (16 students, one 75-minute slot, and they've already got the basics: evidential probability, peer disagreement, the first-order/higher-order distinction) and I need a runnable in-class game for next Thursday on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. Me and two of the other TAs got into it over drinks and I came away thinking the usual cases cheat: they hand you someone who has just been told their reasoning is impaired right now, and then act surprised that the person is stuck. What I actually want students to feel is that the bind is a leftover — it's what remains when three escape routes are shut, and the classic setups shut all three silently, so the akratic conclusion looks inevitable when it's really an artifact of the framing. The routes I have in mind: fold — treat the higher-order evidence as just more evidence about the world and calibrate your first-order credence toward the base rate; bracket — keep the first-order belief but quarantine it, don't act on it; re-open — spend resources going to get better evidence instead of trying to repair the bad evidence you already have. My half-formed thesis is that re-opening is the one nobody talks about, and that whether akrasia is real for a given case is basically a question of how expensive re-opening is; the cases that feel inescapable are exactly the ones where re-opening is impossible or priced out of reach. So: design the game so each route has an explicit price in points, and so participants hit at least one round where folding costs them accuracy, one where bracketing costs them action, and one where re-opening is either cheap (and the bind dissolves) or too expensive (and the bind is genuine). Not sure how you do all that with one mechanism — maybe a per-round "reinvestigation fee" the facilitator sets and only reveals at the moment it matters?

Materials should be cheap (index cards, dice, envelopes, maybe a fourth-year I can draft as a confederate to hand things out), and the task itself should be something like quantity estimation or comparing city populations, where a silent impairment can actually be induced — half the room gets an anchor nudge or an extra working-memory load without knowing the others didn't. Score with a strictly proper scoring rule on both the first-order answer and the meta-answer about whether to trust it, and keep the two currencies separate with no exchange rate, so that on some rounds doing best overall means giving an answer you simultaneously say you shouldn't trust. Several of them are annoyingly competitive about points, so the scheme shouldn't just reward stubbornness. Give me: the setup, the rules as numbered steps, the payoff scheme as a small table, a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto (a) the three routes, (b) why calibration and akrasia come apart, and (c) the question whether the akratic subject is being irrational or is just unlucky. Add a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm guessing most people will just fold everything, or they'll figure out they're supposed to feel torn and perform being torn — and how I handle it, and be honest about which part of the philosophical problem the game cannot stage and I'll have to say out loud myself. No lecture up front and don't name any of this until the debrief. One page max, hard cap 800 words; I'd rather have something tight and actually runnable than something complete.
