I chair the acquisitions committee of a museum of contemporary art with a small collecting budget, a remit that covers a region none of us can travel to more than once a year, and a board that has veto power over every purchase and has never seen any of the works either. We meet for ninety minutes a month, review four to six proposals, and write a two-page recommendation on each. Roughly half of what we consider cannot be experienced first-hand: works still in the artist's studio, works in private collections on another continent, installations whose only documentation is a set of installation photographs and a walkthrough shot taken on a phone, performances captured in a single-camera recording.

The committee splits along a fault line that I gather the philosophy of art has written about at length, though I have no library access and no way to read any of it. Two of us think that a judgment about a work's aesthetic value essentially depends on being with the work, so that buying on someone else's word is buying blind; one of them calls this the acquaintance principle. Two of us think aesthetic testimony works like testimony anywhere else and that the only real questions are about the quality and independence of the source. I do not need the dispute settled and I do not need to be told what the literature says. I need a procedure my committee can run on a file, in about fifteen minutes, with nothing in front of us but the file and our own records.

Please give me that procedure in the form of ordered questions, where for each question you state what would count as a sufficient answer, where the procedure returns a clear no, and where it stops and hands the decision to a person or to the board because the remaining question is about what we value or how much risk we want rather than about what is true. It has to sort the things a file says into distinct kinds and treat them differently: checkable facts about the work (medium, dimensions, condition, provenance, edition), reports of what it is like to spend time with the work, verdicts about its quality or importance, and forecasts about how publics, critics or funders will receive it. It has to cope with sources that look independent but are not (three curators from one circle, one gallery's whole stable), with a testifier who has a stake in our buying (our own curator, who put the artist in a show last year), and with works whose value seems to reside in something documentation flattens — scale, material presence, a room that the camera does not convey. It has to tell us what to do when a step cannot be answered at all because we cannot ask a follow-up: the gallery will not release a condition report, the artist refuses studio visits. And it has to mark, honestly, which of its steps are genuine checks and which are judgment calls wearing a checklist as a costume, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do.

Because we decide repeatedly, please also build in a ledger: what we should record when a work finally arrives and we see it ourselves, and how a source's past verdicts should then change the weight we give their next one. I suspect perfect agreement in the past is being treated by all of us as stronger evidence than it is.

Then run the procedure on three files from last month, and give me the one-page version of it separately.

One: a twenty-two minute single-channel video work that we can watch in full on a screener, whose value does not seem to depend on the room it plays in, recommended by a correspondent we have bought from twice.

Two: a room-sized installation by an artist whose last three works we own, where scale and material presence are said to be the whole point, and where the documentation is installation photographs and one handheld walkthrough.

Three: a small painting, modestly priced, from a dealer whose last twelve recommendations have matched our own verdicts almost exactly once the works arrived; the file contains a confident verdict and a price and almost nothing about what being with the painting is like. The committee was ready to buy this one, and if your procedure says otherwise I would like to know precisely which step stops it and why that step is not just paperwork.
