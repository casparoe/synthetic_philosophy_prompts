I hold a position I would like stress-tested rather than defended, and I would like you to argue directly rather than survey a literature.

The position: an agent's doxastic state is better represented by a convex set of probability functions than by a single one. The width of the set is supposed to track something real, namely the difference between risk, where the relevant chances are pinned down well enough that a precise prior is warranted, and uncertainty proper, where the model class itself is in question and no amount of further evidence of the kind you are currently able to gather would settle which member of the set is correct. Update is pointwise conditioning plus extension to the rest of the algebra; choice is governed by the set rather than by a selected member of it, with admissibility relative to the whole set as the primary rule and a maximin-style rule as the fallback when the admissible options are many.

My interest in this is not antiquarian. I use the framework to reason about safety arguments for advanced AI systems, where the uncertainty I care about is rarely uncertainty about outcomes given a fixed model of the system, and is much more often uncertainty about whether the model of the system is the right one at all. Precise Bayesianism seems to me to force a fake exactness onto exactly the quantities I am least entitled to be exact about.

So: what would count against this? I want a map of the view's exposure, and I want it ranked from what would be fatal through what is serious to what is merely awkward.

Please cover at least the following.

The phenomenon in which conditioning a set of priors on each element of a partition of ambiguous evidence widens the interval for a hypothesis, so that you are guaranteed to become less precise no matter which outcome you observe. Say whether this is a genuine cost, a consequence a defender should embrace, or a sign that the update rule is the wrong part of the package to keep.

The charge that the set is decorative and that all the real work is done by the choice rule layered on top of it: a set plus admissibility, a set plus maximin over that set, a set plus a weighted optimism-pessimism index, and a single precise prior plus a sensitivity check may all be extensionally very close, in which case I have bought representational cosmetics at the price of a harder decision theory. Say what would distinguish these, behaviorally or otherwise.

Dynamic choice. A set-valued state plus a permissive choice rule does not determine a plan, and the familiar options are planning ahead against one's future selves, choosing resolutely, or paying the price of dynamic inconsistency. Which of these commitments is load-bearing for my version, and which is a free parameter I have not noticed I was choosing?

Tractability. Extension over a credal set is expensive, and the sets I actually want in the AI case are over jointly structured propositions, not isolated events. Does a normative theory that only unbounded agents can implement undermine its own claim to normativity, or is that an engineering complaint dressed as a philosophical one?

Then the two things I care most about.

First, the vacuity worry. For almost any pattern of choices I might observe, it looks like some credal set together with some choice rule rationalizes it. If that is right, the framework risks being behaviorally unfalsifiable. Is it? And if it is, is that a defect in a normative theory of this kind, or only in a predictive one? I want you to be explicit about how one tells the difference between unfalsifiability that reveals vacuity and unfalsifiability that merely reflects the fact that the view constrains representation rather than behavior. If the view is well insulated, say so, and say whether the insulation is a problem here.

Second, a case I want worked through rather than gestured at. A lab's safety case rests on a forecaster that outputs a calibrated probability of catastrophic misalignment of 0.02. A reviewer assigns between 10 and 30 percent to the proposition that the forecaster's model class is misspecified in a way that makes the 0.02 uninformative rather than merely noisy. Conditional on that misspecification, the reviewer has almost no grip: her range for the same proposition is, say, 0.005 to 0.9. Taking convex mixtures of the precise forecast with that range across the allowed weights gives a set for the proposition of roughly 0.015 to 0.28, and a maximin-style rule would then have me act as though the probability were about 0.28, which is fourteen times the number the safety case was built on.

Tell me whether that number is a deliverance of the framework or an artifact of the conventions I fed it, where the interval from 0.005 to 0.9 was supposed to come from and whether the framework has anything to say about it or merely relocates the arbitrariness one level up, what stops a regress in which I am imprecise about my own imprecision, and whether a precise Bayesian who reports 0.02 alongside an explicit robustness check is in any worse an epistemic position than I am. If the honest answer is that the framework's advantage shows up only once the stakes make caution independently appropriate, say that plainly, because it would matter a great deal to me: it would mean the framework is not doing the argumentative work I have been using it to do.

Finally, tell me which single consideration you would expect to move a sophisticated defender of the view, as opposed to one its critics favour, and what kind of result, formal or otherwise, would settle the matter if it went the other way. If you think the view is best reformulated rather than abandoned, say what the reformulation is and what it gives up.
