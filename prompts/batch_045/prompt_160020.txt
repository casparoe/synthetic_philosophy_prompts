So — six of us in the reading group have been going at this for about eight months, and we've finally committed to a half-day workshop in March whose only deliverable is supposed to be one document everybody can put their name to. Help me draft it.

The fight is about obfuscated arguments and how much of a problem they are for the idea that we could get safe behavior out of very capable systems by having them argue in front of a bounded judge. Call the two sides:

- Camp A, "it scales": obfuscation is real but it's mostly a symptom of weak models and badly specified tasks; honest debaters have a structural advantage because true claims admit short, checkable, decisive support; and the point of cross-examination is that exposing an error is cheaper than manufacturing a flawless fake. Debate degrades gracefully, not catastrophically.
- Camp B, "there's a wall": for a judge with bounded time there are questions on which a motivated deceiver can produce arguments that are locally impeccable at every step and only globally wrong; raising doubt is cheaper than establishing truth, so the deceptive side wins on points; and this gets worse with debater capability, not better, because the capability asymmetry grows faster than the judge's ability to audit.

Neither camp is a bloc, and I want that handled rather than papered over. Inside camp A, some think obfuscation is basically incompetence in disguise and will more or less dissolve with better models, others think it's a genuine phenomenon that debate is nonetheless robust to. Inside camp B, some think the wall is already here at current capability levels, others place it somewhere past human level but before "can do its own alignment research." If a claim can't be signed by both factions within a camp, say which faction defects.

Two things we've been speccing that I want used as the concrete hooks, since the abstract argument just recycles itself:

1. The "ledger protocol": before any prose, each debater posts a numbered list of claims; each claim may cite earlier claims by number; the ledger freezes once posted; the judge has time budget T and audits by sampling indices, checking local validity given the cited claims and whether cited claims say what's attributed to them; a debater may challenge a specific index and the owner has to answer inside the frozen ledger. Compare it against plain prose debate.
2. A synthetic family we call the "hidden invariant" tasks: long sequences of operations on a multiset where the correct answer turns on a global invariant (a parity, a conserved quantity) that takes one line to state but that you can only confirm by re-deriving over the whole sequence. Knobs: n for length, and a knob for how much of the sequence is actually relevant. We know ground truth by construction; the judge doesn't.

Also: one member keeps invoking the PCP theorem — his version is that any proof can be rewritten so a randomized checker reads only a constant number of bits and still catches false claims with high probability, so we can just demand that debaters' arguments come in locally checkable form and obfuscation is solved. My worry is that the questions we actually care about aren't formalized statements with a known verifier, and that the formalization step is exactly where obfuscation moves to. If his use of it is muddled, say so in the document instead of quietly fixing it.

What I want: a numbered agreed-claims section first — the things both sides genuinely sign, including about what obfuscation is (I think there are at least four different things going by that name and the debate keeps sliding between them), what the judge can and can't do, and what would count as the proposal failing. Then a disagreements table, with each side's position phrased the way that side would accept for itself, and a column marking which disagreements are verbal. Then the cruxes, and for each crux a concrete case or measurement both sides agree bears on it — ideally using the ledger protocol or the hidden-invariant family with the knobs set to specific values — plus each side's prediction written down in advance, and a flag where a prediction is unfalsifiable as stated. Include what each side would count as evidence against itself, and whether there's anything both agree would actually move the debate.

Keep it to something that works as a workshop handout, call it 1500 words, and don't give me a reading list or citations — we've all read the obvious stuff and half of us can't be trusted to recieve author names correctly anyway.
