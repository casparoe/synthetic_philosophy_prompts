I am a PhD student in early modern philosophy, and I have been going in circles over what, exactly, Descartes denied to non-human animals. Both sides of the interpretive dispute have been stated in full for a very long time and nobody moves: the standard reading takes the beast-machine doctrine at face value — no thought, no sensation, nothing it is like to be a beaten dog — while the revisionists argue that what he actually targets is language and universal reason, that he is not committed to denying sensory awareness, and that the cruder view is largely a polemical inheritance. They point to the two tests in Part Five of the Discourse, to his concession that animals surpass us in some particular operations, to the more concessive remarks in the correspondence, and to the passages about passion-like bodily states in the Passions of the Soul.

My working hypothesis is that three separable claims are being run together: a metaphysical claim, given his principle that whatever thinks is immaterial and hence immortal, coupled with his evident unwillingness to extend immortality down to creatures such as oysters and sponges; a phenomenal claim about whether there is anything it is like for the animal; and a programmatic claim about whether the resources of his physiology — the animal spirits, the disposition of the organs — could in principle account for every animal performance without remainder. I suspect the deadlock lives in the third claim, because the completeness of a mechanical explanation is a commitment about what counts as an explanation rather than a thesis any observed behaviour could refute. I also suspect the language test is doing work it cannot do: it is a one-sided criterion, since any behaviour can be redescribed as mere mechanical response, so it can only ever return a negative verdict, and then gets cited as evidence for the very view it presupposes. On top of that there may be a verbal component: if for Descartes thought covers sensory awareness, then a thesis that sounds to us like animals are not conscious may have sounded to him like animals do not reason.

Is that diagnosis right, or is there a substantive disagreement underneath it — a real difference about how much weight behaviour can bear in an argument to another mind, or a hidden empirical question about whether behavioural complexity needs an immaterial cause? Give me a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the readings, and do not send me to secondary literature I would have to look up. Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) your verdict on which kind of disagreement this is; (2) which of my three claims actually carries the weight, or what I have left out; (3) the strongest consideration on each side and why it fails to move the other; (4) what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress here; (5) an honest answer about whether progress should be expected at all.
