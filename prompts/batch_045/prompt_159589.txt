I'm applying for a nine-month doctoral fellowship and need a two-page proposal, and I want it built around a distinction I keep circling back to. The realism fight about whether neural nets really have representations (features, directions in activation space, circuits) looks stuck to me because both sides keep testing one notion of representation when there are at least three in play: a causal-role notion (this direction mediates the behaviour), a content notion (the direction has correctness conditions, it can misrepresent), and a portability notion (the same state type turns up again in a different model trained on similar data). My half-formed suspicion is that the evidence everyone cites — probes, steering, sparse dictionaries — only ever establishes the causal-role notion, and that the content notion needs a different sort of test that nobody actually runs. The test I want to propose is an off-manifold one: something with content should keep its causal role for inputs the network has never seen and arguably could never see, including activations pushed far outside the range the training distribution ever produced, and combinations of features that never co-occur. If a 'dog head' direction stops doing its thing out there, maybe it was never representing dog-headedness, just tracking a regularity of the training set. Turn that into a proposal: the question stated so it has a determinate answer, why the standard evidence doesn't settle it, what I'd actually do month by month (including a cheap pilot that could kill the project in the first six weeks), what the possible results are, and what would count as the project having failed rather than just coming out negative. One committee member will definitely ask whether this is only the old dispute about whether a thermometer represents temperature, so tell me how to answer that without conceding the whole thing. And someone else will ask why the portability notion is even worth separating out — include a short answer. No citations, I'll fill those in myself.
