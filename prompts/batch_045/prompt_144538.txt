I am a novelist by profession, and my current manuscript has obliged me to make explicit a set of commitments about Confucian role ethics that I have until now held loosely. The book is set in a near-future city that has created a new public role, the Care Partner: a person assigned by lottery to provide filial-like companionship and practical care to an isolated elder, with no biological or marital tie and no fixed term of duty. The protagonist, Lian, is made Care Partner to an irritable retired engineer whom she comes to suspect of having abused his own children decades earlier. Late in the book she discovers that he has stolen a small sum from a neighbour, and he asks her to say nothing. She refuses and reports him. I want Lian's choice to be upright, but I am not sure my other commitments let me say that.

Here, as carefully as I can, is the position set my novel is supposed to be exploring. I hold:

1. Moral requirements are fundamentally requirements of roles. To determine what I ought to do, the salient question is not which impartial principle applies but what roles I occupy and what those roles demand: parent, child, teacher, neighbour, and in my fictional setting, newly created roles such as the Care Partner.

2. The normative force of a role does not depend on my having consented to it or on my identifying with it. I can be bound by a role I never chose and may initially resent; being assigned to it is enough to generate duties.

3. Confucian cultivation nevertheless aims at making the role one's own. Through ritual, practice, and time, the exemplary person comes to take joy in the demands of the role, so that duty and inclination converge.

4. A role can be criticized, but only from within. The resources for reforming a role—for example, reinterpreting filial piety so that it forbids blind obedience—are internal to the tradition and to the point of the role, not external moral considerations such as autonomy, utility, or rights.

5. In the well-known early Confucian anecdote about a son who covers for his father after the father has stolen a sheep, the son does the upright thing. This shows that the partiality inherent in a core familial role can legitimately override a general legal or civic duty to report a crime.

6. Nepotism—using a role to unfairly advantage a role-mate at the expense of outsiders, or at the expense of the point of the role—is a genuine moral failing, not merely a breach of etiquette.

7. A newly created role can come to have real normative force even though it appears nowhere in the classical texts, provided it is embedded in a web of rituals, reciprocal expectations, and recognized responsibilities, as the Care Partner role is in my novel.

8. A person who is alienated from a role—someone who performs its duties mechanically and feels no affection—still has the role's duties, but has not yet attained the virtue proper to it.

9. In my manuscript, Lian the Care Partner rightly refuses to conceal the old man's theft and reports him. I take this to be the upright act in the context of a new, state-assigned, companionate role, even though I also hold commitment 5 about the son and the sheep.

I do not know which of these, if any, is the weak point, and I have no prior attachment to keeping any particular one. Please audit the set. Identify which commitments really pull against one another and which pairs only appear to. Order the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything as equally problematic. For each genuine tension, say what would have to be revised, qualified, or abandoned to restore consistency, and what that revision would cost the overall view. Tell me which single revision, if I could only make one, would clean up the most.

Please do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I am asking only for the audit itself. In your reply, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers or scholars who work on early Confucian ethics and role ethics, and do not present a contested reading—for example, a particular interpretation of the sheep anecdote, or a disputed claim about whether role ethics is best classified as a form of virtue ethics—as if it were the consensus. Finally, be explicit about your own confidence and be self-critical: mark which tensions you think follow straightforwardly from the commitments as stated, which depend on a contested interpretation or on how charitably you read them, and which might dissolve under a more sympathetic reconstruction. I need to know not just where you think the problems are, but how sure you are that they are really problems.
