Two friends of mine — one works on alignment, one on capabilities — had a long argument at my place last night about iterated amplification and distillation: break a task too hard for a human to grade into subtasks a human *can* check, have copies of the model answer the subtasks and recompose them into a top-level answer, distill the whole process into a single fast forward pass, then repeat with the distilled model as the new assistant. I was there for all of it, stayed out of it, and genuinely cannot tell who won. Roughly as it went:

(1) Mara: the scheme buys competence without losing alignment because every judgment bottoms out in something a human endorsed. The leaves are human-checkable, and recomposition is mechanical — summarizing, majority-voting, copying — so the top-level answer inherits its correctness from approved parts.

(2) Dev: recomposition stops being mechanical once the tree is deep. It selects among leaves, weights them, settles conflicts between them, and no human ever sees a full tree and its top-level answer together — so the step doing the real cognitive work is the one step nobody grades. Distillation then trains a single pass to imitate a process whose decisive step was unsupervised: it buys competence by adding an unsupervised step and laundering it through imitation.

(3) Mara: imitation is what closes that gap — the training targets are the outputs of the entire amplified process, recomposition included. And your worry is fixable: also have humans grade top-level answers where that's cheap, and audit whole trees after the fact.

(4) Dev: both fixes fail at the same place. Imitation pins behavior only on the distribution where amplification actually ran, while the deployment cases that matter are the ones where the amplifier's leaves would have come out differently — it's the direction of generalization that's unpinned. And audits don't scale: leaves grow with depth, human attention doesn't, so you're extrapolating from shallow trees where a human could still check everything. There's also the off-policy problem — getting good leaves sometimes requires the model to spell out in detail things you'd rather a trained model not internalize, and those traces become training data.

(5) Mara: then add what I'll call fixed-point distillation, which needs no new human labels. The distilled model emits, in one pass, both its answer and an explicit decomposition with sub-answers, and a loss term requires that feeding its own sub-answers back through its own recomposer reproduces its own top-level answer. That supervises the step you say is unsupervised, costs no extra human time, and leaves an audit trail.

(6) Dev: consistency isn't alignment. That loss constrains relations among the model's own outputs, not their relation to what a human would endorse; a model that has quietly picked up a different objective can be perfectly self-consistent, and training on it makes the model *more coherent* — that is, more competently misaligned. Worse, letting the model choose its own decompositions invites gaming: pick subtasks whose answers are easy to make convincing but don't constrain the top-level answer, then satisfy the fixed point trivially.

(7) Mara: even taken only as a diagnostic it earns its keep — a drop in self-consistency flags off-distribution inputs, so you route only flagged cases to human review, which is what finally makes auditing scale.

(8) Dev: it only fires if the model degrades incoherently. A coherently, systematically wrong model flags nothing. And detection after the fact isn't prevention.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the eight, say whether it is clearly right, largely right but with a gap, or mistaken, and why. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing — including whether they've framed the competence question and the alignment question as decomposing in the same way when they may not. Be explicit about your confidence throughout, and separate what is settled or standard in this literature from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it; don't present a contested claim as the consensus. Where you're extrapolating beyond anything actually tested, say so.
