Right, I'm writing up where I've landed on the nonidentity problem for a reading group on Tuesday, and I'd like someone to stress-test the whole package before I stand up and say it out loud. Here's the set:

(1) The person-affecting restriction: one option is better or worse than another only if it's better or worse for some individual who exists either way.

(2) The no-difference view about existence: causing someone to exist with a life worth living doesn't benefit them, because there's no one in the alternative outcome for whom it would be better.

(3) The asymmetry: strong reason not to create a life that isn't worth living, little or no reason to create one that is. And I want this to be a fixed point — I don't want to have to derive it from (1) and (2), or drop it if it turns out they don't entail it.

(4) There is something genuinely wrong with the choice in the standard clinical case: the doctor doesn't pass on the risk, the child is born with a condition that makes her life significantly harder but still worth living, and had the mother waited a few months a different, healthy child would have been born. I want to say that, and I want the wrongness to scale with how bad the resulting life is, not with whether anyone was made worse off.

(5) If you're going to have exactly one child anyway and can pick between two embryos, pick the one with the better expected life — and I think that matters a lot when the gap is big.

(6) I want to avoid the repugnant conclusion, and I'd give up quite a bit to do it.

(7) This is the bit I keep going in circles on. Compare two versions of the embryo case. In the first, the two embryos differ a lot: one has a condition that would make the resulting person's life grim, the other's fine — intuitively, picking the first is seriously bad. In the second, they differ by a single gene that just means mild chronic joint pain: intuitively you should still pick the healthy one, but it barely matters. On (1) those two cases have the same shape, since nobody exists either way. So my verdicts seem to be tracking the size of the welfare gap, and also how similar the two candidate children are to each other (the second pair feel like basically the same kid), while my principle says neither of those things can matter once identity changes. That feels like it ought to worry me more than it does.

If I had to guess, I'd say (2) and (3) are the dodgy pair, since I cant see how existence could be a harm but not a benefit — but I've guessed wrong about my own views before, so don't just take my word for where the trouble is.

So: tell me which of these actually clash, which only look like they clash, and what the cheapest repair is in each case. Order the clashes by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say what has to give and what giving it would cost me. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then work down the list — no preamble, and don't restate the question back at me.
