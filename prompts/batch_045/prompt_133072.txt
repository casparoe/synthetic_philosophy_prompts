I sit on the allocation committee of a small philanthropic fund. We score causes with an ITN-style framework (importance, tractability, neglectedness), and I am rewriting our internal memo on how the three factors should be defined. Before I circulate it I would like to know whether my own position hangs together. Roughly, I hold:

1. What we should be ranking is expected value per dollar at the margin — the value of the next dollar, or the next marginal researcher-year — rather than the size of the problem or the average value of the dollars already spent.
2. ITN is best read as a decomposition of that marginal quantity rather than as a rival criterion: importance gives value per unit of progress, tractability gives progress per dollar at the margin, and neglectedness tells us how far down the diminishing-returns curve a cause already sits.
3. So understood, neglectedness should not be measured by headcount or by dollars already committed. Because other funders' money is substitutable for ours, I want to replace it with a counterfactual-credit term: how much of the good we would be buying would have happened anyway had we not funded it.
4. We should not pick a single best cause and concentrate everything there. The right procedure is to spread the budget until the expected marginal value of a dollar is equalized across the causes we fund, while holding something back for opportunities we cannot yet see.
5. I am not a fanatical expected-value maximizer. I want a genuine discount on very small probabilities of very large payoffs, not merely a gesture toward wide error bars.
6. But I also want a substantial premium on value of information: a cause can deserve funding even when its direct marginal value is low, if a few years of spending there could change which cause everyone else ought to be funding.

Where are the cracks? My suspicion is that some of these are trivially compatible and some are not: (1) and (2) may just be the same claim stated twice, (5) and (6) seem to pull against each other, and (4) looks like it needs an empirical premise about diminishing returns that I have not stated. A colleague also says (3) makes (4) ill-defined, since what would have happened anyway depends on what everyone else — including us — ends up doing; I do not see why that follows. Please rank the tensions by how much damage they actually do rather than flagging all of them as equally bad, say for each which commitment would have to be revised or made more precise and what that revision costs me, and tell me whether a single revision would clean up more than one of them. I would rather not give up (3), since it is the whole point of the memo.
