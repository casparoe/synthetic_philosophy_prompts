I serve as a volunteer trustee of a small grant-making charity — in my working life I ran a printing business, and I have no training in economics, philosophy or anything resembling either — but over the past year I have been reading, entirely on my own, about the ways in which perfectly sensible people end up copying one another's choices. I have become convinced that our grants committee has just done exactly that, and I now have to put something in writing before Thursday.

The particulars are these. We are a committee of seven. Our chair opens each discussion by giving her own view of the application, the rest of us then speak in turn, and the vote is a show of hands taken in that same order. At our last meeting we considered a two-year request for £42,000 — roughly a fifth of everything we will give away this year — from a community kitchen project. Our chair was warm about it from the outset, and the two members with the most relevant experience, a retired accountant and a former council officer, spoke in favour early on. By the fourth speaker the tone had become uniform, and the last two trustees contributed nothing beyond agreeing with everything already said.

I had read the application pack properly. The budget assumes around thirty volunteers a week drawn from a pool that, as far as I can tell, another local organisation already depends on; the applicant's accounts show restricted reserves that cannot legally be spent on running costs; and their own monitoring data is thin. I said some of this, mildly, and it went nowhere. Afterwards one trustee emailed me privately to say he is uneasy too but did not want to be the person holding things up. I will not name him to the others, and I would ask you not to build anything into the memo that would expose him.

The chair wants to approve on Thursday, because the applicant has a matched-funding deadline at the end of the month that our grant would unlock.

My own thoughts, such as they are, run like this. It seems to me that there are two quite different ways of going along with the room: deferring because you take the earlier speakers to have checked something you have not checked, which looks like a sensible use of other people's information; and deferring because of what it costs you to be the one who objects, which produces no information at all. From the outside the two look identical, which is why I cannot tell — and I suspect we cannot tell — how much of our support is real. I also worry that the obvious fix, a secret ballot, cures only the second kind and makes silence ambiguous in a new way. I have been toying with two alternatives: having the chair speak last, and requiring each trustee to send round, before the meeting, one line for and one line against each application.

Please draft the short memo I can circulate to the other trustees on Wednesday. It should recommend a specific course of action — approve, decline, defer, or approve on stated conditions — with a time frame that respects the matched-funding deadline, and it should say how I should handle my own dissent if the committee does not move. It must also deal honestly with the strongest thing that can be said on the other side, which as I see it is this: that the agreement of seven experienced people, one of whom reads accounts for a living, is itself evidence, that a committee which discounts its own consensus will end up paralysed, and that delaying costs the applicant money and goodwill we cannot refund.

Two further requests. Nothing in the memo should cite authors, papers or studies: I have no way of checking them, and I would only be repeating your word for it to people who know no more than I do. And please write it in plain British English, in language that a retired accountant and a former council officer will actually read to the end; if a technical term earns its place, explain it in a sentence.
