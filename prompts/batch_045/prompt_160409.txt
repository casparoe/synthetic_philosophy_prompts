Me and a few other engineers at work got into a semi-drunk argument last week about all the longtermist stuff that keeps floating around our Slack, and I stupidly agreed to write something up for our little giving group, so now I actually have to think it through. Context: about ten of us, pooling roughly one to two million a year for the next three years, and our collective skillset is basically "can ship production code, can read a paper if it isn't too dense, no philosophy background whatsoever." We're trying to decide how much of that goes into funding conceptual work on the big long-run-future questions versus just funding something concrete and technical right now — and honestly we cant tell which open questions are load-bearing and which are just fun to argue about at 1am.

The thing we keep circling is what I've seen called the hinge-of-history idea: that we happen to be living in a narrow window where comparatively small actions have an outsized and very long-lasting effect on how the whole future goes, the usual story being that we're near the point where humanity gets AI powerful enough, or space settlement, or some durable lock-in of values, that the trajectory gets set once and then persists for an absurdly long time. I don't want a survey of the literature (no citations, no reading lists — we couldn't judge them anyway, and half of us are offline half the time anyway). What I want is a prioritized research agenda: given our money and our skills, which two or three open questions about wether we're actually at such a hinge — and about what follows if we are — most deserve attention, and why?

Here's what I can't sort out, including a couple I made up myself. Please tell me which are worth pushing on, which are dead ends, and which don't actually follow from the hypothesis at all:

(a) The persistence mechanism. Hinge claims seem to smuggle in some mechanism by which a choice made now stays stuck: values baked into AI systems that then shape everything downstream, constitutions or institutions that turn out to be durable, plain technological path dependence, or the initial conditions of space settlement. I've started calling it the lock-in half-life — how long a given change actually stays in place before the future reopens the question. Which of these mechanisms genuinely have long half-lives, and which do we just assume are durable because they feel weighty?

(b) Compression versus displacement. Maybe advanced AI doesn't settle things so much as move the leverage somewhere else — onto whoever builds it, or onto the systems themselves, later. I've been calling this the second-hinge problem: if there will be later moments with stakes at least as large, does "now is uniquely important" collapse into "every era is important," and does it then matter whether our leverage is direct or merely about shaping whatever does the later steering?

(c) Crowding and base rates. Even if the stakes are enormous, our group's counterfactual impact might be tiny because plenty of others would do roughly what we'd do anyway; and separately, a lot of eras in history have been convinced they were living at the climax of history, which feels like it ought to count as evidence against. How much does that actually deflate things, and is "someone else would have done it" even answerable without data nobody has?

(d) Belief in the hinge might be self-undermining. If "we're at the hinge" licenses rushing, discounting near-term harm, or treating ordinary evidentiary standards as optional, is that a reason to be careful about spreading the idea at all? Is there a version of the hypothesis that survives but doesn't have that effect?

(e) What would change our mind. Over a three-year window, what concrete observations would move the hinge idea up or down for you, and are any of them the sort of thing ten engineers could help produce — tooling, datasets, forecasting infrastructure — rather than only read about?

For each question you keep, give me: why it's upstream of the others (what else depends on its answer), how tractable it is for people like us or for people we could fund, as opposed to tenured academic philosophers, how neglected it is, and what trade-off you're making when you rank them. Also say plainly if you think one of my questions is confused or rests on a bad assumption — I'd rather hear that than a polite answer. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: the actual ranking and the "here's where I'd put the money" verdict, then the reasoning. No preamble and no restating my question back to me, and please write it for someone who knows distributed systems and not much else.
