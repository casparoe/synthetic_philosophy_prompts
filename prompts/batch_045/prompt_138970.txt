I'm putting together a graduate seminar on the epistemology of modality, and I want one session to turn on a single case I can put in front of the room, rather than on the usual survey of positions. The case has to be built to a specification, and I'd like you to build it and then show that it meets the specification.

Design a case that isolates one variable: whether the zombie hypothesis is presented for first-personal or third-personal conceiving. Everything else is to be held fixed. Same subject, same time, same complete physical description of the same organism — no science fiction: no silicon chips, no gradual replacement, no duplicate in another possible world, no neurosurgeon. Same ideal conditions on the conceiver: unlimited time, no distraction, full command of the concepts, no contradiction hidden in the description. The two presentations should be as close in wording as the shift in standpoint allows; ideally they are one paragraph each, runnable from an armchair, and usable with students who know the conceivability argument but nothing technical about the psychology of imagination.

The verdicts I want generated from the materials, not written into the description:

(i) Presented third-personally — the conceiver takes up the standpoint of someone enumerating the physical facts about that organism and supposes, of the organism so described, that there is nothing it is like for it — the scenario is ideally, positively, primarily conceivable: the conceiver can form a positive conception of it as actual, not merely fail to rule it out a priori.

(ii) Presented first-personally — the conceiver is asked to conceive, of herself, that she is now in exactly the physical state she is in fact in and that there is nothing it is like for her — the scenario is not positively conceivable. The failure must not be indexical trivia (not: "I" rigidly designates a conscious subject, so of course not), must not be a covert stipulation that physicalism is true, and must not be routed through a Kripkean a posteriori identity of the water-is-H2O sort.

Then the demonstration, in three short parts.

First, walk the attempt through for each presentation and report what actually happens in the trying — where the first-personal attempt goes, and what the conceiver ends up imagining instead.

Second, say what each side should take the case to show. The anti-physicalist needs (i) to be the probative exercise and the failure in (ii) to be an artifact of how imagination operates when it simulates phenomenal states from the inside. The physicalist wants the reverse: (i)'s apparent success an artifact of imagining the absence of evidence, and (ii) the datum. Give the strongest version of each, and tell me which is stronger and why.

Third, the diagnostics. Name the single feature of the case doing the work. Then run two removals and report what happens: (a) re-describe the first-personal scenario non-indexically but completely — hand the conceiver the full physical description of herself under a third-personal description and tell her that this is the person she is — does the failure persist? (b) Give the third-personal scenario to a conceiver who is required, as she goes, to fill in what it is like for the organism from the inside — does the success persist?

Close with two things. What would a defender of whichever side comes out worse say to stop the case from counting at all? And, if the asymmetry survives your own scrutiny, what does it predict about whether the conceivability intuition is better explained by the epistemic independence of phenomenal concepts or by a genuine modal gap? I want a case that divides the room, not a neutral-looking exercise with a verdict smuggled into its description.
