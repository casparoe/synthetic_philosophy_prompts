Hey — I'm on the ethics committee at a fertility clinic in a state where compensated surrogacy and compensated egg donation are both legal, and we've got a case for Thursday that has split us pretty much down the middle.

The patients are a couple from an EU country where surrogacy is a criminal offense and where gamete donation has to be anonymous and unpaid. She can't use her own eggs, so they're asking for donor eggs plus a gestational carrier. Two asks: (1) the surrogacy itself, which is fine under our state law but which, if it ever surfaces back home, could mean the child isn't recognized as theirs and they could face charges; (2) a fully anonymous donor, no identifying info ever, because any document linking a named donor to the child could turn up if they register the birth there. Our own policy, adopted about two years ago, is that we only recruit donors who agree to identity release when the child turns 18, so what they're asking for is really a waiver of the policy we just wrote. Their lawyer has also offered a premium if we'll open a separate recruitment track for donors who agree to permanent anonymity, which is a thing we don't currently do.

One of our members keeps making an argument I havent been able to either shake off or refute, and I want the memo to deal with it head on. He says there are two kinds of ban and they deserve different responses. Some bans are expressive, on his telling: the community is registering a view about what gestation and parenthood are for, and the point is that its own members don't do this thing. Others are protective: the ban exists to keep some identifiable party from being used — the carrier, the donor, the child who can't trace their origins. On his reading, you dodge nothing important by crossing a border if the ban was only expressive, but you bring the whole problem with you if it was protective, because the parties who need protecting in this case are our donor and our carrier, not anybody back home. I sort of think it cuts both ways and I can't tell how much weight it should actually carry.

Separately, our director has floated a compromise I need evaluated rather than described: we'd hold the donor's identifying info and the couple's contact details in escrow here, and release the donor's identity at 18 only to the child, on the child's own request through us, never through any state document and never with the parents' consent or involvement. The pitch is that what exposes the couple is legal recognition, and what the child needs is a private channel, and those are seperable, so we can honor both at once. I want to know whether that's actually coherent, what it does to the donor's consent (she agreed to be known to a child, not to be a secret the clinic keeps from the parents, and she was paid for it), and whether a promise we can't enforce across a border is worth anything.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate Wednesday night. It should land on a concrete recommendation — approve, deny, or approve with named conditions — say what we do with the escrow idea and with the paid anonymous-donor track, give a time frame for whatever we decide including when we'd revisit it, and take the strongest version of the losing position seriously. The argument I expect thrown at me is that if we turn them down they'll just go to an unregulated broker in a country with no donor limits and no carrier protections, and then nobody — not the donor, not the carrier, not the kid — is any better off. Don't duck that one. And please don't spend the memo restating that surrogacy is legal here and criminal there; we all know that part. Under 700 words if you can, and keep their names and the country out of it.
