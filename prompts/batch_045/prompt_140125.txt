I am working my way through a popular book on behavioural economics, and last week I heard a professor give a lecture that covered the same material — the Allais paradox and the independence axiom of expected utility theory. The two accounts sounded equally confident and equally settled, but I have since seen it said that decision theorists disagree about almost every step of the argument, and I would like to know how much of what I have been told is safe to repeat.

The book's summary is the part I most want checked. I have broken it into numbered sentences so that you can take it piece by piece:

1. In the 1950s the French economist Maurice Allais put two pairs of choices to his colleagues.
2. In the first pair you choose between a guaranteed $1 million and a gamble that pays $5 million with probability 0.10, $1 million with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01.
3. In the second pair you choose between a 0.11 chance of $1 million and a 0.10 chance of $5 million.
4. Most people take the sure million in the first pair and the 0.10 chance at $5 million in the second.
5. But the two pairs differ only by a common consequence — the 0.89 chance of $1 million — and under expected utility theory a common consequence has to cancel out, so that no assignment of utilities to money can make both choices consistent; in particular, no amount of risk aversion or diminishing marginal utility of money can explain the pattern away.
6. Allais concluded that expected utility theory is false, and every experiment since has found a majority of subjects violating it, so the theory stands refuted as a description of how people actually choose.
7. What the effect really shows is that certainty itself has a special pull on us: we systematically overvalue outcomes we are sure of.
8. That matters normatively, not just descriptively, because anyone with these preferences can be money-pumped — offer them the right sequence of trades and they will pay to switch back and forth and end up worse off with certainty — which is why independence, unlike the merely mathematical axioms of the theory, is a genuine requirement of rationality.
9. The lesson the book draws is that human rationality is bounded, and that institutions should therefore nudge people toward the choices that expected utility maximization would prescribe.

The professor's lecture largely echoed this, but added two things I cannot evaluate.

First, she said the pattern survives even when nothing in the problem is certain. Her version: in one pair, $1 million with probability 0.95 (nothing otherwise) against $5 million with probability 0.10 and $1 million with probability 0.85; in another pair, $1 million with probability 0.10 against $5 million with probability 0.10. Since the two pairs again share a common 0.85 chance of $1 million, she said, this proves the effect has nothing specially to do with certainty and is really about how people treat small differences between large probabilities.

Second, she sharpened the money-pump point: because every trade in the sequence can be offered and settled before the chance mechanism is ever run, an agent who violates independence can be led into a loss that is certain rather than merely risky, and so independence is a requirement of rationality and not just a convenient simplification.

What I want is an accuracy audit. Please go through the nine sentences and tell me, for each, whether a decision theorist would accept it, whether it is a defensible simplification that a specialist would still want to qualify, or whether it is wrong — and where it is wrong, what the more careful version would say. Then I would like your view on the two additions: does the no-certainty variant really carry the weight the professor puts on it, and does the claim that the trades can all be settled in advance settle the normative question, or does it quietly assume something that independence-violating theorists reject? Finally, do the book's closing lesson and the professor's conclusion that independence is a requirement of rationality actually follow once the summary has been corrected?

I should say where I stand, so you can correct me if I am wrong: the money-pump part is what I find most persuasive, because it seems to settle the issue without relying on any psychology — but the same book argues in later chapters that ordinary people's untutored judgments are often better than the models, and I cannot see how both claims can be true. I am also unsure whether the two additions strengthen the case or whether one of them changes the subject. I have no way to look anything up at the moment, so please argue from the structure of the choice problems themselves rather than from citations or further reading.
