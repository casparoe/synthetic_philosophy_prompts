I've been reading around the why is there something rather than nothing? question for years now, mostly at blog and forum level rather than in the journal literature, and I've been sitting on a line of thought I've started calling the Anchoring Argument. I'd like to sharpen it before I post it, because I think it has a real payoff and I want to know how far it actually carries.

The argument, roughly: in the standard possible-worlds semantics every world's domain of quantification is nonempty, so there is no world at which the sentence *nothing exists* comes out true. The empty world simply isn't one of the possibilities. So it is necessary that something or other exists: necessarily, ∃x(x = x). But a bunch of merely contingent things can't be why there are things at all, so there must be something that exists necessarily — some x such that necessarily x exists. And once you have a necessarily existing concrete thing, you have most of what the cosmological argument was after: a necessary ground for everything else, with the remaining dispute only about its attributes. What I like about this is that it seems to put the pseudo-question crowd — the people who say the question dissolves because *nothing* was never a state of affairs on offer — in an awkward spot, since their own diagnosis looks like it hands the necessitarian conclusion over for free.

Two worries I'd like help with. First, someone I discussed this with says the move from *necessarily, something exists* to *something exists necessarily* is just a quantifier-scope fallacy, and that the former is already satisfied by a single contingent particle whose existence is brute, so the argument gains nothing. That strikes me as a dodge, but I can't quite say why. Second, even granting me the de re conclusion, a Platonist about numbers or sets gets to agree that something exists necessarily, which makes my conclusion look far too cheap. My inclination is to restrict the quantifier to concrete, causally-effecting things, but I want to know whether that's principled or just special pleading.

Is there a clean formulation of the Anchoring Argument that survives both objections? And should I be presenting it as an argument for a necessary ground at all, or only as an argument that the space of live positions is narrower than people think?
