I'm applying for a nine-month sole-investigator fellowship — philosophy of cognitive science, jointly hosted with a university type-design program — and I need the research proposal itself: two pages, addressed to a committee that will contain both philosophers and working type designers, so it has to be legible to each without turning into a survey of the field.

The topic is the one at the center of Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project: what makes every 'a' an 'a', and what makes a set of letterforms belong to one style. For background, the project worked in a deliberately simplified domain — letters assembled on a coarse grid out of a small repertoire of parts — with the aim of producing, from a handful of letters in some idiosyncratic hand, the rest of the alphabet in that same hand.

Here is my half-formed idea, which I want sharpened rather than flattered. I think discussions of whether a category like 'a' has an "essence" keep stalling because they run together two very different things that would both answer to that name. There is the essence you need in order to *sort* tokens: whatever makes a reader put a shaky handwritten mark, a blackletter glyph, and a neon sign in the same box — something minimal, tolerant of enormous surface variation, possibly disjunctive, and arguably nothing more than what a competent categorizer's procedure happens to stabilize. And there is the essence you need in order to *make* a token: what a designer has to grasp in order to draw an 'a' that is recognizably that letter and recognizably in a given style. Call them the sorting-essence and the making-essence. My suspicion is that they can come apart in kind and not just in detail — that the making-essence is constructive and generative where the sorting-essence is permissive and backward-looking — and that the real question is what relation has to hold between them (must a grasp of the first constrain the second? could you have one without the other?).

The second thing I want built in: I don't think "style" is a set of shared visual features. My guess is that a style is better understood as a standing *policy for resolving design conflicts* — how this hand handles the tension between stroke contrast and legibility at small sizes, between terminal treatment and rhythm, and so on. That would explain why two hands can share almost no stroke-level feature and still read as kin, and why feature-matching imitation tends to look like pastiche. If that is right, it changes what a generative account of style would have to deliver.

So I need: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; why the obvious candidate positions — invariantist views on which some structural core is necessary and sufficient, family-resemblance or exemplar-style views that dissolve the core, and teleological views that tie the letter to what it is for — each leave that question open rather than answering it; the method, quarter by quarter, naming what I would actually build and do (I'm imagining a small corpus of grid letters, an elicitation study in which designers are shown a few letters in a novel hand and asked to produce others while giving their reasons, and a transfer test in which a style is extended to a letterform outside the Latin alphabet); why that method rather than the alternatives I'll be asked about, namely reimplementing the original computational architecture, doing armchair conceptual analysis, or running a psychophysics study on categorization boundaries; what the two or three possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them; what a deliverable looks like for someone who isn't a philosopher; and an honest scope section saying what would make this too big for nine months and what I would cut.

Do not give me a literature review, a bibliography, or a reading list — I'll fill in citations myself, so refer to positions and arguments rather than to papers.

Two more things. First, be explicit about confidence: mark the places where the proposal is making a real bet and where it is on safe ground, and say which of my guesses above you take to be the shakiest. Second, and more important: if the request rests on a confused premise, say so plainly at the top rather than complying politely. If the sorting-essence/making-essence split is just a relabeling of a distinction that is already well understood, or if "essence" is doing no real work here, or if the question as I've framed it cannot be answered by one person in nine months, I want that objection in the first paragraph, not buried in a scope section. I would much rather hear why the project is misconceived than receive a well-written plan for it.
