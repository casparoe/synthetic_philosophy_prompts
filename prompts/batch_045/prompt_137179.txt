I'm a senior at a large public high school, and I'm trying to write up my own position on moral worth for our philosophy club's end-of-year zine. I'd rather find the holes in it myself than have someone point them out after it's printed. Here is where I think I've landed:

1. An action is morally right just in case it produces the best consequences available to the agent. Consequences are the whole story about rightness, and I don't see what else rightness could consist in.

2. Whether an action has moral worth — whether the agent deserves credit for it — is not a matter of consequences at all. It depends on the agent's reasons: the same act, producing the same outcome and equally right by (1), gets little or no credit if it was done from fear of getting caught or for public recognition.

3. I think people are unreliable readers of their own motives. I frequently can't tell whether I helped someone because I wanted to help or because I wanted to be the sort of person who helps, and I assume other people have the same problem from the inside.

4. Relatedly: I don't think our required service hours are worthless just because they're required and because we list them on college applications. Most volunteering at my school is done for mixed reasons, and I still want to say it has real worth, not none.

5. I admire the person who does the right thing readily and is glad to be doing it, and I think that's the better kind of character to have.

6. But I also think there's something specially creditable about doing the right thing when it is genuinely costly and you're tempted not to — the person who gives up something real seems to deserve more credit than the person for whom it was easy. My teacher says (5) is Aristotle and (6) is Kant and that I have to choose one; I don't see why, but he may be right.

7. I think it's fine to deliberately train yourself into having better motives — practising until you're the sort of person who notices when someone needs help — partly because that training will make you produce better outcomes over a lifetime. And I don't think having trained yourself that way retroactively discredits what you later do from those motives.

Two complications, because this stops being idle at school. Our administration is piloting two changes to the service programme: it would score service by measured outcomes (funds raised, verified tutoring hours weighted by the tutee's grade improvement) instead of counting hours, and it would let students log hours anonymously so that nothing appears on a transcript at all. It looks to me as though (1) pushes me toward endorsing outcome-scoring and (2) pushes me toward endorsing anonymous logging, but I don't much like either policy and I don't trust myself to have derived that correctly.

What I want is an audit, not a tour of the field: which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, and for each real tension what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me. Rank them by severity instead of listing them as equally bad, and don't assume I've hinted at which one I'd give up — I don't know. Please also say plainly if a tension I appear to be in isn't really there.

Two constraints on the answer. First, separate what is settled among people who actually work on these questions from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as though it were the received view. Second, format: exactly five numbered items, ordered from most severe to least severe, each one or two sentences, with no introductory sentence and no closing paragraph. Begin each item with a capitalised tag, SETTLED or CONTESTED, according to which it is. If one of the five turns out to be a tension that dissolves on inspection rather than a real one, place it where it belongs in the ranking and say within that item why it only looked like a tension.
