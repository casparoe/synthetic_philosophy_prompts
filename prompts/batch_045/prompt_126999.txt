I teach a graduate seminar on mental representation, and I would like to close the unit on the original/derived distinction with something other than prose. Please write a sestina — six six-line stanzas using the standard retrograde rotation (1-2-3-4-5-6, then 6-1-5-2-4-3, 3-6-4-5-2-1, 5-3-2-4-1-6, 4-5-1-3-6-2, 2-4-6-1-3-5), followed by a three-line envoi in which all six end-words return — that stages the disagreement between Searle's account of original, intrinsic intentionality and Dennett's treatment of it as derived all the way down. I would be grateful if you observed the following constraints, since the point of the exercise is that the form itself should argue.

On the end-words: choose six from the dispute's own vocabulary — aboutness, brain, bit, word, read, means, and their neighbours — and exploit the fact that a sestina has no privileged first occurrence of any of them. Every recurrence means what it means by the slot it lands in and the company it keeps, and the envoi gathers all six at once without any one of them being the original. Let at least one end-word change grammatical role across the stanzas (means as noun and as verb; read in present and past; about as preposition and as a thing states can have), so that no occurrence is the original sense of which the others are copies.

On the case: invent a single artifact for the poem to turn on. I have been imagining a device found half-buried on a shore and made of nested layers: at the bottom a sensor whose entire sensitivity to the world is two bits — it can tell four states of its environment apart and nothing else, the sort of thing Dennett calls a two-bitser; above it a register moved by the sensor's states; above that a second register moved by the first, and so on up; and at the top a human being who reads the uppermost register as being about the tide, or about whatever you prefer, and who is herself the product of a long selection history. Each layer is interpretable as being about something in virtue of the layer above reading it, and also in virtue of the layer below having been designed or selected to answer to something. The poem's question is where, if anywhere, the aboutness first enters, and whether the human at the top is a different kind of thing or merely one more layer.

On fairness: around the fourth stanza, give Searle his strongest move — that a chain of derivation has to terminate, that if it does not terminate in the intrinsic, non-relational causal powers of the brain then it terminates nowhere, and that a world of pure as-if is a world in which nothing is really about anything. Dennett's reply should also be in the poem and not merely implied by the form: that for a two-bitser there is no fact of the matter, independent of some interpreter's stance, about what its states are about; that the as-if is not a second-best but the phenomenon itself; and that the regress bottoms out not in a first, self-standing link but in interpretive practice all the way through, including the as-if purposiveness we read into natural selection, which is itself read in rather than given.

Two further requirements. First, no name-dropping inside the verse: no Searle, no Dennett, no labels like "intrinsic intentionality." A reader who knows the literature should know which lines are which from what is said about the device and about its reader; a reader who does not should still have a poem. Second, do not resolve the dispute. The final line should be the sentence on which the two views come apart, so that a committed Searlean and a committed Dennettian could each point at it and say that is precisely where they part company.

Please give me:

1. "The sestina" — the poem itself, thirty-nine lines plus a title, in the form specified above.
2. "Notes on form and argument" — 300 to 400 words of commentary on your choices: which end-word does which work and where it shifts sense; how the rotation and the envoi carry the claim about derivation; what the poem concedes to Searle and where; and one or two sentences on which line you take to be the real crux, and why.
