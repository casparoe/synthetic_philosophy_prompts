I chair the rapid-response grants committee at a mid-sized private foundation, and last night I had a long argument with a friend who runs a comparable committee at another organization. His position was that any committee that arrives at a lopsided consensus has simply herded: once one or two people have spoken, everyone else is updating on the room rather than on the world, so our unanimous votes carry almost no evidential weight and we would do as well to flip a coin after hearing the first speaker. My position was that most of our convergence is genuine, because our program officers really are looking at different parts of the world. Neither of us could say what would settle the question in any particular case, and I would rather stop arguing from anecdote and start running something.

The setting, so you know what the procedure has to survive. We meet weekly for two hours and decide forty to sixty proposals a year, each committing between $150,000 and $1.5 million, paid out in a single installment and effectively irreversible. Members are program officers with real subject-matter expertise in their own regions or issue areas and no formal training in probability or economics; two of them are openly hostile to anything that looks like a formula. Votes are cast on an internal tool that displays the running tally live, so each member sees how everyone before them voted. We already do two things that help: every member submits a sealed private vote with a one-sentence reason before the meeting, and the order in which members speak rotates each session. We also have a small informal norm I am now suspicious of — a "dissent credit," the sense that a member who votes against the grain is showing intellectual independence, which I suspect distorts things in the opposite direction.

My time budget is small. I can spend five minutes before a meeting and about ten minutes of meeting time on this, splitting the difference with my friend's claim, and I need to be able to hand the board a one-page convergence note that I sign. I can defer a decision once; deferring twice in a row requires the board, which in practice means the proposal dies.

What I want is a decision procedure that takes a finished vote — the sealed pre-votes, the order of speaking, the running tally, the reasons given — and sorts it into a small number of verdicts, with a different remedy attached to each. The distinctions I care about, and which I think the literature on informational cascades and rational herding points to, are these, stated in my own words:

One, following the crowd can be individually rational while making the group worse-informed, because the visible actions of earlier members look like more evidence than any single member's private impressions. Two, once that happens the sequence freezes: later members stop adding anything, so a larger committee does not fix the problem, and the probability of landing on the wrong option does not fall toward zero as we add people. Three, the resulting state is fragile — a small amount of genuinely new public information can overturn it — and also arbitrary, in the sense that which option we land on depends heavily on who happened to speak first. Four, and separately, apparent convergence has look-alikes that are not cascades at all: deference to a colleague who demonstrably knows more about this grantee, evidence that is merely redundant because everyone read the same diligence memo or the same news story, and plain reputational conformity, where people vote to avoid blame or to look agreeable. My friend's charge is only worth something if we can tell these apart, and I could not.

So: give me the procedure. Number the steps in the order I would run them, and for each say what counts as a sufficient answer, what counts as a non-answer, and where the step terminates in a no. I want named diagnostics I can actually compute from what we already collect — something that uses the gap between the sealed pre-votes and the final tally, something that checks whether members' stated reasons trace back to a single shared source, something that asks how much new public information it would take to flip the outcome, and something that tests whether reordering the speakers would plausibly have changed the result. Tell me which verdicts call for deferring and re-soliciting private evidence in a controlled order, which call for an outside read, which are properly settled by the pre-votes alone, and which require me to stop treating the vote as evidence at all. Mark clearly the points where no procedure can help and a person has to make a call, and be honest about which of your steps are judgment in a lab coat.

Also, if any of my four summary claims above misstates what the theory actually says, say so plainly before you go further, and build the corrected version into the procedure.

Then run it on three real shapes of decision from last year:

The first: a ballot-measure coalition grant that went 9–1 after our two most senior officers spoke warmly in favor at the top of the meeting. One of them had actually visited the coalition's field operation; everyone else had read the same three-page memo. My friend says this is his thesis in miniature.

The second: a grant I personally championed and got through 10–0, the one I am proudest of. The sealed pre-votes were 6–4 in favor. Nearly every stated reason cited the same commissioned evaluation report, and no one besides me had spoken to the grantee directly. Apply the procedure to this one as if I were not in the room, and tell me plainly what it concludes.

The third: a renewal to a long-standing grantee that came in 9–1 against, where I used my one deferral because I thought the committee was piling on. Three program officers turned out to have had separate, unprompted problems with that organization's reporting over the previous eighteen months, and they mentioned them almost in passing. I want to know whether my intervention was justified, and if it was not, what I should have done instead.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, how badly it degrades once my members know I am running it and start adjusting how they vote, and how often I should audit it against a random sample rather than against the decisions that already feel wrong to me.

Please answer in American English, and please keep it usable: no citations, no bibliography, no literature review, and nothing that requires anyone on my committee to have read the underlying theory. Where the theory has a name for something, use the name in passing if it helps me look it up later, but make the procedure self-sufficient.
