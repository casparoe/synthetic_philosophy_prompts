I'm applying for a nine-month fellowship to write up a dissertation chapter, and I need a two-page proposal for it. My half-formed idea is this: the whole guilt-vs-shame debate keeps asking which emotion tracks the moral verdict better, but I think the real disagreement is about what the emotions *do*. Guilt seems tied to a particular act and to have repair built into it — apologising, returning, fixing. Shame seems to be what keeps someone anchored to a self they can live with, and to the people whose regard makes that self possible. If that's right then a culture can't just pick guilt and abolish shame without losing whatever shame was doing — but maybe it can rebuild shame instead: restrict it to a narrow set of commitments that partly constitute who someone is, attach public exit rituals so it doesn't tip into hiding or scapegoating, and let guilt handle everything else. Call it bounded shame.

What I want from the proposal: state the question precisely (my candidate: is bounded shame actually stable, or does the internal logic of shame always push it either to escalate into total condemnation of the person or to collapse into not caring?), say why the standard defences of guilt and the standard defences of shame both leave that open, and set out what I'd actually do over nine months — a month-by-month method, including which test cases I'd work through. I was thinking public apology, addiction, and political betrayal, but tell me if those are the wrong ones. Also: what would count as the project failing, and an honest verdict on whether it's too big for nine months. No bibliography please, I'll drop the citations in myself — it's the argumentative shape I'm stuck on.
