**Q3. Harsanyi's aggregation theorem (20 marks)**

Setup: there are n individuals (n fixed), a fixed set of social outcomes, and lotteries over those outcomes. Each individual i's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each is represented by a utility function u_i that is unique only up to positive affine transformation. The social ordering over the same lotteries also satisfies the vNM axioms. Assume Pareto: if every individual strictly prefers lottery p to lottery q, then p is socially strictly preferred to q.

(a) [6 marks] State the conclusion of the aggregation theorem. What exactly is the role of the weights in the resulting sum, and how much freedom is there in choosing them, given that each u_i is only defined up to positive affine transformation?

(b) [6 marks] Harsanyi's impartial-observer argument asks you to imagine an equal chance of being anyone and to maximise expected utility from behind that veil, which appears to deliver equal weights. Explain why the equal-probability assumption cannot do this job on its own: what else has to be fixed first, and why does it matter?

(c) [8 marks] A critic says: "The real content of Harsanyi's theorem is not the Pareto condition but the assumption that the social ordering satisfies the expected-utility axioms." Explain the criticism, and assess how much damage it does to the argument for utilitarianism.

**Student's answer:**

The set-up is the same as Arrow's, except that where Arrow had a social ordering over social states derived from profiles of individual *ordinal* rankings, Harsanyi has a social ordering over *lotteries*, and each individual's utility is cardinal. Society's ordering is assumed to satisfy completeness, transitivity, continuity and independence of irrelevant alternatives — the same condition Arrow used in his impossibility theorem — so Harsanyi's move is essentially to keep Arrow's framework but add lotteries and cardinal utility, and that extra information is what turns an impossibility result into a possibility result. Given the Pareto condition, there exist non-negative weights a_i, not all zero, such that the social ordering is represented by W(p) = Σ a_i u_i(p). So any rational and Paretian social ordering must be utilitarian in form.

The weights are where the action is. a_i is a pure number measuring how much person i's welfare morally matters: a bigger weight means a given increment of i's utility counts for more in the social ranking. Crucially this is independent of how we have scaled each person's utility function — the affine uniqueness of the u_i is already built into the representation, so rescaling u_i doesn't change what the weights say. That is why the theorem is so striking: it hands you interpersonal comparisons for free rather than assuming them. To be fair, some readers complain that the axioms leave the weights completely unconstrained and so the result is nearly empty; Harsanyi's reply was that the weights get pinned down once you fix how each person's utility is measured, and anyway the important point is that the *form* is utilitarian.

Harsanyi's own answer on the weights is that they should be equal. Behind the veil you don't know who you'll be, so you assign probability 1/n to each person and maximise your own expected utility; adding the anonymity condition then forces a_i = 1/n for everyone. And since, as noted, the weights don't depend on the normalisation of the individual u_i, the argument needs no extra assumption about interpersonal comparability of units — the equal probabilities settle it. This is why the theorem is standardly read as an argument for average utilitarianism; and because it delivers average rather than total, Harsanyi's framework isn't vulnerable to the repugnant conclusion in the way that total utilitarianism is.

On (c): the criticism is right, and it's the most serious objection. The Pareto condition by itself is thin — it only says society should follow unanimous strict preference. The heavy lifting is done by requiring the social ordering itself to satisfy the vNM axioms, in particular independence, which forces society to evaluate a lottery as the expected value of a single weighted sum rather than in any other way. That rules out evaluating lotteries by, for instance, the expected value of an inequality-averse function of the realised utility vector — a symmetric concave function, or the minimum — which is exactly what an "ex post" egalitarian wants to do. Diamond's example makes the point: with one indivisible good and two people, the fair lottery and giving the good to one person for sure have the same sum of expected utilities, so Harsanyi's social ordering is indifferent between them, while an ex post egalitarian strictly prefers the lottery. So the theorem does not derive utilitarianism from Pareto plus mere rationality; it builds in the assumption that the social evaluator is an expected-utility maximiser over lotteries, which already rules out caring about how welfare is distributed once uncertainty is resolved. How much damage this does depends on whether you think a social evaluator ought to be indifferent between those two options; I think the defender of Harsanyi has to show that ex ante expected utility is the right object of social concern, and that is a substantive claim, not a formality.
