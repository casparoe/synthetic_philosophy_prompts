I am a junior at my high school, and this year I was appointed to the four-person student committee that allocates the Kestrel Gift: a one-time donation of roughly six thousand dollars left to the school by a graduating family to support student projects. We have narrowed the field to five finalists and to four possible ways to spend the money. We may not split it, and when we announce the decision we must publish a one-paragraph justification addressed to the whole school, including the applicants who receive nothing.

The proposals are these. A: fund one full project, an independent research placement, which only one student can hold; in the ratings, the strongest applicant gets 10 and the other four get 0. B: buy shared equipment, which gives four of the five a benefit they each rated 3. C: hold a school exhibition evening, open to two partner schools, which about seven hundred people would attend and which each of them, by our sample, rates at 0.2 — including all five finalists, who would otherwise get nothing. D: hold a lottery in which each of the five has an equal one-in-five chance of the placement.

Our adviser told us to decide as if we had an equal chance of being any one of the five finalists. The trouble is that the ratings came from the applicants themselves, each using his or her own sense of the scale: one rates everything either 10 or 1, another rates every option a flat 6, and one wrote a sentence instead of a number, saying the placement would change what she does after graduation. Maya, the applicant everyone agrees has the fewest other opportunities, is not served by A or B; under C she gets 0.2, and under D she gets a real chance at the placement.

Priya on the committee says we should simply add the ratings up and pick the largest total, which hands the money to C by an enormous margin — and she refuses to accept that, because most of the people C helps do not attend our school. Another member says we should instead make the person who is worst off as well off as possible, which seems to select C as well, since it is the only proposal under which Maya receives anything at all, even though it helps her least in any sense anyone cares about. When I proposed the lottery, both said it was obviously worse than simply deciding, because you cannot justify randomness to the four who lose; yet from behind our adviser's imaginary ignorance A and D come to the same total, and D treats everyone identically.

I cannot shake the feeling that all three of us are adding up things that were never measured on the same scale, and that the objection to the lottery is hiding a further assumption nobody has stated. Is the lottery really the worse option, or is our reaction to it doing work we have not acknowledged? Are we entitled to combine these numbers at all, and if not, what should we settle before we vote? And what can I honestly put in the published paragraph?
