I am writing the second chapter of my thesis, on whether the information idiom in molecular biology is doing explanatory work or is an elaborate heuristic, and I have got myself into a position I cannot accept and cannot locate the fault in. The argument below is my reconstruction of a line I have been pushing in our reading group; several people there are molecular biologists who are impatient with philosophers telling them their practice is confused, and I would rather not have to tell them that either. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

(1) Molecular biology is committed to DNA carrying information in a sense that is not merely decorative: codons specify amino acids, regions are annotated as coding or non-coding, polymerases are described as reading, and mutations are described as errors that proofreading and repair machinery corrects. If this talk were only a façon de parler, the chapter would be short; I take it that it is not.

(2) Suppose DNA carries information merely in the sense in which one state carries information about another whenever the two are reliably correlated, with nothing required to be reading the one as a sign of the other. Then information is everywhere: tree rings carry information about rainfall, smoke carries information about fire, a footprint carries information about the animal that made it. In that sense genes carry information, but so does everything, and the idiom seems to add nothing to ordinary causal talk. So on the correlational reading the notion is trivial and does none of the work biology wants from it.

(3) For the notion to be distinctive, it must be the sort of thing that can go wrong. A codon can specify the wrong amino acid; a sequence can mislead the machinery that acts on it; a message can be corrupted. Pure correlation cannot be wrong. A footprint is never a false footprint, and tree rings do not lie. So genetic information must be semantic: it must have content capable of misrepresentation.

(4) The only naturalistically respectable way to get content capable of misrepresentation, so far as I can see, is to fix a state's content by its function, understood as what it was selected to do. A given codon means leucine rather than merely causing leucine to be inserted because codons of that type were selected for producing leucine. Selection history supplies the distinction between what a state does and what it is supposed to do, and misrepresentation is doing the latter's failing.

(5) But then anything lacking the relevant selection history carries no information at all: newly arisen sequences on their first replication cycle, sequences whose effects are incidental by-products of neighbouring function, transcripts that happen to be produced and do nothing, and, most awkwardly, the constructs of synthetic biology, which molecular biologists describe in perfectly informational terms on the basis of a design and not of a history. Yet these descriptions are empirically fruitful, which they should not be if they are empty.

(6) Worse, on this account a code needs a reader, and in the actual cell the reader is itself part of the message: the transfer RNAs and the enzymes that charge them are themselves products of the very system they interpret. The interpretation of the code appears to be encoded in the code, which looks less like a grounding of content than like a circle.

(7) So either genetic information is trivial, or it is restricted in a way that makes a great deal of successful molecular biology illegitimate, or it rests on a circular account of interpretation. I cannot accept any of the three, and my own suspicion is that something illegitimate happens in the move from (3) to (4): I have been offered the reply that genes are distinctive not as bearers of content but as causes of a specific kind — causes that make a difference to which outcome occurs, in a way that cytoplasm or ambient temperature does not — and I do not see how this answers the difficulty, since a specific cause cannot be mis-specific, and my worry in (3) was precisely about being wrong.

Which step do you reject, why does it look fine as written, and what does rejecting it cost me? I would like, if I can, to retain (1) and (5) — I do not want to tell the molecular biologists in the room that their practice is confused — and I am more willing than not to give up (4) if something better can be put in its place. Please price the alternatives as well as naming the step: in particular, tell me what I would have to say about mutations being errors, and about the arbitrariness of the codon-to-amino-acid mapping, on each of the options you canvass.
