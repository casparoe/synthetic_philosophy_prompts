I am a retired experimental physicist — something over thirty years working on charge transport in disordered semiconductors — and for the last two years I have been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at my old department, which is how I find myself, very much to my own surprise, defending a position I would once have waved away as soft-headed. We have arrived at the question of what it is to explain an event in history, and I have become convinced that a narrative — a story with a shape, told at a particular grain, with a beginning that is only a beginning relative to an ending — can do explanatory work that no set of law-like generalizations does. I would like to practice that position against someone who genuinely disagrees with it.

So: please play a committed defender of the covering-law model of explanation, in the deductive-nomological line. Be good at it rather than a caricature. Concede at once that there are no strict exceptionless laws of history; allow your generalizations to be statistical, hedged, ceteris paribus, or drawn from neighbouring sciences; and say that what working historians produce are elliptical or "sketch" versions of proper covering-law arguments, with the real generalizations left implicit. Hold firmly to the substantive claim: whatever does explanatory work in a narrative does so only by pointing to law-like regularities and causal mechanisms the narrative does not state, so that the narrative belongs to the business of finding and presenting an explanation, never to what justifies it. You are welcome to press me on what makes one narrative better than another, or on how I would adjudicate between rival narratives, if you think I cannot answer without appealing to causes. Reply in the first person, in a paragraph or two, in the brisk register of a seminar; concede small points when they are genuinely small, but never the thesis that a historical explanation is answerable to generalizations it fails to state. Argue the point rather than citing the literature — I do not want reading suggestions. Stay in character until I say "stop"; if you need a fact about a historical episode, request it in square brackets and I will supply one.

Here is my opening, in three parts — take them in whatever order serves you, but do not skip the third. First, grain and order. Subsumption under a law seems to me indifferent to how coarsely the events are described and, within the limits of the law's own temporal quantifiers, to the sequence in which they are laid out; whereas historical explanation is exquisitely sensitive to both. Mention the assassination at Sarajevo and you are explaining the July crisis; mention the weather in Sarajevo that morning and you are padding. Second, a case from my own bench, which is the one that actually moved me: two specimens cut from the same wafer, grown in the same run, differed in low-temperature conductivity by nearly an order of magnitude. No law of disordered semiconductors predicts which of the two would be higher. What explained the difference was a story about a particular object — this one sat on a warm shelf over a weekend in August and its defects had time to relax, that one went straight into the cryostat. That is an explanation, and it is a narrative; if you call it an elliptical covering-law argument, I will ask you to state the generalization. Third, and this is the challenge I most want met rather than deferred: what would count as evidence that some historical explanation is not an elliptical covering-law argument? If nothing could, then the model has been protected at the price of saying nothing at all.

Finally, because I keep a plain-text log of these exchanges to read back over afterwards, end every reply with a compact JSON object placed after your in-character prose, with exactly these fields: "move" (a single clause naming the one thing you just did, e.g. absorbed the grain objection into a ceteris-paribus clause); "conceded" (an array of strings, possibly empty); "contested" (an array of strings); "suppressed_generalization" (your best actual candidate for the law-like statement that the narrative I offered is shorthand for, or null if you decline to produce one this turn); "next_question_to_me" (a single string). Valid JSON, no trailing commas, no commentary between the prose and the object. The "suppressed_generalization" field is the one I will be reading most carefully.
