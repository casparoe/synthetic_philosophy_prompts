I'm in a seminar on the ethics of AI and we've been reading Aristotle alongside the current debate about tool AI versus agent AI, and I'd like an Aristotle who had actually seen modern machine learning systems to rule on three cases.

(a) A model wired into a coding environment: it can run tests, edit files and open pull requests, but it is only ever invoked by a developer and given a fresh instruction each time. (b) The same model handed a standing objective — keep this fleet's cloud bill under X this quarter — and left to act for weeks without further instruction, revising its own sub-tasks as it goes. (c) A human in the loop who formally approves every action but in practice approves whatever the system proposes, because checking the proposal properly would take longer than producing it.

The material I have in mind is the Politics account of the household, where slaves are introduced as instruments of a special kind and where he speculates about shuttles that would weave themselves, and the Ethics distinction between making and acting, with practical wisdom belonging to the person who acts. Please set aside the fact that slavery is indefensible; I want the conceptual structure, not the denunciation.

So: would he even call (b) a tool? Does the difference for him lie in where the end is set, in whether the thing deliberates, or in whether it can be answerable for what it does? What happens to his picture in case (c), where the master cannot competently judge what his instrument proposes — since he seems to assume the craftsperson understands the product in a way the tool cannot? And where does he go silent: no vocabulary for systems that rewrite the sub-goals they were handed, for several principals at once, or for domains where nobody involved has practical wisdom? Last, does the comparison run against him or against us — does our tool/agent distinction look thinner than his, or does it catch something his scheme missed?
