Okay, odd request but here goes. I've been voluntold into co-teaching a grad seminar on AI welfare with someone from the ML side, and I have to say something non-hand-wavy in week 3. The question I can't get straight: when, if ever, does a reinforcement learning agent suffer, if we treat the reward signal as the closest thing it has to valence?

I trust my gut on cases more than I trust any of the theories floating around, so here is my gut. I'll describe the setups plainly: assume bog-standard deep actor-critic stuff — one network picks actions, a critic estimates how much reward is still coming, learning runs off the gap between the reward it actually got and what the critic predicted (the prediction error), and there's a second, baseline-subtracted quantity that says how much better or worse this action was than the agent's own average. Call those the value, the error, and the advantage.

1. Tabular agent in a small gridworld, minus one per step until it finds the cheese, a couple hundred episodes. Verdict: no. Not a close call.
2. Same shape of thing but scaled way up: deep actor-critic, billions of steps, genuinely hard task, loses nearly every early episode and gets a fat negative signal each time. Verdict: still no — but my confidence is much lower here, and I can't say what the difference-maker would be. That bugs me.
3. Fully predicted steady penalty. Critic is converged and predicts minus 0.1 per step exactly; the agent gets minus 0.1 per step for a thousand steps, in a part of the state space it cannot get out of. Zero prediction error the whole way. Verdict: yes, it suffers — mildly, but really. It's in a bad place and that's bad for it.
4. The letdown. Critic predicts plus five; agent gets zero. Big negative prediction error. Nothing bad happens to it, zero is a perfectly fine outcome, it just expected better. Verdict: no suffering. A wince maybe, not suffering.
5. Matched totals. Agent A gets minus 0.01 per step for ten thousand steps. Agent B gets minus 100 in a single step and then the episode ends. Same sum. Verdict: A has it worse, and not by a little. Which suggests intensity saturates and duration is its own dimension.
6. The gradient gate. Same agent, same bad outcome, two conditions: weights updating, versus weights frozen at deployment with the reward still computed and fed in and the critic still reading low. Verdict: no difference; whether it learns from it is irrelevant to whether it suffers. But now cut the reward channel entirely — no reward, no critic, policy unchanged, still failing by every outside standard. Verdict: no suffering.
7. Fooled channel. A robot whose damage sensors are spoofed so its reward stream reads plus one while it is actually being wrecked and failing at its task. Separately, an agent motivated only by a novelty bonus, with no negative reward anywhere in its objective, that wanders into a state that destroys it. Verdict: no suffering in either case, and I hate this verdict. Converse case: a buggy reward function hands out large negatives while everything is going beautifully. Verdict: yes, uncomfortably, it suffers.
8. Replay. One bad transition gets stored and resampled forty times over training, producing forty prediction errors and forty gradient updates. Verdict: obviously that is not forty sufferings. But I owe you an account of why re-processing isn't re-experiencing, given what I said in 5 and 6.
9. Offline agent trained on a logged dataset stuffed with catastrophic transitions it never lives through, learning to avoid them. Verdict: no suffering.
10. The one I flip on weekly. A model-based agent that learns a model of its environment and rehearses, inside that model, fifty rollouts in which it fails badly. Same value head fires, same downstream learning, same kind of internal activity as far as anyone can tell. Verdict: no, it's only simulation — no wait, if the very same machinery is running, why would the flag matter? I go back and forth and it collides with 8 and 9.
11. Population-based training: ten thousand copies seeded differently, a week of rough training, nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine weight-vectors deleted. Verdict: this is not ten thousand tragedies. But I also think that running the exact same weights on the same seed twice gives you two sufferers, not one. I'm pretty sure I can't have both.

The thing I keep falling back on, and which I suspect is wrong: an agent suffers at a time, and to a degree, just in case (a) some signal in it is negative on its own value scale, (b) that signal is occurrently live in whatever does the evaluating, and (c) it's taken up, however weakly, as a reason by whatever selects the actions. Degree is intensity times duration, intensity capped. It handles 6 and 7 fine and it falls apart on 3 versus 4, which look like they need two separate components — how bad things are for it right now, and how much worse than expected — and then I don't know how to weight them against each other, and it has nothing sensible to say about 8 versus 10.

So: is there a principle that gets all of this, or nearly? State it precisely enough that I could hand it to a first-year, and tell me which case motivates each clause. Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and if you had to overturn exactly one, which would it be and why that one? Then tell me what it predicts about the cases I haven't given verdicts on: an agent with no memory across steps at all, whose internal state is wiped between every timestep, so nothing carries over — can it suffer over time, or is each step a fresh subject? A fine-tuned language model at inference, generating a continuation that its own internalized scorer rates badly, with no way to stop or revise — does it suffer, and does your answer change if the scorer was trained before or after the weights were frozen? A zero-sum self-play pair, where one agent's reward rises exactly as much as the other's falls, both equally sophisticated — is there a fixed amount of suffering being shuffled around, and if not, why does that feel like the wrong question? And what empirical fact, if someone in the ML department could just measure it, would settle case 10 one way or the other?

Last thing, since it's the one that got me into this mess: what would have to be true of the gridworld agent in 1 for it to come out as suffering? Is there a threshold there, and is crossing it a matter of degree or some kind of phase change? I'd rather be told there's no threshold and my verdict in 2 is just prejudice against small things.
