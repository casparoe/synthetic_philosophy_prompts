I've been reading about forecasting on my own — popular books, blog posts, and a lot of arguments with a friend — and I have no formal training in any of this. I keep getting stuck on the same question, and I'd like your number on it, and more importantly the machinery that produces it.

Here is the proposition I want a credence on, stated as precisely as I can manage. In the year 2125, there will still be a single polity that (a) governs territory covering at least half of the land area of the present-day United States, (b) collects taxes and fields a military across it without sustained armed contest, and (c) does so under a constitutional order continuous with the current one, where I'll count continuity as: the present constitution has not been replaced by a new founding document, even if it has been amended heavily or the system has become considerably more authoritarian. I know clause (c) is the soft spot, and I want that handled explicitly rather than smoothed over.

What blocks me is that the outside view feels both indispensable and useless. Every reference class I try seems to give me whatever answer I was already inclined to: great powers, continental empires, maritime trading hegemons, reserve-currency holders, states with written constitutions, all states, or just all large organizations. The rule I've fallen back on is: take the narrowest class that still has enough members to yield a rate at all, and permit yourself to narrow further only when you can state a mechanism that makes the narrower class different — not just a vibe that this case is special. Maybe that rule is wrong, or maybe it secretly encodes my politics. Tell me whether you'd use it, and how much your number moves if you reject it.

A second thing I can't settle. People throw around figures for how long large polities typically last, on the order of a couple of centuries, and this one is already roughly two and a half centuries old. My friend says you therefore can't reuse that average, because if failure risk were roughly memoryless the expected remaining lifespan wouldn't shrink with age; I say large polities plausibly do senesce — fiscal commitments, institutional rigidities, legitimacy decay — so the hazard rate rises with age and being this old is genuine evidence of being nearer the end. Which of us is closer to right, and how much of your number depends on it?

My own credence is about 0.55. My friend's is about 0.85, largely on the grounds that nuclear weapons and deep economic interdependence have removed the mechanism by which polities of this kind used to break up, so the historical record is drawn from a different distribution than the one we're now sampling from — which is a non-stationarity worry that I think is real but that I also think gets overused, since every era's partisans think their era is unprecedented.
