I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who works on classical Indian epistemology, and I ended up defending the Nyāya position that comparison (upamāna) is an irreducible source of knowledge rather than a composite of perception plus inference plus remembered testimony — the position illustrated by the stock case of someone who has never seen a wild ox, is told that a wild ox is a cow-like animal, and who, on later encountering such a beast, comes to know that the animal before them is a wild ox. I want to know what would actually count against that claim.

Please don't just rerun the reductionist argument my friend gave; give me a ranked map of the position's exposure — what would be fatal to it, what would merely be awkward, and what a Nyāya defender could absorb without real cost. Three cases did most of the work in our argument and I'd like them addressed directly. First: a subject who has never perceived the standard of comparison at all — she has only read verbal descriptions of cows and seen a stylized drawing, is told the wild ox is cow-like, and then recognizes one; if she does come to know, upamāna seems to work without any perception of the compared object. Second: a case where the resemblance is structural rather than sensible — a trainee is told that a certain unfamiliar component functions like a check valve, and on handling one identifies it; here the similarity is relational and functional, not something the eye takes in. Third: a case where the similarity statement comes from a speaker the subject independently distrusts, or is misremembered, and the resulting identification is nevertheless correct. I'd also like to know what the theory is committed to saying about misfires — where the animal seen is a disguised ox-lookalike — given that a pramāṇa is defined as the instrument of a cognition that is by definition true.

Say which of these, if any, a Nyāya defender would themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to what only critics assert. I also want your honest view on whether the claim has been insulated: if each school fixes the reference of pramāṇa by its own causal or evidential criteria, irreducibility may come out true or false by stipulation, and I want to know whether the dispute still has a real upshot or has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and whether that would be a defect in a claim of this kind. My own hunch is that the decisive defeater, if there is one, comes from the non-perceptual similarity case rather than from reduction to inference; tell me if I'm wrong. Finally, be explicit about which of what you say is settled and agreed among people who actually know this literature and which is a contested reading or your own reconstruction, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus view.
