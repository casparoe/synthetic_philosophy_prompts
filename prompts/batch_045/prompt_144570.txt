Some background on where I'm coming from. I spent my career in X-ray crystallography, mostly solving structures of silicate minerals, and I have recently been sitting in on a reading group in philosophy of language with a friend who was a professor of philosophy before he retired. We have got onto Kripke's 1980 book *Naming and Necessity* (the transcript of his 1970 Princeton lectures) and the claim that there are truths that are necessary but knowable only a posteriori — the stock examples being that Hesperus is Phosphorus, that Cicero is Tully, and that water is H₂O. I can follow the surface of the argument: names rigidly designate, a true identity between rigid designators holds in every world in which the thing exists, so the identity is necessary, and yet we learned it by looking. What I cannot see is whether this is a genuine discovery about modality or a clever redescription of our naming conventions. In my own field, the composition of a phase is the paradigm of a contingent empirical finding: we could have been wrong about it, and we say so. To be told afterwards that it is necessary sounds like a word game.

Here is the case I have constructed to force the issue, and I would like it to be the spine of your answer. In 1908 a mineralogist describes a new mineral, calliopeite, from a single specimen in a museum drawer, fixing the name by ostension on that specimen — call it the material in drawer 14, tray 3 — and recording one measured diagnostic, a distinctive powder pattern. It now turns out that the specimen is not one phase but two intergrown fragments, in roughly equal amounts: a magnesium end-member and an iron end-member of an isomorphous series with the same crystal structure and near-identical optical behaviour, much as forsterite and fayalite are the end-members of the olivine series. The recorded pattern was a superposition of the two. Every later use of 'calliopeite' in the literature traces back to that tray. So: (i) is it right to say that calliopeite is the magnesium phase, and if it is, is that identity necessary a posteriori, or does the heterogeneity of the sample instead make the term ambiguous, non-rigid, or its extension simply indeterminate?
