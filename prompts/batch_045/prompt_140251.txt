I'm drafting a post — working title something like "Is there a Coase theorem for values?" — and before I start scoring candidate models I want the specification pinned down. The case I keep coming back to: two factions on a nonprofit's board, one animal-welfare-leaning and one global-health-leaning, have to set a shared budget and an official line on questions neither faction can avoid. They have no agreed cardinal currency between their value systems; money can't serve as a side payment, because both sides think buying or selling the organization's stance is itself objectionable; and neither accepts the other's value system as correct, though each regards the other as reasonable rather than crazy. Everyone I've discussed this with argues from a single favorite case, so I want the full requirements list before anybody gets to argue from one.

So: write the desiderata an account of moral compromise as gains-from-trade would have to meet to count as adequate for that job. Given two or more parties with partly differing terminal values and no common currency, the theory should say when a compromise is a genuine improvement by each party's own lights — as opposed to a modus vivendi, a capitulation dressed up as an agreement, or a refusal to compromise that was correct — and it should be determinate enough to actually recommend a division to someone. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. Here are the things I think belong somewhere on the list, in whatever form you think defensible; I don't know which are real desiderata and which are just my own prejudices. It must distinguish a genuine joint improvement from (i) modus vivendi and from (ii) epistemic hedging between value systems, and say whether hedging is a separate mechanism or a special case of the same one. It must model what I've been calling the integrity tax: the cost a party bears, over and above how the outcome ranks by its own lights, from having authorized or lent credibility to something it thinks is wrong — and I want to know whether a non-trivial integrity tax is compatible with there being any gains from trade at all, or whether it eats them. It must not help itself to interpersonal comparison or a shared cardinalization, that being the entire difficulty, while still outputting something determinate. It must say what the gains consist of rather than asserting that they exist; my own guess, which I'd like stress-tested, is that people conflate two sources — diminishing marginal returns along a single issue, which makes splitting the difference on a policy's stringency better for both sides than either winning outright, and nonconvexities plus differing intensities across separate issue dimensions, which make log-rolling and turn-taking beat splitting — and if that taxonomy is right the theory should predict which mechanism applies from features of the case. Finally, it should be robust to strategic misrepresentation: if a party does better by exaggerating how much it cares about an issue, and both sides know this about each other, the naive gains seem to evaporate, and I want to know whether that is fatal or merely an engineering problem.

The part I most want is your view on which requirements can't be jointly satisfied — I suspect some subset of no-common-currency, determinacy, robustness to misrepresentation, and a live integrity tax is unsatisfiable, and that the list itself is the interesting result, so say plainly which subset.
