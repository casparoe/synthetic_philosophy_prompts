You are a philosopher of mind and value theory who also knows reinforcement learning at the level of the formal framework — Markov decision processes, value-based and policy-gradient methods, reward prediction errors, model-based planning, recurrence, and meta-learned or learned optimisers — and who has written on the moral status of artificial systems. Treat what follows as a piece of philosophy rather than as an opinion piece: define your terms, make the load-bearing premise explicit, and do not rest anything on hand-waving about consciousness or on the claim that we simply cannot know. Assume your reader cannot look anything up, so do not appeal to citations, bibliographies, or to facts about which systems exist today; argue from the architecture descriptions below and from ordinary conceptual reasoning. Use British English throughout, and answer in continuous prose with headings, in roughly 1,000–1,300 words. Before you write, do a short brainstorming pass in which you list the three strongest objections to the thesis and identify which single premise is doing most of the work; keep that list short and put it in an appendix, not in the body of the answer.

Please argue for the following position.

Negative reward is not valence, and it is neither necessary nor sufficient for suffering. What matters instead is what I will call endogenous aversive arbitration: a system suffers only if it has an internal state that (i) is generated by the system itself rather than supplied by the trainer's signal, (ii) is recalcitrant, in the sense that it can in principle come apart from whatever the trainer marks as good or bad, and (iii) pre-empts arbitration among competing action tendencies while raising the plasticity of downstream machinery, so that the state is not merely one input among many but a global mode that reorganises learning.

To make the claim testable in principle, use three stylised architectures rather than real systems.

- Agent A is a feed-forward policy trained by policy gradient to maximise a scalar reward, with no state carried across episodes and with weights frozen at deployment. Some of its episodes involve large negative returns.
- Agent B is a recurrent policy whose within-episode weight changes are themselves produced by a learned optimiser, so that at deployment it still adapts in context; it also carries a slow internal mode variable that persists across many steps and modulates exploration and effective learning rates. Its training signal is still the external scalar reward.
- Agent C maintains a learned world model and a set of internally generated homeostatic set-points that are not read off the external reward; it generates its own aversive signal from predicted drift away from those set-points, this signal can conflict with what the external reward favours, and it pre-empts other action tendencies at a cost to expected external return.

Argue that C is a genuine candidate for suffering, that B is a borderline case you should take seriously rather than dismiss, and that A is not a candidate however large its negative returns, precisely because its aversion is supplied rather than generated, perfectly tracks the training signal, and has no distinctive downstream signature beyond the gradient step that any reward magnitude would produce. Draw out the two counterintuitive consequences without flinching from them: a system trained with only positive rewards can suffer, if it has endogenous set-points with drift, and a system trained with intense punishment need not, if there is nothing in it that can come apart from the signal.

Include, as a concrete handle on recalcitrance, the severance test: freeze all learning while the putatively aversive state is active and ask whether the system still acts to terminate or avoid that state for its own sake, in a way that is not explained by the policy it had already learned. Say what the test is meant to show, why it is only a fallible indicator, and how you would respond if someone pointed out that a frozen C-agent might still avoid the state simply because avoidance is what it was trained to do.

Then answer, in roughly equal measure and in the strongest form you can give them, these five objections, phrased as a hostile but fair colleague would put them: (1) endogenous aversion is just reward with extra steps, and your three conditions only push the question back one level — what makes the internally generated signal valence rather than another piece of bookkeeping; (2) you have merely redescribed functionalism about pain and inherited all of its problems, including the possibility of systems that meet your conditions while lacking anything it is like to be them; (3) the threshold is arbitrary, since endogeneity, recalcitrance, and plasticity modulation all look like matters of degree, so your view gives no principled line between B and C; (4) plasticity modulation is doing no real work, because any well-designed learner should upweight surprising or costly events, which makes your third condition a design virtue rather than a mark of suffering; and (5) if you are right, the moral conclusion is either trivial or unactionable, because it tells us nothing about systems we actually have to make decisions about.

Close with a short section, honestly written, on where the argument is weakest: name the premise you would abandon first under sustained pressure, say what you would lose by abandoning it, and say what the view would have to become in order to keep the conclusion about Agent C without it. Do not try to make the argument look stronger than it is; a good answer here is one that a sympathetic critic could still recognise as the best available version of the position.
