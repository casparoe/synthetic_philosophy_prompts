I am a research assistant on a paper about Augustine's ethics of assertion, and I need a prediction I can put in front of the author. From his two treatises on lying and from his correspondence with Consentius I have reconstructed the following track record of verdicts on cases:

1. A falsehood uttered in jest, where the tone of voice and the whole temper of the speaker make it evident that no one is meant to be taken in — not a lie at all, and not a sin. He sets jokes aside at the outset and never counts them as lies, though he leaves open whether a fully perfect person should use them.

2. A person who asserts what he firmly believes to be true but which is in fact false — he is not lying, though he may be rash or deceived. Conversely, a person who asserts what he believes to be false does lie, even when what he says happens to be true. The question whether someone lies is settled by what is in his own mind, not by the truth or falsity of the thing said.

3. The Egyptian midwives, who deceived Pharaoh in order to keep the Hebrew boys alive — they did lie and it was a sin, but a comparatively light one; what God rewarded was their mercy in sparing life, not the falsehood, and the mercy is what makes the lie pardonable rather than what makes it right.

4. A man sheltering a fugitive from someone intent on killing him, who when questioned simply says nothing, or says only that he will not hand the man over — permissible. Withholding the truth is not lying.

5. The same man, who instead answers with an equivocation carrying a hidden qualification, chosen precisely because the hearer will take it in a false sense — a lie. The reservation does him no good: his heart is double in exactly the sense that constitutes lying.

6. Jacob's words at the blessing, when he presents himself as the elder son — not a lie, on the grounds that the utterance bears a figurative, divinely ordered meaning and so is not aimed at deceiving in the relevant way.

7. Lying to heretics in order to draw them into the open and keep them from corrupting others — forbidden. One may not sin in order to prevent someone else's sin, and the goodness of the end does not make the falsehood allowable.

Those are the data. Here is the case I need predicted.

A hospice chaplain is asked by a dying patient's estranged son, who has just said out loud that he means to make sure his mother never gets the house, "Has she signed a will yet?" The chaplain knows, correctly, that she signed one that morning. He answers in a level tone, with no jest, no irony, and nothing in his manner that would flag the utterance as fictional or figurative: "She has put everything that matters to her in order." The chaplain means the sentence strictly, and by his own lights it is true, but he has chosen it precisely so that the son will conclude that no will exists — and the son does draw that conclusion, and acts on it. A variant of the case, which I also want handled: the chaplain says flatly, "No, she hasn't."
