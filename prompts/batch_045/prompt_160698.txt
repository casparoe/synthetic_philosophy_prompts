I have no formal background in philosophy. Years ago I came across a dialogue in which a surgeon, rather than letting Einstein die, transcribes his brain into a book so complete that you can hold a conversation with Einstein by looking up the page on which he answers your question, and the dialogue circles endlessly around whether the book really is Einstein, or is conscious at all. The two stock answers both leave me cold: that it is only a giant index seems too quick, and that of course it is him seems too cheap. What I keep coming back to is something else — that the book is never at a loss. It never hesitates, gropes for a word, mishears, forgets, changes its mind halfway through a sentence, or cares how you take its answer, because nothing is at stake for it in replying.

I would like a short story of roughly 1,500 words that puts pressure on that thought, rather than one that dramatizes the familiar positions for and against. Here is a setup, which you can alter: brain transcription has become a commercial service, and customers find instant, flawless answers uncanny, so the company sells an optional package they call wear — the book answers a little more slowly as the years pass, grows mildly inconsistent with itself, sometimes cannot answer at all, sometimes refuses, and occasionally asks a question of its own. Tell the story through someone who must decide whether to order the wear for a book made from a parent who consented to the procedure, and let concrete details carry the problem: one particular question asked, one particular hesitation or refusal, one particular way the book fails. A reader should finish genuinely unsure whether ordering the wear is an act of respect, a forgery dressed up as piety, or the moment the book becomes someone. Please write it as fiction that stands on its own, not an argument with characters attached, and without any technical vocabulary or citations.
