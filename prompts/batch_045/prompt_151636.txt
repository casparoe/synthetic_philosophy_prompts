hey — act as a professional philosopher who also designs pedagogy: someone who has run a lot of game-based and socratic sessions for adults and knows where they break. Take your time, brainstorm several options before you commit, and show me the two designs you rejected and why before you give me the one you'd actually run.

The setting: I host a fortnightly "Practical Philosophy" evening in a pub back room. About 16 adults, mixed crowd — a couple of effective-altruism types, a therapist, mostly developers and designers. 90 minutes. Whiteboard and marker only, no projector, no laptops, no apps. I can spend maybe £20 total and I can raid the pub kitchen. So the stakes have to be snacks, tokens, small privileges, mild social embarrassment — not money, not tech.

Why I'm asking now: me and my brother signed up to do a cold outdoor swim every morning in January for a charity thing, and I keep bailing at 6am. What I noticed is that it isn't exactly that I don't want to do it, and it isn't straightforward akrasia either. It's that the night before I can't seem to form the intention, right then, to get in the water tomorrow — even though I'd swallow a pill that made me do it without hesitating. Someone told me this is close to a well-known philosophy puzzle about a billionaire who pays you a large sum purely for intending, tonight, to drink tomorrow a vial of toxin that will make you pretty ill for a day and has no lasting effects. You never have to drink it. You get paid the moment the intention is formed, and the money gives you no reason at all to drink later. I want the group to hit that wall themselves.

What I want from you: a runnable activity, 90 minutes, 16 people, where participants run into the problem before it's named. Rules, roles, materials, a payoff scheme with no real money, and a timeline with rough minute marks. Constraints:

- No lecture up front. Nobody hears the puzzle's name or the philosopher's name until the debrief.
- The aversion should be real and present in the room rather than imagined. My idea: everyone takes a small sip of something genuinely unpleasant at the very start (very bitter tonic, cold black coffee, a lemon rind, whatever is safe and cheap) so they know exactly what they're being asked to intend, and the "tomorrow" version is a much larger dose of the same stuff.
- Three wrinkles I've been kicking around — use, adapt, or discard them, and tell me honestly which ones are bad ideas: (1) a private bidding round where each person writes down how bad the tomorrow-task can get, or how big the reward has to be, before they hit the point where they notice they can't form the intention; then we plot the spread on the board; (2) giving half the room access to a clunky commitment device (a signed card handed over, a deposit left with me, a text drafted to a friend) and half the room nothing, so we can see whether what people are really doing is precommitting rather than intending; (3) a late reveal that the "intention detector" I said I was using was never real.
- The payoff structure should make the reward genuinely independent of whether anyone performs the aversive act, and participants should feel that independence rather than be told it.

Debrief: map specific observable moments onto the moving parts of the puzzle — the gap between forming the intention and acting, intention versus prediction about yourself versus desire, why the reward is supposed to be inert with respect to the action, the fact that tomorrow you'll have no reason left, and the idea that you can't intend what you believe you won't do. Say which moment is supposed to produce which insight, and what I should do if a moment produces nothing.

Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I'm guessing some people feel no resistance at all and will cheerfully intend anything, someone points out that self-report is worthless, one person thinks this is a wellness or productivity thing, and at least one person tries to win the game instead of doing the exercise. How do I handle each, in the room, without turning it into a lecture?

Last, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce. My worry is that the bite of the puzzle comes from an omniscient intention-reader paying out on the intention itself, and any classroom version either can't see intentions or has to substitute a commitment action, which quietly changes the question into a different one. Say plainly which feature only I can supply in words afterwards, and keep that bit of talking short.

What people should walk out noticing: that deciding, predicting what you'll do, wanting to do it, and now-intending to do it are four different things — and that there is something structurally strange about trying to form an intention you already know you'll have no reason to act on.
