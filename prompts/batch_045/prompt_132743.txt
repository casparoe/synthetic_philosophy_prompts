I'm on the grants review board of a small private foundation that funds about fourty independent research and outreach groups — mostly small university teams, a couple of observatories, and two or three well-funded amateur consortia — doing radio astronomy and SETI-adjacent work in a dozen countries. Two things have landed on us at once and I need something in front of the full board in ten days.

First: a small team in Chile wants a modest supplement to lease uplink time and send a short, carefully designed "mathematics first" message toward a nearby exoplanet host star. Their pitch is that this is basically outreach, the physics is benign, and expected harm is essentially nil. Second: an amateur consortium with its own money wants to send a much higher-powered "we're here" beacon at a dozen targets, and has said publicly that they'll do it with or without us. Four other groups have told us informally they're watching what we decide and will follow suit.

One board member circulated a short note making a point I half remember from somewhere — I think there's a name for it, something like the unilateralist's curse? The gist as he put it: when any one of N people can trigger an action on their own, the action gets taken whenever at least one person's estimate of it is positive, so adding more independent groups with trigger power raises the chance somebody acts on an optimistic mistake, even though every individual group thinks it's being careful. If that's right, our forty grantees are in a worse epistemic position than any one of them realizes, and a hands-off policy isn't neutral.

But I'm not convinced the conclusion he draws follows, and that's where I want the memo to do real work. Another member argues the opposite: forty independent estimates beat seven people on one committee, and a committee drawn from the same small field shares the same blind spots, so its errors are correlated and centralizing buys less than it looks. And there's a version of the same point aimed at us — if the danger is that any single actor can pull the trigger, then a board that can unilaterally block all forty groups has the mirror-image problem: the thing won't happen unless we say yes, and we can be wrong in that direction too, especially since contact is a positional good that's worth less the more people do it and the later you are.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate. It should tell the board what to do about the two applications, what standing rule to adopt for the rest of the cycle, and who holds the trigger — where it sits, what happens if we deadlock, and what we do about the consortium that says it'll transmit anyway. Two pages, plain language, a bottom line with a timeline, and don't paper over the strongest argument against whatever you recommend; half the board will make it in the meeting and I'd rather have it answered in writing. One last thing: our mission language commits us to backing bold independent investigators and not second-guessing them, so whatever gatekeeping you propose has to be arguable in those terms or I definately can't get it through.
