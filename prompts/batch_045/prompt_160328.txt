I chair the accountability review panel at a large teaching hospital. Every incident report that reaches us — roughly eight to ten a month — must be routed into one of two tracks: a clinician track, which records individual culpability and can lead to remediation or discipline, or a system track, which records no individual culpability and sends the case to design and training instead. The panel has forty-five minutes per case, consists of a risk manager, two clinicians and an HR representative, none of whom has any philosophy, and must produce a one-page rationale that will survive both a union grievance and a regulator inspection. The regulator's reporting rule further requires that any finding of individual culpability be accompanied by a recorded statement that the clinician "had a meaningful alternative" — a phrase our own draft policy repeats without defining.

Our working question, week after week, is whether the clinician could have done otherwise, and a "no" is treated as closing the culpability question: no alternative, no blame. I have become convinced this is the wrong question, because of theFrankfurt-style cases in which an agent acts exactly as she wants to act while a counterfactual intervener stands ready to ensure the same outcome if she had shown any sign of deviating — an agent who, intuitively, is not thereby excused. But I cannot hand my panel a literature. What I need is a procedure.

Please write the decision procedure my panel could actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "no individual culpability," and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact. It has to do three things at once. It must preserve whatever is true in the thought that the absence of alternatives can excuse. It must tell me how to satisfy the regulator's "meaningful alternative" language in cases where the better finding is not about alternatives at all — including how to write the recorded statement when the two come apart, and when to refuse to sign the finding rather than stretch the wording. And it must be usable by non-philosophers working from an incident report in under an hour, which means saying what to do when the evidence for a step is simply unavailable.

The procedure needs to handle four kinds of case explicitly. First, forcing functions: electronic-ordering hard stops or locked protocols that make deviation physically impossible, and sometimes do the intervening themselves — which is not the same as the hypothetical intervener of a Frankfurt case, who never acts. Second, omissions, especially failures to escalate or to double-check, which make up most of our docket. Third, what I take to be flicker cases, where the clinician could have done something else — refused, recorded a concern, resigned — but nothing that would have prevented the outcome. Fourth, gradual shaping: clinicians whose habits were formed over years of unit culture, so that by the time of the incident the deviation was effortless and unremarkable to them; this is our analogue of a manipulation case, and I want to know whether the panel should treat a long history of acculturation as excusing, aggravating, or irrelevant.

Then run the procedure on three cases and report what it gives. (a) A nurse administers the wrong concentration of an infusion after three interruptions; she skipped the independent double-check because the second nurse had been pulled to another ward, and the barcode scanner had been disabled hospital-wide that week for a software upgrade. (b) A resident at hour twenty-six of a twenty-eight-hour shift does not escalate a patient whose vital signs she has seen and charted; her note indicates she reasoned that she would review after the consultant round. (c) A senior consultant deliberately skips a checklist step because he judges, on the evidence available at the time, that the patient's condition makes it unsafe; his judgment was reasonable, and the outcome was catastrophic. Tell me plainly which of these results my panel is most likely to reject, and why that resistance is not a defect in the procedure.

Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: where it depends on evidence the panel will never have, where it is vulnerable to hindsight bias, where it cannot distinguish an actual intervener from a counterfactual one after the fact, and where it has to stop and ask the board to make a values call.

Begin the response with the procedure itself. Your first sentence should be its bottom line: the single governing rule my panel should apply in place of "could she have done otherwise?" — delivered as a rule I can read aloud at the start of a meeting, with no preface, no restatement of this request, and no closing summary. Keep the whole response under about 1,500 words, and do not include citations or suggested reading.
