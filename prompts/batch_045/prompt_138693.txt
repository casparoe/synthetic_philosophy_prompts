You are a professional philosopher of science who also knows quantitative social science well. Please think carefully: before committing yourself, brainstorm at least four candidate diagnoses of the stalemate described below, and only then argue for the best one.

For two years the research team I work on — we build survey-based audience models at a media company — has argued in circles about whether generational cohorts (Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z) are real kinds or merely convenient bins. Two positions have hardened. The realists hold, in the Mannheim lineage, that birth cohort carries causal information: a shared location in historical process during formative years produces durable, socially consequential dispositions, so "Millennial" names something that does genuine explanatory work and licenses induction about unobserved members. The deflationists reply that the cutoffs are journalistic conventions, that apparent cohort effects are recovered only by model choices and dissolve once you allow smooth age and period trends plus a continuous birth-year effect, and that the well-known identification problem for age–period–cohort models means discrete cohort effects are not cleanly recoverable at all. Nobody has moved.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. My own hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one word: one asks whether cohort position has causal-explanatory power at all, the other whether the named discrete bins carve at the joints — and the slide between continuous cohort and discrete generation does all the work. But it may instead be a hidden empirical question about whether there are genuine discontinuities (a jump at some birth year) rather than a smooth gradient, or a clash about which evidence counts as data, or bedrock disagreement about what a "real kind" must be.

To keep score I have been using three stipulative notions: a kind is projectible if it licenses inductions about unobserved members; cutoff-robust if its associated effects survive shifting the boundaries by two or three years; and self-ratifying if members' beliefs about belonging partly produce the regularities (the "everyone knows Millennials are like that" effect). It seems to me a realist could concede cutoff-fragility and still claim projectibility, which suggests the fight has quietly relocated.

So: is this dispute verbal, empirical-in-disguise, methodological, or metaphysical? Where exactly does the realist have to pay a price — for instance, when two people with near-identical biographies, born nine months apart, get different labels? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal result about identifiability, a discontinuity-detection method, a change of question, or nothing at all?
