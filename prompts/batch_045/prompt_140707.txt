hey — I'm on the board of a small family foundation, maybe 1.5m a year, and we've been going in circles about one program for months. We fund maternal-health logistics in one partner country, and it runs *through* the ministry of health: drugs, cold chain, and a per-diems-for-nurses line. Everyone on the board accepts that a real slice of it leaks — call it 20 to 30 percent — some in ministry procurement, some to a governor who takes a cut of the transport contracts. One trustee has basically ended the argument with an analogy: giving aid through a government like that is like handing a drink to an alcoholic. The state is the addict, the money is the drink, we are the enabler, conditionality is the intervention that never works, and the only honest response is tough love — stop routing anything through state channels, go NGO-only or direct-to-clinic, even if total deliveries drop in the short run.

I don't trust how tidy it feels, but I can't say exactly where it goes wrong, and I don't want the response to be that the analogy is insulting to people with addiction. Two wrinkles I keep coming back to that I want dealt with and not smoothed over:

1. In the addiction story the addict and the person harmed by the drinking are the same person. In our case they aren't: the minister and the woman in labour at the district hospital are different people, and the 70 to 80 percent that does arrive apparently still arrives. Does that non-identity break the analogy outright, or does it just relocate it — is it more like handing the alcoholic's *family* money in a way that partly funds the drinking?

2. A friend flips the mapping. On her version the addict is the donor side: the aid bureaucracy, the contractor ecosystem, our own foundation, which needs a sick, aid-absorbing recipient in order to go on existing, and which quietly prefers a leaky program to no program at all. What happens to the tough-love conclusion if that mapping is the right one? On that reading the withdrawal is something we're supposed to inflict on ourselves, and the analogy stops being a reason to cut and starts being a reason to keep going.

Deliverables:

(a) A correspondence table over every element that's doing work in the addiction story — the addict, the drink, the enabler, the disease, tolerance and escalation, withdrawal, recovery, relapse, the intervention, the family, the harm — columns: addiction_element | aid_counterpart | fit (clean / strained / broken / no counterpart at all) | what_the_mapping_silently_adds. Where there's genuinely no counterpart, say so, that's real information.

(b) A second, shorter table splitting the disanalogies into the ones that are load-bearing for the go-NGO-only conclusion and the ones that are cosmetic. Columns: disanalogy | why_it_matters_here (or just: cosmetic) | what_would_have_to_be_true_for_it_to_stop_mattering.

(c) A JSON object with these fields, each kept short: verdict_on_analogy, tough_love_survives (yes / no / partly), load_bearing_breakage (list, max 3), smuggled_assumptions (list, max 4), conclusion_actually_licensed, scope_by_channel (one short verdict each for budget support, project aid routed through the ministry, NGO-delivered services, direct cash transfers to households), flipped_mapping_verdict, what_our_board_should_do, confidence (0–1) and biggest_uncertainty. If you change or add field names, say which ones and why.

Keep it pinned to our case — the 20 to 30 percent leakage, the per-diems, the ministry channel — not to foreign aid in the abstract. And please don't conclude that both sides have a point: tell me where the analogy breaks, and whether the conclusion was riding on the break.
