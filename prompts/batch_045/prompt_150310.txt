Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether culture evolves in a Darwinian way and whether memes are the right unit for describing it. She works on cultural transmission in a psychology department and holds the cultural-attraction or cultural-epidemiology line: cultural items, she says, are reconstructed rather than copied, the direction of change is fixed by what is easy to remember, infer, or find salient, and there is no replicator with the kind of fidelity that would make selection do real work. I defended the memetics side: that there is descent with modification at the level of populations, that heritable variation plus differential success is enough to count as Darwinian, and that low-fidelity copying is the limiting case of replication rather than a refutation of it. She replied that on my side the word has been stretched to mean anything transmitted at all, which makes it unfalsifiable. The case we kept circling was a fifteen-second dance routine on a video app: no copy is exact, the routine stays recognizable across millions of transmissions, and the versions that travel furthest have a slightly simplified opening and a more legible final gesture. Each of us could redescribe that pattern in our own vocabulary, and neither of us could say what would have shown us to be wrong. I'm going to read your answer on a long flight with no internet, so please don't give me anything I would need to look up: no citations, bibliographies, page references, or reading lists, no attribution of specific claims to particular papers, and clearly flagged rather than invented if a piece of evidence is hypothetical.

What I want is the joint statement the two of us should have written instead of having that argument. Assume good faith on both sides and that each of us has read the other's literature, and give me: first, a numbered section of claims both could sign — what "Darwinian" actually requires (is it enough that variation, differential success, and some form of heritability are present, or must the process also produce cumulative, open-ended adaptation, or exhibit a replicator/interactor division of labour?); what is uncontested about cultural transmission, including the enormous role of the storage medium and of content biases; and what each side concedes to the other. Second, a table of the disagreements that remain, each stated twice, once in the wording a memeticist would accept for her own position and once in the wording an attraction theorist would accept for hers, with a column marking whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. If part of it is verbal, I want to know exactly which part and why, rather than a general announcement that the whole dispute is about words.

Three cruxes matter most to me, and for each I want a case, measurement, or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance and falsifiable rather than diplomatic. The first is the fidelity-threshold or "medium-swap" crux: if one tradition is transmitted only by observation while another is transmitted with an exact external template — notation, a mould, a hard drive — does raising copy fidelity change the kind of process or only its rate, and do we then get tree-like divergence and cumulative complexity, or convergence on the same attractors as before? The second is the identifiability crux: can cultural variants be individuated and their fitness measured from properties ascertainable before they spread, or is a variant always picked out retrospectively by its effects, so that the unit is gerrymandered to fit whatever succeeded? The third is my own suggestion, the release crux: when the pressure that favoured a variant is removed, do cultural items revert toward the attractor baseline, or do they drift while retaining what they had accumulated? Under each, record what a memeticist predicts and what an attraction theorist predicts. Finally, apply whatever joint framework you arrive at to the dance routine: give us one paragraph about it that both of us could sign, or, if it cannot be signed, say precisely where it breaks down.
