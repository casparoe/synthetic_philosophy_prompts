Writing a short paper on the badness of pain for a philosophy of mind seminar, and I want somone to poke at my position before I hand it in. Here's where I've landed:

1. Asymbolia cases — people who feel the pain, can tell you where it is and how intense it is, but say it doesn't bother them and make no attempt to get away from whatever is causing it — show that it's the affective or unpleasant dimension, not the sensory-discriminative one, that does the badness-making work. So asymbolic pain isn't bad for the subject at all.
2. Unpleasantness isn't a second feeling bolted onto the sensory one; it's the state's motivational profile. For a pain to be unpleasant just is for it to push you to stop it, to present itself as something to be got rid of.
3. The subject's sincere report is the last word on whether she's bothered. If she says she doesn't mind it, she doesn't, and there's nothing further to be right or wrong about.
4. But habituated chronic pain is still bad for the patient even when she's genuinely adapted, sincerely says it doesn't trouble her any more and means it — and it isn't bad because of what it causes or stops her doing. It's bad in itself.
5. Some pains are ones people go looking for: chili burn, the ache in your legs on a long climb, the sting of a very hot bath. In those cases I want to say the pain is part of what makes the experience good, rather than a bad thing the person is oddly putting up with.

Which of these actually collide and which only look like they do? Rank the collisions by severity, and for each one say what I'd have to drop or weaken and what that costs me — then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I genuinely don't know where the problem is, so don't just hand me the answer the literature likes; tell me what my own set commits me to.
