I'm a self-taught programmer, and I keep noticing the same maneuver in arguments about whether some system matters morally — an animal, a grown bit of brain tissue, a big neural net, whatever. People fairly quickly end up arguing about whether it's *conscious*, and then when you push on what would actually settle that, they appeal to whether the system is "really processing" or "really computing" as opposed to just producing the same outputs. And the case that always makes everyone bail is the lookup-table case. Imagine a gigantic table with every conversation you could possibly have, each one paired with the right reply: nobody wants to call the table a mind, and the reason they give isn't "tables are the wrong material," because they've already said matter doesn't matter, it's the pattern. It's more like: it didn't *work out* the answer, it just had it written down. I see the same shape in three other places. A chess program with every position precomputed versus one that actually searches. A "brain" spread over a million people each holding one cell's update rule and passing notes, where no individual has any idea what's going on. And a recording: tape everything a system receives, then feed the tape back through it later — identical inputs, identical internal states, identical outputs, step for step the same as the first run, but it's playback, so nothing upstream is where the downstream came from in the usual way. Everyone says the second pass isn't the real thing. And then there's the weird one: run the same program on two machines at once and ask whether that's one mind or two, and suddenly nobody's answer is stable at all, which feels like the same confusion wearing a different hat.

The thing that makes me think this "it has to work for it" condition is being stated wrong is expertise.
