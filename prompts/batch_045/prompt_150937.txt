Bit of a long one, sorry — there was an argument at the pub after the London meetup last night and I've been chewing on it since. Two people (Marek and Nadia, both econ-adjacent, both have read the sequences) got onto discounting, specifically whether a *pure* rate of time preference — wanting a good sooner just because it's sooner, not because of risk or inflation or anything — is irrational. I was there, I've probably garbled bits of it, and I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. Roughly how it went:

M1 (Marek): It's just a bias. A pint tonight and an identical pint next Thursday differ only in date, and the date of an experience isn't a property of the experience, so preferring the earlier one for that reason is on a par with preferring a pain in your left knee to an identical one in your right. And any positive pure rate, run out over an indefinite future, makes distant goods count for arbitrarily little — you end up trading the prevention of a catastrophe a thousand years out for a marginally nicer lunch today. That's a reductio of the rate, not a discovery about the far future.

M2 (Nadia): Both of those overreach. On the first: caring about the *shape* of a life (wanting it to improve rather than decline), thanking someone promptly rather than thirty years later, finishing what you started — those are all concerns whose object is irreducibly time-indexed, and you can't rebuild them as preferences over an unordered bag of dated experiences. So "only what an experience is like can matter, not when it is" is a thesis about what the objects of evaluation are, not a piece of logic. On the second: the reductio doesn't discriminate. If you discount distant goods by the chance anyone will be around to receive them, and there's a roughly constant per-period chance of everything going sideways, survival probability decays geometrically and you get *exactly* the same result — far enough out, everything's expected value goes to bugger all. Marek accepts that kind of discounting, so he can't use this as an objection to the pure one specifically.

M3 (Marek): On shape: fine, those are real concerns — but applied consistently they're reasons to make later goods *bigger*, not to count them less, so they can't underwrite a discount. And the difference that matters is that a shape-preference is a view about which lives are better, evaluable from no standpoint in particular, whereas a discount rate is a parameter of where the evaluator is standing; it isn't a judgement about the world at all. On the risk point: the formalism being the same doesn't make them the same kind of thing. The risk discount is answerable — it tracks something in the world, it's measurable, and it's *reducible*: lower the risk and the far future stops being discounted. A pure rate is a flat coefficient with no evidential handle; no discovery and no intervention would ever move it. Something no possible evidence could dislodge isn't a judgement, it's a taste — and a taste about the timing of other people's suffering isn't the kind of thing that gets to set what we owe them.

M4 (Nadia): That last test is the wrong test. Nothing you could discover would make a partial parent stop weighting their own kid more heavily either, and nobody calls that irrational. Ultimate values are *supposed* to sit still while evidence moves around them; they're what evidence gets fed into. And here's the thing I think the whole argument has been missing: you've both been running this as if the question were "which dated goods are better", asked from nowhere. But the question an agent actually faces is "what do I do now", and the indexical now isn't a bias — it's the location of agency. I can only ever act from here. A discount rate is what a standpoint *is* in a temporally extended agent. Call it standpoint prerogative: if you allow agents any agent-relative prerogative at all in the personal case, you need a difference that matters before you can fund it there and defund it across time.

M5 (Marek): The symmetry breaks under test. Run it backwards: suppose someone in 2005 weighted everything that would happen to you in 2025 at half value purely because it was later, and spent the money on themselves. Would you accept that as fair? Nobody does. But temporal standpoint prerogative applied across the whole timeline *is* that — it licenses every stage to short-change every later stage. In the personal case the prerogative is doing work: there are separate claimants with separate lives, and it adjudicates between them. Across time there's no conflict of claimants to adjudicate, there's just a queue, and the "prerogative" is a licence for whichever stage happens to have its hands on the controls to loot the rest.

M6 (Nadia): The backwards test is rigged. Of course the person short-changed rejects it — a stranger's kid rejects your partiality too, so "the loser objects" can't be the test without sinking all prerogative. But I'll concede this much: the arbitrariness argument bites against a *large* rate, and I don't want to defend one. The live question is zero versus small. And zero is a strong claim too — it says nearness never counts for anything, which nobody believes about anything else. Also your "impure" alternative has its own embarrassment: because risk-discounting compounds, the value of the next million years swings by orders of magnitude on tiny changes in the extinction-risk parameter, so it isn't obviously the humbler option.

M7 (Marek): Sensitivity to the parameter is the virtue, not the embarrassment — it means the view tells you to go and find out. And I think the real fork is this: my picture treats a life as one object to be evaluated, yours has to treat the occupant of each moment as in some sense a separate claimant with its own say. If you want that, you've taken on a view about personal identity over time that's doing all the work, and if you don't want it, I don't see what's left holding the prerogative up.

Can you adjudicate it?
