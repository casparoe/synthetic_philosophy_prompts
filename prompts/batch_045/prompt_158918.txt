I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on self-locating credence, and I'm assembling a one-page diagnostic handout for a graduate reading group on the Sleeping Beauty problem. What I want is not another survey of the positions but a decision tree that a student can walk through before reading anything: every branch node should be a question about a concrete case, or about a claim one can accept or reject, phrased so that someone can answer it without knowing where the answer leads; every leaf should carry the name of a position (or, if the cell turns out to be unoccupied, a flag plus one line on whether nobody is there because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored). Around eight to ten nodes and five to seven leaves. For each leaf I also want one line on its most awkward remaining commitment.

I would rather you build the tree out of the logical space than out of the list of labels I half-remember: set out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit first, then place the usual names — halfer, thirder, double-halfer, and whatever else belongs — into the cells that result, and tell me which of my remembered labels turned out not to name a genuine cell. I also don't want the handout to hang on any particular author's formulation of the argument; I'll attach the reading list myself, and it shouldn't matter to the tree who made which move first. Please don't let more than one node ask directly what the number should be; the rest should be cases whose bearing on the number is something the student has to work out.

The distinctions I most want the tree to separate:

- Whether the accuracy, or the betting performance, of Beauty's credence is assessed per awakening or per run of the experiment. I suspect this does most of the work between the two headline answers, and I want it as an explicit node rather than as an assumption smuggled into the others.
- Whether being told that today is Monday is a real update for her. Some packages of answers move her credence up at that point, some hold it fixed, some move it down; I want the tree to display which of those is consistent with the rest of the package, not which is most commonly held. If one of the combinations is only coherent at the cost of giving up conditionalization, or of assigning probability one to its being Monday, say so at that node.
- The variant where the two tails-awakenings are made qualitatively distinguishable — a red room and a blue one, assigned at random — and Beauty finds herself in the red one. Does that change anything, given that she knew in advance that she would see one colour or the other?
- A variant I've been using in the seminar and am unsure about: the coin is not tossed until Tuesday night, after every awakening has occurred, and Beauty is asked on Monday for her credence that it will land tails. I use it to separate views that explain the interesting answer by appeal to evidence about a coin that has already landed from views that don't, but I can't tell whether it separates them cleanly or merely separates them from views about memory. If it is a bad diagnostic, tell me what would replace it.
- The large-number version: tails yields a thousand awakenings rather than two. Is near-certainty of tails the correct output of the view that yields it, a reductio of that view, or neither?
- A variant I made up to test what the views are counting: on tails the Monday awakening lasts one minute and the Tuesday awakening lasts five hours, everything else as before. Does the ratio of time awake matter, and what does a view have to say about the units it counts — awakenings, days, observer-moments, something else?
- Whether Beauty's Sunday self should be able to settle, in advance, what her Monday credence will be, or whether the diachronic question and the synchronic one can come apart.
- Whether the answer should depend on how many times the experiment is run — once, or in a long series — which I take to be the fault line between an indifference-over-possibilities motivation and a long-run-frequency one, though I may be misdescribing it.

Two closing requirements. First, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really distinguish instead of inventing a question to split them, and say whether the indistinguishability is superficial or reflects a genuine identity of commitment. Second, tell me what I should do with a student whose answers land on no leaf at all: is that a sign they have an inconsistent triple of intuitions, a sign the tree is missing a node, and which node would I add?
