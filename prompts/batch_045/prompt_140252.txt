I've been working through the units-of-selection literature on my own for a while now, and there is one passage that keeps getting quoted at me in opposite directions: some people produce it as the founding statement of group selection, others as nothing more than individual selection with an extra step. I'd like help deciding what the passage actually commits its author to. I'd rather have the argument and the arithmetic than a survey of who has said what, so please don't spend the answer on citations, reading suggestions, or names attached to positions; I can find those myself, and I don't trust myself to reconstruct them anyway.

The passage is from the chapter on the development of the intellectual and moral faculties in Darwin's *Descent of Man* (1871):

It must not be forgotten that although a high standard of morality gives but a slight or no advantage to each individual man and his children over other men of the same tribe, yet that an increase in the number of well-endowed men and advancement in the standard of morality will certainly give an immense advantage to one tribe over another. There can be no doubt that a tribe including many members who, from possessing in a high degree the spirit of patriotism, fidelity, obedience, courage, and sympathy, were always ready to aid one another, and to sacrifice themselves for the common good, would be victorious over most other tribes; and this would be natural selection. At all times throughout the world tribes have supplanted other tribes; and as morality is one important element in their success, the standard of morality and the number of well-endowed men will thus everywhere tend to rise and increase.

Earlier in the same chapter he remarks, in passing, that as a tribe increases and is victorious, it is often still further increased by the absorption of other tribes.

Here is what I want, in rough order of importance.

First, a disciplined reconstruction of the argument in modern terms: what is the trait, what varies, what the causal claim is, and what Darwin means by calling the outcome natural selection, given that in his usage the term is level-neutral in a way it no longer is. I want to know whether the passage asserts that a group-level causal process is doing work, or only that a group-level *pattern* results from individual-level causes.

Second, and this is where I keep failing: three readings seem compatible with the text as written. (a) Genuine between-group selection on a group-level property. (b) Kin or family-level selection, since the tribes in question are largely collections of relatives, so that the between-group advantage might just be the vehicle for a gene-level advantage. (c) A byproduct reading, on which the tribe's success is nothing over and above the summed individual advantages of its members and no group-level property is invoked at all. Tell me what the text would have to say in order to discriminate between these, and whether it says it. My suspicion is that the passage is genuinely ambiguous and that the argument for (a) has to be imported from outside it, but I want that checked rather than confirmed.

Third: what does the passage assume without argument? I can see at least three things — that between-group variance in the trait persists across generations (where does that variance come from, and is it stipulated or derived?), that the group-level difference is heritable in the relevant sense, and that tribes reproduce in a way that supports group-level bookkeeping at all. That last one is where the absorption remark bites: if successful tribes grow partly by *absorbing* the defeated rather than by replacing them, then groups don't have the clean vertical reproduction that group-fitness accounting wants, and the transmission looks more like admixture and biased imitation than like differential reproduction of collectives. Say whether that objection is fatal, merely quantitative, or confused.

Fourth, the closing sentence bothers me: the conclusion is that morality will *everywhere tend to rise and increase*. But the mechanism described has an opposing within-group component running in every generation, so a global directional trend looks like a non-sequitur unless something extra is added. Is it? And what is the smallest additional assumption that repairs it?

Fifth, I built a toy to pin this down, and I'd like the numbers checked rather than taken on faith. Four groups of four individuals each; each individual either performs the costly behaviour (A) or doesn't (S). In a group containing k individuals with A, each A-individual has fecundity 1 − c + b(k − 1) and each S-individual has fecundity 1 + bk, with c = 0.4 and b = 0.5. Group compositions are (4A, 0S), (3A, 1S), (1A, 3S), (0A, 4S). Groups then contribute to a global offspring pool in proportion to their total fecundity; offspring mix uniformly; the next generation is repartitioned into groups of four with the same composition profile, so I am stipulating the between-group variance rather than deriving it from sampling. I get group mean fecundities of 2.1, 1.825, 1.275 and 1.0 against a global mean of 1.55, and an overall A-frequency moving from 0.5 to about 0.556. Please show the two-term decomposition explicitly — the between-group covariance term and the within-group transmission term — with enough arithmetic that I can find an error if there is one, and tell me whether anything changes if group fitness is measured as mean individual fitness versus as output of daughter groups.

Sixth, the question I actually care about. In that toy I specified every causal influence at the individual level: a cost to self and a benefit to neighbours. The between-group term is nonetheless large and positive, and A-individuals are at a disadvantage inside every group they occupy. So: is the between-group term describing a distinct causal process, or is it re-describing the individual-level causes after averaging? I want this answered under an explicit interventionist criterion — a collective-level variable does real causal work only if intervening on it while holding all individual-level variables and their interrelations fixed would change the outcome — and I want to know what sort of trait or population structure would make the answer come out yes. My guess is that it requires collective traits that are not aggregative functions of individual traits, and I'd like that made precise and, if it's wrong, corrected.

Finally: if you compute the relatedness between a focal individual and a random other member of its group in that toy (say which definition you're using) and check the resulting kin-selection condition, you should find that all three frameworks agree on the sign. Does that agreement show that the frameworks are the same empirical claim stated differently, or only that they must agree given the way I specified the model — and what would a case look like in which they genuinely come apart? I'd like the answer to end by telling me what, if anything, is still substantively at stake in the units-of-selection debate once the causal question has been separated from the bookkeeping question, and which of the three readings of Darwin's passage that verdict leaves standing.
