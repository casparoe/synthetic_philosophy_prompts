Long time reader of the usual blogs, first time posting something like this. I've basically swallowed Friedman's F-twist and I want someone to tell me what would actually count against it, because I can't find anything that bites and that worries me more than the objections do.

The version I hold isn't quite Friedman's. Roughly: a model's assumptions don't have to be true, or even approximately true, for the model to be good; all that matters is that it predicts well inside some range, and -- this is my addition, which I've started calling the swap test -- that the prediction survives swapping the false assumption out for a *different* false assumption that's incompatible with the first one. So: frictionless planes, infinite populations, zero transaction costs, agents who maximize expected utility with unlimited computation. None of that needs to be true. But if I can replace "agents are perfect expected-utility maximizers" with "agents follow a fixed imitative rule and never update at all" -- two genuinely incompatible stories about what's going on inside -- and the model still spits out the same aggregate prediction, then whatever it's getting right isn't coming from the specific falsehood. The falsehood is scaffolding, not a load-bearing wall. And if the prediction *doesn't* survive the swap, then I treat the model's track record as basically an accident, or as something that was fitted to the data, and I discount it hard no matter how many times it's been right so far. I like this because it turns "all models are wrong but some are useful" from a shrug into an actual test: the useful ones are the ones that pass the swap.

So: what would count against it? Skip the standard objections, I've read them -- that false assumptions can get the right answer for the wrong reasons, that the view can't tell prediction apart from accommodation, that it makes a virtue of not looking inside the box, that it's silent on explanation and on why we care about prediction in the first place. I want the things that would actually move someone who already knows all that, sorted by how decisive they'd be.

Specifically:

- Cases from real science, described in enough detail that I can see their shape without needing to look anything up, where a model passes my swap test and is still bad science, or fails it and is still obviously good science. I especially want the two ugly directions: models whose predictions are robust to swapping in mutually incompatible falsehoods but which nobody thinks are tracking anything real; and models that are dead in the water under any swap whatsoever but which no sane person would give up.

- What the people who defend this family of view would themselves concede as counting against it, as opposed to what their critics say counts against it. I'd rather have the concessions than the objections.

- Whether my test is even well defined, and whether it's insulated by its own escape hatches. My worry: when a swap "fails," I can always say you swapped in the wrong alternative, or that the replacement model wasn't in the same family, and then nothing ever fails the test. Is that worse than the move on the other side, where every failed prediction gets re-described as falling outside the model's domain? Are those the same dodge wearing different clothes, or is one of them more legitimate than the other?

- The class of cases I'm least sure about: models where the idealization isn't scaffolding you could swap out but is the thing that makes the calculation possible at all. The thermodynamic limit, the continuum assumption in fluid dynamics, that sort of thing. There the falsehood looks load-bearing by construction. Does my test just misfire on those, and if so is that a decisive problem or a scoping note?

What I'd personally find most persuasive is something in the neighborhood of: your test systematically misclassifies this recognizable class of models, and here's why that class matters more than the ones it gets right. What I'd find least persuasive is a restatement of the point that false assumptions can't be explanatory, or one dramatic counterexample I can quarantine as a special case. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me if you think the honest answer is that the view has been qualified into something unfalsifiable -- and if so whether that's actually a problem for a view that's supposed to be doing methodological work rather than metaphysical work.
