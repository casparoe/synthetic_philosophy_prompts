I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on personal identity — 14 students, one 90-minute session, a room with movable chairs and no special equipment. The students have already done reductionism about persons (Parfit-style), the fission and fusion cases, and Hofstadter's *I Am a Strange Loop* (2007), in particular his suggestion that a self is largely assembled out of fragments of other selves and that when someone dies a real, if diluted, part of them goes on existing in the brains that carried them.

What I want is a staged activity — a game with rules and roles rather than a discussion — that makes students *feel* the pull of that distributive view before it is named, and then makes them feel its cost. The claim has to show up in what they end up doing and deciding, not in anything I say. I don't want the phrase "distributed self," or any equivalent, used until the debrief, and I don't want a framing lecture up front.

My rough sense of the machinery, which you should keep only if it serves the design and feel free to replace:

- Each student first produces, from themselves, a small kit of genuinely personal material: a handful of opinions on obscure questions, a verbal mannerism, a specific memory, an unfinished project of their own, a characteristic way of arguing. Real material, mildly exposing — the activity should have small real stakes, not hypothetical ones.
- One volunteer is the subject. Their kit is split **unevenly** among three inheritors: one receives the memories, one the style and mannerisms, one the projects and values. No inheritor gets the whole. The subject then goes silent — out of the room, or bound to silence in it.
- Inheritors must then speak and act as the subject in a novel situation, and the room judges, blind and in writing, how much of the subject is present in each. Because the answer is graded, give it a score: I have in mind something like a *ledger of inheritance* — a weighted sum over the fragments, with the weights fixed by the room before any case is judged, and with no requirement that the weights sum to one. Students should be made to commit to weights and then discover that their own ledger returns verdicts they refuse to accept.
- A degradation round, in which the subject's own continuing contributions are made lossy (I was thinking of constraining them to clichés, or destroying part of their notes) while the inheritors keep their portions intact. If the room is honest, it should arrive at the uncomfortable position that the subject is now more fully present in someone else than in themselves. Then force an indivisible choice: a single voice-token deciding who speaks for the subject, which cannot be divided among three partial carriers.
- A chain round, in which a fragment is passed hand to hand to people who never met the source. The same rule that gave the consoling verdict in the first round should now be visibly giving an absurd one. I want students to notice that they are trapped between a view that over-generates and a refusal that costs them the thing they wanted the view for.
- A closing practical decision about the subject's unfinished project: complete it in the subject's style, adapt it, or leave it. A forced choice with three options and a reason required, taken as a group.

Please give me: the rules and roles; the materials; a timeline with minute marks; whatever scoring or payoff structure keeps students invested without money; and the exact prompts I hand out, in the order I hand them. Then the debrief: how to map the specific moments students will have lived through onto the pieces of the distributive view — graded existence, the loss of an all-or-nothing identity, the gap between being represented in a mind and being a subject of experience, and the point at which we are deciding rather than discovering whether a carried pattern counts as survival.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I am especially worried about: a student for whom the material is raw because of a recent bereavement; the subject treating the whole thing as a lark and breaking the frame; the room drifting into the platitude that of course we remember people after they die, which is commemoration and not what is at issue; and students refusing to put numbers on any of it. Say how to handle each, including what to do about consent and the right to redact material.

Finally, be frank about which feature of the view the exercise cannot reproduce and that I will simply have to supply in the debrief. I assume the first-personal side is the big one — that no arrangement of roles can produce what it is like to be the one who is spread out, or the difference between a pattern being instantiated somewhere and there being someone it is like to be — but tell me if there is a further structural feature that the game necessarily misrepresents, and say plainly which of the seminar's readings I should use to cover it.
