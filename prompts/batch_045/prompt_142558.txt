Next month I have to run a forty-minute session on the Ellsberg paradox and ambiguity aversion for a hospital's clinical guidelines committee: eight or nine people, all numerate, all of whom read trial literature and think fluently in terms of event rates and confidence intervals, none of whom has any formal decision theory, and most of whom are in practice instinctive Bayesians. They will also want to come out of the session with a decision procedure, which I suspect is the wrong thing to promise them. Please don't explain the paradox to me; I want a plan for how to present it to them in the time available.

What I need first is the dependency structure. Tell me what has to be in place before the two-urn choice is put on the table at all. My own guess is that the notion of a subjective probability as something that gets read off a person's preferences across bets on matched stakes has to come first, since the whole force of the case depends on there being no single probability assignment that can generate both preferences at once, and on that being a contradiction rather than merely a taste. Tell me where that guess is wrong, and where the real order is the reverse of what I would assume.

Then the carrying example. I would rather build the session around a case from their own work: Protocol A, whose response rate is well estimated from a large and consistent evidence base, against Protocol B, whose response rate is not, because the evidence is small, heterogeneous across sites, and partly unpublished. Tell me whether that is the right vehicle, or whether I should open with the urn and translate afterwards. Either way, say what the example must have and what it must lack. I assume it has to be a one-off decision with no realistic prospect of learning from repetition, that no one should have chosen the uncertainty adversarially or know more about it than the committee does, and that the ambiguity must not be entangled with the size or kind of the stakes — but tell me which of those constraints actually carries weight and which I can relax.

Then the misconceptions, in the order this particular audience will produce them. I expect at least these four: that what I am describing is just risk aversion, or just the ordinary medical norm of caution; that it is really just having a prior over the possible response rates, a second-order probability, which collapses back into a single first-order one; that the genuine problem is that the state space itself is not fully specified rather than that the probabilities within a known state space are unknown; and that in actual committee work the whole thing is moot, because one would simply commission more evidence. For each, tell me the point in the sequence at which it should be met, and the move that meets it without opening up the formal models of ambiguity-averse choice, which at forty minutes I would rather name once and then decline to go into.

Finally, the check. My candidate closing case is this: the same two protocols, except that now the committee must allocate a fixed stock of B across twenty patients over the coming month and will observe outcomes as it goes, and must say in advance whether it would still rather have used A throughout. Tell me whether that is the right final question, what an answer to it would have to contain for me to believe the idea had genuinely landed, and what a confidently delivered answer that merely mimics understanding would look like. Answer in continuous prose, as you would in an email or a memo — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — numbering the stages in sentences if that helps you keep the sequence clear.
