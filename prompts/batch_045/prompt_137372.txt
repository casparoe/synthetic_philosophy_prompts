I'm in a small reading group on the philosophy of applied mathematics — two philosophers, a physicist, and a friend from the maths department who thinks the whole "Wigner puzzle" is a manufactured mystery. Over several evenings he has built the following argument to dissolve it, and I've come to think it proves far too much. I can't locate the exact step where it goes wrong, and I'd be grateful for a diagnosis rather than a verdict on Wigner. I'm working somewhere without library access, so please argue it out from the structure of the reasoning; I don't want a reading list, references, or citations, and I'd rather you stated a view and defended it than gestured at what "the literature says".

Set-up: call a piece of mathematics M "applied to" a physical domain D. Then:

(1) Mapping account of application. For M to apply to D is for there to be a structure-preserving map — an embedding, a homomorphism, or at least a surjective morphism, depending on the case — from D, as our best theory describes it, into M, such that reasoning conducted inside M can be read back as reasoning about D. Application is representation, and the epistemic payoff is that M becomes a surrogate for reasoning about D.
(2) Expressiveness. A formalism earns the name "mathematics" only if it is expressive: it has models rich enough to serve as a surrogate for more than one kind of domain. A notation tailored to exactly one physical system would be a labelling of it, not mathematics, and its success could not surprise anyone.
(3) Universality. Rich structures are, as a rule, universal — they contain copies of everything smaller. The instances he gives are offered as plain theorems: the Rado graph contains every countable graph as an induced subgraph; the Urysohn universal space contains an isometric copy of every separable metric space; every compact metric space is a continuous image of the Cantor set; and by Cayley's theorem every group of order n embeds in the symmetric group on n letters.
(4) Hence applicability is guaranteed. Any expressive formalism already contains, somewhere inside it, an image of — or a copy of — whatever domain you care to name. Given (1), any expressive formalism therefore applies to any domain of the right size. Whether a formalism applies is not a discovery about the world; it is the price of expressive power, payable in advance.
(5) Survivorship. Add a selection effect: the formalisms we remember are the ones that worked, formalisms that latched onto nothing were quietly dropped, and the history of physics is written from the survivors' side. So even the *apparent* fit looks better than it was.
(6) Therefore the unreasonable effectiveness is fully accounted for by expressive power plus survivorship, and there is nothing left over for Wigner to wonder at. In particular, the fact that our best physics is written in this mathematical idiom rather than that one is no evidence that the world has mathematical structure; a physics couched in any equally expressive idiom would have been just as successful.

I can't accept (6), for two reasons. First, the argument looks like it should entail that an arbitrarily baroque formalism — a numerological scheme, say — would be just as effective as tensor calculus or Hilbert spaces, provided only that it were expressive enough; and that seems straightforwardly false. Second, it appears to make the *order* of discovery irrelevant: the cases Wigner actually leans on are ones where mathematics developed for internal, aesthetic reasons turns out decades later to describe a brand-new domain, and on my friend's account that should be no more remarkable than the reverse order. That can't be right either.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? He insists (3) is just mathematics and that the only soft spot is (1) — give up the mapping account and accept that application is something stronger than the existence of a structure-preserving map. I want to keep (1), because it seems to be the only premise that explains why applied mathematics is a *tool for reasoning* rather than a redescription, and dropping it appears to reinstall the mystery we were trying to dissolve. But I also have two inarticulate worries: that (4) is trading on some equivocation in "applies" between there existing a map and there existing the *right* map; and that (3) licenses a slide from "there exist universal objects" to "the formalisms we actually do physics with are universal" — a particular Hilbert space or Lie group doesn't obviously contain copies of everything, and the objects that really are universal, like the Urysohn space or the Cantor set, are precisely the ones nobody does physics with. I can't tell whether those are two worries or one, or whether either of them is the real problem. Please name the step, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives — including what it would cost to give up (5), and what someone who simply accepted (6) would have to say about the two reasons I gave for rejecting it. Finally, and importantly: separate the propositions here that are theorems or matters of logic from those that are live, contested positions in the philosophy of mathematics with real defenders on each side, tell me roughly how contested each is, and flag where you are yourself speculating. Don't present a contested view as the received one.
