I run a short research-skills workshop for first-year PhD students at my uni — about 18 of them, mixed disciplines, one 75-minute slot, no phones allowed and no money for stakes (chocolate and a mildly humiliating forfeit is about all I can get away with). Most of them won't have met the planning fallacy or reference-class forecasting before, and I don't want to say either phrase, or Kahneman's name, until the very end. I've ran a clumsy version of this before where I just asked them to guess how long a task would take and then showed them last year's numbers, and it flopped: they treated the base rate as an irrelevance and went back to arguing about their own case. So I want something better designed than that.

What I'd like is a proper runnable activity — four or five groups, a real task they actually do (you pick it, but it has to be doable with stuff I can carry in a bag and finishable in 10–30 minutes), and then a sequence where they commit to inside-view forecasts, get handed historical data, and discover the pull of the outside view by running into it rather than being told. Two things I specifically want built in, because they're the bits I think my students will actually resist. First, I want the historical deck to be sortable into two defensible reference classes — say a narrow one ("workshop tasks done by people at this stage") and a broad one ("all tasks of roughly this shape") — that give noticeably different medians, so that "take the outside view" turns out not to be one well-defined instruction and the choice of class is itself a judgement nobody can make from outside. Second, I want a handful of the cards flagged as coming from groups who had already been warned about optimistic planning before they estimated, so they have to decide whether to use, drop or discount those, and the debrief can get at the awkward question of whether using that base rate is double-counting the very warning they just received. If you can also squeeze in a move where each group, before opening the cards, privately predicts how much its own estimate will shift once it does — an outside view on their own openness to outside-view evidence — I'd love that. Score it with something that rewards honest distributions over confident point guesses, and give me a rough minute-by-minute plus a materials list.

Then the debrief: map specific moments onto the inside/outside distinction and onto epistemic modesty, say plainly what will predictably go wrong when I run it (anchoring on the loudest person, groups colluding, the task turning out easier than everyone feared, someone working out that hedging games the scoring rule, ties), and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce. My worry is that it stacks the deck — for a generic classroom task there really is no diagnostic private evidence, so the inside view is bound to look worse than it does in the cases where you do know something real — and that under a scoring rule you can't tell genuine modesty apart from strategic hedging. Those two I think I have to supply by talking, so tell me how. Write it up in American English please, the handout gets passed on to a collaborator in the States.
