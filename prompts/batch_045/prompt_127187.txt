I should say at the outset what I do and don't know, so that the procedure can be pitched at the right level. I translate for a living — patent and clinical documentation, French into English — and I am good at the kind of fidelity that can be checked word by word against a source. I have no training in verse, no ear developed beyond an ordinary reader's, and until recently no interest in the problem.

What changed: a friend of mine, a songwriter who worked in French, died two years ago. His partner, who is the executor and speaks none of the language, has asked me to prepare English versions of the thirty-four short songs he recorded, for a bilingual booklet accompanying a reissue of the recordings. Four of them the family still sings together, and they would like to sing those in English. I have two or three evenings per song, spread over about a year, for no fee; at the end my friend's daughter, who is eighteen and has read none of the French, looks at the English page proofs and says yes or no. She will read them cold, without me in the room. The one book on translation I own, which I read because its title puns on a word for a work made in memory of someone dead, contains dozens of English renderings of a single short French poem, each of them preserving something different; it convinced me that the music is where the difficulty is and left me with no way to proceed.

So: please write me a decision procedure I can run alone, in an evening, on each of the thirty-four songs, and run the same way each time.

The things I need it to contain, in order:

First, the questions to ask about a song before writing anything — what the features of its form actually are, and which of them are doing work. My working assumption has been that content is whatever survives a prose paraphrase and that form is everything left over, so that form is what I sacrifice first. Tell me if that assumption is the thing distorting the procedure: I suspect it is, because several of these songs seem to mean things by their form that no paraphrase captures (a refrain that keeps returning the way the thought it reports keeps returning; lines that get shorter as the speaker runs out of things to say; a rhyme that yokes two words the song is arguing belong together). I need a way to separate load-bearing form — the kind whose loss changes what the song claims — from ambient form, the texture a reader enjoys but which carries nothing a paraphrase would miss. My candidate test is a substitution test: replace the feature with a different one and ask whether a reader who has never seen the French would take the song to be saying something different. Improve it if you can, and tell me where it fails.

Second, the spending order, and the grounds for it. Given a song where rhyme, metre, register, line-length, and literal sense cannot all be kept, what do I preserve, and how do I decide in this case rather than by a fixed ranking? I want the reasoning to be recordable, because of the third thing.

Third, a short written artefact per song — a fidelity ledger, say: what I kept, what I sold, what it cost, and one line of justification — that someone with no French and no poetry can read and use to decide whether to accept my version. It should make the trade-offs visible without inviting her to reopen each one.

Fourth, the melody question. For the four songs the family sings, the tune fixes how many syllables there are and roughly where the stresses fall, and I can test my result by singing it. But I also notice that fitting English to a tune is not one thing: you can set it in the idiom he wrote in or in an idiom the family actually sings, and those give different answers about which syllables may be crunched. Say which choice is mine to make and which is not. And give me a rule, with a stop in it, for when the tune makes nonsense — at what point I leave that song out of the singable set, or write new English words to the tune rather than a translation of his.

Please apply the whole procedure to three songs from the cycle, including at least one where the answer is one I will not want to hear — including, if that is where it lands, that a song should not be translated at all and should appear in French with a prose gloss.

1. A song in which every stanza ends on the name of the village where he grew up, with all the rhymes built on that name. The name is untranslatable, and to an English reader it is noise; rhyme and reference are the same feature here.

2. A song whose refrain, repeated unchanged after every stanza, is two lines of vocables — singable nonsense in the manner of a work chantey — which his daughter used to sing along to as a small child. This is one of the four that get sung. There is nothing here to translate.

3. A song in which each stanza's lines shorten, eight syllables to six to four to two, the taper enacting a speaker running out of breath or of things to say. This one is also sung, and the tune will not permit the taper.

Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: which questions it converts into bookkeeping rather than answering, where it cannot be run by someone without an ear for English prosody, where it hands the decision to a person because the issue is what my friend would have wanted rather than what his text permits, and whether a procedure applied uniformly across thirty-four songs is itself a choice I should be making. I would particularly like your view on whether the ledger risks becoming the product — pages of justification standing in for versions I have not managed to make good.
