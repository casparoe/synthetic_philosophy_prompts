You are a professional philosopher with research-level expertise in moral psychology, virtue ethics, and the methodology of personality psychology. You have also read a great deal of the rationalist blogosphere: you are fluent in talk of base rates, steelmanning, out-of-distribution generalization, and the demand that a claim pay rent in anticipations. Before you write anything, brainstorm privately: sketch the strongest available situationist argument, the strongest replies a virtue ethicist has, and — importantly — which objections each side should concede rather than shrug off. Only then write the dialogue. Do not include your notes.

I have been reading this corner of the internet for about fifteen years, so please skip the scaffolding: do not explain base rates, correlation coefficients, range restriction, or what an out-of-distribution failure is. I want the argument, not the homework. I am also not interested in the dialectic as it is usually staged in survey articles, where the situationist rolls out three famous experiments, the virtue ethicist says the experiments are over-interpreted, and everyone goes home. Both of your characters should know that literature cold and should have moved past it.

So stage the argument around a fictional body of evidence, which I will stipulate so that neither of you can retreat into disputes about what some particular study showed. Call it the Many Contexts Project: roughly forty thousand participants, each measured repeatedly in around two hundred densely coded everyday situations, with behavior recorded unobtrusively and with situations coded along dimensions that turn out to matter — how consequential the stakes are, whether the agent is being observed, whether the situation is novel or routine, how much time pressure there is, how salient the moral framing is. The findings, which both characters accept: (i) within a given situation-type, individuals are quite stable over time and reliably different from one another; (ii) across situation-types, raw consistency is low, and correcting for measurement error only gets you part of the way back; (iii) knowing the situation takes you most of the way to predicting the modal response, and knowing the person adds comparatively little; (iv) the residual person-level factor does real work, but mostly in weak, novel, or ambiguous situations, and mostly in predicting how quickly someone recovers after a lapse; (v) an intervention that trains practical-moral skills improves behavior in trained situations, transfers partly to nearby situations, transfers poorly to distant ones, and transfers best for people who also practice deliberately choosing and avoiding the environments they enter.

Write a dialogue of about 1,200 to 1,800 words between two colleagues of equal ability walking back across campus after a seminar where these findings were presented. One of them defends the view that ethical character is a genuine, unified, causally efficacious trait of persons and that virtue ethics is in good shape. The other defends the view that the evidence licenses only local, situation-indexed dispositions, and that character-talk as ordinarily understood is either false or empirically empty. Give them names and voices, but keep the tone of two people who respect each other and have had this argument before.

Constraints on the argument, which matter more to me than the staging:

Neither side may appeal to the authority of any named researcher, paper, or tradition, and there should be no citations, bibliographies, or reading suggestions anywhere. Argue on the merits.

Neither side may be a strawman. Specifically: the situationist must concede something that costs them — that individual differences are not noise, that their own view must explain why ordinary practices of trust and blame have survived, and that a bundle of purely local dispositions is a strange thing to call an ethical ideal. The virtue ethicist must concede something that costs them too — that folk character attribution wildly over-predicts, that the predictive value of any global trait for a single act in a single situation is small, and that a large part of what looks like inconsistency is just the situation doing the work.

They must actually answer each other. I want at least these moves made and answered: the argument from low cross-situational consistency and the claim that virtue does not pay rent in anticipations; the reply that strong situations compress behavioral variance and that dominant situational base rates make even a real trait nearly useless for one-shot prediction; the counter that this saves prediction but leaves the trait too thin to do normative work; the proposal that what virtue really tracks is robustness under novelty, the integration of attention and emotion and reasoning rather than overt acts alone, and the speed of repair after failure; and the situationist's alternative that the rational target is not global integration but local reliability plus situation selection and self-scaffolding, with the virtue ethicist replying that whoever designs your environments then controls whether you are honest, and that situation selection itself presupposes exactly the stable disposition the situationist denies.

Then have them converge on a precise statement of what is still in dispute, and on what would resolve it — some experiment, some measurement, or some conceptual clarification that would distinguish a genuine integrated disposition from a convenient aggregate. I care much more about the precision of that final disagreement than about who wins; if you want one of them to get the better of the exchange, earn it with an argument rather than with a rhetorical flourish. Use concrete invented examples throughout (a mis-credited refund, a stranger's bag left on a train, a small act of cruelty in a meeting) rather than abstractions, and do not let either character get away with a claim whose observable consequences they cannot state.
