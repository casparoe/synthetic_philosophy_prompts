Write a dialogue between a defender and a denier of the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles — the thesis that no two things can differ in number alone, sharing every quality and relation. Both parties should be equally competent, and each speech should answer the point the other has actually made rather than restate a position. Give me exactly five numbered exchanges, one short speech apiece, with no introduction, no conclusion and no stage directions.

Open with Black's two-sphere world, then have the denier press a variant for which the usual evasions are closed off: a world whose entire concrete content is two qualitatively identical momentary experiences, with no space, no time and no third thing for either to be related to differently, so that neither distance, position nor relations to anything else can do the individuating work. Give the defender the serious replies rather than a straw one — that a world containing two such items may only ever be describable symmetrically, and so may not be a genuine possibility at all; and that primitive haecceities might be admitted. Then have them work out jointly whether admitting haecceities leaves the principle true but trivial or question-begging, and whether one counterexample settles anything when the principle is claimed to hold of necessity. Devote the final exchange to what is at stake: whether the argument against absolute space that the principle was historically enlisted for — that a universe shifted three feet east would be no distinct creation — survives if the variant succeeds. Let each character concede one thing they did not expect to concede, without conceding the argument.
