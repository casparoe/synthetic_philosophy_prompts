I cover science and ideas for a newspaper, which is apparently why I got appointed as one of the two lay members on the grants committee of a mid-sized medical research charity. We meet in a little over a week and I'm supposed to circulate a written recommendation beforehand, and I've drawn the hardest item on the agenda. It's a £3m pragmatic trial in our main funding area (a chronic inflammatory bowel condition) comparing two treatment strategies that are both already used in routine care — starting the expensive biologic drug early versus the usual step-up approach. The two clinicians on the committee wrote in pre-review that their own practice wouldn't change under either result, and the chair has now said that if nobody's mind is going to change we're just buying a very expensive fact. Then our health economist member, who is not a fool, invoked something he calls Good's theorem: for a proper Bayesian expected-utility maximiser the expected value of information can never be negative, because you can always just ignore what you learn, so refusing to buy evidence is never rational and declining this is a way of saying we'd rather not know. Half the committee finds that decisive; the other half thinks it's obviously sophistry and that our remit (we fund research "likely to improve patient outcomes") requires a plausible route to changed practice. I genuinely can't tell who's got it right, though my instinct is that the economist is leaning on a theorem that assumes things about us that we do not have.

A few things I'd want the memo to deal with rather than wave away. First, our patient representative pointed out that the people who'd carry the trial's risks (and the burden of the four years) aren't the same people who'd get the benefit of anyone's better-informed decisions, and that a null result could be picked up by commissioners or insurers to de-fund a service that patients say they value — the economist's theorem seems to be about one decision-maker who also happens to be the one who gets the payoff, which is not our situation at all. Second, is the result even about information that *costs* anything? This isn't free; it's most of our discretionary budget for the cycle, and money here is money not spent on the three or four smaller studies we also have queued. Third — and this cuts against my own side — I don't entirely trust the clinicians' pre-review answers, since people are notoriously bad at predicting how they'd act on a result they haven't seen, and the national guideline body that actually sets policy for the health service is not in the room and hasn't been asked to say what it would do with each outcome. There's a real worry that "we only fund studies that would change our own behaviour" quietly becomes a licence to never test anything established.

So: draft me the memo, 600–900 words, something I can circulate under my own name and defend in the room. I need an actual bottom line — approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions — with the conditions spelled out concretely (who has to pre-commit to what, by when, and what happens at the next funding round) rather than a menu of options. State the health economist's argument in its strongest form first, not as a strawman, since he's going to read this. Please take a definite position and argue for it: I don't want a balanced survey, I want to know which way to vote and why. Where you're relying on a contested reading of the theorem, or on some empirical claim about how clinicians or guideline panels actually behave, flag it in a line so I know what would change your mind. And no references, no reading lists, no "see X 1967" — I can't check them and neither can anyone else on the committee.
