What I think the theorems say: average over every possible way the cases you haven't seen yet could turn out — every possible assignment of outcomes, all of them weighted the same — and every prediction method does exactly as well as every other one. Assume the future looks like the past, assume it flips, flip a coin, use a giant lookup table: same expected score on new cases. The way I understood the proof sketch, if a method does well on some possible worlds it has to do exactly as badly on the rest, so it all cancels out.

Here's my chain, which ends somewhere impossible:

(1) Take the above as given.
(2) So a method's track record on the cases you've already seen tells you nothing at all about how it'll do on the next one. On that average the two things are uncorrelated — chance either way.
(3) And that's stronger than the old Hume worry. Hume's problem was that the past doesn't prove the future. This says the past isn't even evidence about the future. So it's not that induction is unproven; it's that learning from experience does no work whatsoever.
(4) But the average in (1) is itself a choice. Somebody decided to weight every possible world the same. What you actually need, I started calling the Lumpiness Requirement: induction pays off only if the world is lumpy, meaning some possible worlds are more likely than others, meaning it wasn't picked by a maximally fair shuffle. And every supposed free lunch I've seen people offer is just smuggling lumpiness back in — a bias toward simple worlds, or smooth ones, or computable ones.
(5) So the theorem isn't a skepticism result at all. It says: if nothing is more likely than anything else, then nothing is more likely than anything else. Nobody denies that, and nobody needs a theorem to tell them.
(6) Except the Lumpiness Requirement can't be justified either. Any evidence you'd offer for it has already been read through some inductive method, and (2) and (3) just told me no method is better than any other. So it's circular, or it's an assumption I'm making for no reason.
(7) So the argument eats itself. The conclusion — that no assumption about the world can be supported by evidence — rests on an assumption about the world that, by its own standard, can't be supported by evidence. So either the conclusion goes down with the assumption, or the conclusion stands and then the no-free-lunch argument has accidentally handed induction the exact foothold it was supposed to take away, since at least one assumption turned out to matter enormously.
