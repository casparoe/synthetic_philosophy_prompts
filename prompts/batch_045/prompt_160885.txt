I am convening a one-day colloquium in Oxford on the question of whether Buddhist ethics should be classified as a form of consequentialism, and the two invited speakers have agreed to close the day by signing a joint statement. One of them defends a karuṇā reading, on which the centrality of compassion, the aim of removing dukkha, and the apparent absence of side-constraints make Buddhist ethics a welfare-maximising view; the other defends a bhāvanā reading, on which Buddhist ethics is fundamentally about the transformation of the agent through the perfections and the training precepts, and so belongs with virtue-ethical or character-based theories rather than with consequentialism. They have been arguing past one another in print for a decade. Please draft the statement they could both sign.

It should have four parts. First, a numbered section of claims both can genuinely sign: about the diversity of the traditions, about the roles of intention and of karmic fruit, about the status and function of the precepts, about what the compassionate-transgression narratives in the bodhisattva literature do and do not show, and about what would count as classifying Buddhist ethics at all. Second, a table of the residual disagreements, each disagreement stated twice — once in wording the karuṇā side would accept for its own position and once in wording the bhāvanā side would accept for its own — with no sentence either would dismiss as a caricature. Third, the cruxes: the narrower sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, including the two-channel question of whether an act's karmic fruit to the agent itself belongs in the evaluation of the act alongside its effects on others, and the epistemic-standing proviso, that is, whether narratives in which an advanced bodhisattva with knowledge of other minds breaks a precept supply any reason at all to an agent lacking that standing. Fourth, for each crux, the case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance.

Three materials are to be treated as stipulated for the exercise, so nothing needs looking up.

The relic case: a custodian monk discovers that the relic venerated in his village is a forgery. Assume it is established that the villagers' practice, sustained by their belief in the relic, produces a large and durable reduction in their craving and in their harmful conduct, and that exposing the forgery would collapse that practice and reverse those gains. The monk may maintain the deception or disclose it. Both speakers accept that the case bears on their dispute; record what each says about it and why.

The isolated awakening: suppose a being's awakening is stipulated to have no downstream effects on any other being — no teaching, no example, no relics, no one ever learns of it. Both speakers accept that this case bears on whether the cessation of suffering exhausts what is finally good, or whether awakening carries value that is not welfare value.

The longitudinal pattern: assume a twenty-year study of Theravāda forest-monastery practitioners finds that deepening concentration predicts both reduced sensitivity to outcome-magnitude in hypothetical dilemmas and increased unwillingness to breach a precept even where breaching it would help others, and that the pattern holds in every monastery studied. Each side should record, before the fact, which result would have moved it and why.

Where you conclude that part of the dispute is merely verbal — about the word consequentialism, about whether the theory is one of right action or of the good, or about what counts as Buddhist ethics — say which part and what reformulation would dissolve it. Where you cannot find a formulation both would sign, say so rather than smoothing it over. Write in British English, in about 900 to 1,200 words, and build the statement out of argument and cases alone: no citations, no reading lists, no appeals to particular texts or to disputed scholarship.
