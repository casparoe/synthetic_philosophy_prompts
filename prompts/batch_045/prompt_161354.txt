I'm a science journalist and I'm working on a piece on the replication mess in psychology and the med-ish fields, and every statistician I interview reaches for the same analogy. Roughly: a p-value is a smoke alarm. It's built so that it goes off 5% of the time when there's no fire; when it goes off you don't conclude there is a fire, you go and check; and how much you should believe there's a fire depends on how often fires actually start in that building. Everyone seems to like the picture and then use it to arrive at opposite places.

Camp A — the "the tools are fine, the users aren't" people — says the crisis is just badly wired alarms: people fiddled with the wiring until it shrieked, installed twenty alarms and reported the one that went off, and never mentioned the buildings where nothing happened. Preregister the wiring, report every alarm, make the detector more sensitive, and the instrument is fine.

Camp B says: look, the alarm gives you the probability of the alarm given no fire, not the probability of a fire given the alarm, and the whole lesson of the replication crisis is that we had no idea what fraction of hypotheses in these fields are true to begin with. Without that base rate you can't get from "p < .05" to "probably real", and the only honest way to import the background information is a prior — so we should be doing Bayes factors and estimation, not null hypothesis tests.

Then a third guy told me something I havent been able to shake: that the replication crisis actually hands you the base rate, because you can run the numbers backwards from the share of findings that replicate and get an estimate of how often hypotheses in a field are true in the first place — which (he said) means you can keep the smoke alarm and bolt the Bayesian calculation onto it afterwards. I have no idea whether that's a real thing or whether he invented it on the phone.

So, can you audit the analogy for me? I want the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the alarm, of the fire, of the false-alarm rate, of the base rate of fires, of the building, of whoever installed the thing, of the person who decides whether to report it. Then where it breaks. Things I'm specifically unsure about:

- Does the 5% false-alarm rate stay 5% once the person who installed the alarm got to choose which alarm to install after seeing where the smoke was? (I think this is p-hacking and the garden of forking paths, but I want it said in the analogy's own terms.)
- The picture seems to assume one building with a knowable fire rate. Is "the base rate of true hypotheses in a field" the kind of thing that's even a fact about the world, or is it a modeling assumption wearing a fact costume?
- What is the replication itself in this picture? Does a failed replication correspond to "no fire", or to "the alarm went off twice and only one of those times was there a fire", or something else entirely?
- The silent case: in the analogy, no alarm suggests no fire, but everyone tells me a non-significant result from an underpowered study tells you nothing at all. What plays the role of power — the sensitivity of the detector? And what happens to the base-rate point when the detector is mostly deaf?
- And what does the analogy quietly import that the actual situation doesn't have? My guess is something like: alarms are physical devices with a fixed, testable false-alarm rate, fires are the same kind of thing in every building, and nobody installed the alarm because they wanted it to go off.

Then the part I actually need for the piece: does either camp's conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying both of them? And if there's a better analogy — for what a p-value is doing, or for what a replication is doing — give me one I could put in front of readers without a statistician emailing me about it.
