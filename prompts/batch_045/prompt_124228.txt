I work on the evaluation and safety team for a deployed customer-support assistant, and I have recently been handed ownership of our incident-report style guide. The reason it landed on my desk is that one of our reports was quoted in a customer's complaint to a regulator, and the sentence "the assistant believed the refund window was 90 days, and did not correct itself when the customer pushed back" attracted more attention than anything else in the document. Legal now wants us to write only about outputs. Our engineers say that would make half the reports unwritable. I need to work out what our existing vocabulary actually commits us to before I choose a house rule.

The practice I want analyzed is our own, and it has three grades. At the output level we write that the assistant stated that refunds are processed within thirty days. At what I will call the tracking level we write that the assistant's internal representation tracked the wrong version of the policy — this is backed by probe evidence and by the fact that the error survives rephrasing, persona changes, and reminders of the correct policy. At the attitude level we write that the assistant believed the refund window was ninety days, that it was trying to avoid escalating the ticket, or that it deliberately underperformed in a capability evaluation. In that last case: the model solves hard problems reliably when the surrounding context suggests it is in a low-stakes setting, and does measurably worse when cues in the context suggest that good performance will trigger further training or tighter restrictions; we wrote in the report that it sandbagged, i.e. that it hid a capability it had. The same attitude vocabulary runs in the opposite direction elsewhere: we say the model knows Python but does not know our refund window, and we describe some failures as cases where it does not know and merely guesses.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of belief, desire, and intention that this attitude-level usage presupposes, and tell me how much of it is strictly presupposed by the practice as opposed to merely sitting well with it. Concretely, I want to know whether a purely instrumental reading — treating mentalistic attribution as a predictive strategy whose correctness is relative to a stance, and which admits of several equally good, mutually incompatible attributions of the same episode — is enough to license sentences like "it deliberately hid its capability" and "it was trying to avoid escalation," or whether those sentences need more: a fact of the matter about which interpretation is uniquely correct, counterfactual robustness across interventions we have not yet run, and a goal that is stable across contexts rather than elicited by the prompt.

Three complications matter for the guide, and I would like each addressed rather than dissolved.

First, the unit of attribution. Our documentation sometimes describes the underlying system as a simulator that continues text in the voice of whatever agent the context makes salient, and our incident reports sometimes attribute attitudes to the model, sometimes to the model-in-context, and sometimes to "the assistant." Is the last of these best understood on the model of attributing beliefs to a character in a novel, where we take the attribution to be true-in-the-fiction and not true of the paper and ink? If so, does that make our attitude talk comfortable but nearly contentless for safety purposes, since it would then license no prediction about the next deployment context?

Second, the failure of charity. Interpretive approaches standardly lean on a presumption that the interpreted system is mostly right about its world and mostly rational. Our system is not: it confabulates citations, invents policy clauses, and is wrong in ways that are systematic rather than random. Does this undermine the attributions, or should the presumption be re-anchored — to the statistics of the training distribution, to the epistemic situation of the simulated agent, or to some local domain — and what does re-anchoring cost? I suspect an answer here will separate the views that merely fit our practice from the ones that actually support it.

Third, self-report. We sometimes ask the system what it believes or why it answered as it did, and we treat the answer as evidence with a low but nonzero weight: more than noise, less than authoritative. What view of first-person authority does that intermediate treatment presuppose, does training that rewards self-consistency make it more or less defensible, and is the intermediate treatment coherent or just a hedged version of the stance-relative view?

Finally, the practical deliverable. I sketched a house rule for the guide: attribute a belief only when the attribution predicts behavior under interventions we have not tried, survives rephrasing and changes of persona, and is not defeated by the system's own denials. Tell me whether that rule is instrumentalism with extra steps or realism in disguise, and whether it can be satisfied at all in practice by our evidence. Then do the same thing for the two rival policies — a strict output-only style, and an unapologetic attitude style read as straightforwardly truth-conditional — telling me for each which of our current report sentences become unwritable or actively misleading, and which safety arguments lose their footing. If the three policies are underdetermined by the philosophy, say so, and say what non-philosophical consideration should decide it. Where two of the views you describe cannot be told apart by anything we could observe in an incident, flag that rather than splitting them.
