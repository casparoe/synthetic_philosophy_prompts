hey — I'm a PhD student working on the new riddle of induction, and I'm at the point where I've read enough of the literature to be annoyed by it. Every paper I pick up scores theories of projectibility against a slightly different, usually unstated, list of demands, and the scorecards dont line up because the lists dont. So before I write my own chapter: give me the specification first. What does an account of projectibility have to deliver to count as adequate — where the purpose I care about is this: it should tell a finite inquirer, with the evidence actually in hand, which generalisations to bet on, and do it without quietly assuming the answer. Good enough to underwrite scientific induction by agents like us, not just to characterise an ideal limit.

For each requirement I want the case or consideration behind it, and I want them sorted into non-negotiable vs. camp-preference. Here's the data I think any such account has to handle, plus a few I made up myself because I got bored of emeralds: (1) the standard case — grue, i.e. examined before 2035 and green, or not examined by then and blue. All our evidence is grue-evidence and also green-evidence, so the asymmetry has to be derived, not stipulated. (2) ordinal grue: same construction, but the cutoff isn't a time, it's a position in the order in which we happen to examine the objects (the first thousand examined are green iff ..., the rest blue iff ...). If a theory solves the riddle by appealing to something special about time, it should break here — and tell me whether that's a fair test or a cheap shot. (3) the ML analogue I keep coming back to: a predicate defined so that it tracks green on the training split and blue on the held-out split. Whatever you say about grue should say the same thing here; if it doesn't, that's the interesting bit — is the difference between the two cases doing any work, or is one just the other in different clothes? (4) genuinely time- or stage-relative predicates that are perfectly projectible (phosphorescence, caterpillar-then-butterfly), so the theory can't just ban time-indexed vocabulary. (5) revision in both directions: entrenched kind terms that later split into two (the way jade turned out to be two different minerals), and apparently distinct kinds that later merged into one — so projectibility has to be revisable, and the story about entrenchment has to leave room for a newly introduced theoretical predicate to beat an entrenched observational one, which happens all the time in science. (6) grue-ification is closed under nothing: grue is built out of green and blue, both projectible, so the account has to say what happens to projectibility under definition and under Boolean operations. (7) non-circularity: if entrenchment, or a prior, or a simplicity metric, is cashed out in terms of past successful projection, that looks like it already presupposes projectibility — genuine regress, or a harmless fixed point? (8) the grue-speaking community, one that entrenched grue from the start: an account that merely relativises to whatever a community happens to have projected has, I think, stopped answering my question, but say whether that's a real desideratum or just my prejudice. (9) it can't be sceptical: an account on which nothing is ever projectible, or on which the new riddle is just the old Humean problem in costume, hasn't given me what I asked for.

Two things I especially want. First: which of these (and which others you'd add) are genuine desiderata and which are the prejudices of one camp? My hunch is that "must be language-independent / must track the joints of nature" is a metaphysical-naturalness prejudice, and "must be computable, or at least tractable for a bounded agent" is a Bayesian/learning-theoretic one — but maybe both are fine and I'm confused. Second: I suspect some subset is jointly unsatisfiable. My guess is that you can't have an asymmetry that is fully derived from non-question-begging materials and also an account that is neutral between languages or coordinate systems, and that this is the real content of the new riddle rather than a problem for any particular theory. Tell me if that's right; if it isn't, give me the actual unsatisfiable pair and why. A short scorecard at the end — Goodmanian entrenchment, the naturalness/sparse-properties line, Carnap-style language-relative confirmation functions, simplicity-prior and learning-theoretic views including Solomonoff-style ones, and falsificationist attempts to dodge the question — is fine, but keep it brief; the criteria are where I want the thinking. British English please, and end with \answer{jointly satisfiable: yes/no; most exclusionary requirement: <short phrase>}.
