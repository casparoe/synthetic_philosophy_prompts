QUESTION:

The "thinness objection" to the fine-tuning argument runs like this. Suppose the constants had been set by a designer who wanted embodied moral agents. Then you would expect the life-permitting range to be broad — or life not to depend on the values of a few free parameters at all — since a designer who wanted life would presumably want it robust. The actual window is razor-thin. So the thinness of the window is itself evidence against the design hypothesis, and not merely against single-universe naturalism.

Assess this objection. In your answer: (i) state exactly what you take the evidence E to be, and say why your formulation matters; (ii) say whether the objection bears on the prior probability of theism or on the likelihood of E given theism; (iii) say whether a many-universes hypothesis is in a better or a worse position than theism with respect to thinness.

STUDENT ANSWER:

Start with the evidence, because how we state it matters. If we say "some universe or other is life-permitting," then any many-universes theory predicts E with probability 1 and the comparison is rigged from the start. The right formulation is "this universe, the one we're in, is life-permitting" — that's the thing the single-universe theorist owes us an account of, and it's the version I'll use.

The thinness objection is really an objection to the prior, not to the likelihood. A designer who wanted moral agents would have made life robust, so a universe balanced on a knife-edge is not what theism predicts going in. The objection therefore lowers the prior probability of theism. And since the likelihood ratio was never more than modest anyway — even defenders concede that P(E|theism) can't be 1, since a designer might have wanted any number of things — knocking down the prior is enough to sink the argument.

The many-universes reply has troubles of its own, but they're seperate. It needs a measure over the space of constants: you can't just say that every value is realised somewhere and then read a probability off that, and the usual complaint is that any measure you choose is either arbitrary or ends up predicting that most observers are Boltzmann brains. That's a problem about the measure, not about thinness.

Where the many-universes reply actually fails is elsewhere. It has to lean on the point that we could not have observed a non-life-permitting universe, so of course we find ourselves in one of the life-permitting ones. But that is just the inverse gambler's fallacy run in reverse: inferring the long series of throws from the single double-six we happen to observe. The theorist uses our one observation to posit the many trials, then uses the many trials to explain the one observation. That's circular, so the reply undermines itself. Thinness tells equally against the many-universes theorist: a multiverse almost all of whose universes are dead is no better off than a single dead universe plus one lucky one.

So the thinness objection works, and it leaves the single-universe naturalist in the best shape of anyone. She never predicted a wide window, so thinness costs her nothing, and all she owes us is the brute fact that this universe permits life.
