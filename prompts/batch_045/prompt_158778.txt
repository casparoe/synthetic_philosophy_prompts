Me and two mates got into this in the pub a few weeks back and it's been rattling round my head ever since, so I thought I'd just ask. I said what makes pain bad is the way it actually feels — the horrible sensory quality itself — and my friend said no, the feeling only matters because of the part where you mind it, the wanting-it-to-stop bit, and that if you took the minding away the feeling left over wouldn't be bad at all. Then someone brought up asymbolia: apparently there are people, usually after some kind of brain damage or surgery, who can tell you the exact strength and location of a painful stimulus, and still flinch or pull their hand away, but say the pain doesn't bother them one bit. I've obviously never met anyone like that and I only half remember reading about it, so let's just assume it's a real thing for the sake of the argument — I'm not after references or studies. To push it further I made up two cases of my own: in the first, call it "indifference", a pill leaves the raw felt intensity exactly as it was but removes the caring completely; in the second, call it "reverse asymbolia", someone reports no pain sensation whatsoever while insisting, apparently sincerely and with real urgency, that something is awful and must be stopped immediately.

So here's the exact claim I want a number on: the badness of a pain for the person having it is fixed entirely by how much they dislike or mind it, and the felt sensory intensity adds nothing to how bad it is beyond that. Please give me your credence that this is true, decomposed rather than just a bare figure — don't just tell me it depends which theory of wellbeing you like. I'd like a rough probability on each piece: whether the asymbolic reports are really the plain dissociation they sound like rather than a shift in how those patients use pain words or a general emotional flattening; whether, granting the reports, the case actually shows badness tracks dislike rather than badness being disjunctive (sensory quality mattering when it's minded and nothing mattering when it isn't); whether my reverse-asymbolia case is even coherent and, if it is, which way it cuts; and whether "bad for someone" can come apart from what they care about at all. Then how the numbers combine, which single one the final figure is most sensitive to, and what one piece of evidence or argument would move it the most. My own guess is around 0.55 and my mate is at about 0.15, so if you can say where you think that gap really lives that'd be a bonus. Reply as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no intro and no signing off, and in British English please.
