I am writing a novel about a senior civil servant — deputy director of a fictional Office of National Outcomes in a middling, not especially wealthy republic — who has one legislative session to recommend a single national wellbeing statistic. Under the statute my draft calls the Wellbeing Mandate Act, that number will become the formal target of government: budget allocations, ministerial promotions, and the annual State of the Nation address will all turn on whether it rises. I want the argument in her committee room to be a real argument rather than decoration, so before I write it I need the specification: what would an index of a population's happiness have to get right to be an honest thing to govern by, as opposed to merely an honest thing to study?

Please write that desiderata list, with the case or consideration behind each requirement — the concrete situation that shows why the requirement is there, not just the abstract principle. I want it to cover at least three fronts. On measurement: whose experience is in the denominator (newborns, people with advanced dementia, long-stay hospital patients, emigrants, the recently dead, the future); whether the number should be built from real-time reports of experience or from retrospective judgement about a life as a whole, and what is lost by each; whether one person's eight can be assumed to equal another person's eight, and what happens to the total if it cannot; how to treat a population that has quietly re-calibrated its expectations downwards after a long decline, and how to treat one that reports high satisfaction on the basis of forecasts about its own feelings that are systematically wrong; whether positive and negative experience may be netted against each other, and whether a bad hour can be compensated by good years; and how much is lost in translating a question about a good life across languages and moral traditions. On aggregation: average versus spread versus a sufficiency threshold — whether the index must be capable of saying that a country improved when the mean rose because the worst-off became somewhat less miserable, and whether it must be capable of saying the opposite. On the political use: legibility to citizens and to parliament versus resistance to gaming; whether the number should be stable enough to reward long policies; and whether making it a statutory target damages the very thing it measures, in the way a target tends to damage any measure it is attached to — in which case say whether that argues for a deliberately composite and somewhat opaque index, for several published numbers and no target at all, or for abandoning the project.

Then do two more things. First, mark which of the requirements are genuine desiderata that any adequate index must meet and which are really the prejudices of one camp — the demand that the result be a single cardinal quantity, the demand that it track people's own reports, the demand that it be about felt experience rather than about meaning, capability, or the satisfaction of considered preferences — and identify any pair of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied, since the fact that the list is internally unsatisfiable is itself something my committee should be arguing about. Say which single requirement excludes the largest number of candidate indices. Second, briefly score the three broad families of candidate — self-report life satisfaction surveys, experience-sampling or affect-based measures, and objective-list or capability-based proxies — but keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scoring, since the criteria are what I need to stage the debate. Finally, tell me what the committee ought to recommend if the honest conclusion is that no candidate meets the non-negotiable requirements, and why; that decision is the ending of my novel, and I would like it to follow from the specification rather than from my own sympathies.
