In a graduate reading group on internal versus external reasons, one member keeps leaning on a single analogy, and it is quietly doing most of the work: a reason, he says, is like a place you can get to from where you already are. Your motivational set is your position on the map, sound deliberation is the network of footpaths leading away from it, and S has a reason to φ means only that φ-ing is reachable from S's position by some path. External reasons then come out as places printed on the map with no path to them — and that is what is supposed to make them idle: a consideration that is genuinely there for the agent but unreachable by any deliberative route looks unable to guide action, explain action, or underwrite blame.

Please audit the analogy. Give an explicit correspondence table: what plays the terrain, the map, the destination, and the traveller — and, the bit I suspect is doing hidden work, what it means for a path to appear only after the terrain itself is redrawn, since the metaphor seems to conflate changing your starting point with changing the landscape, and thereby slides between a weaker and a stronger internalism. Then sort the disanalogies into those that matter for the use we are making of the analogy and those that do not: reasons are considerations favouring actions rather than destinations to arrive at; the map presupposes a single shared normative space in the first place; following a path is not the same activity as making one; failure of reach gets pictured as a bare geographic fact when the relevant could reach is itself counterfactual and already normative; and the picture has nothing to say about whether an unreachable place becomes reachable once the agent acquires new equipment, such as a corrected belief, an act of imagination, or a shift of attention or mood.

What I want to know is whether the conclusion the analogy was carrying survives: that external reasons are motivationally and explanatorily inert, so internalism wins by default against the amoralist. If it does not survive, propose a better analogy for what the externalist is actually claiming.

Give me a table of no more than ten rows, then at most 400 words of prose, in the order: mapping, disanalogies that matter, disanalogies that do not, verdict.
