I have spent the last two years building a clinical prediction model, and a colleague has recently persuaded me that our leading variable is, structurally, Goodman's grue: it fits every dataset we hold, and the datasets differ from next year's data in a way that might matter. I now have to explain to a steering committee why "it has held in every study so far" is not yet a reason to expect it to hold afterwards. The emeralds are no use for this. Green versus grue is rigged: green is entrenched, grue is not, so Goodman's own entrenchment answer disposes of the case before the interesting part begins.

So I want a grue-style pair built to order, in a realistic contemporary setting — a lab, a hospital, an engineering test programme, not a jewel box — meeting four constraints. Both predicates must be equally entrenched in the relevant vocabulary, so the tie cannot be broken by familiarity. Neither may be defined by a date, a year, or any indexical; the divergence must run along some other dimension. All the evidence available now must lie on one side of the divergence point and must fit both projections equally, with the divergence somewhere we could actually reach within a few years. And there should nevertheless be a clear verdict about which way to project, so that the intuition survives the removal of the entrenchment asymmetry.

Then show that it works. State the evidence base, the two predicates, and the competing predictions; check explicitly that entrenchment really has been neutralised; and say which of the remaining moves — mechanism-level overhypotheses, appeals to carving nature at the joints, simplicity, or a sceptical retreat — is now forced to do the work, and how well it does it.

The case is for deciding whether entrenchment was doing all the labour in Goodman's solution or whether something about the world was doing some of it. Please don't settle matters by stipulating that one predicate is more natural or better entrenched, since that is precisely what is under test; flag it if you catch yourself relying on it anyway. Close with a short note on where your construction is most likely to fail: what would persuade you that the tie is broken after all, that no such pair can be built, or that the whole problem dissolves once entrenchment is out of the picture.
