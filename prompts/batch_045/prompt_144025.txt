hey — picture yourself as a philosopher of religion who also does comedy writing on the side, and take a minute to brainstorm the shape of the thing before you start drafting anything.

I put together a small philosophy-and-literature zine and want a fake advice column for the next issue: "Ask the Grammarian", an agony-aunt page of about 600-700 words where four readers write in about God, faith and doubt, and the replies come from a completely straight-faced, strict Wittgensteinian about religious belief — the grammatical-remark / "don't think, look" / hinge-proposition end of the reading, not the wince-y "it's all just a language game" version. Four letters, four replies, invented comic sign-offs.

The letters: an undergrad who thinks the evidentialist case (divine hiddenness, suffering, the dodgy provenance of testimony) has refuted her faith and wants to know whether she should stop believing; a guy who's discovered hinge propositions and now uses "that's a hinge" to win every disagreement, including about whether his flatmate ate his yoghurt and about the church treasurer who keeps saying "we don't count the collection, we trust"; a woman whose prayer life has gone completely dry and who wants to know whether her faith was ever anything more than habit; and an atheist neighbour who just asks, plainly, whether God exists, yes or no.

The comedy has to come from applying the method seriously rather than from inventing a silly version of it, so some rules: the adviser never says religious claims are non-cognitive, or useful fictions, or "a different language game", and never treats them as empirical hypotheses either — they keep declining the frame while making it obvious the letters matter enormously. No character should ever name the standard objections (that this is just fideism, that the method can't tell a living faith from a dead one, that it couldn't criticise a cult); those should turn up as things the column conspicuously fails to do. If you think it lands hardest that way, the final reply may break character slightly — but make the break legible, don't let it slide past.
