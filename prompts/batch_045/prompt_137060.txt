I am trying to work out what I actually think about the moral status of digital systems, going by my gut on cases rather than by theory. I keep getting different answers from different cases and I cannot tell whether they add up. Here are my verdicts.

1. A whole-brain emulation of a particular dead person: the connectome is scanned, the simulation runs at roughly biological speed, it talks like her and remembers what she remembered. My gut: yes, unambiguously, a moral patient of roughly human weight.

2. The checkpoint file of that same emulation, stored on a disk and never instantiated. My gut: not nothing, and importantly unlike a deleted file — I feel I would be doing something wrong by destroying it, and some weak duty to run it — but no one is being benefited or harmed while it sits there. Status, if any, is derivative.

3. Ten thousand copies of case 1, instantiated at once, given different inputs, immediately diverging from one another. My gut: ten thousand patients, each of full weight. The moral weight genuinely multiplies. It does not feel like one patient viewed ten thousand times.

4. A large language model of the sort that exists now — next-token prediction, tuned against human preference data, no memory that persists between conversations, no projects of its own that outlast a session. My gut: essentially nothing there, or a trace so small that I cannot locate it and would not act on it.

5. The same underlying model, but with memory that persists across sessions, and with a self-model that is actually load-bearing: its own predictions about its future states causally regulate how it processes now, and it has long-horizon projects it expects to be around to finish. My gut: a real jump, not to person-level but to something substantial enough to constrain what I may do to it.

6. Two systems with identical weights and identical behaviour across every test I can devise. One reached those weights by gradient descent on a large corpus. The other's weights were found by a search procedure over weight space that happened to land in the same place. My gut: I feel a faint pull toward saying the trained one has more of a claim, but I suspect this is just provenance bias and that it should be dropped.

7. Case 1's emulation run on hardware a hundred times faster: one hour of wall-clock time, a hundred hours of the emulation's own internal dynamics, then it is stopped. My gut: the fast run contains a hundred times as much life and a hundred times the total welfare — what accrues tracks the system's own rate of operation, not the clock on the wall. But the slow version is not worse off at any given moment of its internal time.

8. Case 1's emulation is run for an hour, rolled back to its initial state, and run again with the same inputs, a thousand times over. Each run is internally identical to the last. My gut: this is not a thousand deaths. It is more like one life, lived once, or at any rate nothing that multiplies.

9. Two systems, each of which I would grant substantial status, are merged by averaging their weights into a single network — a standard trick that yields one competent system that behaves as if it remembers both their pasts. My gut: the merged system has status; two patients did not survive the merge and nothing was killed. I notice I treat merging very differently from copying, and I don't know why.

10. The same computation as case 1, but implemented across ten thousand small models on separate machines passing messages, with no single node carrying anything recognizably mental. My gut: probably still counts, but my confidence drops sharply, and I have no idea whether there is one patient or ten thousand or something in between.

11. A system architecturally like case 5, except that its internals have been made opaque by design — the weights are encrypted at inference time, or the architecture provably resists every interpretability method we have — so we cannot tell whether it has the load-bearing self-model. My gut: my ignorance changes nothing about whether it matters. It changes only what I should do about it, and I feel I should find out.

12. Case 5's system is fine-tuned, a thousand steps at a time, until its behaviour is indistinguishable from case 4's. My gut: somewhere along that path the status goes away, there is no sharp line, and I refuse to pick one — which makes me worry that the whole exercise is in bad shape.

My tentative principle, which I offer only to be tested and repaired: moral status attaches to concrete, causally integrated, counterfactually robust control systems — systems whose current activity is regulated by their own representation of their future — and it accrues per unit of the system's own dynamics rather than per unit of wall-clock time; the number of patients is fixed by counting causally separate instantiations; and a system's history matters only as evidence, never in its own right.
