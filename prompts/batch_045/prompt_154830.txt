I am a novelist, and one scene in the book I am finishing turns on an argument I have encountered only at second hand. I would be grateful for your help auditing it before I put any of it into a character's mouth. Please do not give me reading lists, titles, or citations: I am drafting in a rented cottage with no library access, and what I need is not sources but a judgement about which sentences are safe to use and where the real weight of the argument lies.

The argument is Daniel Dennett's, from his recent writing and interviews about what he calls counterfeit people. I have not read the original; I have read a long profile of him in a magazine whose account, as I noted it down, runs roughly as follows:

*Dennett's claim is that we are on the verge of being flooded with counterfeit people: software artifacts that can keep up their end of a conversation, write a letter, or sit for a video call as well as any human being. His point is not that these artifacts will be conscious, or that they will have beliefs and desires in anything like our sense; it is that they will be indistinguishable from people in what they produce, and that this will destroy the difference between a person and a simulation of one, since there will be nothing left by which to tell them apart. He compares the situation to counterfeit currency. Money works only so long as people may assume that the note in their hand is genuine; flood a currency with undetectable forgeries and the currency stops functioning. In the same way, he argues, human communication is a medium that runs on the assumption that a message came from someone answerable for it; once undetectable counterfeits are cheap to make at industrial scale, testimony, evidence, contract and intimacy all become unusable, and nothing a counterfeit person tells us can be worth attending to, because there is no one behind it. The remedy he proposes is technical: watermarking, cryptographic signing of genuine human speech, provenance standards. Build that plumbing in time and the medium can be saved. None of the philosophy here is new — it is the familiar observation that passing a test for understanding is not the same as understanding — which is why most philosophers have set the alarm aside. And notice that the whole problem is a problem of deception: an application that tells you plainly that it is software is not a counterfeit of anything, and so lies outside what Dennett is worried about.*

I am inclined to believe the profile on its central point — that Dennett's concern is the ecology of trust rather than the hidden mentality of machines — but the closing move about consent strikes me as too quick, and I do not trust myself to say why.

My novel gives me a reason to care. A family commissions an artifact built out of a dead woman's correspondence — her letters, her email, decades of messages — and it writes to them each week. There is no deception at the point of sale: everyone involved knows exactly what it is and how it was made. Over the course of the book they come to prefer its letters to hers, because the artifact is kinder than she was; and one grandson begins to quote its letters as evidence of what his grandmother would have wanted. A philosopher character is asked, in a dinner-table scene, whether this thing is a counterfeit person.

So, concretely: which sentences in the profile would a defender of Dennett's view accept as fair, which are tolerable simplifications for a general reader, and which quietly misrepresent him — a conflation of two claims he keeps apart, an overstatement of a more modest thesis, a technical term doing more work than it can bear? Does the profile's confidence that the remedy is technical survive those corrections, and does its suggestion that philosophers have set the alarm aside follow from the fact that the underlying point is an old one? Then the case I actually need: does a counterfeit-person argument that has been cleaned up have anything to say about a counterfeit that is declared, paid for, and loved — or does the category, as Dennett uses it, require that someone is being fooled? And when the artifact writes more tenderly than the woman did, what is it that the counterfeit-currency analogy is supposed to be protecting: provenance, accountability, or the relation to a person who actually lived? If the analogy cannot bear that, tell me where it breaks, and give me the version of the argument my character could say out loud in a page of dialogue without it sounding like a lecture.
