**Question 3 (answer all parts)**

In Chapter VI of *Gödel, Escher, Bach* ('The Location of Meaning'), Hofstadter argues that the meaning of a message is not intrinsic to the message-bearing object, and he analyses a message into three layers: the frame message, the outer message and the inner message.

(a) State the three-layer distinction and explain, in your own words, what conclusion about the location of meaning Hofstadter draws from it. Illustrate with one example of your own choosing. [20]

(b) Hofstadter holds that the frame message must be in some sense robust: a receiver has to be able to grasp it before, and independently of, knowing how to decode the object. Explain what this requirement comes to, and why it matters. Then consider the following objection: recognising that something is a message is itself an act of interpretation, so the three-layer account threatens to generate a regress — a frame message for the frame message, and so on indefinitely. Does the account have the resources to stop this regress? [20]

(c) A research group releases a trained language model as a bare array of bytes W: no source code, no architecture specification, no tokenizer, no documentation. Some commentators — call them 'weight-realists' — claim that W contains the model's knowledge in something like the way the grooves of a phonograph record contain the music.

Apply the three-layer distinction to W. What, if anything, plays the role of the frame message, of the outer message, and of the inner message?

Now consider the following objection to weight-realism. W yields behaviour only relative to an interpreter: a specific architecture, a tokenizer, a numeric format and a tensor layout, plus inference-time machinery such as a decoding strategy and a temperature setting. And since the interpreter can be varied while W is held fixed — greedy decoding or nucleus sampling, a low or a high temperature — the same W supports many different behavioural profiles. So, the objection runs, the knowledge is not located in W at all but in W together with an interpreter; and because the interpreter is not unique, there is no determinate inner message to be had.

Assess this objection using Hofstadter's framework. You should be careful to distinguish three claims that are easily confused, and to say which of them the framework actually supports: (i) that the inner message is not intrinsic to the object, since it depends on the interpreter; (ii) that the inner message is not uniquely determined by the object, since many decodings are possible; (iii) that the object makes no contribution to what is meant, since its structure does not constrain which decodings are admissible.

Finally, one member of the group replies as follows: very well, the inner message of W is not a set of propositions but a disposition — a counterfactual-supporting pattern of behaviour relative to a specified range of stimulus conditions. Dispositions are perfectly respectable properties of objects, so the objection does not show that W contains nothing. Does this reply succeed? Does it rescue anything that deserves to be called intrinsic meaning? [60]

**Submitted answer**

*(a)* Hofstadter's question is whether a message carries its meaning inside it, as a box carries its contents, or whether meaning has to be supplied from outside. His answer is the latter. He breaks a message into three layers.

The frame message is the signal that there is a message at all — the 'I am a message, decode me if you can' component. It is, strictly speaking, the outermost part of the outer message, since what it does is tell you that the object is to be read rather than merely inspected, and the rest of the outer message tells you how to read it. The outer message is the decoding information: the code, the language, the format, the key. It is usually not shipped with the object; it has to be reconstructed from the object's own regularities, or it is simply assumed to be already present in the receiver. The inner message is the content: what the sender was trying to convey, what you arrive at once the correct decoding has been applied.

A phonograph record illustrates all three. The grooves are not random: their regular, spiral, repetitive structure is the frame message — one recognises that something here is meant to be decoded. The outer message is the information that this is a record, to be rotated at a given speed by a stylus-driven machine. The inner message is the music. Hofstadter's conclusion is that the music is not in the grooves as the contents are in the box: an intelligence or a mechanism capable of decoding is required, and the same grooves decoded differently — at the wrong speed, or by a machine that reads the pattern as data rather than as sound — yield something else. Meaning is therefore not intrinsic to the object.

*(b)* The frame message has to be robust because decoding has to get started somewhere. If one needed the code in order to know that there was a message to decode, one could never begin. So the frame message must be recoverable from the object's own manifest structure — its regularity, its symmetry, its evident manufacturedness — by a receiver who does not yet possess the outer message. This is why a record dispatched into deep space has to be physically anomalous: its shape has to announce 'message' before any of its content can be extracted.

The regress objection is not fatal. The regress stops because the frame message is carried by properties of the object that are not themselves messages: a groove is a groove, and a statistical regularity is a statistical regularity. Recognition of a frame is a response to the object's non-intentional physical structure, so it does not require a further decoding, and hence no further frame.

*(c)* Applying the distinction to W: the frame message is whatever makes the byte array recognisable as a trained artefact rather than as noise — a header, a magic number, or, failing that, the statistical signature of weights (distributions centred near zero, structured correlations) as opposed to random bytes. It is worth noting that a bare array without a header is a poor message: as with a record shipped without the diagram of its player, the frame may simply fail, and no receiver would know to attempt a decoding. That much is a point in the objector's favour.

The outer message is everything needed to turn W into behaviour: the architecture (number and width of layers, attention pattern, activation functions), the tokenisation scheme, the numeric format and endianness, and the mapping from positions in the file to tensors in the graph. The inner message is the function W computes — or, more loosely, the behavioural dispositions, and the knowledge, that users attribute to the model.

The objection is, I think, correct, and Hofstadter's framework supports it. Hofstadter's conclusion is that the object is a physical trigger and that the meaning is supplied entirely by whatever receives it; on that reading W contains nothing, and the knowledge attributed to the model belongs to the interpreter. The variability of the interpreter is decisive here. Hold W fixed and vary the decoding strategy, or the temperature, and one gets different behavioural profiles; nothing about W settles which of them is the real one. So W does not determine any inner message, and since what W determines is nothing, the weight-realist claim that the knowledge is in W is refuted. The three-layer account was designed precisely to show that an object does not dictate its own reading.

Nor does the dispositionalist reply help. A disposition is a property an object has only relative to a stimulus condition, and here the stimulus condition is supplied by the interpreter. Relocating the inner message from propositions to dispositions therefore changes nothing: it remains true that the object does not determine its own meaning. Weight-realism fails, and there is nothing in the neighbourhood that deserves to be called intrinsic meaning.
