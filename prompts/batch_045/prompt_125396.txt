I write a column on science and ideas, and I am midway through a piece on collective agency beyond the human case: automated benefits systems that deny claims, hospital triage pipelines, multi-agent software that divides a task among itself, large scientific collaborations whose papers carry thousands of names, trading desks where the models and the humans each do part of the work. Every philosophical account of joint action I have come across seems calibrated for dyads with a shared plan — two people painting a house, lifting a table, going for a walk together — and then goes quiet about the cases my reporting actually turns up. So I want the specification rather than another tour of the theories.

Give me the desiderata: what an account of shared or joint action has to deliver, for my purposes, to count as adequate. The purpose is this — I want to be able to say, defensibly, when an organization, a crowd, a market, or a sociotechnical system acts, and when it merely produces an outcome that no one intended, and I want to know who is on the hook when it acts badly. Build the list around the cases, and give the consideration behind each requirement.

Here are the cases from my own reporting that I want handled; tell me which of them generate distinct requirements and which I have misdescribed as hard.

One: the rubber stamp. A clinician is legally required to sign off on a model's triage recommendations, a couple of hundred a shift, and forms no view about any individual case. If the hospital's decisions are joint actions, who is acting with whom?

Two: the rotating collective. A volunteer moderation or maintenance group whose membership turns over almost completely within a month, none of whose members has met another, and no one of whom has any representation of the whole activity, yet which maintains a stable policy over years.

Three: the reluctant participant. A junior soldier, or a low-level official, who executes her part of a policy she believes to be wrong. Does doing one's part under authority count as sharing the intention, and what does the account say about the difference between her and the enthusiast one desk over?

Four: the constituted we. A crowd that becomes coordinated only once the police kettle it — a collective whose unity seems to be supplied by the treatment it receives from outside rather than by anything the participants intended or agreed to.

Five: the swarm. Coordinating units — delivery drones, bidding algorithms — that interact by local rules with no representation of any joint goal. Is there a threshold at which this stops being a mere aggregate, and on what does the threshold depend?

Six: the institutional first-person plural. A committee or court speaks in the we form and issues a conclusion while internally split. What has to be true for that we to be more than a stylistic convention, and who believes what when it is used?

Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp — parsimony, reducibility to individual attitudes, refusal of group minds, the insistence that joint action requires communication or mutual responsiveness, the demand for a categorical rather than a scalar notion — and say why you classify it where you do. I would also like you to flag the pairs or clusters of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied: my suspicion is that the pressure to cover the swarm and the rubber stamp (apparent agency with no shared representation anywhere in the system) pulls against the pressure to explain the normative binding between participants, and that a graded account and a categorical one cannot both be had. That part is what I most want to see, since if the list is internally unsatisfiable that is itself the finding.

Finally, name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidate accounts, and tell me in one short closing paragraph which of the three broad families — reduction to individual attitudes plus common knowledge, an irreducibly collective we-mode, and views that put obligation or joint commitment first — is hit hardest by it. Keep that to a paragraph; the criteria are what I am paying for. A table is fine. No citations or reading list — I can find the literature myself. What I cannot work out from the outside is which of the things these theories are asked to do is a genuine constraint and which is a tribal preference dressed up as one.
