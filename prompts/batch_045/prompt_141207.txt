My friend Priya and I got into it at her kitchen table last night about whether self-deception is even possible, and I woke up still wanting to win it, so here I am. The trigger was her brother: for about eight months he's been insisting the restaurant he owns is fine, while not opening the bank mail, asking his wife not to mention the runway in front of him, and getting weirdly irritable whenever anyone offers to sit down and look at the books with him. Priya says that's textbook self-deception. I say it isn't, and I also say self-deception in the strict sense is impossible: for the real thing you need one and the same person to be deceiver and deceived, and the intentional element is what breaks. If you're planning to get yourself to believe p, you have to be working from the knowledge that p is false, but succeeding means losing exactly the knowledge your plan ran on — so the plan destroys its own precondition. And if the idea is that you somehow hold both p and not-p at once, that's not deception, that's just fragmentation or a sub-personal gut state that isn't really a belief. Every case people point at, I say, is really one of three things: motivated sloppiness where the true belief never got formed in the first place, plain ignorance that the person happened to have a stake in landing on, or straight-up insincere avowal that's aimed as much at other people as at the mirror. Her brother is just a guy who has stopped looking at numbers, and the irritability is what it feels like from inside when someone threatens a policy of not looking.

Priya's answer, and this is the part I couldn't knock down, is that I'm demanding simultaneity I have no right to demand. On her version the deceiver and the deceived take shifts: at the start you do know, and what you do with that knowledge is adopt an executive policy rather than form a belief — you don't open that envelope, you don't ask that question, you steer the conversation, you schedule the scan and then never go — the policy does its work slowly, and by the time the belief has dissolved there's no one left at home who knows anything. So the intentional element rides on the policy, not on any occurrent proposition you could avow or disavow, and the "I knew all along" people say when it finally collapses is evidence that something tracked the truth the whole way through. She also pushed the split-competence case: someone who is a genuinely scrupulous handler of evidence in one area of life, who would never get away with that kind of sloppiness at work, and who reliably declines to deploy that same competence in another area — she says that shows the knowledge is present but quarantined, which is deception-shaped and not ignorance-shaped. My reply was that "I knew all along" is probably just reconstructive, and that a person can have a skill and fail to use it without thereby having the belief the skill would deliver.

So: I'm the one holding that self-deception is impossible, and I want you to try to take it apart. What would have to be true for me to be wrong, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and — importantly — which of those would a defender of my view concede as genuinely counting against it, as opposed to what only a critic would say? Be honest about whether I've qualified myself into a corner: if every apparent case can be redescribed as motivated sloppiness plus a policy of not looking, is my claim unfalsifiable, and if it is, is that fatal or is it fine for a claim of this kind? Say what my view predicts that Priya's doesn't — something we could in principle check about what happens at the moment of collapse, or about whether avoidance behaviour should come apart from the "knew all along" reporting, or about the split-competence cases — and which of those predictions I'd most plausibly lose on. One more thing I want your read on: there's a story where self-deception would be useful, because a person who doesn't know is a more convincing liar and a more credible commitment-maker; does that functional story explain away the appearance of self-deception and thereby help me, or does explaining why a mechanism would be selected for count as a reason to think the mechanism is real? And finally, tell me whether my argument actually establishes that the concept is incoherent, or only that it's coherent but never instantiated, or only that it's instantiated but never fully intentional — I have a sinking feeling I've been arguing for the first when I've only earned the third, and I'd rather find that out from you than from Priya. Please don't give me any citations, names, or a reading list — I'll go dig up the literature myself — I just want the structure of where this thing is exposed.
