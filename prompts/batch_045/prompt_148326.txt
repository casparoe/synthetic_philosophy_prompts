Hey, I'm TAing for a undergrad course on classical social theory and we just went through the Protestant ethic thesis, and I keep getting stuck on something that isn't really the empirical part of the debate. Please write the answer in American English.

What I can't get straight is what it even *means* for culture or for values to "be a cause" in a story like Weber's. The object-level arguments everyone rehearses — whether the correlation was even there, whether capitalism was already up and running in the northern Italian city states, whether it was Calvinism specifically or some broader ascetic strand doing the work — I'm happy to set all that aside. My confusion is more metaphysical. Weber obviously doesn't want to say the doctrine caused the modern economic order the way a spark causes a fire: he reaches for the move where ideas are like switchmen, setting the tracks along which interests supply the actual push, and he prefers talk of an elective affinity over straight causal language. But that looks to me like it might be a bait and switch. If the world-image only selects among routes, then it's channeling a process whose motive force comes from somewhere else, which seems like a fairly thin sense of "cause." And if the world-image also helps fix what counts as an interest in the first place — the person gripped by anxiety about his election wants something that arguably only exists inside that doctrinal scheme — then the whole thing starts to look circular: interests push, but the content and direction of the push is already specified by the idea, so it's not obvious we have two separable factors being related to each other at all. A friend in the econ dept I mentioned this to just said "run an instrument, lol", which I dont think even engages with the worry.

So here's the scheme I've been using to sort it out, which I made up and want you to pressure-test rather than just accept. Say a factor is *T-channeling* if it only selects among options whose motivational force comes from outside it. Say it's *S-stabilizing* if it doesn't initiate the behavior at all but makes some consequence of that behavior self-reinforcing, so a pattern that would otherwise peter out keeps reproducing itself — my sense is this is the right box for the unintended-consequence half of the thesis, where nobody was aiming at a capitalist order but the psychological premium on methodical worldly work made the pattern ratchet up over generations. And say it's *C-constitutive* if it partly determines what the agent's interest even is, so that you can't fully specify the interest without mentioning the value. My actual question: is the third of these — the constitutive one — a causal relation in any useful sense at all? And then walk me through what each answer costs. If you say no, does the thesis that culture matters collapse into something trivial, i.e. into a redescription of the action in value-laden vocabulary? If you say yes, are you then committed to values and reasons being causes in basically the same way billiard balls are, and what happens to the contrast with the interest-based explanation that the whole story was supposed to be trading on? One more thing you can take or leave: the "spirit of capitalism" in the thesis is an ideal type, a deliberately one-sided construct that no actual person instantiated — so add, if it's relevant, how something like a methodological fiction is supposed to show up inside a causal explanation at all. No citations or bibliography needed, I want conceptual analysis, and feel free to tell me the three-way scheme is bogus if it is.
