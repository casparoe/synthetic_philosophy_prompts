The script I have to mark (anonymised, submitted as a single paragraph set out in sections):

Epiphenomenalism is the thesis that phenomenal properties are causally inert with respect to the non-phenomenal: they are produced by physical processes but produce nothing outside the phenomenal domain. It is standardly motivated by two premises together — the causal closure of the physical, and a principle of causal exclusion that rules out the systematic overdetermination of physical effects by both physical and phenomenal causes. Given both, whatever causal work consciousness appeared to do is already done by its neural basis, and consciousness drops out as an idle wheel: the whistle on the engine, which makes a noise but drives nothing.

The paradox of phenomenal judgment is that this saws off the branch the view is sitting on. The case for taking phenomenal consciousness as something over and above the physical begins from our own case: I judge that I am in pain, and it is that judgment the physicalist is accused of being unable to accommodate. But if my being in pain causes nothing, then my judgment that I am in pain is not caused by my being in pain, and the same two premises apply to it: the judgment has a complete physical cause and the pain is excluded from the story. It matters here to distinguish causal explanation from epistemic justification; the paradox is not that my judgment lacks a cause, since of course it has a neural one.

Contrastive epiphenomenalism — phenomenal states cause nothing non-phenomenal, but may cause further phenomenal states — looks like it helps, and on the experiential reading I think it dissolves the paradox outright. On that reading the judgment that I am in pain is not a bare belief standing outside the phenomenal domain; it is itself an episode with phenomenal character, rather like the experience of its seeming to one that one is in pain. Since it is a phenomenal state, the earlier pain is permitted to cause it, and so the epiphenomenalist can say after all that my being in pain is what produces my judgment that I am in pain. The causal chain the paradox said was missing is restored, and with it the explanation of why my judgments about my own consciousness are reliable. Contrast the propositional reading, where the judgment is a belief with content and so falls outside the phenomenal domain: there the paradox does bite, and the epiphenomenalist must fall back on saying that the belief is made true by, and non-causally constitutively tied to, the pain.

Two further points. The evolutionary worry — that natural selection could never have reached a causally inert property — is not much of an objection, since the epiphenomenalist can say that selection operated on the neural processes and consciousness came along as a by-product of them. And if the epiphenomenalist cannot answer the paradox, she has refuted herself: a position that removes the justification for believing its own central premise is not one that can be held. That, in the end, is why the knowledge argument and epiphenomenalism stand or fall together — Mary's acquisition of a new fact just is her acquisition of something causally inert.
