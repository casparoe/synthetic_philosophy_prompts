Some background on me, since it fixes what I need the theory for. I am not an academic. I have spent about twenty years in operations at a mid-sized freight company, mostly running regional depots, and I have now watched this same story play out enough times that I think it is a law and not bad luck. The first time, we measured on-time delivery by the timestamp scanned at the depot gate; within two months drivers were parking outside the gate and walking in to scan at their leisure, the number went to ninety-nine percent, and the actual service got worse. The second time, we measured damage claims per thousand shipments and the depots simply stopped filing them, reclassifying breakages as customer handling. The third time, we measured trailer utilization and something different happened: people genuinely started consolidating loads, cost per mile really did fall for about two years, and then it flattened out. That third case is the one that makes me suspicious of the slogan everyone repeats, roughly, that once a number is used to steer a system it stops meaning what it meant. Sometimes steering works. I want a theory that says which case I am in, on the Monday I am handed the metric, before I have run it for two years.

So: write me the desiderata. The purpose is fixed and narrow. I want a theory of Goodhart effects and specification gaming good enough that, given a metric, the thing it is supposed to track, and the people who will be rewarded on it, I can say approximately how long before it rots, by what route it will rot, and what to do instead. Adequacy means adequacy for that.

Here is my own half-baked apparatus, which you should feel free to demolish where it is muddled — fixing it is part of what I am asking for. First, I keep conflating three different failures and I want to know whether they are really distinct: the number stops tracking the thing; the number keeps tracking a thing that has itself been quietly degraded while the number holds steady; and the number wins outright, so that after a while the number is simply what the goal means to everyone involved. Second, my own candidate explanation for why some measures rot in a week and some never do: I call it the slack ratio, meaning how much of the movement in the number can be produced through channels that do not produce the thing you care about. A depot gate timestamp has enormous slack. The clock in a footrace has almost none, and the distance of that race has literally none, because the distance is part of what the race is. Which makes me want a distinction between measures that merely diagnose an activity and measures that partly constitute it, and I want to know whether that distinction is a genuine desideratum for a theory to respect or just my personal hobbyhorse. Third, I think it matters whether the thing pushing on the metric is a person following an incentive, a department defending a budget, or an optimizer actively searching for the cheapest available route to a higher number, because an active search seems to find paths no one would have thought of. Fourth, I suspect the hardest requirement of all is this: the theory has to say what the target is, independently of the measure, or else explain what follows if for some goals it cannot. Good healthcare or good education are not things I can define without circularity, and if the target is undefined then saying the measure has stopped tracking it looks unfalsifiable. Give me a test for when a Goodhart effect is actually occurring, and tell me whether that test is itself gameable. Fifth, I care about dynamics and rates, not just end states, and I need the theory to be usable by someone who cannot A/B test his own depots.
