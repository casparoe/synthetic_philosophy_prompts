I'm in my school's philosophy club, and next week I have to give a ten-minute talk defending the book's side of the dialogue in The Mind's I (Hofstadter and Dennett, 1981) in which Einstein's brain and a book argue about which of them, if either, is the thing that thinks. Almost everyone in the club is sure the brain obviously wins. So I don't want a speech asserting that a book could be conscious; I want a case that makes them feel it, and that I can put across in about a minute of talking.

Call the brain's position the live-causal view: a thing is conscious only if it is actually, right now, caught up in the causal traffic of the world — affected, responding, and then affected again by how it responded, in a loop that was not written down in advance. Call the book's position the repertoire view: what matters is the shape of what the thing would say or do in every situation it could be put in, and whether it is ever actually run, or what it is made of, is beside the point. Correct either one in a sentence if I have it wrong.

What I need is two systems, X and Y, meeting all of this:

(i) Over an arbitrarily long conversation they are indistinguishable — same replies to any line you feed them, same apparent self-knowledge, same capacity to surprise.
(ii) X's replies come from a process with genuine real-time causal give-and-take: its own outputs feed back and causally change its later states. Y's replies come from a fixed structure completed before the conversation began and left unaltered by it.
(iii) The live-causal view must be forced to count X as conscious and to deny it of Y, while the repertoire view must be forced to treat the two alike — each verdict falling out of what the view says, not from anything I stipulate.
(iv) The loop in X is causally real but leaves what X says later unchanged; that is the nerve of the case, so say plainly whether it is what does the work.

Constraints: one paragraph; nothing exotic — no uploading, no brain tissue, no unknown physics, no person hidden inside either system; followable by someone who has never read philosophy. Flag any condition you had to fudge.

Then tell me: which single feature of the case forces each verdict; what happens to both verdicts if I make X's loop change what X says later; what the live-causal theorist has to add in order to escape the verdict, and whether that addition quietly concedes the book's point; and whether the objection my club will certainly raise — that the book only moves when someone reads it, so it borrows its causal life from the reader — lands on your case.

Give the verdicts first as a table with these columns: system (one clause) | verdict under the live-causal view | the one premise of that view that forces it | verdict under the repertoire view | the one premise of that view that forces it — one row for X, one for Y. Then a second table with one row for each of the four questions in the previous paragraph and columns: question | answer in two sentences | what the answer depends on.
