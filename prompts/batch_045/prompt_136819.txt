Two friends of mine — Owen, who works in health economics, and Lena, a lawyer — spent most of a dinner party arguing about a variant of the torture-versus-dust-specks case, and afterwards asked me to settle it. I couldn't. The variant they ended up on wasn't quite the standard one, and I think that is why they stalled rather than converged. Here is the exchange as well as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Owen. A dust speck is a second of mild irritation in the eye, forgotten immediately. Now build a series: a speck; an itch you can't scratch for a minute; an hour of headache; a day of nausea; a broken arm; a month of solitary; fifty years of torture. Each rung is only marginally worse than the one below it. At any rung, there is *some* multiplier at which enough instances of the lesser harm outweigh one instance of the greater — enough headaches are worse than one broken arm, and so on. Run the multipliers up the series and you get: for some number N, N dust specks are worse than one person's fifty years of torture. Pick a number larger than N — 3^^^3 will do — and the aggregationist answer follows. To resist it you have to name the rung at which "no number of these is worse than one of those" becomes true, and every rung is supposed to look like its neighbours.

(2) Lena. Two problems. First, "marginally worse" and even "a harm at all" are vague at the bottom of that series; it is a sorites. Being unable to point to the exact rung is not evidence that there is no cut, it is what vagueness predicts either way. Second, and more importantly, transitivity is not doing the work — the real premise is a separate structural claim: for any bad thing A and any lesser bad thing B, there is some finite number of copies of B that is worse than A. Call it the Archimedean assumption about harms. That is a substantive thesis about how different people's suffering combines into one moral quantity, not a bit of logic, and it is precisely what is in dispute. A lexical-threshold view that denies it is a position, not a failure of nerve.

(3) Owen. Then let me remove the vagueness. Substitute pinpricks: sharply felt, clearly a real if trivial setback, well above any threshold of noticeability, agreeing to be unpleasant. Run the same series. And now consider what I'll call the shredder. Take the fifty years of torture, cut it into its constituent moments of pain — each moment subjectively about as bad as a pinprick — and distribute one moment to each of 3^^^3 distinct people. No one's life is appreciably worse; the total quantity of felt pain is the same as in the torture case; and on your view the result is morally trivial next to the torture. So your position does not merely restrain aggregation, it liquifies atrocity: any horror becomes permissible if you have enough people to sprinkle it over. That isn't caution, it's alchemy.

(4) Lena. I'll bite part of that, and I don't think it's alchemy. Spreading a load across many shoulders rather than breaking one back is what we ordinarily *aim* for. What matters morally is not the quantity of pain-stuff in the world but whether someone's life has been wrecked: a harm is urgent when it is someone's catastrophe, and spread thin enough it is no one's. Note the asymmetry I'm relying on. Within a single life the small items do add up — a life of fifty years of constant mild pain can easily be a worse life than one containing a single terrible month. What I deny is that they add up across lives, because there is no subject for whom the total is a setback. The world's containing more pain is not the same as things going worse for somebody.

(5) Owen. Then your view has costs you won't accept. Take a rule permitting an emission that raises each of three hundred million people's risk of death by one in ten million: thirty expected deaths, not one ruined life, not one person who can point to a setback of their own. Your principle makes that close to negligible beside one identifiable person's ruin, which would dismantle most of environmental and transport regulation — the exact domain where tiny harms to huge numbers are all we ever have to go on. And it cuts both ways: if small harms don't aggregate into reasons, neither do small benefits, so a public-health measure that slightly lowers everyone's risk has no value at all. Besides, your intrapersonal aggregation concedes the Archimedean claim in general form; you just refuse to apply it where it matters.

(6) Lena. Two replies. That isn't my position: I can hold a two-tier view — relieve catastrophes first, and below that threshold aggregate the small stuff as enthusiastically as you like. Lexical priority at the top needn't mean paralysis underneath, so the regulatory case doesn't collapse. Second, look at the size of the number you opened with. 3^^^3 is not merely large; it exceeds the number of people who could exist in the reachable universe. So the option you want ranked isn't "torture one person or speck everyone alive." It is: torture one person, or bring into existence — or stipulate, in another possible world — an astronomically large population of otherwise-fine lives each containing one speck. Once that's clear, you're asking me to rank two options that share no population, and I doubt our intuitions about which is "worse" are tracking anything. Consistency pressure is real, but it only binds across options that are genuinely available.

(7) At this point I interrupted to say that in the real world nobody's baseline is speck-free — the air has dust in it, people are mildly uncomfortable most of the time — so "add one dust speck" isn't a well-defined intervention, and the notion of a marginal speck seemed to me to be doing quiet work in Owen's series. They both told me, kindly, that this was beside the point, and went back to it.

Please adjudicate the exchange. Go through the seven moves in order and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who, if anyone, is more right overall; what both of them are missing; and whether there is a distinction neither drew that dissolves part of the argument. I'd also like to know how committed you are: mark which judgments you would actually defend, which rest on a contested premise you're helping yourself to, and what would change your mind. And if you think the dispute as a whole is ill-posed, or that some move rests on a confusion, say so plainly rather than distributing partial credit to both sides — I'd rather be told the question is broken than handed a diplomatic verdict.
