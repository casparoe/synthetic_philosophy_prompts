I teach philosophy at a small liberal arts college, and next month I'm giving a twenty-five-minute invited talk with ten minutes of discussion at a one-day workshop on explanation in the cognitive and biological sciences. Nobody in my own department works on explanation, so I've been rehearsing at a whiteboard and I have no real sense of what will get thrown at me. The talk is titled "Cranes with Borrowed Footings," and the thesis is this: the methodological pair we all inherited from Dennett — shun skyhooks, shun greedy reductionism — misses the failure mode that does the most damage in ordinary scientific practice. The failure mode I have in mind is an explanation that has the complete surface profile of a crane: stepwise, level-respecting, resting on machinery rather than on a miracle, with no explanatory levels skipped and no appeal to anything spooky — but whose bottom rung quietly re-imports the very phenomenon being explained, dressed in a more respectable vocabulary. My examples are representational explanations of cognition where "representation" turns out to be cashed out by appeal to what an interpreter would take the system to be about; selectional explanations where "selection for" is doing work that a causal story about replication and differential retention should be doing; "internalization" explanations of norm-responsiveness; and "information-processing" explanations of development. The diagnostic I propose is a footing test: take the lowest rung of the crane and try to redescribe it entirely in the vocabulary of the level beneath it; if the redescription fails, or merely relocates the same semantic notion one level down, the footing is borrowed. I want to claim that this is not a greedy demand to skip levels but an anti-skyhook demand to finish the descent, though I'm not confident that distinction will survive contact with an audience.

The room will be: two Dennett-trained philosophers of mind who use the crane/skyhook pair constantly and consider themselves deflationists about content; one senior philosopher of science of the new mechanist stripe (mechanisms as organized entities and activities, constitutive relevance, how-possibly versus how-actually); one philosopher of biology who defends adaptationist explanation and talks cheerfully about genes as carrying information; one enactivist who holds that representation talk is itself the skyhook and that I haven't gone nearly far enough; and a handful of graduate students who will want to know how to run the test on their own material. I'd like a Q&A prep sheet: the six or eight objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it, with a roughly two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Please also tell me what I should concede in the first two slides before anyone has to ask, which single reply has to land for the talk to have a point, and the objection I'm hoping nobody raises — my own guess is that it involves whether the footing test can distinguish a placeholder from a theoretical posit with deferred cash value, or whether it collapses into the very thing I'm criticizing, but I'd rather hear your candidate than have me supply it. Where I am genuinely unsure: whether my test condemns too much, since a great deal of working biology would fail it, or too little, since a borrowed footing can be refinanced later and my test may only be detecting present arrears rather than bankruptcy. Please write in American English, and keep the whole prep sheet under 800 words.
