I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on probability and evidence — 16 students, a single 90-minute session. They have already encountered the Sleeping Beauty problem in the reading and are split roughly evenly; nothing I can say at the front of the room will move them, because each side can produce an argument that sounds airtight to itself. So I do not want another presentation of the puzzle. I want them to run it, repeatedly, under a protocol I control, with real if small stakes, and to run into the disagreement in their own choices before anyone names a position.

The obstacle is that the original story has no moving parts: one person, one coin, one or two wakenings, and amnesia that I cannot actually induce. My working idea is to replace amnesia with sampling. Each round I flip a coin privately and prepare 16 sealed slips; on heads exactly one slip says that you are awake this round, on tails two do, and the rest say that you are asleep. Everyone opens at the same time and everyone writes down a guess every round — the sleepers write a decoy guess that is discarded, so that the sound and sight of writing never reveals how many people are awake. Anyone holding an "awake" slip writes a guess (or a number), seals it face down, and is paid in tokens at the end of the round. Nobody may speak, look at another slip, or reveal how many people were awake until the round is scored. Over a dozen or so rounds the class should watch guessing tails pay about twice as much per round as guessing heads, and should find that roughly two thirds of all the scored guesses were made in tails rounds.

Please design the session around that, or replace the mechanism if a better one exists. Specific things I need:

A first stage that establishes, from the students' own tally, that in the game they are playing the answer is one third and not one half. Tell me the exact expected payoff per round for the candidate strategies under a flat token-per-correct-guess scheme, and also under a proper scoring rule if you think stated numbers are better than forced guesses — I would like students to commit to a number as well as a bet, because I expect some of them to write one half and still bet tails, and I want that moment to happen on paper where we can look at it. If the gap between the two candidate answers is small enough that a dozen rounds is mostly noise, say so explicitly and tell me how many rounds I need or how to rescale the payoffs.

A second stage that separates the positions which agree at the first stage. My sketch: on tails rounds the two awake slips are privately marked first and second, and before committing, the awake students are told something that amounts to "yours is the first awakening of the round" — for instance by announcing that only first-awakening slips are scored this round, so that being scored at all carries the information. As far as I can work out, the frequency of heads among such students is one half rather than one third. Please confirm or correct the arithmetic, and tell me what each of the three positions — one half before and one half after; one half before and two thirds after; one third before and one half after — predicts at this stage, which of them the activity actually discriminates between, and which of them it leaves tied no matter how the tokens are arranged.

A third stage that turns the class on the mechanism it has been using. In the game, being awake is news: you might not have been selected at all. In the story, Beauty is awake either way, so being awake is not news, and the sampling argument may simply not carry over. I would rather the students build this objection than receive it, so tell me how to sequence the rounds and what to ask so that it arrives from them, and how to handle it if it arrives in minute fifteen and deflates everything scheduled after it. Relatedly, I want a student to play the lab assistant, who is never asleep, is told what day it is, answers the same question, and does well by answering one half while the awake students are doing well by answering one third. What are the students supposed to conclude from a case in which neither party can point to a single thing the other does not know about the coin?

Please give me: the rules and the materials; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; the payoff scheme with the arithmetic showing what each position earns and why; the debrief, mapping specific moments in the game onto specific features of the disagreement; an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect students inferring the number of awake slips from movement or noise, small-sample luck, someone arguing that the tokens are doing all the work and that credence is untouched, and the sampling-versus-amnesia objection surfacing at the wrong time, but tell me what else to expect; and a frank note on what the activity cannot reproduce, in particular whether any classroom surrogate can give anyone the experience of genuine self-locating ignorance, or whether that has to be supplied by me in the last ten minutes and how I should do it without it sounding like a verdict.

Please write the answer in American English.
