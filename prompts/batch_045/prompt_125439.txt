I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and my critique group has short-circuited my brain with one sentence, so I'm hoping you can take it apart for me.

Scene I'm stuck on: chapter 19. My narrator is a barrister and a bit of a pedant, and he's trying to box in a witness. She gives him every premise he asks for, cheerfully. Yes, her fingerprint was on the glass. Yes, if her print was on the glass then she was in the room. And then she just doesn't concede she was in the room. So he writes down a third line — roughly, "if those two things are both true, then she was in the room" — asks her to grant that too, and she grants it, and still won't say it. (I know, I know: this is basically Lewis Carroll's thing, the dialogue where Achilles keeps feeding the Tortoise more premises and the Tortoise keeps treating each new one as just another premise and never gets to the conclusion. I read about it years ago and I'm not fussed about the source, I just need the shape of the move.)

The line that broke me came from a writers' forum: "you can't argue someone into logic." Two different people used it to do two different jobs. One used it to say my witness isn't being irrational at all, and that if the barrister ends up sputtering, that's his failure and not hers — no argument could ever have reached her, so she's not at fault. Someone in another thread used the same line to say that all the reasoning in a detective story rests on nothing, because the rules themselves are only ever assumed, so a deduction scene is a hunch with a better tailor.

So: what are the precise claims hiding in "you can't argue someone into logic"? I want them as a menu, from the trivial to the radical, with a verdict on each — platitude, substantive but contested, or just false — and, crucially, which reading supports which of the two jobs the line was being made to do above, and which don't support either.

Some things I'd specifically like pulled apart, because I suspect they're being silently merged:

a) Is the witness missing a premise or missing something else entirely? Here's a wrinkle I want you to take seriously: suppose I make her infinitely patient — she'll grant the entire infinite stack, each new "if all the previous, then Z" line I hand her, forever, and she grants them all at once if you like. Does that help the barrister? My vague sense is no, that with all those premises on the table there's still a way for every one of them to be true while the conclusion is false, so nothing in the pile forces anything, and that what's missing isn't another line of text. If that's right, say it in a way I can put in a barrister's mouth; if it's wrong, tell me what actually changes.

b) Circularity seems to come in more than one flavour. There's using a rule in an argument for that same rule, and there's listing the rule as a premise in the argument, and people on the forum seemed to slide between them. Which kind is the Carroll setup even about? Is one of them benign and the other useless, and does either give you "deduction is unjustified" as opposed to "deduction isn't justified by that particular route"?

c) Does the line mean there is *no* justification of deduction, or that the justification can't take the form of an argument — i.e. that it'd have to be something else, along the lines of rules being constitutive of thought or assertion, or a pragmatic or abductive warrant, or just seeing it? And if it's the latter, does that leave the witness unfaultable, or does she now owe something else?

d) Should I write her as denying the rule outright, or as granting the rule and still declining to infer? I have a feeling the slogan's force is completely different in the two cases, and that one of them is a much more interesting character. Also is she making a mistake, or has she merely stepped outside a practice, and what would the barrister be entitled to say to her in each case?

And the concrete payoff: if a reading says the barrister has no legitimate complaint, give me the beat he should play instead, because right now the scene is eleven pages of a man repeating himself and my beta readers want me to cut it.
