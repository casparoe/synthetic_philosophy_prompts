Scheffler's "afterlife" conjecture, as I have seen it summarized, holds that human extinction is bad in a way that is not exhausted by the deaths it involves, because the meaningfulness of what we do now depends on there being a human future that outlasts our own deaths. I have two worries about this, and I cannot tell whether they are objections or misreadings.

First: meaningfulness is not a mind-independent property; things matter only to creatures who value them. If extinction leaves no valuers behind, then there is nothing left for our lives to be meaningless to, so the claim that extinction would drain them of meaning looks either empty or else a disguised claim about impersonal value.

Second: if the surplus badness really attaches to the loss of the collective afterlife rather than to the deaths, it should also attach to a case in which nobody dies. Suppose humanity survives indefinitely, but each successive generation is raised so that nothing we now value — science, music, friendship, justice — means anything to them, so that within three centuries no living person cares about anything we care about. That "drift" case involves no deaths at all, yet by the logic of the conjecture it seems at least as bad as extinction, which is surely the wrong result.

So the argument appears to face a dilemma: either it collapses into a claim about impersonal value that has nothing specially to do with death, or it is not really about the afterlife at all. I assume I am misreading something, but I cannot see which premise to give up.
