Okay, so I'm teching a seminar on Leibniz and the problem of evil, and I need a case study that goes past the usual Pangloss caricature. The episode I want you to analyze is the 1755 Lisbon earthquake and the argument it kicked off between Voltaire and Rousseau. Here are the facts as I want you to take them; I don't want you to look anything up, and I don't care about exact titles or dates. In 1755, on All Saints' Day, Lisbon was hit by a massive earthquake, followed by a tsunami and fires, killing tens of thousands of people. Voltaire responded with a poem attacking philosophical optimism, and later with the novel Candide, whose Dr. Pangloss keeps insisting that everything is for the best in the best of all possible worlds even as horrors pile up. Rousseau replied to Voltaire by arguing, roughly, that most of the misery was our own fault: humans insist on crowding into big cities and building tall heavy buildings, so when the ground shakes they die in heaps, whereas if they had lived dispersed in the countryside in simple dwellings the same geological event would have been far less deadly. He also said that God governs by general laws, and a world with stable general laws—which will sometimes produce earthquakes as a byproduct—is better than a world where God constantly suspends the laws to shield us from every local catastrophe. Stipulate that reply as part of the case; I'm not asking you to recall the letter or its date, just the gist as I've stated it.

The theory is Leibnizian optimism, the best-of-all-possible-worlds view: God surveys all compossible (mutually compatible) combinations of created things and actualizes the one with the greatest net perfection, permitting evils either because they are necessary conditions of greater goods or because preventing them would mean losing some greater good or wrecking the best set of general laws. To keep this from being a rehash, I'm going to hand you a distinction. Call the first position aggregate optimism: the claim that the actual world contains a greater total amount of value, or net good, or perfection, than any other feasible world. Call the second position distributive optimism: the stronger claim that the world is not only best in total, but that the actual pattern of goods and evils is itself optimal, so that no particular horror could be removed or rearranged without reducing the total, and evils are not concentrated in a way that looks arbitrary or gratuitous from the standpoint of the creatures who suffer them. Leibniz is, as far as I can tell, clearly committed to aggregate optimism; whether he is committed to distributive optimism is a seperate and much murkier question, and that gap is what I want you to exploit.

So, do a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions. Start with the mapping. Who are the players or agents here, both in the theory and in the episode? In the theory, the central agent is God choosing among possible worlds: what are God's feasible options supposed to be (for example, a world with no seismic activity at all versus this world with earthquakes but stable general laws, or a world with the same laws but a smaller, more dispersed human population), and what does optimism say God should have done and in fact did? Then bring it down to the human level: what options does the theory say human agents faced, like building crowded stone cities on a fault line versus living simply and dispersed, or trusting providence versus taking precautions, and what does it say they should have done? And treat Voltaire and Rousseau as epistemic agents in the dispute: according to the theory, what should each have believed about Lisbon, what evidence should have counted for them, and did either of them respond to the right evidence?

Next, ask which features of the episode Leibnizian optimism explains and which it misses or misdescribes. Use my aggregate/distributive vocabulary. As aggregate optimism, does the theory explain the bare occurrence of a deadly earthquake, and does it explain Rousseau's point that a lot of the harm came from human choices rather than from nature directly? Does it help or hurt the theory that much of the death toll may be moral evil (imprudent building) rather than natural evil? Then press on distributive optimism: can the theory explain the specific distribution of suffering, namely that the quake struck on a holy day, killed children and other people who seem innocent, and destroyed a city that just happened to be built there? Does it explain Voltaire's sense that the optimist story has become morally obscene, or is that exactly the kind of feature a purely aggregate view cannot see? Be honest about where the theory is silent: for instance, does it tell us whether we should expect to detect the greater goods that justify the evil, or what even counts as a greater good in the first place?

Finally, run it back the other way: what does the episode show about the theory? Is Lisbon a confirming instance, because it shows how a theodicy can absorb natural evil via general laws and moral evil via free human imprudence? Is it an anomaly, because the concentrated, innocent suffering looks like a counterexample to distributive optimism even if aggregate optimism survives? Or is it a case the theory never anticipated, because Leibniz was thinking about metaphysical and moral evil, not urban geology and tsunami risk? Does Voltaire's rhetorical victory tell us anything philosophical about optimism, or only that satire is more fun than metaphysics? And if Rousseau's blame on human imprudence is correct, does that rescue the theory, or does it quietly turn the theodicy into a kind of victim-blaming that Leibniz himself would have rejected? Give me a verdict: if you were a Leibnizian in the mid-1750s reading this exchange, what should you concede, what should you stubbornly dig in on, and where should you admit the theory is silent rather than confident?
