I run a graduate reading group, and for a session on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument I tried to reconstruct it in the form I find hardest to resist — a form that never mentions determinism, since the determinism-relative version has always struck me as the weaker one. What I came out with seems to establish considerably more than I can accept, and I cannot locate the break.

(1) Whatever an agent does on a given occasion, she does it because of the way she is at the time: her motives, her dispositions to weigh reasons, her deliberative habits.

(2) So if she is truly morally responsible for what she does — responsible in the sense that underwrites desert, in the sense that makes blame and retributive responses fitting rather than merely useful — then she must be truly morally responsible for being the way she is, at least in the respects that produced the action.

(3) For her to be truly responsible for being the way she is, she must have made herself that way: the relevant features of her character must be the product of her own voluntary, knowing, reasons-guided agency rather than something she simply found herself saddled with.

(4) Any act of self-making would be performed by someone who already had a character — desires, a way of weighing reasons, a disposition to remake herself in one direction rather than another — and that antecedent character is itself something she did not make.

(5) Therefore no one is ever truly responsible for being the way she is, and so, by (1) and (2), no one is ever truly morally responsible for anything she does.

(6) The demand in (3) then looks unsatisfiable in principle rather than merely unsatisfied. Suppose a being did create its own character from scratch. At the moment of creating it would have no character to guide it, so its selection of a character could not be guided by reasons it antecedently had; it would be arbitrary, and an arbitrary selection is exactly not the sort of thing responsibility attaches to. So the argument is not really asking for ultimate self-origination: it appears to demand something that even ultimate self-origination would not deliver.

(7) It also spreads in directions I never aimed at. Consider a case I invented to probe it. Aya's character at forty was shaped by revisions she deliberately made at thirty; those were guided by a character she had at twenty; and so on backwards through a finite chain that closes into a causal loop, with no first term and no external source. Every stage of her character was brought about by Aya herself at some other stage, and the cycle is self-consistent. Nothing about her traces to any agency other than her own, and yet there is no point at which she created herself from nothing. If the complaint in (3) is that an agent's character must have no source outside her, Aya satisfies it. If the complaint is that no stage of the chain is ultimate, then Aya fails along with everyone else, and the argument is resting on a condition that nothing could meet — at which point I do not see why the verdict should be that she is not responsible rather than that the condition is the wrong one.

(8) And I get the wrong verdict in the wrong way elsewhere. Take someone whose whole motivational system was deliberately engineered by another person who knew exactly what she was doing. That looks like a paradigm of non-responsibility, and the argument returns that verdict — but only because it returns it about everyone, via (5). It reaches the manipulation case by way of a conclusion that has no discriminating power, which makes me doubt it reaches it for the right reason. It likewise seems to swallow epistemic credit: if deserving credit for a true belief requires being responsible for the cognitive character that produced it, then nobody deserves credit for anything they believe, including the belief that this argument is sound.

I would most like to keep (1) and (2), and I would like to keep the intuition about the engineered agent. I am genuinely torn between rejecting (3) and rejecting the move from (4) to (5).

Where does this go wrong? Name the step, explain why it looked compulsory when the argument was being assembled, and price the alternatives: what do I have to accept if I drop (2) rather than (3), what kind of account of sourcehood am I committed to if I drop (3), and can any such account distinguish Aya from the engineered agent? And if (6) is right that the real engine of the argument is an intelligibility requirement rather than an origination requirement, does the argument still say anything distinctive about moral responsibility at all?
