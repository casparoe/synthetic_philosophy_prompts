I'm finishing a novel set almost entirely on a small tidal island, and the hinge of the third act is an argument I need to get exactly right. My narrator is the island's hydrographer; she keeps a tide gauge on the causeway that is the only thing standing between the settlement and an evacuation order. A visiting engineer tells her she cannot trust it: trusting it would be "bootstrapping," and bootstrapping is what the man in the mire does when he hauls himself up by his own bootstraps. The picture is doing all the work in his speech — the gauge's own readouts are the only evidence anyone on the island has that the gauge is reliable, so her confidence in it is lifted by the very thing that needs lifting, and therefore, he says, she does not know what the tide will do; she only feels that she does. I want to audit that analogy rather than grant it, and I have written the book so that the gauge exists in three versions, because I suspect the engineer's charge does not land on all three the same way. In the first, the Single Dial: on the one morning the plot turns on, the instrument prints a reading on a paper strip and, further down the same strip, a second stamp certifying that the first stamp is accurate — the certificate is produced by the same mechanism, on the same battery, in the same housing. In the second, the Long Ledger: she has forty years of readings, and on the handful of occasions when she could wade out at low water with a graduated rod, rod and gauge agreed; she has also noticed that the gauge never disagrees with itself from one year to the next. In the third, the Erasing Log: the device writes to a sealed log, and when a fault develops the fault corrupts that morning's entry too, so the log displays an unbroken record of self-agreement whether the gauge is working or not — she does not know this, and nothing in the log could tell her.

What I need from you is the audit, not the map. Please don't point me to papers, bibliographies, or named positions: I have no library out here and couldn't check a citation if you gave me one, and I already know the cases travel under labels like bootstrapping and easy knowledge. Work the analogy itself. Give me a correspondence table with these columns — item in the bootstraps picture, item in the gauge case, assumption the analogy smuggles across, whether the mismatch is load-bearing for the engineer's conclusion (yes or no), and a one-line reason — then separate the disanalogies that matter for the use he is making of the picture from the ones that don't, and say plainly what the mire image has that the gauge lacks (a single body, a single jerk, no ratchet, no accumulation, no friction to hold the gain) and whether any of that is what his argument actually needs.

Then commit. For each of my three versions — Single Dial, Long Ledger, Erasing Log — take a definite position on whether my narrator knows, or is at least reasonable in refusing to evacuate, give your reasons, name the single disanalogy your verdict turns on, state a confidence between 0 and 1, and say what would move you off it. I don't want a survey of the options with the choice left to me; I want to be told which way it goes.
