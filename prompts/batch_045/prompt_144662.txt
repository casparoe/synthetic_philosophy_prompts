I write literary fiction, and the novel I am finishing is set in the early 2050s in a small northern European country whose public health service funds preimplantation testing for every IVF patient and, through a body I have been calling the Selection Review Office, publishes a ranked schedule of which traits may be scored at public expense, which may be scored only if the parents pay, and which may not be scored at all. Two of my characters work at a clinic attached to the programme — a counsellor who has to explain the schedule to prospective parents, and a clinician who privately thinks it is a moral mess — and most of the book's argument happens in their disputes with each other and with the couples they see. A friend in my writing group read a draft and said one of them sounded like three different ethicists arguing inside one head, which is exactly my fear: I can write each character holding forth convincingly about a single case, but I cannot tell whether the bundle of things they believe is a position anybody holds, or whether I have stapled together commitments that actually belong to three different views and that only sound compatible in dialogue. So I am asking for a classification instrument rather than an essay: a decision tree that begins from questions my characters could answer in their own words — a lay counsellor, a frightened parent, a clinician with no formal ethics training — and terminates in a named position on whether selecting offspring traits is permissible, and whether it is ever required. Please build it from the logical space instead of from a list of names: give me the branching questions first, in the order you think they should be asked, say which combinations of answers are genuinely available, and only then locate the actual positions at the leaves, tagging each with a short descriptive name of your own and, where you are confident one exists, the label it goes by in the literature — but write "unnamed" rather than guessing, and no bibliography or citations, I only want labels that are uncontroversial.

Two constraints on the nodes. First, every branch must be a question answerable without already knowing where it leads: no node may ask whether someone accepts the therapy/enhancement distinction or thinks enhancement is wrong; it must present a case and ask what should be done, or what the parents are entitled to be told. Second, the tree has to be able to reach at least these leaves, unless you think two of them are the same position or one of them is not coherent, in which case say so rather than quietly merging or dropping it: the strong view that there is a duty to select the child expected to have the best life; the view that parents have wide discretion to select but no duty to maximise; the view that selection is permissible only up to some sufficiency threshold or only to avert serious conditions; a view on which certain selections are objectionable because of what they express about, or do to, existing people; a view on which some selections wrong the child by pre-empting its own later choices; and a view on which each individual choice can be unobjectionable while the practice as a whole — subsidised, marketed, widely taken up — is not. The cases the tree must be able to route are these: ten embryos, every one expected to have a life well worth living, one carrying a somewhat lower polygenic risk of a late-onset disease; the same choice made on the basis of stature, which the parents value partly for its own sake and partly as an advantage; parents who select partly because the resulting child will be a tissue match for an existing sick sibling; parents who ask the clinic to rank embryos on a composite health index and to seal the individual scores, so that they choose a healthier child without ever knowing which traits they selected for — a "closed envelope" option I have invented for the book, which several characters treat as obviously different from open selection while disagreeing about why; the same expected health gain produced not by choosing among embryos but by editing the genome of the embryo that will otherwise be transferred; a couple who want a child temperamentally suited to the isolated, physically demanding life they lead, and say so plainly; and finally whether the state should pay for any of this, which I need you to tell me is either a separate node or a consequence of where one has already landed.

Where I most want your honesty is at the end. Mark any node that is really a values choice dressed up as a diagnostic, and any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate — tell me what further question might separate them and whether you believe any such question exists. Tell me which of my characters' likely bundles of commitments fall into unoccupied regions of your logical space, and whether that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unclaimed. And be explicit about what would make you redraw the tree: if there is a case you have not seen, or a serious argument you have set aside, that would force a different branching order or collapse two leaves into one, I would much rather hear about it now than discover it in copy-edits.
