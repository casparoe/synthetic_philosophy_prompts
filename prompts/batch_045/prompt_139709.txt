Setup as I understand it, correct me if I've got it garbled. Take an agent that licenses its actions by proof: it searches for proofs, in some system S at least as strong as PA, of statements of the form if I output action a then outcome u follows, and then does whatever its search endorses. Because the agent's own code sits inside the world it's reasoning about, the diagonal lemma lets those statements talk about the agent's own search. Löb's theorem says that if S proves the conditional if P is provable in S then P, then S already proves P outright. So S can't hand itself a general license of the form whenever my search turns up a proof that a is best, a really is best — getting that license would already amount to having proved the conclusion, and if S proved its own soundness wholesale it would prove its own consistency and hence be inconsistent by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem. The vivid failure mode is spurious self-referential proofs: the agent can produce something that looks like a proof that taking the smaller payoff yields the larger one, and it has no way to tell those apart from genuine ones, so it can't reliably prefer the ten-dollar bill to the five. I half-remember a name for that last bit, something like the five-and-ten problem — don't worry about getting the literature right, I actively don't want references, I want your own reasoning.

The claim I want a credence on: this is a real constraint on building agents that reason about their own future behaviour, not an artifact of a silly idealization (unbounded proof search, the agent having a crisp copy of its own source code, the demand for proof rather than for high confidence) that stops mattering the moment you build something anyone would actually build.

I'm at about 0.5 on that. A collaborator of mine is at 0.25, and says the following move kills it definately; I'll call it the stepdown trick. Let the agent do its action-licensing search inside a strictly weaker finitely axiomatized fragment W, while doing its reasoning about that search inside the stronger S. Since S can prove the soundness of W — uniform reflection for a finite fragment, which I think is right but check it — the agent gets a perfectly real license: any W-proof that a is best means a is best. Cost is that for licensing purposes it's only a W-strength agent. He says you lose a little strength once and that's the end of it. I say you either bottom out in something too weak to license anything you care about, or the small loss compounds, because an agent that re-licenses at each of a million timesteps slides down a tower of ever-weaker systems, and then the obstacle has been relocated rather than dissolved.
