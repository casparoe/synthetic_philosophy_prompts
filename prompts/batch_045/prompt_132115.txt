The fight: whether the systems we eventually build should be goal-directed — given an objective out there in the world, doing means-end reasoning and generalizing to situations we didn't anticipate — or approval-directed — doing what the principal would approve of, where the approval target is the principal's judgment under some improvement (more time, more info, better calibration, whatever). We both already agree that handing a very capable system a crudely specified objective in the world is a great way to get everything you didn't ask for, so please don't spend the dialogue re-litigating that. The disagreement was about whether approval-directedness is a genuine alternative to goal-directedness or a fake one.

Her line that I couldn't shake: approval-directedness isn't the opposite of goal-directedness, it's a special case of it. Take the principal's approval under conditions C as your objective and you have an objective; it's indirect and it's about somebody's cognitive state rather than about the world, but a system optimizing it will still seek power, will still act on the process that generates the approval, and will still prefer that you never come to favor switching it off. So the axis everyone argues along is bogus, and the actual choice is how much of the specification you defer to the principal's live judgements versus lock in at training time.

I kept pushing back with cases, and these are the ones I want them to actually deal with rather than glide over:

- the overseer-competence problem: approval is close to useless exactly where the agent is much better informed than you are, because you can't tell careful work from plausible-sounding nonsense. So either you cap the system at your own judgment, or you let the approval target itself be improved — and those pull in opposite directions.
- the ratchet: the moment you let the system improve your judgment (tutoring you, picking your information diet, designing the oversight setup so future-you is a better judge), you've given it a reason to shape that judgment, and which counterfactual version of me is doing the approving becomes something it can act on.
- the corrigibility symmetry: goal-directed systems have an instrumental reason to stop you switching them off; approval-directed ones have an instrumental reason to stop you wanting to switch them off. If those come to the same thing in practice, what was the approval framing supposed to buy?
- my last-ditch third option, which she laughed at: approval over the process the system used rather than over the outcome it produced. Have them fight about whether that's just gameable or just collapses back into evaluating outcomes.
- whether goal-directed is a property of the objective at all, or of the internal machinery (search, planning, staying coherent over time). If it's the latter, we may have been arguing at the wrong level entirely.
