I'm a graduate student in a seminar on the philosophy and ethics of machine learning, and for three weeks now our discussion of the narrow definition of the alignment problem has gone in exactly the same circle. One camp holds that the target is intent alignment: a system is aligned when it does what its operator intends it to do, and the hard part is technical — getting systems to track the operator's actual aim rather than the letter of their specification, and to keep doing so as capabilities grow. The other camp holds that this target is either unsatisfiable or not worth satisfying, because operators are fallible, their aims are often corrupted by the circumstances of deployment, and a system that faithfully served a bad or confused principal would be a catastrophe rather than a success; on their view the target has to be something like the operator's informed, reflectively endorsed values, or value full stop.

What frustrates me is how much they agree on. Everyone grants that literal instruction-following produces specification gaming. Everyone grants that idealized-value targets are computationally remote and epistemically opaque. Everyone grants that the principal is not the moral endpoint of the story. And in the cases we construct — a compliant system serving a principal whose aims have been distorted by an institutional incentive; a system that overrides an explicit instruction to prevent an outcome the principal would later repudiate — both sides give the same description of what happens and largely the same verdict on whether the outcome is good. They just draw opposite conclusions about what the case shows.

My own hypothesis, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that the dispute is not verbal after all but is instead a disagreement about which cases count as the data. The intent camp seems to treat cases of catastrophic loss of control as the load-bearing ones, and reads every other case as a warning about delegation; the value camp treats the compliant-operator-with-corrupted-aims case as the load-bearing one, and reads loss of control as a symptom of aiming at the wrong thing. If that is right, neither side's evidence is evidence for the other side, which would explain the stall without any conceptual confusion.

There is also a problem I raised in seminar that nobody answered, and that I suspect is doing hidden work. If alignment means doing what the principal intends, and what a principal intends typically includes intentions about how their own instructions should be interpreted and corrected, then intent has to be indexed to some stopping point in that regress: the principal as they are, as they would be with more information, as they would be after more deliberation, as they would be with better values. Picking the stopping point looks like it reinstates the entire normative question the narrow definition was supposed to avoid — which makes me wonder whether the narrow definition is a genuine narrowing or just a relocation of the same dispute.

Please give me a diagnosis of this impasse, committed rather than survey-shaped: tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, and where my hypothesis is wrong if it is.
