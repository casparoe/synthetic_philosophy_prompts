My housemate (econ PhD student) walked me into this one and I can't find the exit. Concrete case first: our team is doing a database migration before a code freeze. We've looked at the same dashboards, we both put shipping on time at about 50/50, neither of us has private info the other doesn't. She offers me a bet: she pays me $200 if it ships before the freeze, I pay her $200 if it doesn't. Then she argues I shouldn't take it:

(1) A voluntary trade gets accepted by both sides, so at the moment of acceptance each side must expect it to be nonnegative for them given what they know.
(2) If I know she maximizes expected utility and would have declined otherwise, then her accepting is evidence that her information favors her side of the bet.
(3) So conditional on her accepting, my side's expected value drops below zero and I should decline.
(4) She can run (2)-(3) about me too — and as far as I can tell this needs "she knows that I know that she's rational" iterated all the way up, which nobody literally has — and neither of us has to move first, so no voluntary bet can be mutually beneficial.
(5) Nothing in (1)-(4) used money, or bets, or this particular migration. It only used that the thing being traded has one common value for both of us, and that acceptance is informative. So the same goes for stock trades, insurance policies, and really any contract between two rational people who started from the same priors.
(6) But all of those happen constantly, and plenty of them genuinely seem to help both sides.
(7) So either real people basically never share priors — in which case the famous "you can't agree to disagree" result applies to nobody — or most people aren't rational, or most voluntary agreements are quietly exploiting one side.

Two things I can't fit together. First: even with identical information and identical priors we still both want to trade if I'm already carrying a lot of exposure to the migration outcome, since my bonus is tied to it. So gains from trade seem to come from different situations, not different beliefs, and (5) looks like it's overreaching. But if the argument only covers cases where we disagree about the value, I don't know what happens to the part of (4) that I actually find convincing — that I should be suspicious of any bet someone specifically chose to offer me. Second: on a big anonymous exchange I never learn that a particular person accepted, only that the price moved. Does the argument still bite there, or does it need a face on the other side?

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose I'd rather keep the "don't take bets people offer you" instinct than conclude that most traders are irrational, but I want to know what I'm paying for it — and whether keeping it forces me to give up the convergence stuff in (7).
