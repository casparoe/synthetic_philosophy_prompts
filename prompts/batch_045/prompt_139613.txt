Bernard Williams (1929–2003) is now remembered chiefly as a moral philosopher, the author of books such as Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy (1985) and Truth and Truthfulness (2002), and as a critic of abstract, system-building approaches to ethics who insisted that philosophy must come to terms with the complexity, contingency, and emotional depth of human life. Less widely remembered is that he was also a serious writer on opera, and that he held a distinctive view of its conventions: he believed that the apparent artificiality of opera, above all the fact that its characters sing rather than speak, need not be a flight from truth, but can be a way of achieving a truthfulness about action, passion, and human limitation that is unavailable to spoken drama. He also admired the Greeks and returned repeatedly to tragedy, moral luck, and the ways in which human agents are shaped by histories they did not choose.

Imagine the following case, which lies entirely beyond anything he could have witnessed. In the 2020s, a team of musicologists and machine-learning researchers trained a generative model on Puccini's surviving sketches for Turandot, on his finished operas, and on historical recordings of singers associated with his music. Puccini had died in 1924 leaving Turandot unfinished; the opera was completed by Franco Alfano and premiered in 1926. The team used their model to compose an alternative completion of the final scene, and then paired it with a synthesized vocal performance modeled on the recorded voice of a celebrated soprano who had died several decades earlier. By means of holography and spatial audio, a modern audience at a major opera house saw and heard that dead singer apparently perform the role of Turandot, in a completion that many critics described as more faithful to Puccini's intentions than Alfano's traditional ending and as overwhelmingly moving. Other critics denounced the whole enterprise as a sophisticated forgery: a mere imitation, produced without intention, agency, or the historical situation that, on their view, genuine artistic truth requires.

Suppose Williams had lived to encounter this production and the debate around it, not by searching for sources, but by reflecting on it with the resources of his own outlook. Write a relatively formal essay in which you predict and defend the view he would have taken. Your essay should do all of the following.

First, explain how he would analyze the relation between operatic convention and truth in this case, given his belief that singing and stylization can serve truthfulness rather than merely decorate or falsify it.

Second, apply his defense of truthfulness as a virtue, from Truth and Truthfulness (2002), to the question of whether an AI-generated completion and a synthesized dead singer can be truthful, or whether they involve a distinctive kind of deception or self-deception.

Third, consider what he would say, in the spirit of his critiques of abstraction and his appeal to Greek tragedy and moral luck, about the absence of a living agent with intentions, and about the historical embeddedness of both the composer and the singer.

Fourth, construct a serious objection from an equally competent critic who argues that Williams's own respect for truth and for the historical reality of human action should lead him to condemn the production as sentimental and fraudulent, and then give Williams's reply to that objection, making the exchange genuine rather than a matter of talking past one another.

Fifth, conclude by judging, as he would, whether the audience's powerful emotional response is a reason to take the production seriously as a truthful operatic experience, or whether it is exactly the kind of emotional manipulation he would have regarded with suspicion.
