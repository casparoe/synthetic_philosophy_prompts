I'm not a philosopher — one undergrad ethics class, years ago — but I keep hearing that Scanlon-style contractualism refuses to aggregate: that it can't count the fact that a small harm to each of a million people adds up to something much bigger than a big harm to one person, and that this refusal is either a fatal bug or the whole point, depending on who's talking. Can you restate both the refusal and the objection to it as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary — complaints as SLO violations, principles as serving policies, justifiability-to-each as a per-user appeal, the set of relevant alternatives as the design space, whatever maps cleanest — and then give me the residue report, especially on three things: (1) my hunch that "don't aggregate" is just worst-case/minimax thinking with extra steps, (2) where the error budget goes, because in my world scarcity is irreducably aggregative and a millisecond spent on one user can't be spent on another, and (3) the fact that contractualists seem to need both interpersonal comparisons of how strong someone's complaint is and a tie-break by headcount when complaints are equally strong, while my metrics are either cardinal (latency, cost) or a hard threshold (in-SLO, out-of-SLO) with nothing in between. For each of the three: what translated, what you had to fudge to make it fit, and whether the leftover mismatch is my vocabulary's fault or theirs. I care a lot more about what doesn't survive the translation than what does.
