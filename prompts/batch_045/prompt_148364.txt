The question — set jointly by me and my friend, so tell me too if it invites the wrong answer — read as follows:

(a) State Frege's puzzle about identity statements. Why do 'Hesperus is Hesperus' and 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' differ in what a speaker can learn from them, given that both are true and both name one and the same object, and why cannot their truth alone account for the difference? (b) Explain the distinction between sense and reference, what a mode of presentation is supposed to be, and how the distinction is meant to solve the puzzle; say also why, for Frege, the sense of a name is not a private idea in the speaker's mind. (c) Contrast Frege's solution with the metalinguistic alternative, the view that 'a = b' says of the two signs flanking the identity sign that they designate the same object, rather than saying something about the object itself; explain, if you can, what Frege's objection to that alternative is and how his own account differs from it. (d) Apply all of this to the following variant. Suppose a community whose language includes a single public register — a cadastre, open to everyone, always kept current — which lists every object in the domain together with every name under which that object is known, with cross-references between them. Take a speaker who has just consulted the register and found 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' entered under one and the same object. For such a speaker, does 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' still have the cognitive value that distinguishes it from 'Hesperus is Hesperus'? Does the register dissolve Frege's puzzle, relocate it, or leave it exactly as it was? Explain why, and say what your answer shows about whether a mode of presentation belongs to the speaker's mind or to the public language.

The answer in front of me runs as follows (I have numbered its paragraphs for reference). It struck me as confident and well organised; I have no opinion on whether that confidence is earned, and I would rather not let manner pass for mastery in either direction.

1. Frege's puzzle arises because there appear to be two identity statements that are true in exactly the same circumstances and yet differ in what someone can learn from them. 'Hesperus is Hesperus' tells us nothing, while 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' can be a genuine astronomical discovery. But if the meaning of a name were nothing over and above the object it stands for, the two sentences would say precisely the same thing, since the object — Venus — is the same on both sides. Their truth is the same, and the reference of a whole sentence, which for Frege is its truth value, is the same too. So meaning cannot be exhausted by reference; something further must account for the difference in what the two sentences convey.
2. Frege's solution is to bring in sense alongside reference. The reference of a name is the object itself; its sense is the mode of presentation, which is roughly the descriptive content that picks the object out — the way in which the object is given to us, as the last thing visible in the evening sky, say, or as the first visible in the morning. The sense determines the reference without being it. The sense of a whole sentence is a thought, composed out of the senses of the parts, while the reference of a sentence is its truth value. Because 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' differ in sense, 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' expresses a different thought from 'Hesperus is Hesperus', even though both thoughts have the same truth value. That difference in thought is the difference in cognitive value: the first is informative and can only be established a posteriori; the second is empty and, in Kant's terms, analytic and knowable a priori. Frege is emphatic that a sense is not a private idea in the speaker's head: one and the same sense can be grasped by many speakers, and a thought is exactly the sort of thing that can be shared, asserted and disputed, whereas an idea belongs to a single person. This is what keeps his solution from collapsing into psychology.
3. The view Frege rejects here is that identity is a relation of an object to itself, which would make 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' and 'Hesperus is Hesperus' equivalent and so leave the difference in cognitive value unexplained. His own view is that the identity sign relates the two names: 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' asserts that the signs 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' designate one and the same thing. This is what makes it informative. What we learn is something about the signs — that our language co-ordinates two different expressions with a single object — whereas in 'Hesperus is Hesperus' there is only one sign and so nothing to learn. Venus itself is untouched by the discovery.
4. In the Cadastral case the register makes the co-ordination of names to objects fully public: anyone can look up the fact that 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' figure in the same entry. A mode of presentation is simply the route by which a speaker reaches the object, and once she has consulted the register her route is the same in both cases — she reaches Venus by way of the single entry that lists both names. So for her, 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' no longer has any more cognitive value than 'Hesperus is Hesperus': the second statement is informative only for as long as one does not know the identity, and consulting the register removes that ignorance. Frege's puzzle is therefore a puzzle about what speakers happen to know rather than about meaning, and the register dissolves it. What this shows is that a mode of presentation is not a feature of the public language at all: it is a matter of what a speaker associates with a name on a given occasion, and that will vary from person to person and from day to day.
