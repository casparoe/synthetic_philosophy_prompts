I work on evaluation infrastructure for a small team that ships LLM-based "agents" — tool-using loops with a scratchpad, a persistent memory store, and a planner prompt — and I keep walking into an argument that nobody on the team or in the corners of the internet where I go to read about this can settle. Everyone involved agrees on the mechanics: at the bottom there is a transformer trained by gradient descent on next-token prediction over a large text corpus, and everything else is scaffolding wrapped around it. Everyone also seems to agree on the observable behavior: these systems will state a policy and then abandon it when the conversation shifts; they will inhabit a fictional character with apparently coherent long-term aims for hundreds of turns; they will explain their own earlier outputs in language that sounds like reasons and then reverse the explanation just as fluently. From shared premises people draw opposite conclusions. One camp says the thing is not an agent at all but a simulator: it samples from a distribution over possible speakers, so any goal-directedness we see belongs to a simulated persona that the prompt selected, not to the system, and talk of the system's beliefs, desires, or plans is a category error we inherited from talking to chatbots. The other camp says that once you put memory, tool access, and a control loop around the sampler, the resulting system is best described — best predicted, debugged, specified, and held to standards — as an agent, and that pointing at the token-level mechanism underneath is like pointing at logic gates when someone asks whether a program is a database. A third group says neither: that the real object is a distribution over roles, or a tool whose intentionality is entirely borrowed from whoever is using it, or a system-level pattern that the agent/simulator contrast was never built to describe. I have no philosophy training, so I can't tell whether this is a real fault line or three people using one word.

My working hypothesis is that at least three disputes are sharing the label, and I'd like you to tell me whether that's right and which of them is doing the most work. First, a dispute about which object is on trial: the weights, the scaffolded loop, or the deployed product with a name and a billing contact — these can come apart, because I can disable the memory store in my harness at runtime and watch the "same" system stop behaving agent-like with no change to the model at all. Second, a dispute about what an ascription of agency is for: the people on my team who want to say "agent" mostly want to license writing system prompts in the second person, tracking plan-completion rates as a metric, and logging lines like "the agent decided to retry" in a trace, while the simulator side seems to want to warn that this vocabulary will mislead us about failure modes — which suggests the disagreement is downstream of what we each want the words to do. Third, a dispute I think is genuinely empirical but that nobody appears to have run. I've started sketching what I call a commitment-erasure test: take a transcript in which the system has publicly committed to some policy, delete every trace of that commitment from its memory store and context window, present it with exactly the situation the policy was meant to govern, and measure whether anything functionally equivalent is re-derived; then, in a second condition, ask it to simulate a competent agent who had explicitly adopted the opposite policy and measure how much friction it shows. The humans on my team fail the second condition badly — they refuse, or they do it as obvious parody — and the models don't seem to fail at all. I genuinely don't know what it would settle if I ran it.

So here is what I want, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict or a survey: is this disagreement verbal, a fight about the word "agent"; or is it substantive — a disagreement about which system-level property carries agency, about what counts as evidence when the entire training signal was imitation of other agents, or about practical and moral stakes (responsibility, blame, what we owe the thing, what we're permitted to do to it) that no behavioral test will ever reach? If part of it is empirical, what would actually count as an answer rather than another round of intuitions: is my commitment-erasure idea pointed at the right thing, what would I have to see to change my mind, and what confound would you design the test to rule out? And if part of it isn't empirical, is the honest move to dissolve it into two or three separate, better-posed questions — and if so, what are they, and which one should an engineer actually care about? I've read the blog posts and I don't need them summarized back to me; argue for a position and tell me where you think it's weakest. Please answer in American English.
