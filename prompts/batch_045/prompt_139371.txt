I'm in a public policy masters program and we did a module on behavioral economics and choice architecture last term, and ever since I keep noticing a move in the readings and in seminar that I don't have a word for. It's driving me slightly nuts.

The shape of it is this. Somebody rearranges the choice environment -- a default gets flipped, a form comes pre-filled, the vegetarian option moves to the top of the menu -- and then, when asked what justifies the rearrangement, they point at the behaviour that follows it: look, most people now end up registered as organ donors, or enrolled at the higher contribution rate, or ordering the plant-based dish, so this must be what they wanted all along, we're just helping them get it. But the behaviour is downstream of the rearrangement, and the rearrangement was made precisely because people weren't choosing that way on their own. So the evidence being used to show that the intervention tracks people's own preferences is manufactured by the intervention. A second case that feels like the same animal: a savings scheme that auto-enrolls employees and ratchets the contribution percentage up a point every time they get a raise; the resulting take-up and balances are then cited as proof that workers wanted to save more, but nobody asked them, and if you'd asked them at sign-up they'd have said they couldn't afford it.

It isn't just circular reasoning, I don't think, because there usually is a real external claim in the background about people being better off. And it isn't straightforwardly the same as status quo bias or default effects, because my worry isn't that people are inert, it's that a success metric is being asked to do justificatory work it can't do. Someone in my cohort mentioned endogenous preferences and adaptive preferences, but those sound like they're about preferences shifting in response to circumstances over time, whereas what I'm after is a justificatory pattern -- an effect being retroactively read as evidence for its own warrant -- and I can't tell whether those are the same thing or just neighbours. There's also a wrinkle that makes me doubt the harsh version of my own complaint: sometimes people keep the option after the default is removed and say they genuinely like it now, so the "contaminated evidence" story can't just be asserted. Does this configuration have a name of its own, and if not, what should it be called and how does it get carved apart from the things it resembles? Same thing seems to happen with recommendation algorithms, incidentally -- we recommend it, they click it, therefore it's what they wanted -- so I'd like a definition that tells me whether that's an instance or a different pattern.

Please answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no conclusion: (1) whether there's an established name or literature for this, and if there's only near-misses say so plainly; (2) the two or three nearest concepts and exactly how each differs from what I described; (3) whether the stickiness case -- people keeping the option after the default is lifted -- defeats the complaint or just relocates it; (4) whether the recommender-system case is the same pattern, using the criterion you've proposed; (5) if there's no established term, a coined name, a one-sentence definition, and one clear boundary case on each side.
