So I work on a small team that puts numbers on one-off engineering risks. Last month the ticket was "what's the chance this particular forty-year-old spillway gate fails to open sometime in the next decade," and we sent the client 0.03 with an interval around it. My background is statistics, not philosophy: I'm comfortable with estimators and their sampling distributions, confidence coverage, cross-validation, Brier and log scores, i.i.d. assumptions, calibration checks on held-out data, and I now do nearly everything in Stan, so I'm also fluent in hierarchical models, priors-as-regularizers, exchangeability arguments in the de Finetti style, posterior predictive checks, and in saying "the data-generating process" with a straight face. What I don't have is any real grip on the philosophy-of-probability fight I keep having at lunch with two colleagues. One of them says a probability is only ever a long-run relative frequency in some genuinely repeatable sequence, so "this gate fails" is 0 or 1 and the 0.03 is at best shorthand for a class I haven't specified yet. The other says probabilities are degrees of belief constrained only by coherence, that asking whether my 0.03 is *true* is a category mistake, and that my obsession with calibration is just a habit of letting the world grade my homework when nothing requires me to. And I keep hearing about a third option, propensities, which I gather is meant to be a real single-case chance in the object that isn't anybody's belief and isn't a frequency of anything.

Here's what I want. Translate all three of those positions into the vocabulary I actually use — sampling distributions, coverage, exchangeability, hierarchical priors, regularization, calibration and sharpness, cross-validation, posterior predictive checks, "the DGP" — as faithfully as you can, one at a time, and for each one tell me what it says my 0.03 *is*, what it says the interval around it means, and which of my ordinary practices suddenly look indefensible if that position is right. I want the honest translation, not one where you quietly settle the question in advance by importing whichever framework's assumptions happen to fit my toolchain. Then give me the residue report: which claims about probability have no counterpart at all in that vocabulary, which ones only fit after being distorted into something I wouldnt recognise as my own practice, and for each piece of residue whether it's a genuine gap in my toolkit or a sign that the philosophical claim was less clear than it sounds.

Two things from my end got me thinking about this, and I'd like them used as test cases rather than treated as my theory. First, what I've started calling the granularity gap: if I refit the same model on subsets of our portfolio, the intervals have roughly the advertised coverage when I score them on averages over subsets, and clearly worse coverage when I score them ticket by ticket, and the client only ever cares about the one ticket. Is that just the reference-class problem in a different hat, or is it a separate disease? Second, something I've internally been calling the swap test: if I cannot describe what further information, or what experiment, would have made me write down a different number, I start to suspect the number isn't doing any work and I'm just decorating a guess — which seems to bother the subjectivist colleague not at all and the frequentist a lot. And the closing question I actually care about: if the honest answer is that none of the three changes anything I'd do on Monday morning, say so plainly, and then tell me whether that's a point in favour of my toolkit being complete for my purposes or a point against the debate having much substance.
