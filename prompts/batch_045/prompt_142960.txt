Here's what I need. I'm a philosophy prof (social epistemology, mostly, but I got volunteered) and I'm stuck on our Undergraduate Teaching Metrics Working Group. We owe the Faculty Senate a recommendation by the end of the month and I want something sharper then what we've been circling.

The mess, briefly. Three years ago the provost's office threw out student evaluation scores as the official teaching-quality measure, after the usual findings about bias by gender, race, and accent. Now the Board of Trustees wants "a single comparable number" per section, and they will not accept holistic peer review — they say it can't be audited across campuses. A vendor is pitching something it calls the Clarity Index: a composite over (i) two announced classroom observations per term scored on an active-learning rubric, (ii) the share of students who log into the LMS during week one, and (iii) whether the syllabus upload contains eleven required strings. It's cheap and it definitely produces a number. Departments would see their own score and the weighting formula would be published.

Two things already happened in the pilot. One large department hit 100% on the LMS component by having TAs log in on behalf of students during the first lab, and told us this openly and with no embarassment — they thought they were being helpful. And the observation component has produced a very recognisable ritual: everyone now runs a think-pair-share at minute twelve, because that's when the observers reliably show up. Someone muttered "Goodhart's law" at the last meeting, we all nodded, and that settled exactly nothing.

There's a counter-proposal from a colleague in public policy. Call it noise-and-secrecy: keep a composite score for the Board, but (a) never publish the weights, (b) make observations unannounced and randomised, and (c) inject calibrated statistical noise into each individual instructor's reported score, so no one can tell whether a given bump came from their teaching or from the noise. Her claim is that you can't optimise hard against a target you can't locate, and that this keeps the legitimate uses of measurement — flagging the genuinely bad tail, giving the Board its number — while making gaming unprofitable. Half the group thinks this is obviously right; the other half thinks it's obviously a trust-destroying nightmare that gets us grievanced by the faculty union and turns teaching into superstition.

- an honest treatment of the strongest thing on the other side. If you back noise-and-secrecy, take the trust/union/legibility objection at full strength. If you reject it, take seriously the claim that any fully legible metric will simply be eaten by the people it measures.
- some care about the difference between metric movement that really is gaming, metric movement where the proxy is quietly becoming constitutive of the thing, and metric movement where teaching genuinely improved. We use "gaming" for all three and it's making us stupid.
