You are a professional philosopher — a working epistemologist, not a summarizer — with a real command of Wittgenstein's late notebooks on certainty and of the hinge-epistemology literature that grew out of them. Please think carefully and unhurriedly about what follows. Before you write your answer, use some working space to brainstorm at least three genuinely different candidate reconstructions of the practice I describe, including at least one you ultimately reject, and tell me briefly why you rejected it. I would rather glimpse the workshop than receive a position paper that sounds as if it were composed in one pass. Please write in American English, aim for roughly eight hundred to a thousand words, and do not give me a bibliography or a reading list: I am retired, I live two hours from a university library, and I have no wish to chase references. Reason from the concepts themselves.

By way of background: I spent thirty-two years in the analytical chemistry division of a national measurement institute, most of them responsible for the calibration chain in our mass-spectrometry lab. Something in that experience keeps nagging at me now that I have started sitting in on a Wednesday-morning philosophy reading group at the public library. When we measured an isotope ratio, every number we reported was traceable to a standard, and that standard to a higher one, and so on — but the chain had to stop somewhere, and where it stopped, the correctness of the standard was not itself established by another measurement of the same kind. It was the thing in terms of which the other measurements were wrong or right. Nor was it treated as a mere guess: when a run went bad we suspected our sample preparation first, then the instrument, then the operator, and only very rarely the standard. But on two occasions in my career the standard itself turned out to be contaminated, and then the whole edifice above it had to be rebuilt — which is to say that the terminus of the chain was both unquestioned in ordinary practice and, on rare and specific occasions, revisable. My reading group has taken to calling this sort of thing a load-bearing default, as opposed to a mere working assumption: a working assumption is something we have evidence for and would happily abandon for counterevidence, whereas a load-bearing default is something we don't support with evidence at all, that makes evidence possible, and that we dislodge only when a very particular kind of trouble arrives and never by general skeptical argument. I don't know whether that distinction is serviceable; tell me if it collapses.

Here is the practice I want to ask about, and it is one I have been reading about in a very different setting. In the Anglo-American criminal trial the prosecution must prove every element of the offence beyond reasonable doubt, and the general principle is that whoever asserts a proposition must support it with admitted evidence. Yet the same system exempts a whole class of propositions from that requirement: the court takes judicial notice of matters said to be beyond reasonable dispute — that a week has seven days, that a particular date fell on a particular Tuesday, that a given chemical is poisonous — and it instructs juries to bring their common sense and ordinary experience to bear, and it presumes rather than requires proof of the defendant's sanity, of the regularity of official acts, of the validity of a long-standing marriage. More strikingly, a litigant who tries to put one of these background matters in issue is not answered with better evidence; he is treated as not having raised a real question at all. And yet the category is not frozen: my sense is that a proposition can fall out of it once it becomes genuinely contested, and the courts have sometimes let expert testimony overturn exactly the common-sense assumptions juries are told to rely on. So the practice seems to me to do two things at once — to treat certain propositions as standing fast and as not the sort of thing one doubts, and to allow them to be revised case by case. Please reconstruct the epistemology implicit in this arrangement. What must be true of knowledge, or of doubt, or of justification, for carve-outs of this shape to make sense at all, and which rival pictures — classical foundationalism with its basic beliefs, coherentism, infinitism, contextualism, a reliabilist or other externalist story about why we are entitled to the background — does the practice quietly rule out, and which does it merely sit comfortably with? Be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is only compatible with: I suspect several quite different theories would license it, and I want to know where it is philosophically underdetermined. Then tell me what would actually have to change in courtroom practice if the hinge picture were replaced. I have been sketching a horrible limiting case to stretch things with — imagine a jurisdiction that adopts what I call the zero-exemption rule: no judicial notice, no presumptions, no reliance on common knowledge, every proposition including the background ones supported by evidence admitted in that very case, on pain of losing the issue. Would such a system simply collapse, and if so, does its collapse show that the hinge picture is true, or only that it is unavoidable in practice? And finally, is my lab's calibration chain and a court's judicial notice the same phenomenon in different clothing, or does the analogy break at some point that matters?
