Okay so I have this half formed idea about Scanlon-style contractualism and the aggregation problem and I need it beaten into a fellowship proposal — 12 months, ~2 pages, for a committee that knows this literature cold but is broadly skeptical that contractualism is anything more than intuition-mongering dressed up.

The idea: I think "the aggregation problem" is actually at least three different problems and the standard dialectic only looks stuck because they get run together.

1. Cross-person headcount: N people each bear burden b, or one person bears burden b. Does the N-side win just in virtue of there being more of them, holding everything else fixed?
2. Intra-personal summation: one person bears a thousand tiny burdens across a life, or a thousand people each bear one tiny burden. My sense is contractualists are way more comfortable adding up within a single life than across lives, and nobody has said clearly what's supposed to make that asymmetry principled rather than just a habit.
3. The threshold problem: cases where each individual's burden is so small that, intuitively, that individual has no complaint at all — a huge number of people each do something to you that you literally cannot notice, but which jointly leave you badly off. Here the trouble isn't number versus size, it's that complaints seem to drop out of the picture entirely below some level.

My actual hunch is that (2) and (3) are where the real action is and (1) has absorbed most of the attention mostly because it's the easiest to write a tidy case for. But I can't tell whether that's a genuine insight or whether I've just described one problem wearing three hats.

So turn it into a proposal. I need: a precise statement of the question, sharp enough that it has a determinate answer and I'm not just producing forty pages of gesturing; why the usual ways of setting the problem up (including the standard moves for letting numbers in without aggregating, and for letting them in via considerations that aren't anybody's personal complaint) leave it open; be blunt with me if my three-problems framing collapses into one; a method section that says what I'd actually do month by month over the year and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives like just bolting a priority view onto complaints or comparing contractualism to a toy utilitarian benchmark; three to five candidate conclusions with an account of what kind of consideration would decide between them; and a real failure condition — what result would mean the year was wasted. I especially want to know the most likely way it goes wrong: my worry is that everything bottoms out in how to measure the strength of a complaint, and if the project turns out to be about that, I'd rather find out now and spend the year on it directly instead of pretending it's a side issue.
