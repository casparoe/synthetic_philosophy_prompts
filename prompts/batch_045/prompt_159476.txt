A guy in my reading group built this argument and its been bugging me for a week. We've been going through Burke and the Chesterton's-fence business, and he says the principle eats itself. Here it is more or less as he wrote it:

(1) Chesterton's fence: before you tear down an institution, a rule, or a custom, you owe it an account of why it was put there. If you cant say what it was for, you're not reforming anything, you're just demolishing something you never understood.

(2) Why that's a real principle and not just timidity: long-lived practices are where dead people's local knowledge got stored. A custom can be doing work nobody alive can see — holding a coordination together, spreading a risk, laundering a motive nobody wants to name into one that can be said out loud. And the errors are asymmetric (he calls this the "recoverability asymmetry"): if you keep something useless you've lost nothing you couldn't give up later, but if you destroy something whose function you never knew, you cant put it back, because you don't know what you lost.

(3) Nothing in (1) or (2) says anything about what the practice is. So the default in (2) attaches to any long-standing institution whatsoever, whatever it happens to be.

(4) So: for any long-standing institution, the burden of proof sits with whoever wants to remove it, and unless they can positively show that removal is safe, you keep it.

(5) Chattel slavery in the antebellum South, widow-burning in Bengal, the Magdalene laundries — all long-standing, all defended at the time by perfectly intelligent people who said, quite reasonably, that nobody could see what would replace them.

(6) So the abolitionists owed the defenders a working model of the replacement, and lacking one should have left the institution alone.

(7) That's false. Those things should have been burned down yesterday and the reformers didn't owe anyone a blueprint.

(8) So the fence is bunk as a general principle: either the move from (3) to (4) is broken, or (1) was never right in the first place.

The bit I can't let go of is that some version of the fence is obviously correct in the small. At my university there's a statute forbidding bicycles in college basements, nobody knows why, and every few years someone proposes deleting it as pointless clutter — and I always think: find out first. Basements flood, or there was a fire, or its about insurance. That instinct feels right and it is exactly (1). But I also can't accept (6), and I'd rather keep (1) than (7).

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look fine when you read it? I want the price list too — what does rejecting that step cost someone who wants to keep the fence for cases like my bicycle rule. Two exits I've tried and neither works: (a) say the fence governs small local rules and not whole institutions — but (3) looks airtight and I can't say where the size cutoff would come from; (b) say (1) is really about the reformer's state of knowledge while (4) is about who has to prove what, so the slide happens between them — my friend says that's just restating his argument in softer words and I think he might be right. Is he?

And one more thing he added that I keep turning over: even when you've got the original reason written down in the parish records and its plainly obsolete, he says the fence still applies, because the reason a thing was put up is rarely the reason it stayed up. Is that a rescue or a cheat?
