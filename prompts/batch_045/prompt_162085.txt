Got into an argument in the comments under a Fermi paradox video and someone closed it out with "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence," and I want to know what that sentence is actually supposed to prove.

The setup: a radio survey listened to something like a million nearby stars for a few years, sensitive enough to catch a beam like the strongest one we could build ourselves out to a few hundred light years, and heard nothing. The guy's claim was that this survey tells us literally zero about whether there are technological species in the galaxy, so nobody should move their credence at all. I think that's too strong, but I keep seeing that slogan do totally different jobs and I can't tell which one he needed.

So: lay out the distinct precise theses that one sentence could be standing for — the trivial ones, the substantive and defensible ones, and the ones that are just false — and for each say whether it's actually true and whether it would deliver his conclusion. The ones I can think of: a logic point about non-entailment; a point about search space (a million stars out of a hundred billion, and a few hundred light years next to a hundred thousand); a point about modality (we looked for narrowband radio, they might be doing something with neutrinos or with starlight or with something we wouldn't recognize as a signal at all); a point about duty cycle, which is the one I actually want you to spend time on; a point about how far a null result should move a Bayesian; and a point about what's fair to say out loud in a public argument, like not letting one survey get a field defunded.

The duty cycle thing. My friend calls it the lighthouse problem: suppose every technological species eventually builds a beacon that runs for a short stretch, say a few centuries, and then goes quiet forever, and the galaxy is ten billion years old. Then even if the Milky Way has been full of beacons the whole time, a survey that listens for five years is sampling an absurdly thin slice of the timeline rather than a thin slice of space, so hearing nothing is close to guaranteed on either hypothesis. He says this makes SETI nulls structurally different from not finding your keys, and that this is what the slogan is really pointing at. Is that a real distinction, or is he just dressing up "we got unlucky"? Does it change anything that we could in principle search for the remains of dead beacons instead of live ones? And does any of this depend on which hypothesis is on the table — "no technological species ever, anywhere" versus "one within a thousand light years right now" versus "one somewhere sometime"?

Give me the readings as a list, with a verdict on each and a note on whether it actually gets him his "don't update at all" conclusion. I want to bring it back to the thread.
