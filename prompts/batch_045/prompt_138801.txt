Hey — I've been trying to work out what I actually think about the inside view vs. outside view thing, and my gut verdicts on real cases don't add up to anything I can state. I'd rather you tell me which verdict has to go than watch me keep fudging.

1. Kitchen reno. My bottom-up plan says three weeks, and I did list the tasks. The reference class of comparable renos in my city says about three months, with a nasty tail. I'm going with three months. Easy.

2. My marriage. Fifteen years, we're happy, we actually argue productively. Base rate for divorce is somewhere around 40%. I refuse to apply it. And I notice that the exact thing that damns my kitchen estiamte — everyone in the reference class was confident too — applies here as well, and I still don't care. This is the one I expect you to take from me.

3. Grant application. The funder pays about 8%. But I have written reviewer comments from the last round: near miss, two specific fixable complaints, and I fixed both. I move to roughly 25%. So concrete third-party evidence buys a lot.

4. A work coding project where the prototype already runs and does maybe 70% of the thing. Bottom-up: two weeks. Reference class for same-shape projects: two months. I go with two weeks and feel fine — the plan isn't prospective, part of it is already executed. My reno plan was entirely prospective.

5. A friend's startup. Broad class: most fail. Narrow class of startups with a signed letter of intent from a first customer: much better outcomes. But that narrow class is selected on exactly the salesmanship and optimism that gets you the letter, so I trust the narrow class *less* than the broad one. That feels backwards, since narrowing is supposed to make a class more informative.

6. An online course I signed up for. Completion rates are awful. Here I do want the outside view — but I think that's only because the number is for *choosing* (drop to the easier section, block the hours), not for bracing. When I can't act on a forecast, I find myself reaching for the inside view just to feel better. Principled, or just mood?

7. Thirty small angel bets versus one big career move. For the thirty, the outside view just is my probability. For the career move, the reference class is so wide that its mean isn't a forecast about me at all, and I'd rather start from the inside view and shade toward the class than the other way round.

My own stab, which I know is incomplete: go outside unless I have information that is (a) about the case rather than about how I feel about the case, and (b) not the kind of thing everyone else in the class also had when they turned out to be wrong. Call (b) the "everyone had a plan too" clause. But #2 violates (b) and I won't give it up, and #5 makes narrowing the class actively harmful, which my rule is silent on.

One more wrinkle I keep bumping into: whenever the class I can actually observe is made of cases that were *started*, and I'm deciding whether to start, the class is downstream of the same optimism I'm trying to correct for. I've started calling that the selection shadow, and I can't tell if it's a real problem or if I'm just double-counting the bias.

So: is there one principle that gets 1 through 7, or do I need two, or three? Which clauses are doing the work, which case motivates each one, and what does it say about #2? And what does it say about the one I actually go back and forth on — quitting my job to write a novel, where there is a reference class but it's mostly people who didn't finish, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's the world talking or just the selection shadow?
