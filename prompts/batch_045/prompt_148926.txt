I work as a software engineer on machine-learning infrastructure and have no formal philosophy background, so please define any term of art you use and keep the reasoning self-contained. Also, please don't cite papers or give me a reading list — I have no way to check them, and I'd rather have the arguments themselves.

My team keeps having the same argument. Some of us think that work aimed at reducing risks from very powerful future AI systems is where nearly all the expected value sits, because of how many morally significant lives could exist over the coming centuries and millennia. Others think our ability to predict or steer anything that far out is so poor that the huge numbers are doing illegitimate work, and that we should stick to work whose benefits we can actually measure. What I want is the document an adversarial collaboration between these two camps would produce.

Treat the two parties as: (a) a committed strong longtermist — someone who holds that the expected value of our actions is dominated by their effects on the very long-run future, and that this should set our priorities; and (b) a committed near-termist skeptic — someone who holds that long-run effects are too model-dependent to act on, and that priorities should be set by effects we can measure.

Deliver three parts.

First, a numbered section of claims both could sign. I want them non-vacuous: each should be something a reasonable person might deny, not something like "both sides want to do good." I expect the list to cover what both accept about the size of the future, about the difference between affecting how much future there is and affecting what it is like, about the fact that ordinary expected-value reasoning gets strained when probabilities get very small and payoffs get very large, and about what each side would still concede to the other about the value of extinction-risk reduction.

Second, a disagreements table: for each disputed proposition, the wording the longtermist would accept for their own view, the wording the skeptic would accept for theirs, and a note on whether the disagreement is substantive or only verbal.

Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — with, for each, a case or piece of evidence both would accept as bearing on it and each side's prediction written down in advance. Please include the following, which is where I think the real argument lives.

I suspect much of the cluelessness dispute hides a single empirical question that both sides should be able to agree is empirical, even while disagreeing about the answer. Call an influence basin-shifting if it moves the future into a different long-run regime — a civilization that never becomes space-faring versus one that does, or one that settles into a stable set of values versus one that keeps changing. Call it directional washout if the effects of local actions mostly decay, and what survives is only a generic tendency whose sign we cannot recover. Have both sides say which picture they think describes technological and social history, what historical or model-based evidence they would count as settling it, and what they predict that evidence would show.

Have both sides also commit to a number: of the interventions longtermists actually prioritize, what fraction do they expect to have a long-run effect whose sign is robust across the range of plausible models of the future — call it the sign-stability ratio. Each side should state a figure and say at what value of that figure they would change their practical recommendation.

Use two concrete cases for the predictions. In the delay case, an intervention slows the development of a dangerous technology by roughly five years. In the seeding case, a fixed budget is spent making one particular set of values or institutions more likely to persist into a much larger future civilization. For each, give both sides' verdict, the condition under which they would flip, and the evidence they would count as decisive.

Finally, add a short appendix in your own voice, not the collaborators': flag the two agreed claims most at risk of being vacuous or slanted, the single disagreement most likely to be merely verbal, and — for each side separately — the one argument or finding that would in fact change its mind. Say where this document's framing is most likely to fail, and what you yourself would need to see in order to conclude that the skeptic, or the longtermist, has the better of it.
