I have somehow ended up on the committee that decides which projects in our new "evolution of mind" cluster get the first round of seed money, and I am the only member who is not an evolutionary psychologist — my own work is in developmental methods, so I am the person who is supposed to ask the annoying questions. Every application has the same shape: a human behavior, a story about why it would have paid off for our hunter-gatherer ancestors, and a flourish. Some of them feel like lactase persistence to me — a case where the adaptationist story has really been nailed down — and some feel like Kipling with footnotes. I cannot reliably tell which is which, I do not trust my gut, and I am going to have to defend the ranking to a dean, so before I read another application I want a written standard I can actually apply.

What I need is a specification: what does an adaptationist hypothesis about a human psychological trait have to deliver to count as a real explanation rather than a just-so story, where "real" is relativized to the purpose — is this worth $40,000 of pilot money and a year of someone's time. Give me the requirements with the case or consideration behind each one: the phenomenon it must explain, the case it must get right, the distinction it must not collapse, the finding it must not contradict. For each requirement, tell me whether it binds any adaptationist explanation as such, or whether it is a preference of one research program dressed up as a methodological demand. My draft list, which I half-suspect is a mix of genuine desiderata and camp prejudice, runs like this: (1) the three ledgers — the story must name the currency it was maximizing and in what units, the timescale over which selection acted, and the selective regime, rather than "fitness" in the abstract; (2) the contrast class — it must say which alternative designs were actually present in the same population and lost, since "it was useful" is otherwise compatible with everything; (3) no phantom preferences — no positing a desire or module whose only evidence is the very behavior it is invoked to explain; (4) the orphan-trait discount — a trait present in exactly one lineage has no comparative leverage, and the hypothesis should be marked down for it; (5) the lag-load constraint — mismatch stories ("stone-age brains in a modern world") have to show that the environment changed faster than the trait could have tracked it by selection; (6) reachability — the design must be reachable from the ancestral design by tinkering, not engineered from scratch; (7) the engineering signature — reliable development, precision, economy, efficiency in the trait, as you would expect of something built for a job; (8) heritability and non-social transmission — cultural transmission must be ruled out; (9) cross-cultural universality; (10) some identifiable genetic basis; (11) novel predictions rather than post hoc fitting of known findings; (12) a stated finding that would kill the hypothesis.

For each, say keep, revise, or drop, and say why, and add whatever I have missed. Three things I especially want. First: which of these are jointly unsatisfiable — my suspicion is that "generates predictions we can test now," "cites ancestral selection pressures no one can observe," and "uniquely human traits need comparative leverage" cannot all stand together, and if so the interesting output of this exercise is the impossibility result rather than the list. Second: which single requirement does the most exclusionary work, and which one, if I dropped it, would let in the worst offenders. Third: how the list handles the cases that are giving me trouble — morning sickness and food aversions in the first trimester, where pregnancy-protection and mere-byproduct stories are both on offer; the content effect on the Wason selection task, where a specialized cheater-detection device competes with general reasoning plus familiarity; a mate-preference difference that shows up in large cross-cultural surveys but reportedly shrinks in more gender-equal societies; a preference found in Western student samples that reportedly fails to appear in the small-scale societies I have read about; the mirror-image problem, where the same selective story is told for opposite findings (risk-taking shows mating competition, caution shows parental investment); and a byproduct explanation that is offered with exactly the same confidence and exactly as much evidence as the adaptation story it replaces. Any list that also rules out lactase persistence, camouflage, or the peacock's train is too strong, so use those as calibration. One further wrinkle specific to my situation: some hypotheses are fine as science but not cheaply testable, and I need to know whether "checkable within a pilot budget" is a desideratum on the theory or merely a fact about my committee.

Only after all that, and briefly, score the main contenders against whatever list survives — the massive-modularity program, human behavioral ecology, cultural-evolution and dual-inheritance approaches, and the developmental / niche-construction style of explanation. I care far more about the criteria than the scorecard, so keep the weight on where the requirements come from and what each one is for.
