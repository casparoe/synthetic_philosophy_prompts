Hey — a work thing that's turned into a theory thing and now I'm overthinking it. We're about 35 people picking between two vendors for a migration we're doing, and I fell into a rabbit hole on information cascades and now I'm weirdly spooked. As I understand it, in the classic setup once two people in a row make the same call, everyone after them rationally throws away their own private information and just copies, and the chance the group lands on the wrong option basically stops improving as you add more people. So I want to design this decision so a cascade can't even get started.

My plan is: everyone submits their pick in a private survey, results hidden, and we go with the majority. Then, to still get some benefit from discussion, we do one round where the couple of people who voted against the majority have to explain their reasoning out loud. I also want to add a "front-loading" rule: the three people with the most domain knowledge announce their pick first, and everyone else is told to weight that heavily, since the early movers' signals are apparently what end up driving the whole thing.

Things I can't figure out. One, is there an actual formula for how much weight to give the first few speakers relative to everyone later? Like, if those first three agree, what are the odds the majority is still wrong — is there a number people use? Two, someone on my team said the real cascade problem is just reputational, people don't want to be the one who stuck their neck out, so we should reward dissent and that solves it. That sounds right to me, but is that the same thing I was reading about or a different problem? And three, I keep seeing that cascades are "fragile" — does that mean one sufficiently confident person can shatter one, and should I be worried about that too, in the opposite direction?
