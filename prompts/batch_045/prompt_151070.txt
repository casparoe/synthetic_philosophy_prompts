I sat in on a reading group last week where two of the regulars spent most of an hour arguing about whether epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness is self-stultifying, that is, whether the view undercuts the very judgments about experience it was supposed to accommodate. One of them kept calling this the paradox of phenomenal judgment. I have no settled view of my own and would rather have a move-by-move verdict than a survey of the topic. I will call them Marta and Yusuf; the exchange, as nearly as I can reconstruct it, went as follows.

Marta: If experiences cause nothing, then my pain plays no role in producing my belief that I am in pain. The neural correlate does all the work, and I would believe exactly what I believe even if the pain were absent and only the neural state present. But a belief that would survive unchanged the absence of its supposed subject matter is not knowledge of that subject matter. So on epiphenomenalism my introspective judgments are evidentially worthless, and a theory that makes them worthless cannot be one that I come to know by way of them.

Yusuf: The belief and the pain share a neural cause, call it N, and two effects of a common cause can track one another perfectly: thunder tracks lightning without being caused by it. What justification requires is reliable tracking, not causal influence from the object. If N nomically necessitates the experience, the tracking is not accidental either. So the judgment is reliably produced, and nothing self-stultifying follows.

Marta: Correlation is not aboutness. Thunder does not refer to lightning. Unless the experience itself enters into whatever fixes the reference of my phenomenal concept, the best candidate for what my judgment is about is N, and then every judgment of the form that it feels like this is either empty or aimed at the wrong object. Yusuf has secured reliability at the cost of intentionality, and a reliably produced judgment aimed at the wrong object is not knowledge.

Yusuf: Two replies. First, content need not be causal; a nomic-covariation story, or a teleological one, can fix the reference of the phenomenal concept on the experience rather than on N. Second, the difficulty, if it is one, is not peculiar to this view: the identity theorist, who holds that the pain just is N, owes the very same account of how a belief produced by N comes to be about a pain. So this cannot be what refutes epiphenomenalism in particular.

Marta: Then take the challenge from the other side. Grant that tracking suffices for content and justification, and consider a harmony theory of the external world: physical objects exert no causal influence on my perceptual beliefs, which are produced by an internal mechanism, but the mechanism's outputs happen to covary with the way the world is, so my beliefs reliably track objects. If tracking is enough, the harmony theorist's beliefs are justified as well. Nobody accepts that conclusion, so tracking must be too weak a condition, and the burden is on Yusuf to say what separates the cases. The obvious candidates — that we can act on the objects, that we can check one route to a belief against another — are precisely what epiphenomenalism denies about experiences.

Yusuf: The disanalogy is that the harmony theorist's covariance is brute, whereas the epiphenomenalist's is grounded. The experience depends on N and the belief depends on N, so there is a genuine metaphysical dependence that explains why they covary rather than a coincidence. Explained covariance is not the same as unexplained covariance, and only the harmony case is unexplained.

Marta: Grounded covariance is not free. For the grounding to secure both reliability and content, the link from N to the experience must be nomologically necessary, not merely de facto, and that is a heavy commitment: it sits badly with the conceivability arguments usually offered for the dualism that motivates epiphenomenalism in the first place, and it buys the view its epistemic credentials with a law that no experience ever gives us a reason to accept. Worse, the causal-exclusion argument that motivates the view — that the physical story already explains why we judge as we do, so experiences are idle — is the very thing Yusuf leans on to make the judgment reliable. If explanatory completeness is compatible with knowing the idle item, then the paradox was never really about causation, and the common-cause reply answers a different question from the one the paradox raises.

Please go through the exchange and say, for each move in turn, whether it succeeds outright, fails outright, or succeeds only partly, and why — paying particular attention to any move that is right for the wrong reason, or right only against a weaker version of the objection than the one actually on the table. Then say who has the better of the argument overall, and what, if anything, both of them left out of account.
