Hi. I'm one of two people who cover the clinical ethics consult line at a 400-bed community hospital — the other is a nurse practitioner, I'm a hospitalist by training, and between us we have maybe one undergrad philosophy course that I mostly slept through. We get paged three or four times a month, almost always at 2am, almost always by phone, with the family at the bedside and the charge nurse wanting an answer before shift change. I've got 20–30 minutes and whatever's in the EMR. What comes out the other end is a short note in the chart that the attending signs and, if anything is even slightly unusual, risk management has to be able to defend a year later.

The consults that actually wreck me are all the same shape underneath: is what we're about to do *stopping something* or *making something happen*, and does that difference matter here. The team's working distinction is our medical director's slogan — "we don't do things to kill people, we just stop doing things to them" — which is fine as a bumper sticker and useless at 2am. And the half-remembered philosophy I have doesn't convert either: something about double effect, and something about whether you initiate a lethal sequence or just fail to interrupt one (the "direction of agency," I want to say? whoever it was seems to think *that* is the real cut, not the bodily-movement one). I can't turn either into a checklist, and I've noticed we can't even get started because "letting die" needs a baseline and I genuinely don't know which baseline to use: the disease's natural course (in which case nobody in an ICU is ever "allowing" anything), the patient's current supported state (in which case every withdrawal is a killing), or just whatever this unit normally does for this diagnosis (which feels like it makes the whole thing a local custom with moral language stapled to it). Pick one for me, tell me what breaks under the other two, and mark clearly which part of that choice is a values call I should be naming out loud in the note instead of burying.

So: write me a decision procedure my NP or I can actually run at 2am, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where it hard-stops with a no, and where it hands off to a person because the question is one of values or of facts nobody can get in thirty minutes. Please keep three questions separate that we constantly mush together: (1) is this permissible at all, (2) is this what a decent clinician should be offering here (those come apart — we can all feel it, I can't say how), and (3) is it fair to ask *this* nurse to be the one in the room, given that "I'll write the order but I don't want to be there" is a position half our staff holds and I've never decided whether it's coherent. The procedure should also tell us when the right move is to do nothing for twelve hours and re-page, and it should say what the chart note has to contain so it survives risk management — I don't want elegant reasoning that reads badly to a lawyer. Give me a one-page cheat-card version and then a slightly longer annotated version, and be straight with me about which steps are really just the intent/foresight distinction in a lab coat and which ones are doing independent work.

Then run it on three cases.

1. Terminal extubation, 58-year-old, hypoxic brain injury, family wants it now, two nurses have said they'd rather not be assigned to the room, and the resident has already put in orders for fentanyl and midazolam "for air hunger" starting before we pull the tube.
2. Deactivating a destination-therapy LVAD in a 61-year-old who is not otherwise dying — no disease process is going to stop his heart, the pump is the only reason it keeps beating, and if we switch it off he dies in minutes. The team is split on whether this is the same as extubation or is something categorically different, and I can't tell if that split is moral or just squeamishness about hardware.
3. A 79-year-old on the ventilator with a catastrophic GI bleed, where the plan everyone has quietly agreed to is "no escalation" — no more blood products, no more pressor — while leaving the vent running, which means he dies slowly over a day and a half instead of quickly. I have a horrible feeling your procedure is going to tell me this is not morally different from withdrawing and is *worse for him*, and that we only do it because it feels better to us. If that's where it goes, say so and then tell me what I'm supposed to do with that at 2am with his daughter in the room.

Finally: tell me where the procedure misfires. I already suspect it'll get gamed by framing (we're very good at describing things as "not starting"), that it'll get used to rubber-stamp whatever the attending wanted, and that getting the metaphysics right does nothing at all for a nurse who's been crying in the break room. But you'll see failure modes I haven't. And please: no citations, no reading lists, no "you should really read X" — I have no library access and I want your argument in your own words, not a map to somebody else's.
