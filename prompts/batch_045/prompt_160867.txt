I'm doing a PhD in political theory and I've been stuck on one bit of my literature review for about six months now, and I think I need someone to tell me why. The argument about open borders in migration ethics has been going round in circles for decades and nobody seems to move an inch. I've worked though the standard stuff, so please don't give me a reading list or footnotes — I want a diagnosis.

Here's how it looks from where I sit. One side says that citizenship is inherited privilege on a par with feudal birthright, that excluding someone from a labour market and a welfare state is coercion that has to be justified to them, and that the gains to the excluded person swamp anything the recieving society stands to lose. The other side says a political community is entitled to decide who joins, that this is at bottom the same freedom of association that individuals have, and that a generous, redistributive welfare state is the sort of thing that only exists among people who see themselves as a bounded 'us' with obligations to each other. Both sides then argue for another ten years.

My hunch — and it is only a hunch — is that three quite different worries get bundled together under the phrase 'open borders would undermine the welfare state', and that each side answers whichever one it finds easiest. Something like: a fiscal worry about net contributions; a political worry about whether the composition of the intake erodes electoral support for redistribution; and a constitutive worry, on which bounded membership isn't a causal input to a welfare state at all but part of what makes the institution the thing it is — take away the boundary and you haven't got a smaller welfare state, you've got a different institution going under the same name. I also suspect 'open borders' itself slides between open entry (nobody turned back, nobody deported) and open membership (straightaway access to the whole package, benefits included), so that some of the exchanges aren't even about the same proposal.

But I can't tell how much of the stalemate each of these explains, or whether something else is doing the work: a disagreement about which historical cases count as evidence at all (does mid-century labour migration into European welfare states tell us anything, given that it wasn't open borders?), or about whether 'integration' is a genuine condition on admission or just a polite word for exclusion, or just two bedrock intuitions — one about coercion, one about membership — that neither side can argue for.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, a disguised empirical question, people answering different questions under one heading, or foundational all the way down? Give me reasons rather than a survey of the positions, and work with what each side actually says rather than with a caricature of it. And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a piece of evidence, a change of question — or should I stop expecting any? Be explicit about how confident you are in each part of what you say, and tell me what would change your mind about the diagnosis, or where you think it's most likely to be going wrong.
