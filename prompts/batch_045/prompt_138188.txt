im in a philosophy of mind seminar this term (cross listed with cog sci) and we had a guest lecture last week from someone who works on whole-brain emulation. She kept using the mind:brain :: software:hardware analogy, and she used it to carry a pretty strong conclusion: that a detailed enough functional emulation of someone's brain running on GPUs would have the same conscious experiences as them, and that once you've bought multiple realizability there's nothing left for the biology to matter — neurons are just an implementation detail, like whether your code ends up on an Intel or an ARM chip. Some people in the room seemed to find this really persuasive and I do kind of feel its pull, but I also feel like the analogy might be doing all the work here, and I want to figure out how much.

A couple of things from the seminar are making me suspicious, and I'd like you to actually use them rather than give me the generic 'functionalism vs. the knowledge argument' thing.

First: my TA drew a distinction (her own terms, i dont think they're standard) between *thin* realization and *thick* realization. Thin: there just exists some mapping from physical states of the system onto the functional states such that the transition table comes out right. Thick: the mapping has to be non-gerrymandered in some way — it has to support counterfactuals about inputs the system never actually recieves, or group states in a way that isn't just hand-picked case by case. Her point was that multiple realizability is nearly trivial under thin realization, because of the old worry that under some mapping any sufficiently complex physical system implements any program you like (a weather system, a wall, whatever), and that functionalism only has content if it can demand thick realization — but then it's much less obvious that a GPU emulation is a realizer in the relevant sense.

Second: 'the same program' seems to mean three different things, and the analogy slides between them — same source code, same compiled machine code, or just same input–output profile. And there's the giant-lookup-table objection: a system consisting of nothing but an enormous table of if-then rules can match a person's entire behavioural profile without doing anything you'd want to call processing. Those seem to me to hit different readings of the analogy differently, but I can't really work out which reading her conclusion needs.

So, concretely: can you audit the analogy for me? Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the program, the source code, the compiler, the machine language, the emulator, running vs. being installed, the user, the hardware failure — and then say where the mapping breaks and which breaks actually matter for the emulation claim versus which are just noise. Which reading of 'same program' does her argument need, is that reading available to a functionalist who wants thick realization, and is there any version of the analogy that survives? Also: what does the analogy smuggle in — I suspect something about the hardware/software boundary being a natural rather than a conventional one, and about compilation preserving something real. And if the analogy is basically broken here, is there a better one for what she's trying to argue, or does the argument just not go through at that level of abstraction?

Please don't just tell me which papers to read, we have a syllabus for that. I want the actual audit.
