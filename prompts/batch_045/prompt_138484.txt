Roughly, the handout says: (a) Mises's claim was that without private ownership of the means of production planners have no information to go on and the economy grinds to a halt, so he was really predicting that socialist economies would be poor and would fail; (b) Lange showed this was flatly wrong, because a planning board can impose accounting prices on the managers of state firms, tell them to treat those prices exactly as if they were market prices — maximise profits, produce where marginal cost equals what the board tells them it is — and then nudge prices up when there's excess demand and down when there's a glut, in which case a socialist economy can simulate a competitive market and reach the same allocation, so the theoretical question was settled against Mises back in the 1930s; (c) Hayek basically conceded the theory and retreated to a practical complaint, that running all this trial and error would take too long and cost too much, which the handout then says is just a claim about 1930s accounting technology, and has been overtaken — with modern sensors, machine learning and cloud computing you could in principle update shadow prices more or less in real time, and if so the calculation problem is dead and buried and always was only a contingent, technical one.

My own hunch — and do tell me if I'm the one being unfair — is that (a) misdescribes Mises by turning a conceptual claim about what economic calculation even is into a bad empirical forecast, and that (c) is doing much more work than it admits, because it makes Hayek's worry sound like a worry about arithmetic speed when I keep hearing that his point concerned the kind of knowledge involved — local, tacit, inarticulate, constantly changing — rather than how fast you can multiply matrices.
