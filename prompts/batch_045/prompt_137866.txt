Something about securities law has been bugging me. The package looks like this: firms have to publish true material facts; insiders aren't allowed to trade on facts the other side doesn't have; and no regulator ever says a price is wrong — instead the price is exactly what courts, accountants and tax authorities use to say what a thing is worth. And yet huge volumes get traded every day, with both sides of each trade presumably thinking they're getting the better of it, which is more or less what the no-trade theorem says shouldn't happen if traders share priors and are only disagreeing about information.

What epistemology of markets does this setup presuppose? I don't mean "efficient markets" as a slogan, I mean the underlying commitments. Does mandatory disclosure only make sense on the view that prices are belief-aggregators that get better when you feed them truer inputs, and does the insider trading ban quietly give that up by assuming the informed would exploit the uninformed rather than enlighten them? Where does the no-trade result actually bite, and if it does bite, is disclosure still doing any epistemic work at all? And if we decided prices were never an authority about value but only a device for coordinating who ends up holding what, which parts of the package would have to go — would the disclosure rules, the insider trading ban, and price-as-measure-of-value all fall together, or does some of it survive?
