You are a professional moral philosopher who teaches an upper-level seminar in normative ethics, and you have published on the limits of obligation. Before writing anything, spend a little while brainstorming where the real difficulty here lies: the deliverable should be a question that cannot be answered by a student who has memorised the standard package on supererogation but cannot think with it.

I need one essay question for a 60-minute in-class exam. It must be proof against recitation: a student who simply lays out the two-faces-of-morality picture that moral philosophy has worked with since Urmson's 1958 "Saints and Heroes," the deontic/axiological distinction, the "if it is morally better, why isn't it required?" paradox, and the standard conditions (optional, praiseworthy, costly, no blame for omission) should get a middling mark, not a first.

The crux I want to test is whether supererogation is compliance-sensitive: whether an act's status as beyond duty can be eroded by the mere fact that most people now perform it. Use the case below, and rewrite it if you can make it sharper.

At t1, a student volunteering scheme asks undergraduates to pledge forty hours a year of unpaid visiting to isolated elderly residents. Two per cent sign up; participants are called exemplary, and nobody criticises those who decline. At t2, five years later, eighty-five per cent participate, it is simply what students do, non-participants are informally criticised and report feeling guilt, and a college citizenship award quietly goes to participants. Assume the good done is continuous in total hours, so there is no threshold past which further hours are pointless, and assume the cost to any individual student is unchanged.

Ask whether the pledge is supererogatory at t1 but not at t2, and what the answer shows about what supererogation consists in. Build the question so that a strong answer has to do the following: separate social expectation and criticism from blameworthiness, and say whether a genuine fourth category is needed, one that is permissible yet criticisable, praiseworthy if done and criticisable if omitted; distinguish the weak route from changed expectations to obligation from the stronger fair-play route, on which a cooperative scheme with assurance and shared reputational benefit comes into existence only once participation passes some level; confront the objection that other people's generosity cannot by itself add to what I owe, on pain of obligations arising by diffusion, without my consent and without any change in my situation; and handle the university's proposal to make the pledge compulsory, together with the thesis that some acts are worth more precisely because they are not required, so that there can be reason to leave an act optional even where requiring it would produce more of the good.

Deliver: the question exactly as it will appear on the paper; a model answer of 700 to 800 words that takes a definite position rather than surveying options; a 20-mark marking scheme organised around five or six named moves, stating what an A answer must do that a B answer need not; and, for each of four or five predictable mistakes, one line naming the mistake and what it should cost the candidate. No bibliography or citations are needed anywhere.
