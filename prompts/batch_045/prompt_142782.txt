I'm writing a short position paper on rational choice under limited time and limited computational capacity, and I want the position audited for internal consistency before I circulate it. Here, as cleanly as I can state it, is what I believe:

(1) Rationality does not require maximizing. A choice can be fully rational if the option selected clears an aspiration level, on the condition that the level is one I could defend if I were asked to.

(2) But I don't want that defensibility condition to open up a second decision problem. I set the level by judgment, then treat it as fixed for the decision in front of me: I refuse to deliberate about how much to deliberate, because that way lies a regress. Correspondingly, I think rationality is assessed at the level of the threshold-setting practice rather than act by act — a poor outcome produced by a level I could have defended is not a rational failure on my part.

(3) I accept what I'll call the collapse argument: once the costs of thinking — time, attention, error-proneness — are priced into the options themselves, an agent who satisfices is representable as maximizing an all-things-considered utility function. So I'm happy to say the difference between satisficing and optimizing is procedural, and not a difference about which verdicts are correct.

(4) I nonetheless want satisficing to come out rational in a case where further search would clearly have paid. I am buying a laptop; the second machine I look at already clears my aspiration level; I stop and buy it; I later learn that the third machine was substantially better for the same money and that I would have seen this within about thirty seconds. My verdict is that the choice was rational — not merely excusable, and not merely because a policy of stopping early does well on average across cases like this one.

(5) Normativity is constrained by capacity: "ought" implies "can" applies to thinking as well as to acting, so a decision standard that no agent with my cognitive limits could meet is not a standard that binds me.

(6) Aspiration levels that adapt — rising after easy successes, sinking after a run of failures — are a feature rather than a bug. A threshold that never moved would be badly tuned to a world in which what is achievable keeps shifting.

(7) Even so, I think we are often rightly blamed for deciding too quickly, and I do not think (5) lets us off.

Several people have told me this package is inconsistent, but they disagree about where. Please go through it and tell me which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be; rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad; for each one say precisely what I would have to give up and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Where a pair only looks inconsistent, say what the dissolving distinction is and how fragile it is. Please also indicate how confident you are in each tension you flag, and single out the one claim in your audit that you are least sure of.
