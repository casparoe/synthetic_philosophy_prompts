I'm an undergraduate taking a philosophy of physics seminar — about 16 students, nearly all philosophy majors whose physics stops at high-school algebra — and I've been asked to lead one 80-minute session on Bell's theorem and locality. The professor's only condition is that there be no lecture at the front of the room, and that the words Bell, locality, and entanglement not be spoken until the debrief; he wants the students to hit the wall before anyone names it. So what I need is a runnable activity: a game with explicit rules, a materials list, and a payoff scheme that doesn't require real money (candy or participation credit is all I have). I've been toying with the standard two-player version — partners are separated, each is handed one of two yes/no questions chosen at random, each answers yes or no without seeing the partner's question, and the pair wins a round unless both got the second question, in which case they must disagree — where anything they agree on in advance puts a ceiling of 75% on their win rate, while the quantum setup gets about 85%. But I can't work out how to stage the 85% case honestly, since the only ways I can think of to beat 75% in a classroom all involve someone peeking at both questions, and I can't put my players on opposite sides of the galaxy. I'd also like a recommendation on a design point: a friend in the physics department says I should instead run the three-player version, where each of three isolated players gets either an X-question or a Y-question, the referee only ever asks three X's together or one X with two Y's, and the parity the three answers have to match flips depending on which combination came up — so that the contradiction shows up in a single round with certainty rather than as a statistical trend over dozens. I can see the appeal of the all-or-nothing version, but I worry it hides the fact that real experiments are statistical and have loopholes, and that it makes the no-signalling point harder rather than easier. Tell me which to run, or how to sequence them, and why.

Two things I especially want the debrief to land, and one I'm not sure is stageable at all. First: I don't want anyone leaving thinking entanglement lets you send messages faster than light, and I don't want anyone leaving thinking it's just ordinary correlation either — they should be able to say precisely which condition fails and why the ability to signal survives. Second: the possible cheats should become visible rather than invisible. Every way to beat 75% in my room — passing notes or coughing between the separated players, the person handing out the questions choosing them after seeing what the players brought in with them, quietly discarding the rounds that went badly — has a counterpart in the real experimental and philosophical debate, and I want the debrief to map each classroom cheat onto its counterpart and then tell me, honestly, which of them my setup can't possibly close. Third, the bit I'm most doubtful about: is there any way to get them to feel that 85% is not arbitrary, that a box which won every single round while still not permitting signalling would be too much of a good thing, and that something beyond no-signalling must be doing the work? If that only works as a lecture, say so and tell me to cut it.

Please also give me the arithmetic of running it: how many rounds we'd need before a 75% strategy and an 85% device are reliably distinguishable, what to do in 80 minutes if that's more rounds than I can run, and what will predictably go wrong the first time — students colluding, students concluding the fix is pre-agreed telepathy, small-sample noise making the quantum case look worse than the classical one, or someone spotting a cheat I hadn't thought of. Close with a frank note on which feature of the real argument the activity cannot reproduce and which I'll have to supply in words at the end. Please write your answer in American English.
