I write about science and ideas for a newspaper, and I am working up a piece on a move I keep encountering in readers' mail: people who fully accept that some collective outcome is bad — emissions, say, or the number of animals killed for meat — but deny that anything follows for what they themselves should do, on the ground that their own contribution makes no difference. I gather philosophers call this the inefficacy objection and have a family of replies to it, some of which try to answer it by pointing to a small but non-zero probability that one's own act is the one that tips some threshold, and others of which treat that probability as a sideshow and put the weight somewhere else — on participation in a pattern, on failing to do one's share, on what the act expresses. I know this material only as a well-informed outsider and do not want to paraphrase it badly in print. So what I want is a translation, and a translation that is honest about where it breaks.

The vocabulary I want it translated into is the one I use most weeks when writing about epidemiology and about extreme-event attribution: exposure and dose; thresholds versus smooth dose-response with no threshold anywhere; attributable fraction, in the sense of how much of a burden in a population can be laid at the door of one exposure; potential-outcomes and counterfactual contrasts; the sufficient-component-cause style of picture in which several factors together complete a causal pie; statistical power, and the gap between an effect's being real and its being detectable in one individual or a small sample; and the standing distinction between an intervention's population-level effect and any given individual's risk under it. I am comfortable with all of that — reading preprints is part of the job — but I am not fluent in philosophy, so please do not assume I know what a reason for action is supposed to be beyond the ordinary sense, and please do not hand me a reading list or point me at papers: I cannot chase references on a deadline, and I need the arguments themselves, not the sources.

One structuring distinction I have been toying with, which you should feel free to discard if it does not carve anything real: I want to separate sharp-threshold cases, where the outcome changes only at discrete boundaries and my single act has some small chance of being the one that crosses one; uncertain-threshold cases, where a boundary exists but nobody knows where it lies, so from where I stand my act has a small probability of being pivotal without my being able to say when; and what I have been calling smear cases, where the response is smooth and there is no boundary at all, so that no individual act ever makes a difference to any identifiable outcome even though the sum of the acts plainly is the harm. It seems to me that the pivotality reply has real force in the first two and none in the third, and that epidemiologists handle the third by switching to a different quantity altogether — expected burden per unit of exposure, excess cases per tonne — which is a population-level rate and not a claim about any single person's effect. So: restate the inefficacy objection and each of the main replies in my vocabulary, one by one, saying for each what maps cleanly, what has to be stretched to fit, and what cannot be expressed in it at all. I am especially interested in whether the replies that appeal to fair shares, complicity, or doing one's part have any counterpart in a vocabulary built entirely out of population-level counterfactuals and per-unit rates, and whether that is a limitation of the vocabulary or a sign that those replies are doing less work than they appear to. There is a tempting maneuver here — replacing the question of whether the outcome would have differed without me with the question of whether it would have differed without me together with a great many others — and I would like to know what that question even is in counterfactual terms, since it is not a standard intervention contrast.

Finally, since I may end up quoting some of this: be explicit about which parts of your answer are settled and which are your own reconstruction, and mark clearly any place where you have coined a term or a distinction because the literature does not supply one — I need to know before it reaches print whether a phrase is mine or is established usage. Give me your verdict on whether the inefficacy objection survives in the smear case, then tell me what would change your mind about it and where your reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong: which consideration, if I raised it with a philosopher who disagrees with you, would do the most damage to your translation or to your residue report.
