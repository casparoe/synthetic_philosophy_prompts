I'm in the second year of a doctorate that sits, awkwardly, between a marine policy institute and a philosophy department, and I spend most weeks in a joint lab meeting where the same argument about unrealistic assumptions recurs in a new costume. My supervisor has an unusually long and consistent paper trail of rulings on exactly this question — in survey articles, in referee reports we have all seen circulated, and in a running argument she has been having with an economist on the floor below — and I have started collecting them, because next month I have to present our group's stock assessment model to a fishery management council and I would like to know in advance how she will react to the use we now want to make of it. Her verdicts so far, on models whose assumptions are false in ways nobody disputes: a mechanics model that treats the bob as a point mass and the pivot as frictionless, used to get the period of a real pendulum, gets waved through; so does a model with a continuum of infinitesimal voters, used to show how vanishingly small the chance of being pivotal is; so does a model of industry entry in which firms maximize expected profits, even though the managers in that industry are documented to use simple markup rules and never solve anything, provided the industry has enough churn that selection does the work; and so does a bargaining model with zero transaction costs when it is used as a benchmark or null hypothesis against which to read lab data. But she has called the very same zero-transaction-cost model objectionable when it was used to write the actual allocation rule for a permit system; she has called an infinitely lived representative household objectionable when used to score a long-run pension reform; she has called a rational-expectations asset-pricing model objectionable when used to explain the historical excess return of equities over safe assets; and she has called a unitary-household labor supply model, in which the household pools its income and has one set of preferences, objectionable when used to predict whether it matters which parent receives a child allowance — there the falsity is entangled with the very variable being varied.

The three rules I can think of all seem to fit that list equally well: an as-if instrumentalism of the Friedman sort, on which only predictive performance over the intended domain matters and the falsity of assumptions is irrelevant or even a sign of generality; a de-idealization requirement, on which a false assumption is harmless only if the model's result survives relaxing it toward the truth, so that the idealization is a limiting case rather than a load-bearing fiction; and a mechanism requirement, on which the falsity is harmless only when the model does not get its answer by way of a feature the target system lacks — the cited process has to be really there, though perhaps only as a population-level tendency, as in the selection case she accepts. My case separates them, or so I think. Our model assumes a single representative vessel with perfect knowledge of catch rates everywhere, moving effort instantly and costlessly to wherever profit is highest; vessel-tracking data show that real skippers return to grounds they know, learn from a small circle of kin and friends, treat some grounds as theirs by custom, and are slow to move. That has not stopped the model from predicting total catch and total effort for eight consecutive seasons, genuinely out of sample and better than anything else on offer, which is why the council already uses it to set the season's allowable catch. The new proposal is to use it to score a shift to individual transferable quotas, which everyone agrees would make effort far more mobile and far more responsive to profit differences, and would therefore move the real fleet toward what the model has always assumed. But the reform is also what makes the question interesting: a parallel agent-based model with sticky effort and social learning, calibrated to the same eight seasons, fits that history just as well and predicts far less consolidation of the fleet under tradable quotas than the representative-vessel model does, and the model has obviously never been tested in a regime that does not yet exist.

So: what will she say about using the model to score the reform, and why? I would like the candidate rules stated precisely enough that I can see exactly which clause each one trips over, what each predicts, and which of her precedents is the closest analogue — the profit-maximization-by-selection case and the unitary-household case seem to me to pull in different directions. I also want to know whether her verdict should be different for different outputs of the same model, since the council wants two things out of it, a total catch number and a claim about how much the fleet will consolidate, and it is not obvious to me that one verdict covers both. Give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and then tell me what further case would actually settle which rule she is running: I have access to a neighbouring fishery where tradable quotas were introduced a decade ago, and to vessel-level data both where quota trading turned out to be thick and where it stayed thin for customary reasons, so I can in principle construct almost any test she would consider fair.
