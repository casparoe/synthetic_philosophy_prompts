I run a once-a-month philosophy discussion group at the public library — mostly retirees like me, 10 to 14 of us on a good night, 90 minutes in a room with a whiteboard, bad coffee, and a hard stop when the librarian flips the lights. Nobody there has done philosophy since a required freshman course. I'm the one who organizes it; I spent about thirty five years as an analytical chemist, mostly instrumentation and a lot of arguing about whether a signal was real or an artifact, and I've gotten into philosophy in the last couple of years the way you'd expect — messily, and with a bias toward questions where I think someone is fooling themselves with a measurement. This month's topic came out of an argument after a meeting: a friend said "well of course Gen Z doesn't answer the phone," and I said something like "that's not a kind of thing, that's a confound," and now I'm on the hook to run a session on whether generations are real kinds of things or just lines we've drawn on a continuous smear.

The bit I keep pushing, from the science side, is what we'd have called an identifiability problem: if you know a person's birth year and the year they were surveyed, you automatically know their age, so "age effects," "period effects," and "cohort effects" can't be pulled apart from one table without sneaking in an extra assumption — three stories, one dataset, and the data can't tell you which is right. And I've been toying with a distinction I made up and can't find anywhere, so maybe it's confused: call it the window picture versus the wall picture. On the window picture a generation is a moving band, say any fifteen years of births, so the population gets continuously re-sorted and two people a year apart are almost always in the same generation together; on the wall picture there's a hard boundary, and someone born in 1964 and someone born in 1965 are on opposite sides of it forever. I'd like the group to feel that both pictures can be fit to the same numbers, and that the wall picture is what all our naming ("Millennial," "Gen Z") actually assumes.

So: design me a runnable activity, not a lecture, where something like this shows up in what people do. What I have in mind — change it if you have better — is a prediction game with tiny stakes (poker chips, or just tally marks and bragging rights): you make up a small fictional survey dataset, maybe 36 respondents spread over birth years 1950 to 2005 with a handful of attitude items, and small teams each get a different rule for sorting people into groups and then compete to predict held-out answers. One team sorts by the standard generational labels, one sorts by age band only, and one gets something I find very funny and also a little disturbing — a deliberately silly partition, say grouping by the last digit of the birth year, or by something with no causal story behind it at all. The kicker I want, if it's honest to arrange it, is that the silly partition does better than anyone expects on at least some items, so people have to sit with the fact that a grouping can earn its keep predictively while being, on the face of it, gerrymandered nonsense. No lecture up front, and please don't let the phrases "natural kind" or "social kind" out of the bag until the debrief. Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific things — the age/period/cohort tangle, the window versus wall thing, whether the labels might be feeding back into how people answer, whether a kind needs sharp boundaries to be useful — plus an honest note about which part of the question the game can't reach and I'll have to say out loud.

Give me the design as prose first, then two structured bits. A run-sheet table with exactly these columns: phase | minutes | what participants do | what the facilitator does and specifically does not say | which part of the metaphysics this moment is meant to surface | most likely way it goes wrong and the fix. Then a JSON object with exactly these fields: "materials" (what gets printed or brought, no laptops needed if you can manage it), "dataset" (the 36 fictional respondents with birth year, survey year, and their answers), "partition_cards" (each team's grouping rule with its actual cut points), "scoring_rule" (how predictions are scored and how the winner is decided), "reveal_sequence" (what gets uncovered, in order, and when), "debrief_questions" (five or six, in the order I should ask them), "moment_to_idea" (a list pairing moments in the room with the thing they're supposed to make vivid), "failure_modes" (what will predictably happen the first time I run it, including the version where someone in the room is a retired sociologist or just refuses to play), "cannot_show" (what the format can't do and I have to supply myself), and "variants" (what to cut for a 45-minute version, and what to change if twenty people show up). Assume I have a whiteboard, a printer, and no internet in the room.
