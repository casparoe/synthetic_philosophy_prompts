I have no academic background in philosophy, but I've been reading on my own for a couple of years, mostly early Chinese material, and I've got stuck on the butterfly episode in the Zhuangzi — the one where the narrator dreams he is a butterfly and, on waking, says he doesn't know whether he was Zhuangzi who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is Zhuangzi. I keep coming across it treated as an ancient version of the dream argument: maybe this whole life is a dream, so maybe nothing I take to be real is. But the more I sit with it, the less sure I am that the episode is doing the same job as that argument, and I'd like to spend my limited remaining time where it actually matters.

Please act as a professional philosopher with a working knowledge of both classical Chinese thought and contemporary epistemology, and think the question through carefully before you commit to an ordering — I'd rather have a judgement you've tested than a tidy list.

What I have is roughly forty hours of evenings over the next three months, no classical Chinese, no library access, and no way to look anything up. My aim is to finish with a position I can defend in a long essay for general readers on what that episode is really doing, and on what, if anything, follows from it for how one should live. I don't want reading lists or citations — I can't follow them up, and I'd rather have arguments I can test in my own head.

Here are the questions as I've provisionally sorted them. Please tell me which of them deserve the forty hours, in what order, and — more importantly — why, and feel free to add ones I've missed or to split or merge mine:

1. Containment versus identity. Is the doubt in the episode about whether the current scene is contained inside a dream (is the world really there at all?), or about which thing the dreamer is (was he a man or a butterfly?)? Does it matter that the second reading doesn't obviously threaten the existence of anything, only the boundaries between things?

2. Symmetry. The Cartesian-style dream doubt I've met seems to lean on waking being the default and dreaming the exception that has to be ruled out. Here the two candidate states seem to be presented as on a par, with no privileged standpoint from which to adjudicate. Is that symmetry genuine, does it do real work, and does it make the doubt stronger or does it hollow it out into indifference?

3. The persistence question. If the change between Zhuangzi and butterfly is being taken seriously, is there something that persists across it — a subject, a standpoint, a name — or does the episode presuppose that there isn't? Which way does the text need it to go for the rest of what it says to hold up?

4. What follows for living. The episode is often read as therapeutic: take the boundaries between things less seriously and you'll be less knocked about by fortune, status, death. But is that upshot actually delivered by the sceptical reading, or only by a separate claim about things being equal or perspectives being interchangeable? I strongly suspect some of the usual implications are overrated or simply don't follow — that nothing is real, that nothing matters, that one should just drift — and I'd like you to say which, and why they fail.

5. Which of these are really scholarly rather than philosophical. Some questions I could sink years into and never settle from an armchair: which stratum of the book the episode belongs to, what the vocabulary would have connoted to its first audiences, how much of the apparatus comes from later commentary. I'd like you to mark explicitly which questions are of that kind and should be set aside, bracketed as assumptions, or converted into something I can reason about without sources.

Please defend the ordering rather than just assert it. I want to know what criteria you're using — how much downstream depends on a question, whether it's tractable for one person with no languages and no library, whether the answers people give are predictable in advance, whether getting it wrong corrupts everything else — and how you're trading those criteria against each other. Then tell me what would move a question up or down your list: what kind of consideration, available to someone reasoning alone, should make me re-rank.
