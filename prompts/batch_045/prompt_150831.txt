hey — I'm running a 45 minute session at the philosophy cafe I help out at, and I've foolishly agreed to do panpsychism, or more precisely the combination problem, since that's the bit that's actually interesting and the bit nobody outside the literature has heard of. No handouts (they won't read them), whiteboard only, audience is smart adults with no philosophy background who've maybe read one pop-sci book; they will arrive thinking panpsychism is the view that your toaster has a rich inner life, and there's one guy who's read a fair bit of Chalmers and will be restless unless I give him something to chew. So please don't explain the combination problem to me — I've read enough to follow it — plan how I should teach it: the order the moves have to come in, what depends on what, and how many of the 45 minutes each bit deserves.

Here's my rough sense and where I think it breaks. I want to start by getting them to *grant* the micro-subjects as a dialectical move — you accept tiny subjects somewhere down at the bottom because it's supposed to get you out of the hard problem, not because you like the idea — and only then spring the combination problem as the bill coming due. Is that the right opening? My worry is the Chalmers guy will say, somewhere in the first ten minutes, that this is just the hard problem in a new hat, and I dont know whether to head that off before I let them grant the micro-subjects, or to use it, or to concede half of it and walk on.

Second, the carrying example. I've been toying with a wall of a thousand tiny screens, each showing something different, and asking what would have to be true for it to be correct that the wall itself is watching one thing. I half like it and half dont: I think it sneaks in a second-order watcher standing behind the screens, and it makes combination look like it happens *somewhere*, in a space, when part of the point is that there is no room for it to happen in. Tell me what the one example has to do and what it has to avoid, and either fix mine or replace it. Misconceptions I'm expecting, in rough order: toaster consciousness; "it's just parts making a whole, like cells and an organism"; "emergence" doing load-bearing work as a word; "fine, so then the whole universe is conscious"; and someone bringing up quantum consciousness, which I want to close down without being a jerk about it. Where in the sequence should each be defused, and should the "why this grain — why cells and not molecules, or hemispheres, or the whole nervous system?" question be in there at all, or is that a door I shouldn't open in 45 minutes?

Last thing: I want one moment where they have to commit out loud. My idea is to start removing screens from the wall one at a time and ask when the wall stops watching — does the watching fade out gradually or does it stop all at once — on the theory that either answer costs them something they didn't want to pay. Is that the right place to apply pressure? Then give me the question I should end on that would show me they'd got the *problem* rather than just absorbed the mood of the room, including what a confident-sounding wrong answer to it would sound like, because I can already hear a couple of them. And tell me what I should explicitly announce I'm not going to explain, and why skipping it is fine at this level.
