It felt clear enough while I was reading it, but some of it is definately too neat, and I don't trust myself to spot where. Here's the central passage, more or less as written:

Vague predicates like 'bald', 'tall' or 'heap' have borderline cases, and the question is what to say about 'Jones is bald' when Jones is one of them. Two theories dominate. Supervaluationism says that our language simply fails to settle on a single sharp cut-off: instead there are many admissible precisifications of the language, each drawing the line somewhere slightly different, and no one of them is the correct one. A sentence is true if it comes out true under every admissible precisification, false if it comes out false under every one, and if the precisifications disagree it has no truth value at all. That is why supervaluationists have to give up the law of excluded middle: in a borderline case 'Jones is bald' isn't true and 'Jones is not bald' isn't true either, so the disjunction 'Jones is bald or Jones is not bald' cannot be true. Epistemicism goes the other way and keeps bivalence and all of classical logic: there is one and only one sharp cut-off, it is fixed by nothing more than how speakers in fact use the word, and vagueness is purely a matter of our ignorance of where it falls — ignorance which, since knowledge requires a margin for error, we can never in principle eliminate. But the decisive objection to epistemicism is simple: if there really were a cut-off, then of two men differing by a single hair one would be bald and the other not, and we could simply look at them and see which is which; since no amount of looking settles it, there is no such cut-off and epistemicism is refuted. Supervaluationism is thus the more honest view — it admits that no line exists, and it blocks the sorites by refusing to say of any particular grain that it is the one that turned a non-heap into a heap, at the cost of making some classical logic fail.
