I help run a tiny volunteer concert series, and for two years five of us have been pouring our savings and weekends into converting a disused chapel into a performance space. We have a board vote coming up on whether to put in the last chunk of money, and one member keeps invoking the sunk cost fallacy at us, so I went back to a chapter from a popular behavioral-economics book my book club read last year. Here is roughly what it says: the sunk cost fallacy is our tendency to keep investing in something because of what we have already put into it — time, money, effort — even when that investment cannot be recovered and the sensible thing would be to stop. Economists call unrecoverable expenditure 'sunk,' and the rational rule is that it should play no role at all in the decision: the past cannot be changed, so a rational person weighs only the marginal costs and benefits still ahead, exactly as she would if the project were handed to her today for free. Continuing because you do not want what you have already spent to have been for nothing is therefore always a mistake, and it is the same mistake whether the currency is cash, years of work, or emotional energy. Psychologists trace it to loss aversion: we hate realizing a loss, so we gamble more on the chance of avoiding it, which is why the same failing turns up in birds and other animals and is known in biology as the Concorde fallacy. The practical upshot it recommends is a single test — at each decision point, ask what you would do if you had invested nothing yet, and do that.

Two things make me distrust how clean that is. First, my brother-in-law says the classic questionnaire studies (the lost theatre ticket, the ski trip) are confounded, because in real life a more expensive ticket or trip usually is a better one, so subjects who say they would carry on may just be drawing a sensible inference rather than committing a fallacy — which, if right, would mean the effect is smaller or shakier than the book implies. Second, a friend of mine, who has finished three long projects I would have abandoned, defends what he calls the completion rule: never abandon a project once begun unless the case for stopping is overwhelming. His argument is that he knows his future selves, that each of them will always have a good-sounding reason to quit, and that he gets more done across a lifetime by being the kind of person who does not walk away from a half-finished thing — even though on any particular occasion, with the cost already spent, continuing will look like it is being done for the wrong reason. That sounds to me like it might be a genuine rational exception to the book's flat claim, but it also sounds like exactly the sort of thing a stubborn person would say to justify himself, and I cannot tell which.

So please take the paragraph apart for me: for each of its claims, tell me what is standard economics or psychology that I should simply keep, what is a simplification a defender of the orthodox view would accept anyway, and what is actually wrong or a conflation of things the literature keeps separate — and where it goes wrong, what the more careful version would say. I especially want a verdict on three things rather than a survey of opinions: whether the 'as if you were starting fresh' test is equivalent to ignoring sunk costs or quietly a different and more misleading test; whether my friend's completion rule is a real counterexample to 'honoring sunk costs is never rational' (because a policy can be rational where a one-off act is not) or just the fallacy with better public relations; and whether my brother-in-law's confound, if it is a real one, changes how much weight the whole literature deserves. Please commit: say plainly which way you think each of these goes and why, rank the three by how confident you are, and tell me which one you would change your mind about most readily and on what argument. Do not send me to papers or name studies or authors — I cannot chase them up, and I have to make this argument to four tired volunteers around a folding table, not win it by citation.
