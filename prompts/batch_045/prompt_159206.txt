I run a small reading group in modal epistemology, and after two terms spent on the conceivability literature I have a suspicion I would like tested rather than confirmed: that the dispute between those who treat ideal positive conceivability as a genuine guide to possibility and those who treat it as a defeasible heuristic whose verdicts need independent support from counterfactual reasoning has stopped moving for structural reasons, not because either side has failed to notice an argument. What I want is a diagnosis of the shape of the disagreement, not a survey of the literature and not a verdict on who is right.

The two positions, as I understand them. The conceivability theorist holds that when a scenario can be positively conceived under ideal reflection — where ideal means: no logical or conceptual confusion, no ignorance about the non-modal facts, and unlimited time and attention — its possibility is thereby established, at least for the kind of possibility that figures in metaphysical debates, and that this is what licenses argument from the conceivability of a philosophical zombie or of the negation of an identity claim to its possibility. The critic replies that this is either close to trivial or unsupported: we have no independent way of telling when we have reached the idealised verdict rather than merely a vivid and stable intuition, our imaginative capacities are known to fail in patterned ways, and the cases in which conceiving seems to deliver possibility are exactly the cases in which we already had the modal knowledge by other means.

My hypothesis, to be argued for or against, is that part of the dispute is verbal, because the qualifier ideal is doing the work: if ideal conceiving is partly constituted by not going wrong about what is possible, then the guiding thesis collapses into the truism that modal knowledge is knowledge of modal truth, and the appearance of a substantive epistemological claim is an artifact of the vocabulary. But I do not think that is the whole story, and I suspect the residue is a hidden semi-empirical question about the reliability of a specific cognitive capacity.

I would like the diagnosis to do the following: say which part, if any, is verbal, and whether there is a reformulation of the guiding thesis that is neither vacuous nor a bare restatement of what the other side denies; say whether the residue is a factual question about the reliability of imaginative simulation and offline counterfactual reasoning, and what the well-posed version of that question is; and say whether the two sides are answering different questions under one label — for instance, how agents actually come to know modal truths, as opposed to whether conceptual or a priori reflection can entail modal ones.

Two cases I want the diagnosis to engage rather than skate past.

First, the computational-ceiling case. Take a proposition whose truth, if true at all, is necessary but whose necessity is not accessible to any amount of ordinary reflection by a bounded agent — say the claim that a particular Diophantine equation has no solution, where the equation is chosen so that no human reasoner, and no community of human reasoners, can settle it either way, and where the negation may be perfectly conceivable to everyone. Does the conceivability theorist idealise away computational boundedness? If so, say what grip we retain on the resulting notion of ideal conceiving and why the thesis does not thereby become untestable in principle; if not, then concede that conceivability is admittedly a partial guide and say what the interesting question then becomes — a question about which region of modal space the capacity covers, and why that region rather than another.

Second, the calibration proposal. Suppose we try to settle the reliability question empirically by checking conceivability verdicts against modal facts we know independently — facts about what is possible relative to the laws of a well-understood physical theory, or about small, tractable mathematical or logical spaces — and then ask whether reliability in those domains licenses any confidence about verdicts in the metaphysical cases the debate actually cares about. I want your view on whether the transfer step can be made non-question-begging, or whether the domain difference is itself one of the cruxes.

Relatedly: address the asymmetry between conceivability as evidence of possibility and inconceivability as evidence of impossibility, and whether the Kripke-style cases of necessary a posteriori truths are best read as counterexamples to the thesis, as scope restrictions on it, or as data that the two sides are simply describing differently.

Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction between notions of conceivability, a formal or computational limit result about what any bounded agent can know of modal space, a pre-registered study of whether conceivability verdicts converge among trained respondents under prompted idealisation, a change of question, or nothing at all. Be explicit about which crux, if it were settled, would move at least one of the two sides, and state plainly if you think some part of the debate is ill-posed rather than hard.
