I'm in the upper sixth and I captain the school debating society. Our last motion of the year was "This House Believes That Taxation Is Theft"; I spoke against it in every round and won most of them, mostly by attacking the idea of a pre-tax baseline. What I can't do is say what I actually believe, and I've been asked to present my own positive view to the philosophy club next week rather than just rebutting other people's. I'd rather find the holes now than in front of thirty people. Here is roughly where I think I've landed.

1. Property rights are legal conventions. There is no morally loaded, pre-legal answer to the question of what is yours for the state to be measured against, so the sentence "the state takes what is yours" has already gone wrong before you reach the word theft.

2. I don't think hypothetical consent does any justificatory work. The thought that I would have agreed to this arrangement from a fair starting point doesn't bind me; only my actual consent would.

3. Relatedly, I don't think benefits I never asked for and could not have refused can generate a debt. If a firm resurfaced our road overnight and then invoiced every household on it, I'd say they had given us a gift, not sent us a bill, and I don't see why the state's case is better merely because it is the state. I picked this up somewhere in the libertarian reading I did over the summer and I can no longer reconstruct why it struck me as obvious.

4. But I do think there is a real duty to pay one's taxes, and I don't think it rests on consent. Someone born here who has never voted, wants nothing from the state, avoids every public service he can avoid, and would emigrate tomorrow if he could, still owes what the statute says he owes. I find the reply that he is free to leave unsatisfying, but I can't say why.

5. I think the person is different in kind from property. A state that takes thirty percent of my wages and a state that takes one of my kidneys are doing morally different things, and I don't want that difference to consist only in the law's happening to permit one and forbid the other. At the same time I think the standard reply to the forced labour line — that nobody makes you work, since you can always take leisure instead — is basically right, and I've used it in rounds; yet I'd also say that a system taking everything you could earn above bare subsistence would be forced labour.

6. I think redistribution toward the least advantaged is a demand of justice rather than charity, and that a tax system under which some live in destitution while others live in grotesque luxury would be unjust even if it were lawful and properly enacted.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and how badly, which only look as though they do, and for each real tension what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost me. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally serious, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most at once. Please also flag where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong: for each tension you identify, say what argument or distinction would persuade you it was only apparent, and for whichever repair you recommend, say what would change your mind about it.
