I'm a high school senior, and I got into an argument about the old ship puzzle — the one where a ship has its planks replaced one at a time — after helping a friend's uncle rebuild a truck. What has stuck with me since is that we don't only argue about this as a puzzle; we settle it every day by rule, and I'd like to know what those rules assume.

As far as I can make out, where I live a car's legal identity is carried by its vehicle identification number and its title. You can replace the engine, the transmission, the body panels, the interior, and it remains the same car. If it is wrecked badly enough that the insurer pays out instead of paying for repairs, the title is branded as salvage, and if it is rebuilt and inspected the brand changes to something like rebuilt — but the number, and so the car, is unchanged. Yet a running car built up out of the parts of two wrecked cars is, as I understand it, treated as a new vehicle and issued a fresh number, rather than as one of the two originals, or as both of them, or as two cars sharing a body.

Two things puzzle me. First, the insurer's decision that a car is a total loss looks like an official declaration that this car is gone, and yet the same paperwork keeps treating it as the same car afterwards. Second, the whole thing seems to hang on a stamped plate: if someone swapped the identification plates between two otherwise identical cars overnight, the law's answer to "which car is which" would swap with them, as far as I can tell.

So: what view of identity over time does this pattern of rules actually presuppose, and which rival views does it quietly rule out? Please separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where the practice is underdetermined — where several different theories of identity would all license exactly these rules, so that the rules reveal less about identity than they appear to. Does the practice even need a metaphysical theory, or only a public and cheap way of tracking whatever identity turns out to be, and can those two possibilities be told apart by looking at the practice alone? And is it consistent with itself? It treats the rebuilt wreck as the same car for registration, but as a different kind of car for insurance and resale.

Now suppose the system were rebuilt around a graded view instead. Imagine a title that carried a continuity record rather than a single number: the share of the car's original parts still on it, whether the chain of possession was ever broken, whether the chassis and drivetrain had ever been separated from each other — so that instead of same-or-not you got something like a continuity profile buyers could read. What would have to change in the rest of the practice if that replaced the all-or-nothing rule? Would anything really be gained, or does the blunt yes-or-no convention do work that a graded one cannot, for theft, recalls, liens, and resale?

I haven't studied philosophy, so please define any terms you use and keep things concrete. Please don't give me reading lists or citations; I can't judge them, and I care about the argument rather than who made it. For each of the main things you claim, say how confident you are, and single out the point you are least sure of. Also tell me if my description of how titles and identification numbers work is wrong, and whether your answer would change if it is.
