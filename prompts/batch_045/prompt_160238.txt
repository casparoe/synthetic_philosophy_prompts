So I'm on a tiny team at a small education funder — we hand out renewal grants to after-school tutoring programs, maybe forty of them a year — and we've been going in circles for weeks on how to decide who gets renewed. We can't measure learning directly inside a grant cycle (no common pre/post test, and we'd never get one past the programs anyway), so the proxies on the table are things like median minutes per session, share of kids who come back for a second session, gain on a short in-house quiz, or a quarterly spot-check where one of us sits in and rates a session against a rubric. Every time we get somewhere, my colleague Dev just says "Goodhart's law" and the meeting disolves. His move is basically: any of these stops being informative the second programs know it's what the money rides on, so we may as well renew everyone on vibes and relationships.

I've seen the law stated in two shapes and I suspect they aren't the same claim: "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes" versus the version everyone repeats in evaluation circles, "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." One sounds probabilistic and about statistical relationships used for control; the other sounds categorical and about a measure losing its quality. I've also been trying to sort out failure modes with my own dumb labels, and I'd like you to tell me which of them are actually distinct and which collapse into each other: signal rot (the proxy just stops tracking the thing we care about), effort theft (programs shift effort off the unmeasured parts of tutoring that actually matter), cliff gaming (there's a threshold so everyone optimizes right at the boundary), and number laundering (the reported figure is simply falsified, so we don't even learn the proxy). And a related confusion: is this supposed to be a claim about strategic humans responding to incentives, or about optimization pressure as such — does it cover the cases people bring up from ML where a system with no intentions at all drives a proxy number up in a way no one wanted? That seems to matter a lot for which remedies make sense, so please also say, per reading, whether things like keeping some measures hidden, rotating metrics, using several at once, random audits, or deliberately picking a proxy that's constitutively tied to the goal rather than merely correlated with it are even coherent responses.

What I actually want to bring to the next meeting is a menu, not a verdict: lay out the distinct precise things Goodhart's law could be claiming, from the nearly vacuous (surely something like "correlations are contingent and can break" is true of everything and tells us nothing) up through the substantive and empirically supported, up to the radical reading that would genuinely get Dev his conclusion that no metric can be used for allocation at all. For each one say whether it's a platitude, a defensible empirical generalization, or straightforwardly false — and, importantly, which readings a fan of the law would themselves concede are too strong, and whether the strong version has quietly become unfalsifiable (if a metric survives targeting, well, it just wasn't pressured hard enough yet). Then tell me which reading Dev has to be relying on for his "so don't bother" conclusion, and whether that reading survives. Also worth flagging: on some readings the number keeps looking fine while the actual tutoring gets worse, and on others the number goes bad while the tutoring is unchanged — if those are different failures, say so, because I think Dev is sliding between them and it's definately doing work in his argument.
