I'm a political-theory PhD student working on the governance of dual-use technologies, and I was at a small workshop dinner last month where two of the other participants got into an argument that I haven't been able to settle for myself. I'd like a careful adjudication of the exchange rather than a general essay on AI risk.

Two stipulative definitions, so we're on the same page. By the competitiveness constraint I mean the pressure on any actor developing a powerful general technology not to fall behind its rivals: because influence, survival, and access to the frontier track relative capability, any self-imposed cost on capability tends to be selected away, so the actor who restrains himself does not prevent the capability from being built — he only ensures that someone else builds it. By the alignment tax I mean the capability cost of making a system safer: the performance, generality, or usefulness you give up when you train or configure a system to refuse harmful requests, submit to oversight, or operate under restrictions. The dispute was about whether a safety-motivated developer should accept such a tax at all.

M1 — Marcus (an ML researcher at a frontier lab) opened: under a competitiveness constraint, paying an alignment tax is self-defeating. Whatever safety properties he buys for his own system he buys at the cost of being slower, and the lab that declines the tax reaches the frontier first and builds the system anyway. The tax therefore does not reduce the total risk; it reassigns who is holding the risk at the moment it materializes. If you care about outcomes rather than about the cleanliness of your own hands, the rational policy for a safety-minded lab is to keep its tax as small as possible and try to be the one that arrives first, carrying whatever safety techniques it can carry without losing the race.

M2 — Lena (a political economist who works on dual-use regulation) gave two replies. First, the argument proves too much: applied consistently it tells you to skip every costly practice — evaluation, interpretability, security, internal review, anything that takes calendar time — so it cannot be an argument against the alignment tax in particular. It is an argument against spending resources on safety at all, dressed up as an argument about incentives. Second, and worse: the race does not select for the lab with the best safety methods, it selects for the fastest one, and speed correlates with having cut precisely the verification work that would tell you whether your safety methods do anything. Winning the race therefore does not buy you a safe system, it buys you an unverified one; the selection pressure favours actors whose safety case is cheapest to assert and least checked. So let the good guys win the race assumes the point at issue, namely that whoever wins will still know what they have built.

M3 — Marcus conceded the second point but said it pushes toward a distinction rather than toward a larger tax. Some taxes are distributed: safety training degrades general capability in ways that are hard to localize, with refusals bleeding into reasoning and coding. Others are localized: the cost shows up in a narrow, monitorable place, such as declining a well-defined class of requests, running under inference-time oversight, or being confined to a sandbox. Under a competitiveness constraint only the localized tax is worth paying; a distributed tax is close to pure loss. He added that the headline numbers for the distributed tax are inflated: a large share of the apparent capability loss is an artifact of scoring, since a model that declines an ambiguous or dual-use prompt is recorded as failing it. Once you score with a metric that separates cannot from will not — abstention-aware scoring, where declining is its own outcome rather than an error — much of the measured tax disappears. The real tax, he concluded, is smaller than the usual framing suggests and concentrated where you can see it.

M4 — Lena granted the artifact point for benchmark conditions but said abstention is not free in deployment, and deployment is where the tax actually gets paid. Systems increasingly run inside scaffolds — agents with retries, fallback models, and tool use — and in that setting a decline is not a safe outcome but a failed subtask that triggers a retry, a hand-off to a weaker but unfiltered model, or a human override that rubber-stamps whatever the model would have said. She called this tax pass-through: the cost of the tax is not borne by the party that imposed it but pushed downstream, and downstream actors have every incentive to route around it — fine-tuning refusals out, routing to open-weight models, wrapping the model in code that supplies the missing step. A tax that looks locally rational for one firm can thus raise risk at the level of the whole system by relocating the activity to venues that are harder to govern. The reply to the tax is small and localized is not so pay it, but small where you measured it and not where it lands.

M5 — Marcus answered that this is an argument about which tax to impose, not about whether to impose one. If downstream actors can strip it, it was never safety: post-training refusals, system-prompt restrictions, and filters in the serving layer are etiquette. What you want is a tax whose cost is structural and whose removal is expensive — hardware-level controls, capability limits built into the training pipeline rather than bolted on after it, provenance or attestation requirements that make an unmodified model unusable downstream. Choose taxes by their removability, not by their size. And the pass-through point cuts both ways: if strippable restraints will in fact be stripped, then imposing one and claiming credit for it is worse than imposing nothing, because it manufactures assurance that is not there. Safety theater is cheaper than safety, and a firm under competitive pressure will buy the cheap version.

M6 — Lena said she thought that was right and that it generalizes further than Marcus was taking it. The competitiveness constraint applies to one currency, capability. But firms also compete for the licence to operate: regulatory forbearance, procurement, insurance, the willingness of states to let them keep building, the ability to hire people who want to work on this. Safety spending buys legitimacy, and where permission to deploy is the scarce thing rather than raw capability, that makes the tax not a cost but a competitive asset — the tax can be negative. The deeper point, she said, is that both of them had been treating the constraint as a fact about the technology, like latency, when it is a fact about an institutional regime: the tax is a cost relative to a set of rules, and the rules are themselves something actors can try to change. Whether restraint is self-defeating is endogenous to the regime, not a fixed parameter of the situation.

M7 — Marcus replied that this is an end-state story and that the transition is the whole problem. A market in legitimacy only exists once states have begun to regulate, and states regulate after something has gone visibly wrong, so wait until permission is the binding constraint means paying the tax only after part of the risk has materialized. And legitimacy is gameable in exactly the way her downstream actors game the tax: it is cheaper to acquire the appearance of restraint — publishing a framework, joining a voluntary body, releasing evaluations you selected yourself — than to bear its costs. The two-currency argument shows that the tax is not necessarily self-defeating; it does not show that, in the regime we are actually in, the actors who bear it will outlast the ones who do not.
