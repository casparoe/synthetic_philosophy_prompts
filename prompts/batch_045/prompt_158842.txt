Hi — I'm a PhD student in philosophy here and I've somehow ended up organising our termly "set theory and philosophy" seminar. Two of the set theory people in the department have been having the same arguement in the corridor for about a year, and I've finally got them to agree to an adversarial collaboration. I need you to draft the document they'd produce — I'm going to hand it to both of them, so it can't be slanted, and it can't be one of those documents where everything agreed is vacuous.

The two camps:

- Universe View (UV): there is one intended universe of sets; CH has a determinate truth value whether or not our current axioms settle it; the Gödelian programme of new axioms (large cardinals, inner model theory, canonicity-style ideas) is the right route to it; independence results are a limitation of ZFC, not of the concept of set.
- Multiverse View (MV): there are many universes of set theory, each with its own forcing extensions and grounds; "is CH true?" is closer to "is the parallel postulate true?" than to "is there life on Mars?"; the independence results are the answer to the question, not a failure to give one.

Ground rules: no citations, no bibliography, no "see X 2010" — I want something that stands up on its own for someone with no library access, so nothing should hang on a reference. If there's a technical claim you're not confident about (what exactly a given axiom pins the continuum at, the status of the definable version of CH, what a particular programme currently delivers), mark it [check] rather than asserting it. Define any jargon as you go.

What I want, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — things both of them could actually sign. Please cover at least: that ZFC neither proves nor refutes CH and that forcing is why; that CH is a perfectly well-formed sentence, so the dispute isn't about syntax or about whether the string is meaningful; that neither side is proposing to settle it by fiat (an axiom that literally says "the continuum is ℵ1" is not a candidate either would defend); that large cardinals have been a success story — they line up by consistency strength and they settle questions about definable sets of reals via determinacy; that the two live research programmes currently point in opposite directions (forcing-axiom-style maximality pins the continuum at ℵ2, the Ultimate-L/canonicity programme gives CH — correct me if I've got that backwards); that the analogue of CH restricted to definable/projective sets of reals is settled by large-cardinal-plus-determinacy assumptions, in the sense that every uncountable projective set of reals has the size of the continuum; and that mainstream mathematical practice is largely insensitive to how CH comes out, which is a problem for anybody hoping for "downstream" confirmation. Add any I've missed.

2. A disagreements table. Columns: the question; UV's own formulation; MV's own formulation; and whether you think the disagreement is genuine or verbal. Each side's formulation has to be a positive statement its own side would sign, not a denial of the other. At minimum I want: whether "there is a set of reals of intermediate size" has a determinate answer; what a forcing extension is (an alternative universe, or a technical device for proving independence, or a model inside a background V); what "maximality" of the universe of sets means, given that the two programmes seem to pull opposite ways on it; whether the large-cardinal success story transfers to CH or stops at the definable sets of reals; whether the multiverse thesis can even be stated without presupposing a single background universe; and what would count as having settled CH at all. If you think one of these is verbal rather than real, say which and why in the table rather than quietly dropping it.

3. A cruxes table: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each, give a case or hypothetical result both would agree bears on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. The cases I keep mulling over:

- The Fork. Suppose that within twenty years the field produces two principles, each coming out of an established research programme, each consistent with all the large cardinals we currently believe in, neither containing a clause that mentions the continuum, each with a rich body of consequences in its own area — and they disagree about CH. Would that be a discovery about the concept of set or a discovery about our methods? What exactly would each side predict about how the community would react, and what would each count as the Fork's being resolved rather than merely noticed?
- The transfer test. Everyone agrees large cardinals earned their keep through consequences for definable sets of reals. So: what class of consequences would a CH-deciding axiom have to have, outside set theory proper or at the level of second-order arithmetic, in order to count as confirmed? And is the isolation of CH — its lack of such consequences — evidence about the question, or only about us?
- The flip. A leading figure spent a decade with a framework that seemed to point to not-CH and then moved to a programme that gives CH. Both sides should say what they predict about a second flip, and what a flip would have to look like to count as evidence rather than fashion.
- The background-universe objection. Suppose a clean formal framework for the multiverse appears (a modal logic of forcing, or a multiverse treated as a structure with no ambient universe). Does that answer the objection that MV is stated from inside a V it says doesn't exist, or does it relocate it? Both sides' predictions, please.
- The twenty-year question. Each side writes down a rough probability that in twenty years the community treats CH as settled, and describes in advance what it would take — what kind of argument, with what level of uptake — for them to count it as settled. I want the numbers, not a diplomatic "it's hard to say".

4. A short closing section on which parts of this dispute are about words rather than the world, and which are not, with a one-sentence statement each side could literally put their name under at the bottom.

Aim for about 1200–1500 words; compact tables are fine. And no hedging everything into mush — where the two really do disagree, I want it sharp enough that they'd both recognise their own position.
