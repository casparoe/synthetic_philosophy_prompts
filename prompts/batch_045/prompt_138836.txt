Hi — I need a hand turning a half-formed idea into a short research proposal. It's for a small interdisciplinary seed grant: six months, part time, no research assistant, and the deliverable is one working paper plus a talk at the funders' workshop. Deadline's the end of the month and I've been going round in circles with it.

The idea, roughly. The argument about path dependence — whether, once some technology, standard or institution gets established, it's "locked in", or whether the same outcome would have arrived anyway by a different route — looks to me like it's stuck because both camps are answering different questions without noticing. Three things I keep coming back to:

1. Nobody fixes the grain of description at which the outcome is counted: is it "some arrangement that ends up performing function F" or "this exact arrangement"? Almost every case I can think of looks contingent at one grain and inevitable at the other.
2. "Would the same thing have happened if we reran it?" is ambiguous between nudging the initial conditions slightly and redrawing the whole later sequence of shocks — and those test quite different claims.
3. Both sides argue from hypothetical reruns, when there's a class of actual evidence nobody seems to use systematically: episodes where an established arrangement was in fact displaced, or very nearly was. I've been calling this revealed lock-in — inferring how strong the lock-in was from the size of the switching costs at which real populations actually did switch, loosely the way revealed preference infers preferences from choices rather than from what people say.

And the bit I think might be genuinely new, though I'm not sure it survives contact with a supervisor: belief in lock-in might partly cause lock-in, because agents coordinate on expectations, which makes the answer to "was it locked in?" endogenous. Which also means a lock-in story told too early can backfire and help a challenger coordinate a defection.

What I want from you is the proposal itself: the question stated sharply enough that it has a determinate answer; why the two standard positions don't settle it (and where they're each right); the method, including roughly what I'd do in each of the six months; what a decent deliverable looks like for people who aren't philosophers; what would count as the project failing; and an honest note on scope — I worry it's really three papers. Also make the case for why this is a conceptual project that makes progress without new data collection, since the panel is full of people who'd rather fund a dataset.

Two practical things. Please don't give me a bibliography or reading list — I know the literature, I'll slot the citations in myself, and I don't want invented references. And for the two or three illustrative cases you use, pick ones so well known you don't need to check anything, and flag it where you're unsure of the details of the case.

Last thing on tone, because I'll be adapting this: where the proposal makes claims, I want it clear which bits are basically settled among people who work on this and which are live controversies, and I don't want a contested position presented as the consensus view. If the honest answer is "everyone sensible agrees history sometimes matters, and the interesting disagreement is only about how much and when", say that rather than dressing up a side as orthodoxy.

Casual register is fine, British spelling, and don't worry about polishing it — I'd rather have something I can argue with than something tidy.
