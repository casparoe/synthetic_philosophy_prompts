I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend who works in brand marketing about whether an expensive gesture is automatically a credible one. Her position was that a firm willing to burn real money on a promise has thereby proved it means the promise; mine was that the burn proves nothing unless it hurts a firm that does not intend to follow through more than it hurts one that does. We got as far as agreeing on that differential-cost point — she conceded that a deep-pocketed company can buy exactly the same display as a sincere one — and then stalled on three things I could not settle. First, whether the cost has to be wasted in order to count: her examples were all pure waste (a huge advertising buy, absurd packaging, a monument of a headquarters), and she thought money spent on something that also makes the pledger better at delivering was not a real signal at all. Second, whether a costly gesture that nobody outside the counterparty ever learns about can signal anything, or whether it collapses into an ordinary contract term. Third, a case she raised that I had no answer to: some expensive things a company builds are partly what enable it to keep the promise in the first place, so the gesture does not merely reveal a quality that was already there but helps produce it. I suspect the biology literature on costly signaling and the handicap principle has something precise to say about all three, but please state whatever the relevant conditions are in plain English and do not hand me a reading list or citations — I have no way to chase them down, and I need questions I can ask, not authors I can read.

The reason I need it operationalized is my day job. I direct a four-person program staff at a regional foundation that makes outcome-linked grants to service providers; roughly sixty applications a year arrive with some kind of commitment attached — money placed in escrow, refunds promised if targets are missed, assets built or dedicated, public pledges with named deadlines. My staff have program and field backgrounds and no economics; each of us has about twenty-five minutes per application; every decision over two hundred fifty thousand dollars goes to a board that wants a single page of written rationale; and we are currently using an in-house scoring sheet whose largest single component is the headline dollar figure attached to the commitment, weighted more heavily than whether the commitment is even verifiable. I now think that weighting is close to backwards, and I want to replace it. So please write me a decision procedure my team can actually run: the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and what to write down when a number we need simply is not in the documents. It has to be usable by someone who has never heard the theory, it should say which distinctions genuinely have to survive that simplification and which can be dropped, and it should be honest about the places where its central estimate is guesswork dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three commitments of the kinds we actually see, telling me what verdict each gets and why. One: a large national provider places two million dollars of its own reserves with an independent trustee for three years, forfeited automatically if a named, independently verified service target is missed. These are my own favorite category, and I want to know whether the procedure endorses them or tells me that for a firm with that balance sheet the differential cost is close to zero. Two: a mid-sized provider spends about a million and a half building an internal data and case-tracking system it says is required to hit the target, on hardware and software it would keep and use elsewhere if the grant ended tomorrow. Three: a small nonprofit with very thin reserves offers, in a binding but confidential filing, to repay the entire grant plus twenty percent if the verified outcome is not met, and asks that the pledge never be publicized because it would alarm its own donors and clients. I want the third case to force the procedure to say what a costly but invisible pledge actually is, and whether a pledge that would bankrupt the pledger is worth anything given that there would be nothing left to collect. Please also tell me plainly what the procedure cannot tell us: which kinds of commitment it is simply the wrong instrument for, where the honest answer is that we do not know, and which of our existing categories should probably be retired rather than scored.
