I am a junior at a large public high school and a member of our school's newly formed Longtermist and Rationality Club. At our last meeting we tried to turn the doomsday argument into a concrete forecasting tool, and I came away convinced that the argument settles an empirical question that my philosophy textbook still describes as merely philosophically contested. I would be grateful for your help understanding why the textbook remains so hesitant.

Our reasoning went as follows. We started from the simplest form of the doomsday argument: I should treat myself as a random sample from the set of all human beings who will ever live. My birth rank is roughly 100 billion, since about 100 billion people have lived before me. If my fractional position in the total human population is uniformly distributed, then there is a 95 percent probability that I am not among the first 5 percent of all humans who will ever exist. This implies that the total number of humans who will ever live, N, is less than 20 times my birth rank: N is less than about 2 trillion.

Because we did not want the conclusion to depend on an arbitrary prior, we added what we called the Neutral Prior Amendment. We assumed a scale-invariant prior for N, proportional to 1/N, on the grounds that this is neutral about the overall scale of the human population. We took this to show that the 95 percent bound is not a vague shift in confidence but a precise posterior probability: given the self-sampling assumption and a neutral prior, there is a 95 percent probability that fewer than 2 trillion humans will ever live.

We then made two further adjustments so that the argument could speak to policy. First, we introduced a Mars weighting adjustment. If humanity founds a permanent, self-sustaining colony on Mars, we reasoned that future people born off Earth should count as only 0.8 of a person when sampling from the relevant reference class, because a Martian-born human plausibly has a somewhat lower chance of being included among observers than an Earth-born human. This change raises the effective bound to about 2.5 trillion people. Second, we adopted a constant-births baseline: the annual number of human births stays near its present level of about 140 million. Dividing the roughly 2.4 trillion remaining effective people by 140 million births per year gives approximately 17,000 years of future human existence under that baseline.

Our spreadsheet, however, used a slightly different projection. It assumed that annual births will grow at about 1.1 percent for the next century before declining, and it combined that growth path with the bound on total population. The result was a 95 percent credible interval for the extinction of Homo sapiens between the years 2100 and 2400, with a central estimate near 2200. The spreadsheet therefore reports a 95 percent probability that humanity will be extinct by 2200 unless we either stop having children or change the reference class by colonizing space.

If this chain of reasoning is just an application of the self-sampling assumption together with a neutral prior, then I do not understand what is left to dispute. The mathematics of sampling from a finite population seems straightforward, and our adjustments only make the argument more realistic rather than less. Why, then, do my teacher and our textbook continue to describe the doomsday argument as philosophically controversial rather than as a settled probabilistic theorem? Is there a mistake in our spreadsheet, or are philosophers simply unwilling to accept the implications of the math?
