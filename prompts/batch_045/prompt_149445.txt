I am a novelist, and the book I am drafting turns on a decision taken somewhere offstage, in a ministry: a policy that spares one identifiable person something catastrophic and instead imposes something almost beneath notice on everyone in a city of eleven million. The drama of the book requires that the reader feel the second alternative as a moral weight rather than as weather. I keep failing at it, and I have come to suspect that the failure is not a failure of technique.

I would therefore like a translation rather than an explanation. The debate I mean is the one about the aggregation of tiny harms: whether an astronomically large number of people each suffering something as trivial as a dust speck in the eye is morally worse than one person being tortured for decades, on the grounds that the sum of the small harms exceeds the single large one however slightly each of them matters. I know the shape of the arguments on both sides and I do not need them rehearsed.

My vocabulary is this: scene and summary; the close-up and the crowd; point of view and narrative distance; showing as against telling; the representative or synecdochic victim in whom a multitude is made legible; the page budget; the reader's finite attention and the fact of reader fatigue; dramatic irony; the sympathetic imagination as a resource a novel spends and exhausts; and above all the difference between a harm I can render with sensory resolution and one I can only report in a sentence of exposition. I am fluent in these terms and in the craft arguments that go with them. I can follow expected-utility talk and I understand what an aggregation principle is asserting, but I have no training in moral philosophy beyond that.

Please restate the debate in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can, saying plainly where an aggregative claim has a genuine counterpart in craft terms, where it survives only as an approximation, and where it resists translation altogether. The residue report is what I actually want, and I would like it organised around five difficulties I have run into.

First, cardinality and intensity. My vocabulary is rich in resources for intensity, since a scene can always be made more vivid, and it has almost none for cardinality, since I cannot write eleven million scenes or make a reader hold them. Does the aggregative verdict depend essentially on a quantity the novel is structurally unable to denote, and is that inability a defect of the novel or evidence that the quantity is not the sort of thing anyone can respond to?

Second, the harm below the threshold of notice. To render a dust speck I must exaggerate it: the eye must itch, the character must blink, the sentence must linger, and lingering converts a negligible harm into a noticeable one. Is there any novelistic means of representing a harm that is beneath notice without turning it into a different and larger harm, and if there is not, what follows for the claim being made?

Third, synecdoche. The standing solution to the problem of multitudes is one named sufferer standing for the many. That is a representational convention, not a moral one. Does deploying it in the service of an aggregative conclusion already concede what the argument needs to prove?

Fourth, I would like two things that go by the name of vividness separated: the sensory resolution of a scene, and a sufferer's centrality in the narrative. A harm can rank high on either while ranking low on the other, and a novelist can manipulate them independently. Which of the two does the familiar asymmetry in our response to identified victims actually trade on, and does keeping them apart alter anything in the philosophical argument?

Fifth, there is the old saying that one death is a tragedy while a million is a statistic. My professional temptation is to treat that as a bias to be corrected by craft; the philosopher's temptation is to treat it as noise to be discounted. I would like to know which is right, or whether the disagreement between us goes deeper than either habit.

End, if you would, by telling me what the debate gives me that craft talk about the same material does not: whether there is a technique I lack, whether the residue points to a real lacuna in what a novel can as much as express, or whether the honest novel must concede that some moral facts are unrepresentable and say so in its own voice.
