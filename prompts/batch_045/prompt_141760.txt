hey — i'm putting together a series of short imaginary memos for the intro session of an AI safety reading group i run. the conceit is that some dead thinker gets hired as a one-day consultant by a frontier lab and has to answer whichever question the lab actually has on its plate that week. the bentham one and the ada lovelace one went fine. i'm stuck on blaise pascal (1623-1662) and i want yours to be better than the obvious thing, because the obvious thing is wrong in an interesting way.

the setup in the memo: the lab, which i'll call Helios, has to pick between two strategies. strategy W ships a system they can give a hard worst-case bound on — a narrow, verifiable, interpretability-friendly design where they can prove that across every scenario covered by their formal model the harm stays under a small ceiling; the cost is that it does far less and can't do most of what Helios wants. strategy A ships a much more capable system that does great on their evals and on the deployment distribution they actually expect, with expected harm that looks tiny, but with no formal guarantee and some small residual probability of a catastrophic failure the evals wouldn't have caught. so: worst-case guarantee versus average-case guarantee.

every version i've tried to write myself turns into "pascal = the worst-case guy, obviously, because of the wager." i think that's half right at best and i want you to actually work through it:

1. the wager isn't on its face a worst-case argument at all, it's an expected-value argument — and pascal himself is one of the people who invented expected value, in the correspondence with fermat about how to fairly split the pot in an interrupted game of chance. by temperament and by his own mathematics the man is arguably on team average-case.
2. but he also rigs the payoff table so the worst case for betting on god is a trivial loss: you give up a finite span of finite life, and if you win you win an infinite happy one. once the downside is nothing, betting dominates no matter how thin the probability, and that's a worst-case-flavoured move smuggled inside an expected-value one.
3. and there's a genuine disanalogy i want him to notice rather than paper over. pascal's upside is a literal infinity, which is exactly what lets the argument survive an arbitrarily tiny probability. alignment risk gives you finite-but-astronomical stakes — call it something like 10^50 future lives — and finitely large numbers don't behave like infinities: make the probability small enough and the expected value really does come back down, so the argument starts resting on how confident you are about the tail. (there's a label for the way this style of reasoning gets hijacked by huge payoffs at tiny probabilities, but i don't need you to name it, i need you to handle it.) i'd like pascal to be the one who spots the crux and says whether his argument survives the swap from infinite to merely astronomical.

so: write the memo. 800-1000 words, addressed to Helios's head of safety, whoever you want to name her. in pascal's voice but not in costume — no verilys, no pasted prayers, no period French. what he actually does is compress an argument into a few hard lines, refuse to let the reader stay comfortable and neutral, and be a little brutal about the motives behind the question. i want that.

three things it should contain:

- he should say something about why they've come to him for a number at all. in the provincial letters he went after the Jesuit casuists for a method of reasoning that always seemed to hand the penitent permission to do what he wanted anyway. i want you to decide, and defend, whether he'd think "our expected harm is below threshold" is that same trick in a lab coat — or whether that's unfair and the cases come apart.
- he should take an actual position on W versus A, or explicitly refuse to, and either way he should be honest about where his own framework won't reach. no sitting on the fence by saying both matter.
- there's a jansenist wrinkle i'd love if you can use it: pascal didn't think you can simply will yourself into belief. going through the motions — masses, holy water, keeping the company of believers — is how the belief arrives, not a symptom of already having it; the practice is supposed to blunt the clever part of you that keeps objecting. is there any honest analogue for a lab that wants reassurance about a system it doesn't fully understand? do the practices that generate their evidence also build the thing they're reassured about, or is the reassurance itself the hazard?

please don't invent quotations from the pensées and put them in his mouth — paraphrase instead. don't let it turn into a generic sermon about how scary low-probability extinction is, and don't have him discover bayesianism in 1654. end where pascal would end: pressing them to do something before they feel certain, not handing them a probability.
