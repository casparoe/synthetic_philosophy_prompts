I teach a seminar on perception and the senses, and I am writing up a lecture — and, I hope, a short paper — on Molyneux's problem: whether someone blind from birth who has learned by touch to distinguish a cube from a sphere would, on first gaining sight, be able to tell which is which by sight alone. I want to spend part of it on a literary case, and I have settled on H. G. Wells's short story "The Country of the Blind": the mountaineer Nuñez tumbles into an Andean valley whose people have been blind for many generations, recites the proverb about the one-eyed man, discovers that he cannot convey to them what seeing is, is judged unsound for persisting, consents to the removal of his eyes as the price of marrying Medina-Saroté, and at sunrise walks out of the valley instead.

What I want from you is a reading of the story as a Molyneux case, and specifically as a Molyneux case of a kind the literature does not usually isolate. The standard question is a recognition question: can the newly sighted subject match a seen object to a felt one. But Wells's story is not testing that. Nuñez is already sighted, and his failure is a failure of transmission — he cannot get the blind to so much as grasp what it is he claims to have. Call this the didactic or reverse Molyneux question. I want to know how much weight it can bear: whether failure of transmission is evidence about the concepts the blind possess, or merely about the difficulties of testimony concerning a sense one lacks, or about something else entirely — the authority a community grants to a single dissenting reporter, say.

I also want a distinction drawn between two ways of answering Molyneux in the negative. On one, the subject's spatial concepts are in order but the visual input is not mapped onto them; on the other, there is nothing the subject is judging at all, because the relevant concepts are modality-specific and simply absent. The datum I keep returning to is that Wells's villagers are superbly competent — their paths, their windowless houses, their farming, their games — while having no notion of sight whatsoever. Does that competence tell in favour of the first negative answer and against the second, or is it a red herring, since amodal spatial competence was never what Molyneux's cube was testing?

Three further constraints. First, I do not want an empirical survey of cataract-reversal cases; the reading should stand on its own philosophically. Second, a colleague in disability studies reads the story purely as a fable about the social construction of normality, and I need to know whether my reading collapses into hers or adds something hers cannot. Third, tell me where the story resists me: it is narrated from the sighted side, its irony depends on our seeing, and no blind character in it ever gains sight — so say plainly if Wells has staged the wrong experiment for my purposes. I would rather have that pointed out than have a tidy reading.
