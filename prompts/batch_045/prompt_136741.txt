You are a professional philosopher who specializes in metaphysics, and who has also spent years teaching skeptical adults — journalists, editors, software people — outside a university. I would like you to work in two stages. First, reason to yourself for several paragraphs before committing to anything: what is genuinely hard to convey here, why the obvious activity fails, and what single experience would make the difficulty land in the body rather than in the head. Then give me the design. I would rather see your reasoning than a polished answer that skips it.

The setting: I write a column on science and ideas, and I have agreed to run a ninety-minute breakout session at a conference for science writers. Eighteen adults are expected, possibly as few as twelve or as many as twenty-four. They have no philosophy background, they are comfortable with abstractions, and they are professionally allergic to jargon — the moment a technical term appears, half of them start composing a paragraph about how the term is unnecessary. I am the only facilitator. I have a projector, index cards, and pens. I cannot use real money, I cannot ask anyone to read anything in advance, and I do not want to carry props.

The subject, as I understand it in my own words, is this. We constantly describe objects by durable properties that are not just records of what has happened to them: a glass is fragile, a tablet is soluble, a wire is conductive, a match is flammable. Saying this seems to commit me to more than a list of past and future events; it seems to point at something the object is like right now, in between the episodes. But when I try to say what the extra commitment amounts to, the natural move is to cash it out in terms of what would happen: the glass is fragile just in case it would break if it were struck. And then the machinery comes apart, because the world can be arranged so that the property is present but the behavior never shows up, or the behavior shows up although the property is absent. I want the room to feel that collapse rather than be told about it.

So: no lecture at the front end, and no names for anything until the debrief. Design an activity — a game, a simulation, a structured exercise with roles and rules — in which the participants generate the problematic cases themselves out of the activity's own mechanics, and only discover at the end that what they produced has a literature attached to it. Specifically, I want the design to make these arise from what the participants do, in this order of importance to me:

A case in which the very act of testing for the property destroys or removes it, so that the test can never return the answer the test was designed to return.

A case in which the object has the property but something in its surroundings permanently blocks the behavior.

A case in which the object lacks the property but the surroundings produce exactly the behavior anyway.

A case in which one and the same external condition blocks the behavior of one property while producing the characteristic behavior of a different property in the same object at the same time.

A case in which the interference is partial or delayed: the object behaves exactly as its property predicts for the first few trials and then stops, or it manifests at half strength, or it manifests only after a lag.

A case in which the behavior is irreducibly chancy, so that no number of single trials settles whether the object has the property at all.

The last three are the ones I care about most. The first three are the standard pair I half-remember from an undergraduate course, and an activity that only re-derives those will feel to my audience like a party trick with a known ending. I want the room to get somewhere they could not have gotten from a summary.

What I need from you:

One. The activity itself: explicit rules, roles, at least two phases, and a scoring scheme that needs no money. The second phase must change the rules in a way that forces the later cases out of the participants rather than out of my mouth. Tell me exactly what I hand out, what I write on the board, and what I am permitted to say in the first phase without spoiling the mechanism. If the scoring can be gamed, tell me how — and preferably design it so that the participant who tries to game it produces useful material for the debrief.

Two. A minute-by-minute timeline for ninety minutes, with the debrief given real space rather than the last ten minutes.

Three. The debrief: a mapping from specific moments in the activity to specific parts of the philosophical structure, including an account of what each of the six cases does to the would-be conditional analysis, and clear guidance on when the technical vocabulary may be introduced and how much of it to introduce. Include the moves available to someone who wants to defend the conditional analysis, and what each move costs.

Four. An honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with fixes. I would rather hear about the boring failure modes — people not understanding the rules, one dominant voice, the person who solves it in the first five minutes and gets restless — than a list of philosophical worries.

Five. A frank note on which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and which I will simply have to state in my own words.

Six. If something quotable falls out of the design, one sentence I could use in the column.
