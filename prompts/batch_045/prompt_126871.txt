You are a political philosopher who also works in formal social choice theory and has spent real time thinking about how machine-learning systems are actually built. Please reason carefully and step by step. Before you answer, spend a short unstructured passage brainstorming: list the candidate readings of the practice I describe below, the strongest objection to each, and any place where you suspect I have set up a false contrast. Then give me the answer you would defend in print. Do not cite or recommend readings — I already have a seminar bibliography, and I want the positions reconstructed and argued for, not located in a literature.

I am an undergraduate in a joint computer-science and philosophy programme. This term I am taking a seminar on value alignment and, in parallel, a course on social choice, and my paper is about the implicit political philosophy of what everyone in the first course calls the standard alignment pipeline. I want to describe that pipeline concretely, because I think the design encodes a view that nobody on either syllabus states.

The arrangement I mean: a developer collects pairwise comparisons of model outputs from a pool of a few thousand crowdworkers, recruited to be demographically and ideologically varied. The workers' judgments are screened: those whose ratings diverge too far from a written set of principles about helpfulness, honesty and refusal are down-weighted or dropped. The surviving judgments, taken as cardinal strengths of preference, are averaged into a single scalar reward model. That one reward model is used to fine-tune one set of model weights, and those weights are served to every user. When users disagree with each other, the system handles the disagreement at the output layer rather than the training layer: on contested questions the model is trained to lay out the main reasonable positions, attribute them, and decline to pick, with the ordering and framing of those positions itself learned from the same averaged reward signal.

Two features strike me as pulling in opposite directions, and I would like to know whether they really do. First, the training layer seems to presuppose that many people's values can be put on one scale, compared across persons and aggregated — a Harsanyi-flavoured commitment, as my seminar would put it — while the output layer seems to presuppose the opposite, that the system should not act on any contested comprehensive doctrine at all and should instead hand the choice back to the user. Second, the constituency looks gerrymandered: the values that get input are those of paid raters and, downstream, of users, while people who are affected by deployment but never use the system have no standing anywhere in the pipeline.

So I want the implicit theory recovered, with its weak points marked. Specifically:

1. Reconstruct the view about legitimate value aggregation that this arrangement only makes sense under. Be explicit about what is strictly presupposed — what would become unmotivated or incoherent if denied — as opposed to what merely fits the practice comfortably. My hypothesis is that very little is strictly presupposed and that several incompatible theories would license the same pipeline; if you think that, tell me where exactly the underdetermination sits.

2. Say what the practice quietly rules out. Does the averaging step commit the developer to interpersonal comparability of the relevant quantities, or can the same step be read as a modus vivendi among groups who share nothing but a willingness to accept a common procedure? Does the screening of raters commit the developer to a substantive claim about which values are admissible, and if so does that collapse the neutrality claim at the output layer, or can the two be held apart in a public-reason style?

3. Assess the coherence of the combination: aggregation at the base, deference at the surface. Is there a single theory of legitimacy on which both are required, or is the arrangement a pragmatic overlay of two theories that would conflict if either were taken seriously, and what would each theory, taken seriously, change about the design?

4. My social-choice instinct is that the pipeline's designers are tacitly relying on the classic impossibility results not biting here. Say what exactly has to be true for that: that the relevant preferences are effectively single-peaked or otherwise structured, that cardinal reward magnitudes somehow escape the ordinal aggregation problem, that raters are not strategically misrepresenting anything, that the choice is not really among more than two alternatives in the relevant sense. For each of these, give your view on whether it is credible for this domain, and whether the impossibility results are being escaped or merely relocated. Also tell me whether the delegation to the user at the output layer is itself subject to a strategy-proofness worry I may be missing.

5. Finally, suppose we instead took a rival view of the constituency or the aggregation rule seriously — per-jurisdiction or per-user reward models, an ensemble of models combined by an explicit voting rule, or moving the deference down into the training layer itself. For each, say what philosophical commitment would motivate it and what it would cost the developer, and then close with one sentence naming the single commitment of the current arrangement that you think is most load-bearing and least defensible.
