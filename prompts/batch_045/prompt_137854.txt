I sit on the allocation committee of a private foundation with a $40M annual payout. Our charter commits us to an impartial welfarist view: maximize expected welfare across all sentient beings, with priority weighting for the worst off. Our credence is not monolithic, though — roughly 60% on that view, 20% on a more strongly suffering-focused variant, 15% on a person-affecting view, and 5% on a view that assigns intrinsic value to wild nature and to the preservation of species. In our internal accounting one welfare unit (WU) is what $1M buys in our best direct program, unrestricted cash transfers to the extreme poor; every proposal below should be scored against that benchmark of 1.0.

We are evaluating six proposed moral trades — arrangements in which each side spends its own money on what the other values, and both expect to come out ahead by their own lights.

Our staff has proposed a screening principle I will call the Conversion Principle. Disagreement about what is valuable does not by itself create surplus. If two parties differ only in what they care about, and each would otherwise spend its own budget on its own priority, then swapping who writes which check leaves the funded allocation exactly as it was, and each side is no better off by its own lights — because each side's baseline already includes whatever the other was going to buy for it. Surplus requires either a gap in conversion efficiency, so that a dollar produces more welfare in one party's hands than in the other's, or access to an outcome the other party cannot reach at any price. Value disagreement fixes who wants what; efficiency and access fix whether there is anything to gain.

The six proposals, with our analysts' estimates:

A. Bednets through a faith-based delivery network. Their clinics reach districts we cannot enter, and their cost per net delivered is a little over a third of ours, so a dollar routed through them converts at roughly 2.4 WU per $1M. They ask that we fund scripture translation and religious broadcasting dollar-for-dollar alongside their nets; that output is worth 0.0 to us, and two of our secular grantees have hinted they would find the arrangement objectionable. Leakage 20%. Verification 0.9 — nets are countable. Capacity $3M.

B. A co-funding window with a multilateral nutrition program. For every $1M we commit they add $1M of their own to the same program, which converts at about 1.15 WU per $1M; their marginal cost is only slightly below ours, since we could run something parallel. Our analysts treat 60–75% of their matching share as non-additional: the same envelope would very likely have funded nutrition anyway, and their budgets are fungible. Verification 0.95. Capacity $8M, the largest headline figure on this list.

C. A corporate cage-free campaign run by a conservation NGO, in exchange for our purchasing a conservation easement they want. Their corporate access gives them about 1.6 WU per $1M on the campaign. Leakage 25%. Verification 0.7: the commitments are checkable, but the welfare effect per animal is contested. The easement is worth about −0.2 WU to us under our main view, because of expected wild-animal suffering, but about +0.3 under the 5% nature-intrinsic view in our credence distribution. Capacity $2M.

D. Paid neutrality. A teachers' union will withdraw its active opposition to a staffing reform we value at roughly 2.0 WU per $1M if we fund a political campaign of theirs that we value at −0.15. We can find no route to buy the reform ourselves, and the union cannot be induced to support it, only to stop blocking it. Probability the reform passes conditional on payment: 0.4, already folded with the risk that they take the money and obstruct anyway. Verification of their compliance 0.6. Capacity $2M.

E. A thirty-year handoff. We transfer $3M now to a perpetual endowment in exchange for a binding commitment that its trustees will spend an inflation-adjusted $3M in 2055 on whatever our charter's criteria then rank highest; in the meantime we fund their current priority, biomedical aging research, which we value at 0.1 WU per $1M and they value enormously. Neither leg has any efficiency advantage — either side could write either check — so the case rests on value difference plus the return leg. Our staff discounts that leg to about 0.6 WU per $1M even before enforcement risk, since a dollar spent in a richer world buys less welfare. Enforcement over three decades: 0.4, given interpretive drift, turnover among trustees, and the doctrines that let courts redirect a charitable gift whose purpose has become impracticable. Capacity $3M.

F. A belief-difference swap. A foundation with the same charter and very different empirical rankings will fund $2M of our cash transfers if we fund $2M of their research on institutional reform. We value that research at 0.15 WU per $1M; they value it at three times cash transfers. Conversion efficiency is 1.0 on both legs, and compliance is certain. What differs is belief, not access. Capacity $2M.

Please give us:

1. A tier list — execute as proposed, negotiate terms, accept only if restructured, decline — with one sentence of justification per trade.
2. For each trade, a single expected multiplier to one decimal place: expected WU per $1M we commit, net of leakage, net of enforcement risk, net of what the other side's own money was already buying for us by our lights, and including the value (possibly negative) of what we do for them. The benchmark is 1.0.
3. A flag on any trade whose net sign flips across the four views in our credence distribution.
4. Two sentences: which single parameter does the most work in separating the tiers, and which placement you would revise first if one estimate moved.
5. One restructured version of exactly one trade — say which, and specify the change.
6. One sentence on whether the Conversion Principle is correct, and what, if anything, it leaves out.

Rank by total expected surplus at the stated capacity, not by rate alone and not by the size of the sums moved. If one of these is confused in its framing — if it is not really a trade at all, or is really something else — say so rather than scoring it. The arithmetic matters less than whether you have identified the right structural question for each deal.
