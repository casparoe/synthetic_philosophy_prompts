Hey — I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book whose narrator, Marielle, is one of those people who'll tell you, totally straight-faced, that she doesn't have emotions, and I keep tripping over what I'm even allowed to claim when I write that she felt dread. I was arguing about this after a reading in Philly last week with two friends: one does sentiment analysis on customer-support transcripts and insists emotions are basically just valence/arousal vectors plus an appraisal, the other is very Stoic-adjacent and says an emotion just is a judgement (or a cluster of judgements) about what's good or bad and up to you. Neither of them could tell me what to do with the scenes I actually have to write, so I'm coming to you. Please answer in British English (colour, organise, that sort of thing), and skip any bibliography or reading list — I can't check sources and I don't want citations anyway, I just want your own reasoning.

Here are the five invented cases that are giving me trouble, so don't go hunting for them in the literature. (A) Cold grief: for six months after his wife dies, Edmund shows nothing — no tears, sleep and appetite unchanged, resting heart rate flat, and when asked he says only that he notes she is dead; yet he abandons his own career to finish her half-built sailboat, keeps paying for her birdwatching magazines, and goes coldly furious at a friend who suggests he sell the boat. (B) The stairs: Nadia insists she isn't afraid of lifts, she takes the stairs for the exercise, and she isn't lying or being defensive; but her stair-taking tracks lift-features with absurd precision — a glass-walled one is fine if she can see the lobby, never more than three stops, never mirrored walls — and in eleven years she has never once wondered why. (C) Objectless four a.m.: Marielle wakes most nights at 4:12 with a full-body alarm reaction, heart hammering, dry mouth, the works, and there is nothing she is afraid of — no thought, no image, no judgement she can recover, and asked mid-episode she'll say, correctly, that everything is fine. (D) The fudge and the cardigan: after a small brain injury, Grete cheerfully eats chocolate shaped like dog turds and laughs about it, but she absolutely will not wear a cardigan that once belonged to a colleague she now despises, and can't explain why except that it's contaminated. (E) The two-hundredth performance: an actor playing a repentant son has, by his 200th show, all the bodily and behavioural machinery of guilt running perfectly, and delivers the judgement that what he did was unforgivable and was his, without any inner resistance; yet he reports feeling nothing, would do exactly the same again in that situation, and is entirely unaffected the moment he leaves the stage.

What I want from you is a ranking, not an essay. Rank these seven ways of thinking about what emotions are, by how well each one handles all five cases at once — i.e. which gives you the least embarrassing story across the whole set rather than the one that nails a single case: (1) the judgement or evaluative theory, an emotion is constituted by a normative judgement that something valuable is at stake; (2) the perceptual or attitudinal theory, emotions are experiences that present evaluative properties, analogous to perception but neither belief nor bare feeling; (3) the feeling or somatic theory, emotion is the experience of bodily change, so no body change means no emotion; (4) the dimensional or core-affect theory, emotion is a position in valence-and-arousal space plus an interpretive label, with the felt core itself non-intentional; (5) the motivational or action-tendency theory, what matters is the set of actions the state leaves you poised and ready to perform; (6) the component or appraisal-bundle theory, emotion is a synchronised package of appraisal, arousal, expression, motivation and feeling, which can degrade into partial instances when pieces drop out; (7) the enactive or dynamical theory, emotions aren't inner states at all but patterns of engaged coping with a situation over time. Give me a tier list (S through F, or whatever tiers you like, ties allowed) plus a rough percentage of your credence next to each, adding up to about 100, and one sentence per entry saying why it lands where it does. If it's helpful, also tell me in a line or two which single case does the most damage overall, and whether you'd let me write that Marielle genuinely has emotions, or whether I should keep it all behavioural and definately stop writing sentences like she felt dread.
