I spent a career in freshwater ecology, and since retiring I have been attending a university reading group; this term it is on the concepts of liberty — the negative/positive distinction and the modern republican revival. At our last meeting someone produced a proverb (I was told it is Russian, which I have no way of checking) in order to end an argument: "the freedom of the pike is the death of the minnows." The work it was made to do was to finish off the negative conception. If liberty is simply the absence of interference, then a pike at liberty just is the destruction of the minnows, and a theory that is prepared to call that arrangement one in which liberty prevails has, supposedly, lost its hold on the thing itself. I said I thought the proverb might be doing several quite different jobs at once, and was invited to come back with them written down.

So I would be grateful for a menu rather than an argument: lay out the distinct precise theses the proverb could be expressing, ordered from the near-trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it is true, whether it is a platitude or substantive, and — the part I actually need — whether it would deliver the conclusion it was brought in for, that is, whether it shows the negative conception to be refuted, as opposed to merely incomplete, or in need of a supplement about whose liberty counts, or about how liberties are to be weighed against one another. Please keep separate, in the classification, claims about what liberty is from claims about how much it is worth, whose it is, how it aggregates across individuals, and how it is to be measured or detected; I suspect the proverb's apparent force comes from sliding between these, and I would like to know which slide is doing the work. It would also help to be told which reading you think people who reach for the proverb are usually reaching for.

Two things from my old field that I would like each reading to be tested against, because I think they sort the readings differently than the proverb's own wording suggests.

First: we distinguished consumptive from non-consumptive, or trait-mediated, effects of a predator — what is now called the landscape of fear. Most of what a pike does to a minnow population is done by the minnows changing their own behaviour, abandoning productive shallow water, feeding less, stunting their growth, and involves no minnow being eaten at all. The proverb's word "death" points at the consumptive effect, which is in fact the case a simple "how many of your options are closed" metric handles worst, since the eaten minnow is no longer there to possess an option set. If liberty is measured by interference that actually occurs, the interesting case is invisible and the extreme case is unrepresentable. Which readings can register an unfreedom consisting entirely in the anticipation of another's will?

Second: the proverb assumes a closed system with no refuge. Please say what each reading implies about (i) a tank with no weed cover, where leaving is impossible; (ii) a lake with refuges the pike could enter but mostly does not; (iii) a pike that is old, well fed and in fact harms no one; (iv) a pike introduced and fed by a keeper to protect the minnows from something worse, and able to eat them whenever it chose. My guess is that (ii) and (iii) do most of the work of separating the three concepts, but tell me if that is a prejudice imported from thinking about populations rather than persons.

Finally, say how confident you are in each verdict, flag anything that rests on a contested reading of a view you are summarising, and single out the point you are least sure of.
