Write a dialogue of roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words between two members of a small working group that has been given one afternoon to produce a single agreed formal semantics for the fourfold rejection used in Madhyamaka thought — the pattern in which a claim is denied, its negation is denied, the conjunction of the two is denied, and the denial of both is denied as well. The group's brief is to deliver an entry for a handbook of non-classical logic, so whatever they settle on has to be stated precisely enough to be applied to a sentence and to yield a verdict.

The two characters have argued about this before and neither is a novice.

Ilse Vogt holds that no part of classical logic needs to be revised. On her reading the fourfold rejection is not a discovery about truth-values but a dismantling of the question: each of the four lemmas presupposes that there is a subject with an intrinsic nature for the predicate to be true or false of, and the denial is a cancellation of that presupposition rather than an assertion of the opposite. Her formal analogue of choice is a free logic with presupposition failure, and she reads the slogan that all things are empty as an instruction about how to stop asking, not as a universal generalization ranging over a domain of empty things.

Ravi Menon holds that this understates the revision required. He argues that the fourfold form has to be closed under some principle — otherwise the group cannot answer the question what happens to a fifth view, and the list of four was either a logical partition or a catalogue of positions held by opponents, and only the first reading lets the rejection generalize. He proposes a semantics with four values, true, false, both, and neither, together with a distinction between a negation that remains inside that algebra and an external negation that says of a sentence that it takes none of the four values, so that the rejection of the fourth lemma is not itself a fifth lemma.

They agree to test their proposals on three sentences.

First, the empty-subject case: the sentence that the Tathāgata exists after death. Ilse treats it as failing to be a candidate for truth at all. Ravi objects that on her treatment the rejection of the second lemma and the rejection of the fourth lemma come to the same thing, so the fourfold collapses into a twofold and the form does no work.

Second, the level-relative case: the sentence that a chariot exists, which is said to be true by convention and not true in the ultimate analysis. Ilse says the apparent contradiction is an equivocation on the verb and no glut is needed. Ravi replies that if level-relativity is available then the third lemma is exactly the conjunction of a truth at one level with a falsity at another, and needs a principled reason for rejection rather than a shrug, and he presses the collapse worry: if both levels are about the same chariot, what in her apparatus prevents every sentence and its negation from being true together?

Third, a sentence the group constructs for the occasion. Let E be a sentence that attributes emptiness to itself, that is, a sentence which says of itself that it is empty. Since all things are empty, E is empty; but what E says is therefore the case, so E is true; and a sentence that is true looks like a sentence with something of its own. Ravi uses this to argue that Ilse's own apparatus is revisionary after all, since free logic combined with a non-classical treatment of quantification over a domain with no stable members is not classical logic unchanged. Ilse replies that E cancels its own subject, that this is what it means to say that one has no thesis, and she presses the question whether an argument can have a conclusion that is not a proposition. Ravi must also answer the charge that his four values plus an external negation turn emptiness into a fifth value, and hence into a thesis his position is supposed to lack.

Write the exchange as a scene rather than a report. No footnotes, no citations, no mention of secondary literature, no narration from outside the two voices beyond brief stage directions. Each character must state the other's position in a form the other will accept before objecting to it, and each must make at least one genuine concession under pressure, conceding the narrow point while holding the main one. Neither may win the argument outright and neither may convert.

Because the deadline arrives, they must close by writing three things down: one sentence about the fourfold rejection that both can sign; the single sentence that separates them; and one further test sentence, not among the three above, on which their two semantics return different verdicts, with each verdict stated.
