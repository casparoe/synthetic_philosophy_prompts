I'm a junior in a seminar on agency and practical reason, and we have spent the last two weeks on Kavka's toxin puzzle — the billionaire who deposits a large sum in your account tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a vial tomorrow afternoon that will make you painfully ill for a day, with no requirement that you actually drink it. I already have the reading list (Kavka's original paper and Gauthier's reply among them), so please don't spend space on citations, summaries of the literature, or suggestions for further reading. What I want is a classification instrument I can actually hand out in section.

Specifically: a decision tree of roughly eight to ten leaves that takes someone from ordinary, non-technical answers about a handful of cases to a named position on whether — and by what route — the agent can intend to drink the toxin. Every branch node should be labeled with the claim that does the separating, and every node should be phrased as a question about a case that a person who has never read the literature could still answer. No node may simply ask "can you intend to drink the toxin?," and no node may ask anyone which position they call themselves by.

Three constraints on how the tree gets built:

1. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Start from the questions and the answer-combinations they permit, then locate the actual positions inside that space. Where a combination is unoccupied, keep the empty cell and give me one sentence on why nobody lives there — incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Where two leaves your tree reaches are not genuinely separable by any of your nodes, flag that openly instead of inventing a contrived question to split them.

2. The first split should concern the nature of intention, not decision theory. Our TA keeps steering discussion toward causal versus evidential decision theory, and I want to know whether that dispute does any independent work here or whether it is a proxy for a prior question — whether intending requires believing that you will do the thing, whether intentions can be formed at will or only by coming to see reasons, whether there can be reasons for intending that are not reasons for acting, and whether an intention formed earlier constrains what it is rational to do later. If the decision-theoretic disagreement is genuinely independent, give it its own node; if it collapses into one of those questions, tell me which one.

3. Use the three variant cases below as the diagnostic material. I constructed them to pry apart views that the original case runs together, and I would like to know which ones actually discriminate and which are idle — please tell me plainly if any of them moves no one.

- The Bland Button variant. Same structure, except that what you must intend at midnight is to press a button tomorrow afternoon that does nothing except inconvenience you slightly: no illness, no aversion, nothing to overcome. A view on which the obstacle in the original case is the cost or the aversion should change its answer here; a view on which the obstacle is that you will have no reason to act later should not.
- The Lockbox variant. At midnight, before anything is recorded, you may pay a small sum to have a machine make you drink tomorrow afternoon. Would you pay, and does your willingness to pay count as — or substitute for — the state the billionaire is paying for?
- The Serial variant. The offer repeats nightly for a month, the money arrives only on the first night you succeed, and drinking on any night ends the series. Can the intention be built up across nights, or does each night inherit the difficulty of the first?

Finally, I need a short scoring key so a reader can check which leaf they landed on, and one line per leaf on that position's most awkward commitment. I would like the planning-style response (intending as adopting a plan or policy rather than as believing you will execute it) and the response on which forming an intention is simply not under the will at all to appear among the leaves; elsewhere, name leaves descriptively and mark them "unnamed" if nobody has defended them.
