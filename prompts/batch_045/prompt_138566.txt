I'm writing a one-page position statement on time travel for a metaphysics reading group — where I stand, before the others take it apart — and before I circulate it I want to know whether the set of things I believe actually hangs together. I've tried to state each commitment the way I would defend it out loud.

(1) I'm an eternalist: past, present, and future times are all equally real, there is no objective flow and no moving now, and "present" picks out a time the way "here" picks out a place.

(2) I think the grandfather paradox shows backward time travel to be metaphysically impossible rather than merely nomologically impossible, and my reason is the ordinary one: the past has already happened, there is nothing left there to act on, and one cannot bring it about both that something did happen and that it did not.

(3) The constraint I appeal to, and which I have started calling the Ledger Condition, is that history is a single ledger: every object is entered once, gets exactly one biography, and no entry is ever overwritten or revised. The grandfather case violates it. The bootstrap case — the traveler who carries back the blueprints she will later receive from her future self, so that nobody ever designs the device — does not, because every entry in that story is consistent even though the information in it has no origin. I am therefore permissive about causal loops and strict about contradictions.

(4) If I am wrong about (2), my fallback is the self-consistency principle associated with Novikov: only globally consistent assignments of events occur, and inconsistent ones have probability zero.

(5) What I don't like is where (4) seems to lead. I want the traveler's failure to come out of local physics — she slips on the stair, the pistol jams, she misreads the address — and not out of a constraint that surveys the whole four-dimensional arrangement and vetoes the offending histories. Were I to meet a constraint like that anywhere else in metaphysics, I would call it a conspiracy.

(6) I also want that failure to leave her an ordinary agent: her attempt is a genuine attempt, she acts for reasons, it is true of her that had her reasons been different she would have acted differently, and she simply doesn't succeed.

(7) Relatedly, I don't think anything in my view forbids successful prevention. A traveler who goes back and stops a famine before it begins leaves a consistent history; that she would then have had no reason to make the journey strikes me as an oddity of the case, not a contradiction.

(8) And I hold that branching-timeline stories, on which the traveler's arrival initiates a new history, aren't stories about time travel at all.

Please audit this. Which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look as though they are, and how badly? Rank what you find by severity; for each tension say exactly what would have to give and what that costs me; and tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what it would leave me unable to say. Two things I'd like you to rule on rather than pass over. A colleague insists that (2) and (4) cannot be held together, on the grounds that (4) tells us what would happen under a supposition I have called impossible — I don't see the force of that, but I may be missing it. And I'm not certain (3) is stable, since it lets me welcome loops with no origin while rejecting loops with contradictions in them. I can't locate the fault myself, so please don't assume I've pointed anywhere near it; and I'd rather you not quietly restate the view as the one you would defend, since what I need to know is which of my own commitments cannot stay as I've written them. Write your answer as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you'd write an email to a colleague — in American English, and keep it to about a page.
