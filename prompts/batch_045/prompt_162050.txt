I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep tripping over the same shape of argument. Most recently it happened twice in one day at a workshop on computation and physics where I was the only non-specialist in the room.

Case one: someone claimed some kind of exotic physical setup could "do something uncomputable." A computer scientist shot back that this was confused, because whatever the setup does isn't computation at all — the class of computable functions is fixed once you've settled what an effective procedure is, so there's nothing here to discover. Fine. But twenty minutes later the same person used the very same claim to argue that the human brain is simulable in principle, and said that if it turned out not to be, that would be a discovery about brains, not a decision about what "computable" means. Stipulation in one breath, empirical finding in the next.

Case two: someone raised a hypothetical machine that outran a Turing machine. One speaker said no physical device could ever refute the thesis, since anything that did that would ipso facto not be computing. Another speaker said the version anyone actually cares about is the physical one — the kind quantum computing people argue about — and that it really could turn out false. Then a third person pointed out that everyone treats the fact that wildly independent formalisms all land on the same class of functions as powerful evidence for the thesis, which only makes sense if it isn't a stipulation. Nobody resolved it; they all just moved on.

What I'm after isn't the computability literature as such. It's the general move: a claim about the co-extension of a formal and an informal predicate ("computable by a Turing machine" / "computable in principle, by anything, ever") that gets to act as a definition when it's under pressure and as a discovery when it's doing work, where both roles are individually respectable. So it isn't motte-and-bailey — there's no indefensible version being smuggled — and it isn't quite plain equivocation either, since the same people hold both ends honestly and the shift isn't a bait-and-switch. I've since noticed the same shape in a couple of other places, e.g. with "fitness" in evolutionary biology and with the equivalence principle.

Does this thing have an established name? If the nearest concepts are things like Carnapian explication, the analytic/synthetic distinction, "criterion" versus "hypothesis," or a computability-internal taxonomy (a thesis versus a principle versus an axiom, or the bare claim versus the complexity-flavored one where "feasibly" does the work), I want to know which fits best and exactly where my description stops fitting. A bare label won't satisfy me. And if there genuinely isn't one, coin a term, define it, and tell me where the boundary cases are: when does this double life cross over into being an illegitimate move, and when is it just how such claims have to work?
