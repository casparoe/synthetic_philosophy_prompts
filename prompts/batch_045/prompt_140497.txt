Write a modest proposal in the form of a British government White Paper — a Command Paper, with the full apparatus of the genre: a title page giving the department, a command paper number and a date; a ministerial foreword; an executive summary; a section setting out the options considered, with costings; a preferred option with a statutory target and an implementation timetable; an equality impact assessment; and a short annex. Aim for 950–1,150 words. Write the whole thing in British English — organisation, recognise, whilst, costings in pounds sterling — and in the flat, euphemistic, noun-heavy register of the civil service.

The paper is issued by a fictional department of state (invent its name, its acronym, and its minister) and it defends a real view, which it then proceeds to take at its word. The view is the evidential-decision-theoretic argument for cooperation in large worlds: an agent facing a one-shot prisoner's dilemma may rationally cooperate with a counterpart she cannot causally influence, has no contract or reputation with, and will never encounter again, provided the world is large enough and the counterpart similar enough that her decision is statistically correlated with theirs. What does the justificatory work is correlation, not causation. The correlation required is a function of the payoff matrix: the greater the temptation payoff, the stronger the correlation needed, and beyond some point no attainable correlation short of certainty will suffice.

Requirements for the content:

1. The paper must never claim that one person's cooperation brings about another's. Every statement of the mechanism should be scrupulously evidential — what an agent's decision is evidence for, what the conditional probabilities are, what follows under the agent's uncertainty. But the surrounding apparatus — delivery, rollout, levers, targets, interventions, pilot regions, guidance to local authorities — should be saturated with causal language, and no character in the document should ever notice the mismatch. The reader should notice it.
2. The Department should set a statutory minimum correlation, with a name and a figure, and a note that the figure will be kept under review. In the options section it should discover that the cheapest way to meet the target is not to raise correlation but to lower the temptation payoff, and should therefore propose a levy, a cap, or a licensing regime for high-temptation situations, costed as unremarkable policy.
3. Include a passage in which the Office for National Statistics is asked to certify that the world is large enough: to estimate the number of relevantly similar agents, with a confidence interval, and to note the awkwardness that the estimate moves as the policy itself changes what counts as similar.
4. The equality impact assessment is where the reference-class problem should do its damage. Similarity is a matter of degree, and the Department must decide which respects count. It should find, deadpan, that the most reliable correlations obtain within narrow occupational, educational and demographic bands, and should go on to recommend measures — a register of approved decision procedures, an inspectorate, statutory guidance on lawful deliberation — that entrench those bands, while describing them throughout as technical measures for improving match quality.
5. Include one short paragraph on the anti-correlated counterpart: the near-twin who defects precisely when you cooperate. The Department should dispose of this by reclassifying such persons out of the reference class, without noticing that the reclassification, rather than any fact about the persons concerned, is doing all the work.
6. Include a section on gaming and uptake: citizens who make themselves predictable, or who self-modify into the kind of person who cooperates, in order to improve their correlation. The Department should treat this behaviour as simultaneously the programme's principal uptake target and a serious compliance risk, and should leave unresolved whether these are two phenomena or one.
7. Carry the logic to its terminus without flinching. The cheapest and most reliable way to raise correlation is to reduce the number of distinct decision procedures in the population; the paper should approach standardisation — and the limit in which the correlated parties are, for decision-theoretic purposes, a single agent, enjoying perfect cooperation at the cost of there being no one left to cooperate with — as an efficiency saving, with a costing attached.
8. Register: sustained deadpan. No winking, no authorial footnotes, no scare quotes, no present-day proper nouns, no named philosophers, and nothing anywhere that states the joke. Citations, if any, should be to fictional departmental publications and earlier fictional White Papers; do not cite real authors, and include no bibliography, reading list or references section.
9. A reader who knows the literature on evidential decision theory and large worlds should be able to tell that the target has been understood rather than caricatured: the mathematics of the threshold, the gradedness of similarity, the largeness condition and the reference-class worry should all be recognisable. A reader who knows none of it should still be able to tell what is being sent up, from the content alone.
10. Close with the annex: four questions that local authorities have asked, with the Department's answers, the last of which is a question the paper cannot answer and answers anyway in the standard idiom; followed by the usual invitation to submit responses by a stated date.
