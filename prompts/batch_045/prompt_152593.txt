I am applying for a twelve-month master's-by-research fellowship and need a two-page proposal for a dissertation on Mill's late socialism. My half-formed idea is this. The standard question is whether Mill became a socialist late in life, and the standard answers are either that he did — a late rupture with the liberal of On Liberty — or that he never really did, his sympathy for socialism being a consistent extension of his individualism. Both readings, as I see it, assume that socialism names a single position that Mill either held or did not. I want to split it three ways: a *prediction*, that once labourers are educated, cooperative production will gradually displace wage labour not by law but by out-competing it; a *prescription*, that cooperative ownership of the means of production is what justice or the general interest requires, whether or not it would ever arise on its own; and a *permission*, that socialism ought to be legally and socially permitted as one experiment in living among others, and that the present order unfairly forecloses it. My suspicion, which I cannot yet defend, is that Mill's other commitments — to competition between forms of life, to the stationary state, to individuality as the thing progress is for — commit him to the first and the third but not the second, so that his socialism is considerably thinner than either his admirers or his critics have supposed; and that the crux is his answer to the incentive objection, i.e. how a cooperative community is meant to secure labour effort without legal compulsion.

Turn this into the proposal. I need the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; why the rupture reading and the continuity reading each leave it open, and what each would have to establish in order to close it; the method, meaning what I would actually read and do, and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives such as tracing revisions across the successive editions of the Principles or reconstructing the influence of Harriet Taylor; the range of possible conclusions and which textual or argumentative findings would decide between them; what a useful deliverable looks like; and, above all, what would count as the project failing — in particular, how I could tell whether my threefold distinction is doing real work or is only redescribing the old dispute in finer words. Please do not supply a bibliography or a literature review by citation: refer to positions and texts only, and I will add the references myself.

Structure the response with these sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths: The Question, about 250 words; Why the Standard Readings Leave It Open, about 300; Method and Its Alternatives, about 350; Possible Conclusions, about 250; Failure Conditions, about 200; Scope for Twelve Months, about 150. Keep the whole response under 1,600 words.
