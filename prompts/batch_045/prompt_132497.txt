I sit on the oversight committee for a hospital system that is due to switch on an algorithmic triage tool in about ten weeks, and I need help with an argument a colleague used to kill a proposal of mine. The tool has two parts. The base model reads a patient's chart and outputs a probability — say 0.91 — that the patient will need urgent intervention within six hours. Bolted onto it is a second component I'll call the reliability head: it never looks at the outcome, only at features of the input and of the base model's internal activations, and it raises a flag when the input sits in a region where, in validation, the base model's error rate ran roughly four times its baseline. In flagged cases the base model's 0.91 is, we think, worth considerably less than 0.91. My proposal was simply to have the composite system report a lower number when the flag fires. My colleague says this is not merely unwise but incoherent, and gave me this argument:

(1) Evidence that the process which produced your belief that p is unreliable is itself a reason bearing on whether p; there is no special category of "higher-order" evidence that behaves differently from any other evidence. So if the reliability head is trustworthy, the force of the base model's output is diminished.

(2) The reliability head is itself a process with failure modes, so by the same principle a meta-reliability head — a third component flagging regions where the reliability head misfires — would be admissible too, and it too would bear on whether the base model's 0.91 is worth anything.

(3) Nothing turns on the level. For every n, evidence about the reliability of a level-n assessment is admissible if evidence about the reliability of a level-(n−1) assessment is; and the chain has no natural stopping point, because no process can certify itself — a broken process would be at least as likely to report that it is working as a functioning one.

(4) So for any consideration C that seems to support p, there is a higher level at which the force of C is called into question; and since no level is self-certifying, that questioning is never itself removed. A reason that lies under an indefinitely renewable cloud of doubt is not a reason.

(5) Hence nothing ever supports anything. Every consideration, the base model's outputs and the reliability head's flags alike, is already defeated before we begin.

(6) Which is not only absurd but self-undermining: if nothing is ever justified, then (1)–(5) are not justified either, and the argument gives us no reason to accept its conclusion.

(7) Therefore (1) is false, and my proposal with it: no amount of evidence that a process is unreliable can bear on whether to trust its output. The reliability head should be ignored entirely, and the composite system should just report the base model's number.

What makes me suspicious is that the argument seems to prove much more than my colleague wants. Run it on a person: a surgeon who has just been told she is concussed, or a reviewer who learns that half the expert panel dissents, has learned something she would be irrational to file under "irrelevant to whether p." And if no process can certify itself, then it looks as though the base model's own output was never evidence either, which my colleague very much wants to deny. My first thought was that (2) is the weak point, since a meta-reliability head sounds like science fiction — but when I grant it as a stipulation, the argument still seems to run, so that was the wrong place to push. I would dearly like to keep (1); beyond that I cannot locate the bad step, and I need to before the next meeting. Complicating things, another committee member has started using the word "akrasia" at me: she says that even granting (1), a system that reports 0.91 while simultaneously reporting that its own 0.91 is untrustworthy occupies a state no rational agent or artifact can occupy, and that this is a separate difficulty from the regress one.

So: which step do you reject, and why does that step look sound when it isn't? If instead you think the argument is valid, which premise is cheapest to give up and what does giving it up cost you elsewhere — in the concussion and disagreement cases, or in what we should tell a clinician to do with a flagged prediction? And is the akrasia objection genuinely distinct from the regress objection, or the same problem wearing different clothes — in which case, which one of them is the real one?
