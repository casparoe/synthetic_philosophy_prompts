I hold a particular, sharpened version of the extended mind thesis, which I'll call the surrogate-substitution version. Its test is this: an external resource counts as a constituent of a subject's cognitive process just in case replacing it with an internal surrogate — a neurally realized structure with the same input–output profile, the same failure modes, and the same characteristic error signature — would not change what kind of cognitive process it is. The point of the test is to rescue the parity principle from triviality: rather than asking whether a notebook or a phone plays "the same functional role" as an internal store, which almost any external aid can be said to do, we ask whether the errors transfer. The view then commits itself to something checkable: a genuinely extended cognitive system should exhibit the error profile of the capacity it extends — reconstruction effects, interference, systematic distortion under load — at the level of the coupled system, and not merely inside the skull.

Tell me what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which defeaters a defender of the view should concede and which they would rightly dismiss.

I want the empirical exposure mapped first: what pattern of results would be decisive, and what would only be inconvenient? Say what to make of the often-cited result that people who expect later access to information retain where to find it rather than what it says, and of the related literature on couples and teams who divide up remembering — do these show a single system with one error profile, or two agents each with their own? What observation, if someone ran the experiment, would settle it against me?

Then the conceptual exposure: cases the surrogate-substitution test cannot describe or describes wrongly — a resource a subject uses reliably but disowns and has no metacognitive access to, a predictive system that supplies thoughts before the subject has them, resources that pass the test for storing but have no analogue for perceiving or acting. Does the test secretly work only for memory-like cases?

Then the structural questions. Does the view collapse into the weaker claim that cognition is causally dependent on and embedded in the environment, or into the claim that we are coupled to tools — and if so, is the disagreement merely verbal? Has it been insulated by treating "cognitive" as a term of art, and is that insulation legitimate here or a symptom? Finally, two things I personally find most threatening, on which I want your honest verdict: the argument from explanatory relevance, that no working cognitive-scientific explanation needs to cite the external resource as doing representational work, so that the thesis if true is idle; and the downstream commitments, that if the notebook is part of my mind then damaging it is a harm to me and its contents are mine in a way that shifts responsibility. Are those genuine defeaters, or just costs a defender should pay?
