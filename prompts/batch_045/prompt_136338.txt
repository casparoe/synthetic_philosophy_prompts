I cover science and technology for a newspaper, and I keep bumping into three shapes of story that I dont have the vocabulary for.

One. A materials company pitched me a "self-healing" coating: scratches close up on their own. But the microcapsules that do the closing are wrecked by UV, and the cracking theyre supposed to fix is caused by UV. So in sunlight the mechanism that would repair the damage is gone at exactly the moment it's called for. My editor's line was that then it just isn't self-healing. But in the shade it is.

Two. A sedative co-formulated with a compound that blocks drowsiness, so nobody in the trial got drowsy — and the moment you drop the blocker, there the drowsiness is. Everyone involved went on describing the drug as sedating and nobody felt they had to take that back.

Three. A benchmark result I wrote up: a system aced a reasoning test, and it turned out it was pattern-matching off artifacts in the data set. Right output, but it doesn't have the thing the test was built to detect.

A colleague says one and two are the same problem: in both you never see the behavior, so no experiment could ever tell them apart, and the difference is invisible from the outside. Another says two is the only interesting one, and one is just a company overselling — the coating isn't self-healing in sunlight, full stop, and saying it's "really self-healing but blocked" is a way of protecting a claim from evidence rather than a real distinction.

I'm not hunting for a label. I want to know how many genuinely different things are going on across these three; what the difference between the first two actually consists in, given that they look identical from outside; whether "still has the capacity, just can't show it" is a real difference or a way of insulating a claim from being tested; and where the line falls — is there a version of my first case where you'd say the capacity was never there at all, and how would you tell it apart from the version where it was?
