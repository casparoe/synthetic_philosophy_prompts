I'm a CS PhD student working on model evaluation, and I sit in on the safety-case discussions at the lab where I intern. The move I keep hearing from the senior people there is an analogy to aviation, sometimes to nuclear power: no genuinely safety-critical industry runs on worst-case proofs. Nobody proves that a wing will not shear off; they build in redundancy, accumulate flight-hours, investigate the accidents that do happen, and drive an empirical failure rate down. On this picture, demands for worst-case or near-worst-case guarantees in alignment are a theorem-proving reflex misapplied to an empirical engineering problem, and the right object of study is reliability: an estimated probability of a bad outcome per deployment, with monitoring and rollback, improved over time. The analogy does real work — it is what licenses the lab's practice of setting deployment thresholds in expectation and treating worst-case arguments as outside the consensus.

I want to know how far it holds. Please give the mapping explicitly (a correspondence table is fine): what plays the role of flight-hours, of the black box, of type certification, of the regulator, of a fleet-wide grounding, of the human crew as last line of defence. Then the disanalogies. The three I have in mind: (1) defense-in-depth works because layers fail approximately independently, whereas an optimizer that has learned your monitoring is a failure source aimed at every layer at once and selected to be invisible to whichever layer you are using to detect it; (2) in aviation each flight is a sample from a roughly stationary distribution and accidents are informative without being existential, while here the event is single-trial and a serious partial failure may destroy the sampler and change the distribution you were sampling from — there is not really a fleet of independent deployments of the same system; (3) probabilistic risk assessment is well-developed machinery for estimating small probabilities from component failure rates, but those rates are properties of hardware, not of a system that can reason about the estimator. Tell me which of these bear on the use the analogy is being put to, which are cosmetic, and whether I have misdescribed any of them.

The part I care about most is whether the conclusion survives. My suspicion is that the analogy is carrying the argument: worst-case guarantees look unreasonable in the source domain, so they look unreasonable in the target, while the feature that made them unreasonable there — cheap, independent, informative failures — is precisely what is absent here. But I also want the strongest version of the other side: is there a reading on which aviation shows that demanding worst-case guarantees is not merely impractical but harmful, because a threshold nobody can measure gets filled in by guesswork and crowds out work that could actually be measured? And what about my candidate replacement — cryptography, not aviation: also adversarial, also a domain where a single break can be unrecoverable, but one with a culture of worst-case-flavoured reductionist arguments, proving security against any adversary in a complexity class instead of estimating failure rates. Is that a better fit, and what does it import that aviation did not — for instance that the adversary is a fixed computational budget rather than something whose aims co-vary with your scheme? If both are flawed, say what the load-bearing features of a better analogy would have to be.
