I'm a third-year PhD student in ecology — my own work is on plant–soil feedbacks and has nothing to do with social behaviour, though I did take one graduate seminar on social evolution a few years ago, so I have some of the vocabulary but nothing I trust. Which is why I'm writing about what is, on the face of it, a silly problem.

For two years I've shared a small community woodshop with exactly one other regular member. Whoever uses the space last on a Sunday is meant to spend about forty minutes closing up: sweeping, putting the tools back, emptying the dust collector, locking the building. There are a dozen or so occasional users, but he and I are the ones who are reliably there. The catch is that I almost never observe him doing it or not doing it — I see the state of the room when I next come in, several days later. A bad Sunday morning might mean he skipped his forty minutes; it might mean an occasional user left the mess; and twice I've later learned he did have to run off early for a genuine emergency. So I'm reacting to a signal I know is unreliable, and I don't think he's in a better position regarding me. There's also a coarse reputation mechanism: the owner sometimes photographs the room and posts it to the group chat, but a photograph can't show who was responsible.

Two things changed. First, the building's lease ends on a fixed date — we've both been told, we both know there are exactly eleven more Sundays, and he's moving to another city afterwards, so this arrangement has a definite and commonly known end. Second, our situations aren't symmetrical: closing up costs him about twenty minutes since he lives around the corner and costs me about an hour round trip, and I currently have a commission — a set of bookshelves — that depends on the shop staying usable, whereas he only does small repairs and would barely notice a messy room. I could switch to another shop across town, but it's worse equipped and more expensive and I'd have to move my half-finished work.

A friend, hearing this, said the whole thing should already have collapsed: if we both know the last Sunday is the last, then neither of us has any reason to do our bit then; but once that's settled, the second-to-last session is effectively the last, and so on backwards all the way to this week. Yet we've both been closing up reliably for two years, which suggests something is wrong with that argument — but I can't locate where it breaks, or whether it starts breaking only now that the end date is real rather than vague.

I've sketched two rules for myself and can't choose between them. The first is to keep doing my share to the very last session regardless of what I find, and to tell him now that I intend to. The second is to mirror what I observed last time, but with built-in forgiveness: after one bad Sunday I still do my bit; only if the next one is also bad do I skip. What I'd like from you is the reasoning, not reassurance. Which of the two is better here, and is there a third rule that's better than either? Does the fixed, known end date change the answer in the way my friend thinks, or does the unreliability of my information undercut his argument, and in which direction does each of those two facts pull? Does the asymmetry — that I have more to lose and that skipping would hurt him less than it hurts me — mean that my responding in kind has no real force over him anyway? Is announcing my rule in advance a way of protecting myself or a way of inviting exploitation? And is there some restructuring of the situation — a deposit, a checklist, changing what gets observed and when — that would do more for me than any rule for my own behaviour? I'd especially welcome being told if one of the frames I've just used is confused.
