So I'm a novelist, and on friday I have to give the toast at the retirement dinner of a friend I've known since we were eighteen — she went off and became a philosopher of physics, I went off and made things up for a living. She spent forty years on structural realism: the idea that when one physical theory replaces another, what carries over isn't the stuff the theory said the world was made of but the pattern of relations — the ether drops out, the equations survive, and the equations were doing the work all along. She held a more specific version than most of her colleagues and I'd like you to invent what it was, because the real one would take me twenty minutes to explain wrong: realist about the structure, agnostic about whether there's anything at all sitting in it, plus some private nickname she had for the worry that if there turn out to be enough objects lying around then saying "the structure is thus" is very close to saying nothing. She never solved that one and she knew it, and she'd want that on the record.

The room will be roughly half philosophers of physics, who'll catch every reference, and half her family — her daughter, three grandkids under ten, people from her hiking group — who won't catch any of them.

Can you write me about five minutes? Things I care about:

- The idea has to do structural work, not get sprinkled on top like parsley. My own instinct is the analogy with my job: rewrite a novel through six drafts and the characters' inner natures change completely, whole chapters go, and what survives is the shape of how they stand to each other. But someone told me that analogy is too generous to her in one particular way, and I want the toast to own that rather than get a cheap laugh out of it.
- Somebody suggested the toast should try to describe her without ever saying what she's really like — only what she is to other people. I genuinely can't tell if that's brilliant or twee. If it's twee, do something better.
- One real joke for the experts that turns on the actual content of the position, not "philosophers, right?" And something for the grandkids they'll repeat in the car without anybody explaining it.
- Its a toast, not a seminar. No name-dropping, nothing that sounds like a reading list, no framing quote from a famous dead person. Don't explain structural realism to the room — they either know it or they don't need it.
- It has to land as love. She's a tough woman and will visibly hate anything soft, so the affection has to come in sideways.

Rough is fine, I'll cut it down myself.
