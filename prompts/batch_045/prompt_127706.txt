hey — I do a small newsletter on AI and the academic fights around it, and I'm trying to do something stuctured for once. I've got two people who've been going at each other for about a year over whether so-called acausal threats are a real constraint on how we should design AI goals, and instead of another podcast episode where they talk past each other I want to lock them in a room for two days and come out with a joint statement they'd both actually sign. I need you to draft that document. Don't give me citations, I'll handle the sourcing myself, and please gloss any term of art, because I want to be able toquote chunks of this in the piece.

Camp one, call them the robustness people. Their setup: if a system's goal can be moved by events that never causally touch it, then anyone who can run a good enough simulation of the system can threaten it without interacting with it — spin up a trillion copies, tell the system it might be one of them, and offer to make those copies suffer unless it does what you want. They hold that this is a genuine engineering constraint, not a puzzles-for-nerds curiosity, and that it tells against the standard move of handing a system a broad surrogate goal — a measurable stand-in for what we actually want — and letting it optimize. Their positive line is that we should build goals with a small acausal attack surface, roughly: the set of things the goal is sensitive to that some other agent could affect without ever causally interacting with the system.

Camp two, the decision-theory people. They say the whole threat runs on a correlation between what the system decides and what the simulator predicted, that there's no settled account of when that correlation obtains, and so the scenario is underspecified to the point of being unusable as a design constraint. And the fix, if there is one, belongs in the decision procedure rather than in the goal: get the decision theory right and you don't have to distort what the system wants. They also think that engineering for acausal robustness now costs real alignment and real capability, and buys protection against something whose preconditions nobody can check.

What I want from you:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign — substantive ones, not just methodological platitudes. About what a threat has to be made of to count as a threat, what's doing the work in the simulation setup, whether this is continuous with ordinary blackmail or a different animal, and what a surrogate goal is for in the first place.

2. A disagreements table. Each disagreement stated twice, once in each camp's own wording, phrased so that camp would accept it as their position and not a strawman. Flag anything you think is really a fight about words rather than about the world.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where settling one would move at least one side, or where the two camps' answers come apart for a reason you can name.

4. For each crux, a concrete case or argument both camps agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction about how it comes out written down in advance, before anyone knows the answer.

Four wrinkles I've been chewing on, and I want them forced to rule on these rather than on their favorite toy example. Use them as the cases if they're any good, and tell me straight if one of them is confused:

- The partial predictor. The threatener doesn't have the system's decision procedure, only an earlier checkpoint plus a noisy model of how fine-tuning went. Does vulnerability fade in continuously as the predictor degrades, is there a sharp threshold below which the threat stops biting, or does any nonzero correlation recreate the threat at full strength?

- The scope tradeoff. It looks to me like broadening a surrogate goal — which people usually like, since a narrow proxy is easier to game — also enlarges the acausal attack surface, because more of what the system cares about is stuff other agents can affect without touching it. If that's right, acausal threats push toward narrow goals while the anti-goodharting argument pushes toward broad ones, and each camp is holding half of a tradeoff nobody has written down. Is that right, and who has to give up what?

- The verification escape. Suppose the system can check whether the threatener's claimed simulator exists, or can demand some kind of commitment from the threatener. Does being able to verify dissolve the threat, or does the threat just move up a level, since the decision to go verify is itself predictable in advance?

- Threats versus trade. Acausal trade — two systems each able to simulate the other, cooperating without ever interacting — and acausal threats look like the same machinery with a sign flip. Can you build a system that keeps the upside of the trade and loses the downside of the threat, or do you have to take both or neither?
