Writing up the position bit of my thesis proposal for Tuesday and I want someone to poke holes in it before I commit to it in print. Context: I'm a late-stage ML PhD student, scalable oversight is the load-bearing part of my whole thesis, so this matters more than it should.

Here's roughly where I've landed on iterated amplification and distillation.

1. Decomposition bottoms out. Any judgment worth making can be broken into subtasks whose answers a (human, or lightly amplified) overseer can *grade* even when they couldn't have produced them. Verification stays easier than generation all the way up the tree. This is where I think the scheme gets its leverage.
2. The target is approval, not an objective. What we want the system to be doing is whatever the amplified overseer would endorse, and we should deliberately not write down a final objective, because writing one down is where the Goodhart lives.
3. Distillation is just compression. Squashing the consultation tree into a flat model is fine as long as behaviour matches on the distribution the amplified overseer actually graded. Nothing safety-relevant lives in the recursion itself; the tree was scaffolding, not the product.
4. I call the rest the distillation gap: inputs where the flat model acts but no amplified overseer ever issued or graded a verdict. I think the gap is where all the value is, and I also think it's fine, because a model trained to seek approval on-distribution will seek approval off-distribution.
5. The point is capability past any fixed human level. This is my answer to how you supervise something smarter than you, and I expect the result to do real work on situations nobody has overseen, not just imitate the training distribution.
6. The ratchet. Each round should leave you at least as aligned and at least as capable as the last, so you can just keep going — that's the whole "iterated" part.
7. Deference is a feature. A sensible level-n overseer defers upward on questions they can't grade, and that's not a symptom of the decomposition stalling.
8. Transparency isn't load-bearing. I don't think the safety case should depend on mechanistic interpretability; the structure of the training process is what carries the weight, not being able to read the weights.
9. It's continuous with ordinary practice. RLHF with a stronger grader is basically the first rung; amplification is what you get when you keep cranking.

My concrete case, if it helps: amplifying human judgment about whether a claimed result in an ML paper actually follows from the experiments reported. That's the thing I want the scheme to work on, and it's the thing I test all this against.
