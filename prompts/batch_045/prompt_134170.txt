I sit on the Committee on Commemoration at my college — six of us, four faculty, one trustee, one student, with me as the junior member — and we rule on names, portraits, statues, and endowed honors whenever a petition is filed. Our enabling resolution gives us a fixed menu of dispositions and nothing else: retain unannotated; retain with a permanent contextualizing installation; retain but relocate out of prominent space; remove; or remove and transfer the honor to a different person or purpose. We owe a written reason for whatever we choose, and over six years we have accumulated this record. Jefferson, the college's founder, an enslaver who fathered children with a woman he owned and wrote the sentences our own mission statement paraphrases: name retained on the founding college, statue moved off the front steps into a courtyard with an installation, reason given that the wrongdoing was grave but the college honors the authorship rather than the slaveholding and there is no other founder to substitute. Columbus, whose statue stood on the town green and whose holiday the town still observed: statue removed, holiday renamed, no contextualization offered, reason given that the cruelty was recorded and denounced by people alive at the time, so there was no excuse open to him that is not open to us. Churchill, a bust in the library: retained unannotated on a three-to-two vote, the majority saying that whatever his record on empire and famine, the thing he opposed was worse and he was not replaceable, the dissent saying that is a verdict about history rather than about whom we honor. Dr. Elias Prynne, founder of our School of Public Health, author of a model sterilization statute enacted in four states, deviser of an index still called by his name: name removed from the school, the index left alone on the grounds that a word in the textbooks is not ours to rename, reason given that the instrument can be separated from the man but the honor is not owed. Cyrus Vandeventer, Gilded Age benefactor whose fortune came from a company whose strikebreaking ended in strikers being shot, and whose money built the library, the art museum, and a scholarship for immigrant children: name retained on the library with a plaque about where the money came from, scholarship renamed for one of the strikers. The poet Hallen Vane, fascist sympathies in essays published abroad and in private letters, nothing of it in the poems: name retained on the poetry prize, no plaque, reason given that we honor the poems and the poems are not implicated. And a geneticist who signed a 1939 eugenics manifesto, never acted on it, and later spent much of his salary getting refugees out: retained unannotated.

Our next petition concerns August Kellerman (1849–1919), our second professor of comparative anatomy, who founded the Institute that carries his name. Between 1878 and 1894 he assembled a collection of several thousand human remains, taken from burial grounds and from fresh graves in three states and two countries, and the comparative framework he built on it is the reason the Institute matters and is still used in research and teaching. The takings were reported in local papers at the time, and in one case a church petitioned its state legislature; the profession of the day did not regard any of it as scandalous. Kellerman also, and this is the part two of us think must count, publicly attacked the racial hierarchy orthodox in his field using his own measurements, was passed over for a chair elsewhere because of it, paid out of his pocket for litigation against a state sterilization law, and left part of his estate to a fund that still supports several Native American students a year at the college. The petition before us comes from descendant and tribal historic preservation groups and asks for repatriation and for the name to come off; a separate letter from current Kellerman scholars asks that the fund not be liquidated while we are about it. Our lawyers add two complications: the repatriation statutes probably do not reach the foreign remains or the remains that cannot be tied to any present-day community, which is perhaps a third of the collection, and the deed of gift requires that the Institute bear Kellerman's name so long as it carries out comparative anatomical research, with a family foundation threatening to sue for reversion if we remove it.

We have four decisions to make in March, and I want them argued as one: the name on the Institute and on the Kellerman Professorship; the bust in the lobby; the remains themselves, including the portion no statute reaches; and the fund. Please do the following. First, reconstruct the rule or rules our seven precedents are jointly consistent with — I assume there is more than one, and I want at least two stated sharply enough that they could disagree, with the verdict each predicts on each of our four decisions. Second, tell me which precedent is the closest analog to Kellerman and what turns on choosing that one over the others. Third, give me the set of verdicts that best coheres with what this committee has actually done, and where our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say plainly what principle we would be adopting with each option rather than pretending the record decides it. Fourth, name the single further fact about Kellerman, or the single hypothetical variant of the case, that would best separate the leading rules, so I can put it to the committee. Finally, tell me briefly whether the deed-of-gift clause should be allowed to do any work in our reasoning, or whether it is merely a constraint on what we can implement. Please write your answer in American English.
