Do some unstructured brainstorming first about which distinctions are doing the real work, and only then commit to an organized list.

A bit about me: I'm a retired field biologist (soil inverts, mostly) who has started going to philosophy talks in the evenings, maybe a few to many of them. I keep getting stopped dead by one slogan: "genes are selfish". Last month at our natural history society someone used it to end an argument outright. A member had described a case — invented, I think, as a thought experiment — of a colonial bee where workers carrying a certain allele stop laying and instead feed the queen's brood, and the reply he got was just: well, genes are selfish, so that's kin nepotism or it's nothing, groups can't be adaptive units. I want to know what precise claims that one-liner could be making, and which of them, if any, actually earns that reply.

Here's the crude sorting I've started with, which you should feel free to fix or replace: some readings are about the causal target of selection (what varies, what gets inherited, what selection "acts on"), some are about the bookkeeping or currency (what the accounting comes out in, what gets maximized), some are about phenotype (organisms behaving nastily toward each other), and some look like they're only a claim about timescales or about which entities persist intact across generatoins. Lay the readings out as a menu, and for each one say whether it is a truism, a substantive empirical claim, or false — and whether it supports the "kin nepotism or nothing" move above.

Two invented cases to test the readings against, so please don't go looking up any real species:

1. A "driver" allele that cheats during gamete formation so it ends up in 90% of the functional gametes of heterozygotes, and that also halves the total number of offspring its carrier rears.
2. An allele that makes every individual in its group more likely to survive the winter when the group contains many carriers, but within any single group leaves carriers with fewer descendants than non-carriers.

Which readings count case 1 as "selfish"? Which count case 2? Does the verdict on case 2 change if groups are formed by relatives versus formed by random mixing each season? And finally: is there a reading on which the slogan is unfalsifiable, such that no possible observation could tell against it? If so, say exactly what makes it unfalsifiable, because that's the one I suspect is being smuggled in.
