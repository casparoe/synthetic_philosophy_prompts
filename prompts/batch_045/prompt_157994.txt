I'm in a tiny decision-theory reading group this term (two philosophers, an econ guy, and one ML person) and we keep hitting the same wall: someone floats the idea that some pattern of preferences might be OK, someone else says 'intransitive preferences turn you into a money pump', and the conversation just ends, as if the slogan were doing the work of an argument. It came up twice last week. First our ML person argued that a trained system whose pairwise comparisons cycle — show it A vs B and it takes A, B vs C and it takes B, C vs A and it takes C — isn't thereby irrational, just sensitive to which pair you put in front of it, and that 'irrational' is the wrong word for a thing with no plans and no wallet. The philosophers said the pump settles it. The next day the same line got used on a friend of mine who genuinely has cyclic tastes over three paintings: she'd trade the de Kooning for the Twombly, the Twombly for the Rothko, and the Rothko for the de Kooning again, she knows she does this, and she doesn't care. Someone said that makes her irrational full stop, and I want to know what exactly they were claiming.

What bothers me is that I think the slogan is several arguments stacked on top of each other, and they might not all survive the details of these cases. Three details in particular. One: in the classic setup the exploiter offers you swaps one at a time and you never see the whole sequence coming, so you accept each trade as it arrives; but if you see the whole sequence up front and plan around it, you don't get drained — you just refuse some trades and end up holding an option that at the start you'd have ranked last, arriving there by your own choices. And there's a third style where you commit at the outset to a whole plan and stick to it even when a later step looks bad from where you're standing. I half-remember there being names for these three styles of chooser, something like myopic, sophisticated and resolute, but I never remember which is which — so describe them rather than assuming I know the labels, and tell me whether the pump argument has the same force against each. Two: the pump drains money, and money is only a stand-in. My friend isn't short of cash, and a museum with cyclic acquisition preferences wouldn't care about a few dollars at all, so the argument seems to carry a hidden premise about money being exchangeable for whatever the agent actually cares about — and that premise might fail differently for the model, for my friend, and for the museum. Three: what if each swap costs the exploiter a small fee, so the pump stops being worth running to them after a bounded number of rounds? Is a bounded, known-in-advance loss still supposed to show irrationality, or only mild imprudence?

So here's what I want, and I'd rather have a menu than a verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims that 'intransitive preferences turn you into a money pump' could be making, kept separate rather than blended. The directions I can see are: a descriptive or empirical claim about what a chooser of a given kind will in fact do over a sequence of offers; an instrumental claim that it's irrational to be disposed to end up worse off by your own lights; a stronger constitutive claim that cyclic preferences aren't coherent preferences at all; a merely prudential claim that exploitability is a risk to manage rather than a defect in reasoning; a claim about the granularity of the pump (how big a cycle has to be before anything follows, given that small cycles may cost less than the exploiter's fee); and a distinction between two shapes the argument can take — what I've started calling the round-trip version, where you end up back where you started minus cash, and the one-way version, where you end up at an option you yourself ranked worst but no cycle ever closes, which I suspect people conflate. Add any other reading that is genuinely a different thesis and not a rewording. For each: say whether it's trivial, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's actually false or rests on a premise I should push back on, and state its hidden premises explicitly. Then — the part I actually care about — say for each reading whether it supports what the slogan was wheeled out for in each of my two cases, i.e. calling the model irrational and calling my friend irrational, and if a reading only gets you a weaker conclusion than the one it was used for, say so outright. Don't cite anything or point me at papers; I need to walk into Thursday's session with the list in hand and ask them which one they meant.
