I teach a seminar in political philosophy and I've backed myself into a corner with my own intuitions, and I'd like you to help me see whether they add up to anything. We did Nozick last week — entitlement theory, the Wilt Chamberlain argument, a million people each voluntarily handing over a quarter to watch him play, and whatever pattern you started with gets upset — and it worked on my students the way it always does. But when I started inventing variants on the walk home I noticed that I mostly agree with Nozick about cases shaped like Chamberlain, and mostly disagree with him about cases that don't look like Chamberlain at all, even when the distribution that comes out at the end is exactly the same. Which makes me think I don't believe what I tell my students the argument shows.

So here are my verdicts, going by gut. First, Chamberlain himself: fine, and taxing him back down to the old pattern strikes me as something I'd have to be dragged into. Second, Chamberlain's money arrives instead as a mandatory surcharge folded into every ticket, which fans grumble about and would opt out of if they could: not fine, and undoing it bothers me hardly at all, even though the outcome is the same as in the first case. Third, the same quarter is collected by default — season tickets auto-enroll in a "fan appreciation" add-on, cancelling takes about one click, and nearly nobody bothers: I go back and forth, but I lean toward saying this is worse than the first case and better than the second, which I realize is doing a lot of work with one click. Fourth, a man owns a field, oil is found under it, he recieves a fortune without a single transaction: fine. Fifth, a woman owns a warehouse of whoopee cushions, the things become an inexplicable craze, she sells at the new price: fine, and somehow more clearly fine than the oil. Sixth, someone buys the last undeveloped stretch of coastline in a region, fences it, and charges admission that people pay voluntarily and cheerfully: not fine, and I can't shake that even though it looks like Chamberlain with turnstiles. Seventh, same but it's the only spring in a dry valley and he charges what the market will bear for water: not fine, and worse than the coastline. Eighth, a dying eccentric leaves his estate to a stranger who flattered him: fine, but I hold it less confidently than the earning cases. Ninth, a reverse Chamberlain — a coordinated boycott leaves a brilliant young player nearly destitute while mediocre ones do fine, and I feel no obligation at all to top him up; but if I add that the boycott was organized for the purpose of starving him out, my verdict flips and it feels bad. Tenth, the one I keep circling: an entertainer's fortune comes from millions of tiny automatic payments that no one ever explicitly authorized, a fraction of a cent per unit of attention, so that the money arrives without anyone choosing to send it.

The principle I keep coming back to is something like: a holding is fine if whatever made you richer was something other people actually chose to hand over, and not fine if it came from closing off something they would otherwise have been able to use, or from a payment they couldn't reasonably have declined. Does that hold together, and does the Chamberlain argument actually refute it or only a cruder patterned version of it? Doesn't my second clause secretly turn the whole thing into a proviso on acquisition rather than a rule about transfers, and if so why does the coastline case feel different from the oil case? Which of my verdicts does the best version of the principle force me to drop, and which of them do you think I should drop for reasons of my own? And tell me what the repaired principle predicts for a case I haven't put on the list: someone who gets rich selling something that has value only because everyone believes a rumor he himself started.

Give me the whole thing as one continuous prose answer, like an email to a colleague — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — and in American English.
