I am in the Lower Sixth and I run a fortnightly philosophy society at my school. We usually get between eight and fourteen people, from Year 10 to Year 13, one hour after lessons in an ordinary classroom with a whiteboard, paper, pens and whatever I can carry in. Almost nobody has any formal background; last term we worked through trolley cases, personal identity and a little on testimony. I cannot offer prizes of real value, but I can bring a few bags of sweets.

What I want is a session on this thought: that you can be led, one trade at a time, from something you have to something you like less, paying on each occasion for the privilege, while every single trade is one you wanted at the moment you made it. I have come across the idea under the name of a money pump, and the claim that preferences which go round in a circle leave a person open to one. I would like the words money pump, intransitive and diachronic kept out of the room until the debrief, if that can be managed, and I would rather the group ran into the problem than heard it described.

My own difficulty, which I should like built into the session rather than bolted on afterwards, is this. If someone's preferences really do form a circle, then wherever they start there is always something else they would rather have. A trader who wishes them ill can charge them for each move. But a kindly trader could simply hand them free improvements for ever, and they would accept every one and never settle anywhere. Those strike me as two different complaints, and I am not convinced that an activity which makes the first vivid also makes the second. Please tell me plainly if only one of them can be made to land in an hour, and which.

More specifically, I should be grateful for the following.

First, a way of drawing out each person's pairwise preferences privately so that the circle is discovered rather than assigned. I do not want the ranking handed to them: I want each of them to find that the three comparisons they made do not line up, and I want the mechanism to be one where a circle is a natural thing to end up with rather than a silly mistake — something with several dimensions to weigh against one another, perhaps. Tell me what to do with the participants whose preferences turn out to be perfectly tidy, since I expect there will be some.

Second, the trading round itself: rules, a rotating trader role so that more than one person sees where the tokens go, a payoff in counters redeemable for sweets at the end, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the hour.

Third, a round in which there is no trader at all. The same swaps are offered at intervals across the session, the counters go into a jar that nobody owns, and the group is left with the question of who ended up with what they gave up. I want the pacing to carry the point here, because the answer is meant to be that nobody took it.

Fourth, a condition in which half the room, chosen at random, may write down a binding plan beforehand in a sealed envelope and must then follow it, while the other half decide on the spot. I want people to see that knowing exactly what is going to happen is not the same as being able to stop it, and for it to be visible that the two groups can disagree about what ought to have been chosen at the very first opportunity, even though both avoid the worst outcome.

Finally, a debrief that maps particular moments in the session onto particular parts of the idea; an honest list of what will go wrong the first time I run it — I expect someone will smell the trick and refuse to trade, someone will decide the fee makes the options incomparable, someone will insist they simply changed their mind, and the envelopes may well be ignored — with advice on how to handle each; and a frank statement of which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and which I shall simply have to say myself. My guess is that it cannot show that being exploitable is what makes such preferences irrational, as opposed to merely expensive, but I would like your view.
