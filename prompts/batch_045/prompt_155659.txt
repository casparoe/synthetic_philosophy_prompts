I help out on a community dig most summers and read site reports for fun, and there's a pattern I keep bumping into that I can't find a word for. Whenever they turn up something whose use nobody can work out — a pit of carefully stacked cattle skulls, a bronze axe far too thin ever to have cut anything, a figurine with no wear marks anywhere — the report files it under 'ritual' or 'ceremonial'. So the label for 'we don't know what this was for' and the label for 'these people had religious institutions' are the same label. And then it gets used as evidence: the more unexplained stuff turns up, the more settled the religion at that site looks, so confidence in the institution grows in step with the ignorance that produced it. There's a second half that strikes me as worse. The objects we *can* explain — querns, spindle whorls, blades with edge damage — never get counted as religious even when they come out of the same pit, so religion gets built almost entirely out of the material we understand least, and a culture whose stuff we happen to find legible gets written down as a secular one.

My mate says it's just the argument from ignorance, and I suppose it's a bit like god of the gaps, but both feel off: the ignorance isn't being filled by anything, it's being relabelled as a finding, and the gap and the filler are the same objects rather than two separate things. Does this have a name in archaeology or in philosophy of science? I don't just want a label dropped on me — I want the move carved up: what exactly it is, what nearby things it isn't, and where its edges are, including what a legitimate version of this inference would look like. If there's no established term, coin one and define it with boundary cases. Continuous prose please, no headings, bullets, bold text or tables.
