Longtime lurker here — I've been reading this stuff since the old days of the blogosphere and finally got talked into giving a talk at our local AI safety reading group next week, and I'd like some Q&A prep. My thesis is that the reporter problem has a cleaner *diagnostic* form than people usually give it: forget trying to get the reporter right by adding training signal, and instead notice that the two candidate reporters — the one that reports what's actually going on in the world, and the one that reports what a well-informed human would say if you asked them — are *functions of different things inside the model*, and you can pull them apart by intervening on activations rather than by gathering more labels. Concretely, I want to defend two moves. First, use delayed ground truth: train and evaluate only on cases where the fact of the matter comes out years later (someone finds the diamond in a pawn shop in 2041), so that "match the human's answer" and "track the world" come apart on the training distribution itself, since at training time the humans were confidently wrong. Second, what I've been calling twin-patching: build pairs of inputs that agree on the world-state but differ in what the operators believe, and pairs that agree on operator belief but differ in world-state, then patch activation vectors between the twins and check which intervention actually moves the reporter's answer — if patching the model's representation of the world moves it and patching its representation of the operators' beliefs doesn't, you've got a world-tracker. I've been calling the bad case the "press-release reporter": it outputs what the footage would make a reasonable person conclude, which agrees with the good reporter almost everywhere and diverges exactly where the cameras have been tampered with, which is of course the only place we care about.

The audience is about fifteen people and it's kind of a worst case for me. Three mechanstic interpretability grad students who live in feature/circuit land and will treat anything not phrased in terms of directions in activation space as vibes. One senior ML postdoc who thinks the whole framing is a category error — the model is a next-token predictor, there is no fact of the matter about what it "knows," and asking for latent knowledge is like asking what a lookup table believes. Two philosophy grad students, one philosophy of mind and one epistemology, who I expect will press on whether the model's "belief" is well-defined at all given content indeterminacy, and on whether the reporter is *retrieving* something or *interpreting* it, since on their view those collapse into each other. And then a bunch of alignment-curious engineers who mostly want to know whether any of this survives contact with a model much smarter than its operators, or whether it's a cute trick that works on today's models and dies the moment the thing can model the patching procedure.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come out of this specific room, phrased the way each of these people would phrase it — I don't need the interp students' objection translated into philosophy-speak, I need to hear it in their vocabulary, and vice versa. Order them by how much damage they'd do to the talk, not by how deep they are. For each: a reply I could give in about two minutes with a slide, and an honest note on where that reply is soft, because I'd rather know now than in the room. Also tell me what I should concede up front, in the first two minutes, to take the sting out of the ones I can't answer — I suspect the category-error thing is one of those, and I'd rather say "yes, and here's the weaker claim I actually need" than fight it for ten minutes. And please include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, which I think is something like: if the model is smart enough to notice that it's being patched, doesn't it just make its representation of the world *look* like whatever the patching test rewards?
