Hi — I cover science and ideas for a living, and a philosopher friend cornered me at dinner the other night and I still haven't found the exit. Hoping you can help.

Her target was the intuition that there is something epistemically special about *doing* a thing as opposed to reading about it. Here's what I reconstructed from my notes the next morning (I cleaned up the numbering; the spelling errors are mine):

(1) Knowing how to do X is just a case of knowing that: knowing how to ride a bike is knowing, of some way w, that w is a way to ride a bike. Call this intellectualism. She says every anti-intellectualist alternative dies on Ryle's regress, and honestly the regress does sound convincing when she explains it.

(2) Knowledge is of propositions, and propositions are coarse-grained in the relevant way: if it's the same proposition, then knowing it is the same piece of knowledge, however you came by it.

(3) Knowledge-that isn't graded. You either know that p or you don't. There is no knowing-that-p *more thoroughly*, or knowing the very same thing *in a better way*.

(4) Now take a trainee who has read and memmorized everything there is to know about bicycle riding — grant that she even knows that w is a way to ride — and set her next to a lifelong cyclist. Both know that w is a way to ride a bike.

(5) So by (2) and (3) the two of them have the very same knowledge of how to ride. What seperates them is only execution: the cyclist's body runs w reliably, the trainee's doesn't.

(6) If the only difference is reliability of execution, then practice, apprenticeship, muscle memory, and the feedback you get from actually trying — none of that is in the business of producing knowledge. It just tunes the machinery that carries out what you already know.

(7) Which means: a system that has absorbed every text ever written about, say, emergency surgery knows how to perform that surgery in exactly the sense the surgeon does, and is missing only the ability to act on it. And a surgical residency teaches you nothing, epistemically speaking, that a sufficiently good manual wouldn't.

I can't accept (7), but I also can't put my finger on where it goes wrong, and my friend just smiles and says the conclusion follows.

Two wrinkles I should flag. First, the cyclist usually *can't tell you* what w is — ask her and she says something like "I don't know, I just do it." My friend says that's a fact about verbal access and ability to report, not about knowledge. Fine, but isn't it at least some evidence that she doesn't know that p? Second, someone at the table pointed out that reliability of execution might itself depend on stuff you can only get by doing — proprioceptive, indexical, situation-specific stuff — in which case (5) is false even if (1) is true. My friend said that move collapses back into (2) being false. I'm not sure she's right about that either.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you elsewhere? I'm especially curious about the price of each option — I've heard there's a version of the view where the trainee and the cyclist grasp the same proposition *under different modes of presentation*, one practical and one theoretical, and if that's the move then I want to know what the trainee's relation to w actually is supposed to be, because "she knows it, just worse" sounded like something that (3) forbids. And if the answer is to give up (1) and go anti-intellectualist about abilities and dispositions, I'd like to know whether the regress really is fatal or whether that's just a rhetorical habit.

If I have to choose I'd rather keep (1) than (7) — but tell me what I'm paying, and be specific about which step looked fine and isn't.
