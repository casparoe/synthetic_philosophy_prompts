I serve on the graduate committee of a history department, and over the last few admissions cycles I have heard the same picture used to settle arguments about scope. The archive, it is said, is like a satellite image of the past, or like a map you can zoom: microhistory and history from below are the same map at higher magnification. What you see from far away — states, elites, wars, price series — is an averaging over small lives; the small lives are the terrain, and you only see them close up. In our meetings the picture is made to carry a strong conclusion: that a dissertation on one village across one decade is not a complement to a wider study but a superior substitute for it, and that the macro level is an artifact of distance rather than a structure with causal powers of its own.

I want an audit of the analogy, not a verdict on microhistory. Please answer in exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion.

1. Set out the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of the map, the terrain, the resolution, the zoom control, and the person zooming — and note whether the picture quietly conflates the past itself with the surviving record of it.
2. Give the single disanalogy that does most damage to the strong conclusion above, and say why it is not a quibble. I have two candidates in mind and want your judgment on which one bites: that magnification on a map is lossless and reversible, whereas the close view of the past is selected rather than enlarged, since most of what survives was written down for someone else's purpose (tax rolls, notarial acts, court and inquisitorial case files); and that nothing guarantees the same structures repeat at every scale, so zooming in may conceal the very pressures — fiscal extraction, grain markets — that set the terms of village life.
3. Name one disanalogy a critic might reach for that does no real work here, and say why it does not matter; then say whether the analogy can still carry its political claim — that ordinary people are agents rather than backdrop — if it fails its methodological claim.
4. State what the zoom picture imports into the past that the past lacks: at minimum, scale-invariance, the assumption that the macro is a statistical average of the micro rather than something with causal powers of its own, and a view from nowhere. Say whether the superior-substitute conclusion survives once these are on the table, and whether a different analogy does the work better — I have been trying the archive as a fishing net whose mesh was cut for another purpose, so that what you haul up is evidence about the mesh at least as much as about the sea.
5. Say what would change your mind: which feature of the archive, or which kind of historical case, would make the zoom picture adequate after all, and where your audit is most likely to be wrong.
