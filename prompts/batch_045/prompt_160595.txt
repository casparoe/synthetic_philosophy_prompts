I cover science and ideas, and for some years I've relied on a Hegelian frame I've never had to defend in print: that self-conscious agency depends on being recognized by another self-consciousness, that one-sided recognition is structurally unstable because the master comes to depend on acknowledgment from someone he has declared unfit to give it, and that many disputes presenting themselves as fights over resources, facts, or doctrine are at bottom fights over standing. Lately I've been leaning on a corollary: that a well-designed mirror — a sycophantic chatbot, an algorithmically curated audience, a follower count, a deity who never answers back — can supply the felt thing without the risk of refusal, and thereby dissolve the instability the dialectic predicts.

Tell me what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and say which of the defeaters a committed recognition theorist would themselves concede rather than reinterpret. I'd like: what the view predicts that interest-based or prestige-as-just-another-currency rivals do not, and where those predictions have held or failed; whether long-stable hierarchies of one-sided recognition are counterexamples or cases the story can absorb; whether recognition from a source that is not another self-consciousness — or from strangers who don't know you exist — is recognition at all; and whether the standing qualifications (latent, unconscious, vicarious, misrecognized) have insulated the view to the point where nothing could disconfirm it, and whether that matters for a theory of this sort. I'd personally be most moved by cases where recognition is entirely withdrawn and agency does not fragment, and by cases where the mirror plainly works.
