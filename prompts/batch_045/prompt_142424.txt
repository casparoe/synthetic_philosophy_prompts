the version i actually hold, in my own words: population grows, labour gets cheap, the people at the top do well, more people at the top have kids and train those kids for top jobs than there are top jobs, so you get a growing pool of educated, broke, blocked aspirants; at the same time everyone below is getting squeezed; the state's finances go bad because the rich have got good at not paying; and then something breaks. after it breaks, population is lower, wages are higher, there are fewer aspirants, and it starts again. on the old cases that loop looks like somewhere between two and three centuries. i have a spreadsheet where i've tried to code up maybe fifteen of them on three variables: real wages, the number of elite positions that exist vs. the number of credentialled people who want them, and something like state solvency.

two things i've bolted on that i don't think are in the standard version (i read patchily, so correct me if they are):

first, a lag. the stress shows up decades before anyone with power acts on it, because the numbers only get collected and argued about slowly. so the lag should shrink as measurement and communication get faster — which means, if the mechanism is real, modern cycles should be noticeably *shorter* than premodern ones, not the same length. i think i can see this in my spreadsheet, but i also went looking for it.

second, synchrony. once regions are tied together by trade and capital flows, i think their cycles should start locking to each other instead of running independently — so the crisis phase should arrive as one genuinely global event rather than as staggered regional busts. that's the bit that makes me think about the present at all.
