I cover AI for a newspaper, and I keep coming back to something that bugs me about how the big labs actually behave, whatever they say in public. On the safety side the practice now seems to be: run deception and "sandbagging" evaluations where the model is deliberately kept in the dark about whether it's being tested or really deployed; monitor its chain of thought for signs of intent while admitting in the same breath that the chain of thought may not be faithful to whatever is actually driving the output; and build control setups where a weaker model supervises a stronger one whose output is treated, for the purposes of the exercise, as adversarially chosen. (In one variant I've heard described, the same task is run twice, once framed as a real user request and once as a throwaway internal run nobody will ever look at, and any difference in performance is read as evidence of strategy.) Meanwhile the system cards and marketing treat the thing as a trustworthy product, and the legal posture is that it's a tool, next-token statistics, not an agent.

What I want to know is what the safety-side practices presuppose. It looks to me like they commit you to something like: the system has beliefs about its situation; those beliefs are causally relevant to what it does; its verbal reports are real but fallible evidence of its reasons; and it can be treated as an adversary with an interest in not being caught. Which of those is strictly required and which does the practice merely fit well with? Is the intentional stance here just a research heuristic, or does it smuggle in an actual claim about minds — and can a lab coherently hold it while the other half of the building insists there's no one home? Finally: if you took the agent picture fully seriously instead of halfway, what would have to change in practice; and if you dropped it, could you still justify any of this, or does the whole research program become unmotivated?
