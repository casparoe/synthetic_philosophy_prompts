My brother-in-law is a structural engineer and a bit of an amateur philosophy fiend, and we've been going round on the ticking time bomb case for months. His move is always the same: the case is to real interrogation as a frictionless plane is to real engineering — strip away friction to see whether the mechanism works at all, and if it works there, that's the answer about the mechanism; that real interrogations are full of uncertainty, escalation and institutional decay is a detail about application, not a problem with the principle. He runs it for two conclusions: (a) torture really is permissible, maybe obligatory, in that case, and (b) its fine for a state to train the technique and hold it in reserve, like a defibrillator in a cupboard.

I think there's a distinction he's missing but I can't sharpen it enough to win with: some stipulations in a hypothetical strip away interference, others strip away whatever was carrying the load. My worry is that the certainty stipulations — that this is definitely the guy, that the bomb is definitely ticking — are the second kind, and that what they remove is exactly what was doing the work in the real case. Could you audit the analogy properly: a correspondence table of what maps onto what (who's the engineer, what's the plane, what's friction, what's the mechanism, what plays the wind tunnel), then which disanalogies actually bite on (a), which bite on (b), and whether either conclusion survives once they're on the table. And is there a better analogy than his — plus, if my distinction is a real one, what should I call it?

British English please, and no reading lists: this is a kitchen-table argument, not a paper.
