The reason I'm asking rather than just reading around is that I don't think the standard framing is the interesting one, and I want to know whether my sense of that is right. The version everyone repeats is "am I right now a thermal fluctuation with false memories," and the standard reply is that the hypothesis is cognitively unstable / self-undermining, so it can't be rationally endorsed. Fine, but that makes the whole thing feel like a skepticism seminar that cosmologists are entitled to shrug off. What seems genuinely dangerous to me is a different version: in a fluctuation-dominated cosmology, the things I treat as *evidence* — the CMB, the fossil record, the lab notebook, my own apparent memories — are themselves overwhelmingly likely to be fluctuation debris rather than traces of an actual past. That's not a skeptical hypothesis I can wave off by pointing out that it's unstable; it's a claim that a specific inference I actually made (data → hot early universe) was never licensed. I've been calling it the Boltzmann archive problem in my head. And there seem to be other variants I'm not sure are even the same problem: what if what fluctuates isn't a lone brain but a whole low-entropy patch with an internally consistent apparent history, or a physical process whose dynamics are isomorphic to someone performing a Bayesian update over a lifetime of fake data — is a "Boltzmann inference" cheaper to fluctuate than a brain, and does that matter?

The third thread is the one I'm most tempted by, mostly because I think it's neglected. Instead of asking what *fraction* of observers in a cosmology are fluctuation brains, ask what each hypothesis predicts about my very next observation. A fluctuation brain's next moment should look like more equilibrium noise; a real observer's should look like a lawful continuation of their apparent past. If that's right, every second of continued orderly experience is enormous evidence against being a fluctuation, and you can grant that your cosmology contains vastly more fluctuation brains than evolved ones — dominance, as it were, without defeat — and still be overwhelmingly confident you're not one. Which, if it holds up, would suggest that the literature's obsession with *counting* observers under a choice of measure is aimed somewhere downstream of where the real action is.

So, concretely: given ~18 months of one person with my background, aiming at one or two papers that a cosmology audience would actually read rather than only philosophers, which of these threads (or one I've failed to separate out properly) deserves the time? I want the dependency structure: which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable for someone who can do the physics but would have to learn the epistemology as they go, which are the ones where getting an answer wouldn't change much downstream anyway, and what would move things up or down the list if it happened in the next couple of years — e.g. if the dark energy equation of state comes back definitely not a cosmological constant, or if the measure problem gets sorted, or if it doesn't. And please skip the reading list; I can find the literature. I want the reasoning about what's worth the time and what isn't.
