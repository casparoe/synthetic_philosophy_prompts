You are a professional philosopher of law who has also spent years drafting constitutional instruments for real bodies, and you are very good at building diagnostic instruments: dichotomous keys, decision trees and taxonomies that sort people by what they say about cases rather than by the labels they prefer. Take your time and think carefully before you write. Do not begin drafting the key immediately. First brainstorm: list candidate diagnostic questions, then test each one against two filters — (i) could a non-specialist who knows only the facts of the case answer it without already knowing any doctrine, and (ii) does it actually separate two positions you want to keep apart? Keep the questions that pass both filters and quietly drop the rest. Only then assemble the tree. Say briefly what you considered and rejected, and why, in a short note at the end.

The commissioning context. I am the clerk to the Charter Review Commission of the Palimpsest, a self-governing online polity whose rulebook can amend itself, including the rule that governs its own amendment — a real-world instance of the kind of thing Peter Suber built into the game of Nomic. The Commission is about to publish a short public appendix called "Which theory of self-amendment do you already hold?", and I need the centrepiece written: a dichotomous key in the style of a botanical field guide. A reader starts at couplet 1 with a live dispute in front of them and is led, by a series of concrete forced choices, to a numbered doctrine. The point is that readers should discover what they are implicitly committed to, not pick a badge.

The rulebook, which is all the raw material you need. The Palimpsest has: R0, "the Engine" — any rule may be replaced by a resolution carried by two-thirds of the Assembly; R0.1, "the Lock", later adopted under R0 by a two-thirds vote — R0 may be replaced only by a resolution carried by four-fifths; the Assembly of four hundred seats; a Chair who counts votes, rules on order, and maintains the Register, the official list of rules in force, since only registered rules are enforced by the moderators; and a custom, not a rule, that the Chair registers a resolution within twenty-four hours of its passing. There is no court.

Cases the key must be able to run. Use at least six of the following and add up to four of your own; the population of the polity has drifted, so several of these are live rather than hypothetical.

1. The Repeal: a resolution carried by 68 per cent purports to delete the Lock.
2. The Freeze: an 82 per cent resolution adds a rule that no rule may ever be replaced again and directs the Chair to stop recording votes; a year later an 85 per cent resolution purports to undo it.
3. The Bootstrap: a resolution carried by 51 per cent purports to lower the Engine to a simple majority; the Chair, mistakenly, registers it; for three years every rule is passed by simple majority and everyone treats those rules as binding; a 70 per cent resolution then purports to restore two-thirds.
4. The Double: a single document that both lowers the Engine to a simple majority and, in the same resolution, repeals the Lock.
5. The Janus Resolution: carried by 70 per cent, it redefines "the Assembly" to mean forty thousand registered users voting directly.
6. The Ratchet: eleven resolutions, each lowering the threshold by two points and each lawful under the threshold then in force, ending with a 51–49 rule stripping protections from a named minority.
7. The Retro-Repeal: an 80 per cent resolution repeals the Lock "with effect from the date of its adoption", i.e. it declares the Lock never to have had the force it in fact had.
8. The Unregistered Rule: carried by 90 per cent, but the Chair never lists it on the Register; a year later someone relies on it.
9. The Vanishing Assembly: the membership register has not been updated for years, so three hundred of the four hundred seats are held by accounts whose holders have left; resolutions now pass on the votes of about thirty active members and are recorded as near-unanimous.
10. The Silent Century: over a century every original rule is replaced one at a time, always in accordance with the rule then in force, while the name, the membership and the servers all change.

Requirements for the key itself.

Eight to ten numbered couplets, yielding between ten and fourteen terminal doctrines. Every couplet must be a concrete case from the rulebook, stated in a sentence or two, offering two (occasionally three) plain alternatives. No couplet may contain the name of a doctrine, a theorist, an "-ism", or any of the following words, which are banned from question text though not from your notes: valid, invalid, binding, legal, sovereign, authority, constitutional, entrenched, constituent, recognition, legitimacy. Ask instead what the moderators must enforce, or whether the new rule is part of the rulebook now, or whether anyone was within their powers.

Leaves. Each terminal entry gets a name, one sentence of doctrine, and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment — the thing that view has to swallow, not the thing it likes to advertise. These must be reachable: a pedigree or hierarchy view on which each amendment inherits its force from an authorising rule that was itself in force at the time; a view on which the amending power can reshape its own scope and so bind its own future exercise; the opposed view on which it cannot and every purported fetter is defeasible by the majority that imposed it; an official-acceptance view on which the test is what the Chair and other officials treat as identifying the rules; a view on which sustained efficacy replaces the test altogether, so that the three simple-majority years changed the test whether or not anyone authorised it; a popular-sovereignty view on which the ultimate power lies outside the rulebook and cannot be alienated, so no irrevocable self-fetter or self-dissolution can succeed; a content-limit view on which some amendments cannot produce law whatever their pedigree; a view on which the persistence of the rulebook through total lawful replacement is a separate question from the fate of any single amendment; and a sceptical view on which there is no test at all and every question collapses into a prediction about what officials will do. Include also at least one leaf labelled "unnamed" for a coherent combination you think nobody actually defends, and say what would have to be true for someone to occupy it.

The logical space, before the names. Before or alongside the key, lay out the diagnostic questions in your own order and state roughly how many answer-combinations they formally permit. Identify three or four combinations that are combinatorially available but unoccupied, and for each say briefly whether the region is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to explore it. Note any place where two of your questions are not logically independent, so that answering one constrains the other.

Inseparable leaves. Flag any pair of doctrines your key cannot separate, say what does separate them in principle — a further case, a difference in what they say about the moment of transition, or a difference that shows up only in a case the Palimpsest cannot generate — or say plainly if you think nothing could separate them.

Audience, register and constraints. The readers are Commission staff and interested members with no legal training; a couplet must be answerable in about thirty seconds by someone who knows the rulebook and has read no jurisprudence. Aim for roughly 1,200 words in total, the key itself fitting on two printed pages. Theorists may appear as leaf labels only, never as the content of a leaf. Do not give me citations, footnotes, bibliographies or reading lists: I do not need them and I cannot check them here. If a real constitutional device is genuinely useful for making a leaf vivid — requirement-of-referendum provisions, unamendable-core or "eternity" clauses, manner-and-form requirements, successful-revolution doctrines — describe it in one generic clause without naming any country's article numbers, cases or dates, and mark it [verify] if you are not confident of the detail, since I can check those myself. If you think one of my ten cases is a poor discriminator, drop it and say so in your note on rejected material.

Write the whole document in British English, with British spelling and usage throughout, and with single quotation marks where scare quotes are needed.
