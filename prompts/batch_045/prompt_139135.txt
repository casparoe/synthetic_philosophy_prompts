i'm doing a philosophy of mind paper this semester and also working as a TA for a first-year logic course, and the two things have kind of collided on me now that it's exam season. We did the extended mind stuff — the famous thought experiment where one person remembers the museum address in her head and the other has to check a notebook he always carries — and my prof's line in the lecture was that the thesis is either trivial or obviously false, and that university exam rules are a good way to tell which. I think she's being too quick but I can't say exactly why, which is what I want help with.

Here's the practice, as concretely as I can give it. In the course I TA, students may bring a non-programmable calculator but not a phone, even though the phone contains a calculator that does strictly more and is arguably more reliable. They may bring one handwritten A4 sheet of notes, but under the rule as it was rewritten last year the sheet has to be "continuously available and actually relied on for the majority of study sessions for that paper" — so a sheet you made in week two and used all term is fine, a identical sheet printed out the night before is a breach, and a classmate who happens to be standing in the corridor is a breach no matter how many hours you spent revising with them. Meanwhile the disability office will approve a screen reader, a spellchecker, or a human scribe for students with dyslexia, but the same essay written with a grammar-and-rewrite tool switched on is academic misconduct, and so is paying a friend to proofread it. So: same computation, same output, wildly different treatment, and the differences don't seem to track how much thinking the tool is doing for you.

What I want is the implicit theory. What does this rulebook presuppose about where a person's cognition stops — is it that the permitted aids have been reliably available, easily accessible and automatically trusted over time, roughly the conditions the reading talks about, or is it something else entirely, like a view about credit or about who deserves praise for the answer? Which rival views does it quietly rule out: does it rule out the skull-and-skin view, or does it instead rule out the really permissive view on which any external process you're coupled with is part of you? Are the parts even consistent with each other — the scribe case and the software case look identical to me from the outside, and the calculator/phone distinction can't be about computation — and where is the practice just philosophically underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would license exactly the same rules? And finally, what would actually have to change if a department took the extended view seriously: does a closed-book exam start to look less like a fair test and more like inducing a temporary disability, or does the whole thing collapse the other way, into nothing being bannable at all? I'd especially like to know how much of the metaphysics the rules are really committed to, versus how much is only loosely compatible with them, since that's the bit I'd need before I try arguing back in tutorial.
