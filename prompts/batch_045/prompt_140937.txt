Construct a thought experiment — a matched pair of cases, plus one worked "retuning" of it — that isolates the particularist's holism about reasons (the position about moral reasons associated with Jonathan Dancy) in its strongest form, and that holds up against the standard generalist replies. I will use it in a graduate seminar to test whether the generalist can answer the "hedged principle" objection, so the case has to generate its verdicts from the theories rather than announce them: if the intuition you need turns out not to hold, say so, and either repair the case or explain why the specifications cannot all be satisfied together.

Specifications:

1. Setting. One ordinary, realistic scene — an office, a clinic, a school, a family. One paragraph per case. No trolleys, no exotic technology, no saints or monsters.

2. Matching. The two cases must involve agents in the same role and the same relationship to one another, and must differ in no element of the moral base other than a single descriptive feature F. In particular, no difference in roles, promises, prior transactions, or personal relationships: those are differences a generalist can codify easily, so they make the case too cheap.

3. The flip. Choose a target feature P — something thin and recognisable, of a kind everyone agrees can be a reason, such as "it would slightly embarrass a colleague" or "it involves a small breach of trust" — that is a reason in favour of the action in Case A and a reason against that very same action in Case B. Genuinely reversed, not merely outweighed. Include a brief test showing why the reading "P is still a point in favour in Case B, it is just overridden" is the wrong reading of Case B, and say what in the case makes that reading unavailable.

4. The inert difference. F must be a feature nobody would list as a reason for or against anything in its own right — a matter of timing, of how the information came to light, of what else happens to be on the table. This is what forces the generalist either to call F an enabler or disabler, and so to explain why that is not just relabelling, or to find a difference that your description does not contain.

5. The retuning. Give the strongest hidden-difference objection a generalist would raise against your pair. Then retune the case — move a non-moral dial: a number, an amount of money, a length of time — so that the difference the generalist points to disappears while the flip in P's valence remains. Present the retuned variant and explain why the objection now has nowhere to go.

Then, in roughly 500–700 words: set out what the particularist takes the case to show, and what remains open to the generalist — denying the intuition, positing a further unstated difference, or accepting a hedged, pro tanto or ceteris paribus principle — and write out what that principle would actually have to look like, so that we can judge whether it still guides action or has quietly collapsed into "do the right thing". Close by naming the single feature of the design that is doing the work, saying what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and giving the strongest version of the generalist's attack on it.
