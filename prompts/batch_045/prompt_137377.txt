For the past four years I have sat on the panel that allocates our region's budget for home-care hours, and I have started to notice a pattern in how we do the thing we are officially not supposed to be able to do. Formally we are asked to weigh how much difference an allocation would make to each applicant. Informally, we do this by imagining ourselves in each applicant's life. What I did not expect is that the comparisons we produce are not biased in any single direction, and that is what makes me think there is something specific here rather than ordinary prejudice plus ordinary ignorance.

Two examples. Mrs. A is eighty-two, largely immobile, and by every report cheerful; she asked for four hours a week so that someone could help her into the garden. We found ourselves concluding that four hours would make very little difference to her. I think this happened because we imagined ourselves with her body and found the prospect grim, and being in the garden then looked like a negligible consolation. Her own account of her life was not grim at all. Mr. B is forty, with a degenerative condition, and asked for the same four hours in order to continue a hobby; there we inflated the benefit, because the hobby is one that several of us share and we could vividly imagine losing it. In both cases we were confident, and in both cases I suspect we were estimating the wrong quantity: not how much the hours matter to her or to him, but how much they would matter to one of us if we had her limitations or his diagnosis. So the failure is not that we underrate people in bad circumstances. In one case we underrate, in the other we overrate. What goes wrong is that the object of the estimate silently changes, and the direction of the resulting distortion seems to depend on whether what separates us from the applicant is a difference of situation or a difference of temperament and values.

The second pattern appears when we try to correct for the first. When we defer to the applicant's own report instead, we get judgments that do not look comparable to one another: people seem to describe their own gains and losses on a scale calibrated to the range of their own experience, so that someone whose life contains few satisfactions describes a small improvement in terms that another person would reserve for a large one. We therefore have two routes to the same comparison, and they seem to supply opposite halves of what we need. Imagining ourselves in the person's place gives us something comparable across people but attributes it, arguably, to the wrong subject. Taking the person's own report gives us the right subject but nothing that can be set against anyone else's report. And it is not clear that we are entitled to combine them, since the reason for distrusting the first route is precisely the reason the second looks attractive.

Two things I should say about what I am not asking. This is not the familiar worry that we cannot really know what another person's experience is like: in both cases we had plenty of information and were not in doubt, and the problem is that the confidence was not tracking what we had been asked to compare. And I have since gathered that this whole activity has a name in welfare economics and philosophy, and that much of the discussion of it concerns whether such comparisons are possible in principle and what formal structure they would have to have. My question is different: it is about what predictably goes wrong in the room, and about why the obvious repair makes things worse rather than better.

So: is there an established concept for the structured substitution of one's own response for another person's, in the specific case where the substitution is the method rather than a lapse, and where the resulting error can run in either direction? And is there an established concept for the related dilemma, that the two available methods appear to deliver comparability and correct subject-matter in mutually exclusive packages? I gather that some writers have treated imaginative identification as the correct procedure for comparing how well off different people are, rather than as a source of error, and if so I would want to know how that procedure is meant to avoid the substitution I have described. I am also aware of nearby notions about systematic error in predicting feelings, and about preferences that adjust to what is available; I do not think either is what I mean, but I would like the differences stated exactly.

Please do not just give me a label. Whether or not the phenomenon has a name, what I want is the carving: how the concept draws its boundaries, what falls outside it, and which neighbouring concepts a careless description would conflate it with. If there is no established term, coin one and define it precisely enough that I could tell in advance which cases are instances and which are not, and give me the edge cases — for instance a case in which the substitution is not a mistake at all, or one in which the two routes coincide.
