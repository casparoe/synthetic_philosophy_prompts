ok so I've been re-reading the akrasia bit in NE book 7 on my own (yes I know how that sounds) and the thing I keep getting stuck on is that basically none of the cases my corner of the internet actually argues about fit his setup. I want you to help me figure out what Aristotle would say about them, and, more interestingly, where he'd have to admit his taxonomy just doesn't reach.

The setup as I understand it, so you can correct me if I've got it wrong: there's the four-way thing — the temperate person whose appetites already line up with reason so there's no fight, the continent person whose appetites pull the wrong way but whose choice wins, the akratic who loses despite judging correctly, and the self-indulgent person who's wrong and doesn't think he's wrong (with the brutish/pathological stuff sort of off to the side). Plus the move where the akratic's "knowledge" isn't really being exercised — it's knowledge he has the way a drunk guy reciting poetry he doesn't understand has it. And the mechanism is meant to be local and perceptual: practical reasoning, a last premise that's about this pleasant thing right here right now, and appetite or spirit shoving the general knowledge offline at the moment of action.

Now the modern cases. Four of them, and I want each one pressed separately rather than lumped:

1. The relapsing addict, described using the "chronic relapsing brain disease" framing you see all over public health messaging — cue-triggered craving, high relapse rates, the person insisting they didn't want to do it. Is that Aristotle's akratic, or is it closer to the stuff he sets aside as compulsion or bodily/pathological — i.e. not akrasia at all but a different box? And here's the bit I find genuinely hard: the same population's behavior is quite sensitive to prices, penalties and whether there's an attractive alternative activity available, which looks like evidence of responsiveness to reasons, while the cue-reactivity data looks like evidence against it. I don't know which one Aristotle would weight more, or whether the question even has a stable answer on his framework.

2. The "temperance pill." Someone on one of the new anti-craving drugs (the GLP-1 stuff, but anything would do) whose appetites now quietly line up with their judgement — not through habituation but because the pull has been chemically removed. By his own ranking this looks better than continence, since continence involves an internal fight, but it also clearly isn't a state you acquire by practice, and I take it he'd insist virtue can't arrive that way. So what state is it? Continence with no opponent? A counterfeit of temperance? Is there a hole in the schema here, or does he have resources I'm not seeing?

3. Precommitment and present bias. Someone whose preferences are time-inconsistent and who never actually wins in the moment, because they've deleted the apps, handed their passwords to a friend, locked the router in a timed box, put money on a commitment contract. Intuitively that's continence, but they didn't win a battle, they arranged not to have one. Aristotle has no discount-rate concept and as far as I can tell no box for "secures the outcome by making the option unavailable," and continence sounds like it's supposed to be an exercised capacity, not something you outsource to a safe with a timer on it. Is this continence, or prudence, or cleverness, or the abdication of continence? Does it matter whether they'd have lost if they hadn't precommitted (i.e. are we grading the person or the outcome)?

4. Akrasia of not-doing. Sitting on the couch scrolling instead of writing; not going to the gym; not sending the email. His machinery is built around an action performed against better judgement, and here what happens is closer to a default, with the "action" dissolving into a stream of micro-actions none of which feels like a decision. Does the account extend to omission at all, and if you extend it, does it still need something playing the role of the last premise / the perceptual pull of a pleasant object — because in the couch case there's arguably no object of appetite doing the pulling, just the absence of any pull toward the thing you meant to do. My suspicion is that this is the one where his picture breaks rather than bends, but I'd like that argued rather than agreed with.

Two cross-cutting things I also want:

- His account seems to presuppose a stable "what I really judge best" that appetite fights against. Present-biased discounting arguably dissolves that: at 11pm and at 9am you get different all-things-considered verdicts and there may be nothing that privileges one over the other. If he takes that seriously, does akrasia start to look empty — is he pushed back toward the Socratic position he's explicitly arguing against, that nobody goes wrong willingly? Or is there a move that saves him, e.g. indexing the relevant judgement to the agent's own longer-run, better-informed, or less-aroused self, and would that be a repair or a change of subject?

- And: what's the taxonomy *for*? It reads to me like the categories are doing evaluative work, not just descriptive work — continence is second-best, akrasia is a failure of a particular shape, temperance is the real thing. So which of those verdicts survive if the modern cases force the boxes open? In particular, does "temperance is better than continence" still mean anything once a pill can produce the temperate-looking state, and does "akrasia is a culpable failure" survive case 1?

What I'd like back: for each of the four cases, which box he'd put it in and how much of the text he'd have to stretch to get it in there; which case is the actual stress test (I've guessed at 4, argue me out of it if I'm wrong); and a sentence or two on what a fifth category would look like if he had to add one, and what principle would make it a genuine addition rather than a relabeling. No citations, no reading list, no bibliography — I've got the text in front of me, I want the argument.
