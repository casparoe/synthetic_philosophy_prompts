I'm a novelist, and I'm midway through a book set mostly in the meeting room of the allocations committee of a public health system in a small invented country — the body that decides, quarter by quarter, which treatments get paid for and which do not. One member, a retired moral philosopher, holds the view that a benefit matters more the worse off the person is who receives it. A colleague keeps baiting her with the old charge that any such view must be committed to levelling down: that if what matters is how badly off people are, then taking from the better off counts as an improvement even when it does nobody any good. She keeps replying that the charge misses. She is right about one version of it and not about the other, and I would like that to be visible in the poem without any character in the book saying so in as many words.

Please write me a pantoum to stand as the epigraph to the committee's final report — five quatrains, twenty lines, no more than 170 words, plus a title of no more than five words. Two of the eight distinct lines should be, roughly: one saying in plain, concrete language that a gain counts for more the lower down it lands; one saying in plain, concrete language that cutting someone down to the level of the person beneath him leaves nobody better off. Every other line should be drawn from the committee's actual material — a ward, a waiting list, a budget line, a flood, whatever you choose — and the verse should contain none of the vocabulary of the argument: no prioritarian, no egalitarian, no utility, no levelling, no weighting in the arithmetic sense.

The form should carry the philosophy. Lines two and four of each quatrain should become lines one and three of the next, so that a line returns unchanged and yet means something different because the company it keeps has changed — which is precisely what happens to her conviction between the meeting room and the ward. The distinction I need the poem to draw, silently and correctly, is this: where a great deal is taken from the better off and nothing is given to anyone at all, so that total well-being simply falls and no one is benefited, her view does reject the move, and rejects it flatly; but where a great deal is taken from the better off so that a little can be given to someone worse off, so that the sum of well-being falls while the gain lands lower down, her view does require the move, at least once the extra credit for the worse off is steep enough, and she cannot answer it by saying that nobody gains — somebody does. If a returning line can also hint that the flat rejection depends on the extra credit being fixed by how badly off someone is in absolute terms, and that it would collapse if the credit tracked where he stood compared with others, so much the better.

Close the loop the way a pantoum does, by restoring the poem's opening line as its final line — but altered by a single word, so that a reader hears the same sentence and understands that the reprieve has narrowed. Someone who works on distributive ethics should be able to point to which stanza is which version of the charge and say why the last quatrain is not a resolution. It should be legible read aloud in a scene, with no apparatus around it.
