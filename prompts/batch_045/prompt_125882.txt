I'm in a philosophy elective this term, and last week we spent a session on the argument that having incoherent preferences can literally cost you money: if your likes can be arranged in a circle, someone can walk you around it and relieve you of your cash one small trade at a time. I got into an argument afterward with a friend, Priya, who said those cases only get off the ground because the victim is shortsighted — a sensible person would see the circle coming and refuse to get on. Our teacher said that was exactly the live question, and that the two positions are roughly do whatever you most prefer at each moment, then trust your future self to do the same versus form a plan now and stick to it even when a later moment would rather not, but we ran out of time before settling anything. I'm writing the case up for my school's philosophy blog and I'd like your help designing it, because every version I try either cheats or collapses into the textbook one.

What I want is a money pump that punishes someone who is never, at any single moment, doing anything other than what they most prefer. The constraints:

- Setting and stakes: something a teenager could actually be in — a trading-card or sneaker swap meet, shift-swapping at a summer job, a phone-contract upgrade, a school fundraiser. Under fifty dollars changing hands, the scenario itself one paragraph, and no casinos, no science fiction, no predictors better than a shrewd person, no contracts or devices that physically force anyone.
- The victim's preferences have to be otherwise respectable: complete, with strict preference transitive. Their single defect is a threshold — they genuinely cannot tell the difference between two options whose values differ by less than, say, two dollars, so they are indifferent between them, but they strictly prefer the better one once the gap is wider. That gives indifference that fails to be transitive: roughly, A indifferent to B and B indifferent to C while A is strictly better than C. Give me the actual numbers you are using and where the threshold sits.
- The exploiter may only make offers. Every offer, if accepted, must be at least as good for the victim at the moment of acceptance as refusing it, and the exploiter must not deceive anyone, hide anything, or threaten anything. If the victim ends up poorer, it cannot be because anyone was fooled or coerced; it has to be their own preferences over time doing the work.
- The part I care about most, and the part Priya says is impossible: give the victim foresight. They can see the whole sequence of offers in advance, in order, before accepting anything, and they know their own future tastes. If you can make the scheme extract something from them anyway, show me how. If you think foresight always lets them simply decline the first trade and walk away clean, say so plainly, and then build the strongest nearby case you can: one where what they lose is the price of the only way out actually available to them — the cheapest real pre-commitment or escape, which you should name and price — and where the exploiter collects that price. If your case works by having them pay the exploiter to withhold an offer their future self would otherwise take, that is allowed, but the withheld offer has to be one that future self would genuinely accept and be glad to accept, and the exploiter's profit has to come from the victim's own preference structure rather than from a harm the exploiter could have chosen to inflict. Otherwise it is just a protection racket, and a protection racket is not a pump.

After the scenario, I need the machinery shown rather than asserted: a ledger of each offer, why it is accepted at that moment, and the running total, followed by the net extraction and an explicit statement of which single feature of the design is doing the real work. Take the two candidates to be the intransitive indifference and the combination of foresight with no power to bind a future self, tell me which one your case actually rests on, and show what breaks if you remove it. Then argue the other side as well as you can: the view that the victim is not irrational at all, because rationality is a demand on moments rather than on lives, and a pump only shows that a certain preference structure has strange consequences, not that anyone has made an error. Give me the strongest version of that reply to your own scenario, say where it bites and where it does not, and finish by telling me whether your case is better read as an argument that those preferences are defective or as an argument that being rational over time requires something extra — making plans and holding to them.
