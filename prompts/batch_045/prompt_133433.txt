After a long argument with a friend last night I am left defending the claim that the world has become less violent — the usual figures: per-capita homicide in Western Europe roughly an order of magnitude below medieval levels, no great-power war since 1945, battle deaths per capita down even as population grew. She more or less granted the numbers and denied that the claim is the sort of thing that could be true. I would rather have it tested as a single empirical position than dissolved, so: what would count against it?

Three things she raised that I could not answer, and which I would like you to engage rather than set aside. First, violence looks disjunctive rather than a natural kind — homicide, battlefield death, state killing, famine produced by policy, colonial pacification, domestic abuse — and the components trend in different directions, so I do not know what gives an aggregate like "total violence" a determinate value, or on what basis one would weight the parts. Does that deprive the claim of a truth value, or only of precision? Second, war and atrocity deaths look heavy-tailed to me; if the tail is thick enough, a couple of centuries of data barely constrain the mean, and seventy-odd years of great-power peace may be weak evidence about expected harm. What observation should move me — one more very large war, a revised tail estimate, something else — and by how much? Third, the per-capita framing conceals a choice: falling annual risk per living person is compatible with rising expected total harm in a growing population, and even a constant annual rate implies a larger cumulative lifetime risk as life expectancy rises.

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what a defender should concede in advance from what only a critic would say, and tell me honestly whether the position has been qualified into something no statistic could refute — and if it has, whether that is a defect. I would find a measurement-independent argument most persuasive, if one exists.
