The reason I'm writing is partly professional. My team ships an anomaly-detection service over a large corpus of longitudinal blood panels. It learns a representation of "physiologically unremarkable" and then flags patients whose labs sit far from that learned manifold. About three percent of flagged patients are, on follow-up, entirely asymptomatic: no symptoms, no functional complaint, no measurable reduction in lifespan or fertility, and no downstream pathology even years later. The flag is real in the sense that the model is not malfunctioning — those patients really are statistically atypical relative to the population and relative to their own baseline — but there is no agreed story about what, if anything, is wrong with them. My colleagues keep saying the model has "found a disease" or that it "hasn't found anything," and I've come to think the disagreement isn't medical or statistical at all. It's about what the word is doing.

The proposition I want a credence on is this: *An adequate account of the concept of disease must include an irreducible normative or evaluative component — that is, purely descriptive facts about biological function and statistical typicality cannot, even in principle, settle every case of whether a condition is a disease.* Please give me your credence in that proposition as a single number, and then decompose it.
