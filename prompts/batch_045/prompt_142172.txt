I do moderation (volunteer, unpaid, and yes I know how that sounds) on a long-running sandbox survival server — Minecraft-ish, except the world's been up for eleven years, the nightly snapshot only goes back thirty days so anything we miss is gone for good, and there's a house rule that a structure "belongs" to whoever built it for as long as someone in their group is still active. We're trying to write an actual policy document and I've realised I have very firm gut verdicts that don't obviously fit together. I want to know whether one principle is behind them or whether I have to give some up.

1. Someone spends maybe 300 hours on a cathedral; another player burns it down with an in-game mechanic the rules explicitly allow. Verdict: real harm, we roll back and we ban.
2. Same cathedral, wiped by a bad plugin update, no person did it. Verdict: nobody harmed anyone, and yet I want us to spend exactly the same effort restoring it. So the wrongness disappears but the badness doesn't?
3. A player quits in 2014, never comes back, his enormous castle is still standing, nobody visits it. A new player knocks it flat. Verdict: feel bad about it, I'd stop them — even though the builder will never know and no current player cares. What's even being damaged there?
4. Client-side defacement: someone runs a mod so that on their own screen the cathedral renders as rubble. Nobody else sees it, the save is untouched, they can toggle it. Verdict: not a harm at all, don't care, kind of funny. But if that's right then what I was protecting in (3) can't be "people's experience of the cathedral."
5. Duping: someone uses a bug to copy a rare item instead of taking it. Nobody is deprived of anything, the economy shifts a bit. Verdict: genuinely wrong, but I can't say who is harmed.
6. Someone griefs a character that turns out to be an automated one — no human behind it, indistinguishable in chat. Verdict: nothing happened. Same behaviour, no harm. Which suggests the harm is always to a person, never to the thing.
7. Spawn-camping a real player for an hour so they effectively can't play. Verdict: real harm, and honestly worse than (1) — which is weird, because in (1) something that took 300 hours ceased to exist and here nothing in the world changed.
8. Three players stand very close to someone's avatar in VR, silently, no chat, no touching, no damage, for twenty minutes. Verdict: definitely a harm, and it's worse in VR than the identical thing on a flatscreen, and I don't think that's just about intensity.
9. Scamming someone out of an item that's on the official real-money marketplace (so it has an actual cash price) vs. scamming them out of an equally rare, equally hard-won item that can't legally be sold. Verdict: same harm. Which probly kills any purely economic account.

My own attempt: "a virtual thing is real in the way that matters iff it's the kind of thing other agents can orient their behaviour and expectations around, and it was made by someone who meant it to persist." That gets (4) and maybe (6) right, but I think it makes (2) look fine and (1) look like nothing, and it says nothing about (8).

Is there a principle that does better — ideally one that also gives verdicts on two cases we haven't had yet: a player who "claims" a naturally generated cave formation someone else then destroys, and an AI-run character that's been around for years and that current players genuinely care about, which we're about to delete in a cleanup.
