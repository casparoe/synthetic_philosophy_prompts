I should say at the top that I have no academic background: I left school at sixteen, I have driven a delivery van for a living ever since, and everything I know about the ethics of war comes from reading on my own in the evenings for the last two years. Take the question seriously anyway; I would much rather be corrected than humoured. Please answer as a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of killing and just war theory would: think it through carefully rather than reacting, and start by sketching out — for yourself, before you commit to anything — the different rules that could produce the five verdicts below, and then test each of them against the new case. And please don't send me to books or papers: I have no library access, and I want your reasoning, not a reading list.

The verdicts are my friend Hallam's. He did twenty years as a UN military observer, he has thought about this far longer than I have, and he is annoyingly consistent. In conversation he has ruled on five cases, all set in the same war: the state of Avaria invades its neighbour Belmar, and the invasion has no just cause, although almost everyone in Avaria has been persuaded that it does.

1. An Avarian conscript, brought up to believe that Belmar sheltered the men who bombed his town, shoots at Belmaran soldiers advancing on his position. Hallam: he does nothing wrong, he is not a murderer, the Belmarans have no right to kill him — they are only excused if they do — and if he is captured he is a prisoner of war, not a criminal.

2. A volunteer from a third country who joined the Avarian army for the pay and the excitement, and who believes the same story as the conscript. Hallam: exactly the same verdict. The money and the volunteering make no difference to his standing.

3. The Avarian head of government, who ordered the invasion knowing that the intelligence about Belmar had been fabricated. Hallam: guilty of a crime, punishable after the war, and may be deliberately killed during it.

4. A civilian lorry driver who hauls shells to the Avarian front for good wages; he thinks the war is probably just but does not much care either way. Hallam: Belmar may bomb the convoy, and permissibly so, even though he is a civilian and has not been convicted of anything.

5. A Belmaran soldier defending his own country who fights on only because he has discovered that he loves killing. Hallam: what he does is permissible and he may kill Avarian soldiers, but he is a bad man and something is badly wrong with him; it is his country's cause, not his motives, that makes the killing permissible.

I think Hallam is running some version of the idea that soldiers on both sides are moral equals and that the justice of the war settles only who at the top is a criminal. But I can think of more than one rule that fits all five verdicts, and they do not agree about the case I actually want to put to him. There are two wrinkles in it, and I put them there on purpose.

A second war, ten years later. Six months before it begins, a standing international commission — twenty years of calling these questions correctly in both directions, no stake in the outcome — publishes a full public report, evidence as well as conclusion, finding that Avaria's planned invasion of Belmar has no just cause and that Belmar's defensive preparations are legitimate. The report is broadcast inside Avaria; the evidence is not reasonably deniable; Avarian officers concede to each other in private that the commission is probably right, and fight on anyway out of loyalty to their units and fear of their own military police. Consider:

(a) an Avarian infantry officer who has read the report, thinks it probably right, and still leads his company over the border;

(b) an Avarian drone operator who flies armed aircraft from a base on another continent, where nobody on Belmar's side can reach him and he runs no risk at all; he has never thought about whether the war is just, and would repeat the official story if asked.

What I want: what will Hallam say about each of them — are they doing something wrong by fighting, are they culpable, are they liable to be killed (or, for (b), is his base a legitimate target), and are they the moral equals of the Belmarans they are trying to kill? Then the rule underneath rather than just the two verdicts: name the rules that fit all five earlier verdicts but come apart on this one, tell me which of the five is the closest analog to each of my two men and why, give me your best guess at what Hallam will say with your reasons for preferring it, and tell me what further case would settle it if I put one more to him.

Two conditions. First, I would rather be told than flattered: if the five verdicts cannot all be true together, if one of my two wrinkles is doing no real work, if I have asked the question about moral equality in a confused way, or if my set-up quietly assumes something nobody should grant, say so at the top instead of answering me politely — and mark the places where Hallam's view, or yours, rests on something contested rather than settled. Second, open with a single sentence giving me your bottom line, put rough numbers on how confident you are in it, and keep the whole reply under 600 words.
