I'm giving a work-in-progress talk next Tuesday and I want Q&A prep, not a defence of the view — I can write the paper, I just keep getting ambushed in questions.

The room is a joint session of our CS theory group and the philosophy department, maybe twenty people, fifteen minutes of questions at the end. Who actually matters in it:

- Two learning theorists who work on PAC-style generalisation bounds and think the no-free-lunch theorems are a parlour trick that only bites when you stubbornly refuse to assume anything about the distribution. They'll want to know why we're still talking about this.
- A Hume scholar who cares what Hume actually argued and will bristle at treating "induction" as function extrapolation from iid samples with a loss function. Expect a point about anachronism and one about whether the sceptical conclusion is supposed to be psychological or normative.
- A formal epistemologist who thinks the problem of induction just *is* the problem of priors, and that NFL is a long-winded way of saying no prior dominates across all worlds. He'll rephrase everything I say into Bayesian and then ask what's left.
- An objective-Bayesian / statistics person who thinks symmetry and exchangeability constraints (de Finetti-style, and the sufficientness-postulate stuff) give you a principled narrowing of the hypothesis space that isn't an empirical guess about the world. She's the one I'm most worried about, because my second claim is aimed straight at her.

What I'm arguing, in three moves:

(1) The easy move. The NFL results — the ones everyone in ML theory knows, where averaged uniformly over all possible target functions every algorithm has identical expected off-training-set error — aren't a new argument for Humean scepticism. They're Hume's argument in a different notation. Hume: the step from observed to unobserved needs a premise (uniformity) that can't be had deductively or inductively without circularity. NFL: you need a bias over targets and you can't get it for free. Same shape. I expect step one to go through fine, which is exactly why I want it stress-tested.

(2) The bit I actually care about. What NFL adds isn't sceptical *force*, it's a price list. I've got a lemma I'm calling the exchange-rate lemma (mine, not from anywhere, so please lean on it): if your bias writes off a fraction p of the space of possible targets under the uniform measure, then the most it can buy you in expected off-training accuracy is on the order of p. So "how much induction you get" and "how much of logical space you've excluded" are the same quantity, and the old problem turns into a quantitative one — you can price any inductive assumption in the currency of worlds excluded. Corollary I'm committed to: the standard rescues — Occam/simplicity priors, Kolmogorov-complexity weighting, exchangeability — each deliver only if the world is in fact simple, compressible, or exchangeable, and *that* is a contingent empirical claim about the world which we believe on inductive grounds. So they can't be what grounds induction; they're downstream of it.

(3) The wrinkle I want to concede before someone takes it from me. NFL is standardly stated for a fixed training set with a uniform measure over all functions on the whole input space. Hume's problem bites at n=1 — one raven, one prediction — and it's about *any* unobserved instance, not an average over the entire input space. So there's a gap between the formalism and the philosophical problem and I need an argument that the gap is bridgeable rather than a fatal mismatch.

There's also something I haven't handled and can't quite formulate: if the choice of algorithm is itself something we learn or meta-select, does the "no lunch" result just repeat one level up, and is that regress vicious or benign? I'd rather you found it than me.

Phrase each one the way that person would phrase it, in their vocabulary, and where the learning theorist and the Hume scholar would object to the same thing differently, give me both versions. For each: a two-minute answer, and an honest note on where the answer is soft. Tell me what I should give away in the first two slides so it doesn't get taken from me in the question period, and finish with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
