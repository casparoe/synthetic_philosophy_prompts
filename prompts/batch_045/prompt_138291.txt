Hi — I'm a philosophy PhD student (thesis is nominally formal epistemology but I've drifted a long way into paraconsistency, and I run a reading group on it), and I do a day a week on contract for a hospital trust, where I've somehow ended up owning the thing that merges records from primary care, the labs, the device feeds and about six specialty systems into a single patient view. Which means I spend a lot of time with contradictions. Plain ones: ALLERGIES: NONE KNOWN sitting next to ANAPHYLAXIS — CO-AMOXICLAV; two consultants with active care plans that can't both be followed; a monitor reading and a manually entered obs taken two minutes apart that disagree. Right now we have three moves and all of them are bad: block the write (so clinicians start hiding things in free-text notes), last-write-wins (so we silently destroy information), or flag it for a human (about forty a week, triaged by a junior data steward with no logic background, in maybe fifteen minutes each, and she's definately stopped reading them properly).

I've been sketching a taxonomy and I want it turned into something usable. Roughly: (i) *source conflict* — the claims can't both be true and somebody is wrong, so this is an evidence/provenance question; (ii) *drift* — both are true relative to a different time, threshold, or sense of the term, so the fix is to qualify rather than choose; (iii) *normative clash* — the contradiction is in what we're going to do, not in what is the case, and no amount of better data settles it; (iv) *coding artefacts* — the contradiction is an artefact of our vocabulary, e.g. patient-reported "allergy" that's really an intolerance, so it lives in the ontology, not in the patient. I'm not attached to those four or to the labels.

What I want from you: a decision procedure my steward can actually run in ten to fifteen minutes, in that order — the questions to ask, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a "no" (quarantine, leave both visible), where it hands off to the clinical safety officer because the question is really about values or about who gets to overrule whom, and where it just has to say "can't settle this." Mark honestly which steps are judgment calls in procedural costume, and where you'd expect it to misfire. It needs to leave an audit trail the information governance people will accept, and it has to be writable on one side of A4 plus a short notes page.
