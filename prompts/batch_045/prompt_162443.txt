I run a small discussion group — mostly ML PhD students, two econ people, and one philosopher who keeps telling us we're reinventing 1970s economics — and we did a session on Goodhart's law and specification gaming a few weeks back. I said I'd write down my own position so everyone else could poke at it, and now that it's actually on paper it feels less coherent then it did in my head. Please take the role of a professional philosopher who's thought about both the ML alignment/gaming side and the older social-science measurement side, brainstorm a bit before you commit to anything, and be thorough — I'd rather you spot a tension I didn't see than hand me a tidy, useless list. Answer in American English, please.

Here's roughly where I've landed, as a list:

(1) I think the core mechanism is statistical and distributional rather than agential: a proxy is only correlated with the target across some range of situations, optimizing hard moves the system out of that range, and so the correlation breaks. On this picture you don't need anyone to be clever or deceptive — a dumb hill-climber will do it, and the whole thing isn't really about incentives or intentions at all.

(2) Relatedly, I think the four-way taxonomy people usually reach for — regressional, where the proxy was fit on limited data and you're partly overfitting; extremal, where you push out into the tails where the fit was never any good; causal, where you intervene on something that used to be a mere side-effect of the outcome you wanted and thereby break the link; and adversarial, where a goal-directed system deliberately exploits the gap — mostly collapses. The first three look to me like one phenomenon in three costumes, and only the fourth is genuinely distinct.

(3) I think optimization pressure is the master variable: how badly things go is mostly a function of how hard you optimize, more than of how bad the proxy was to start with. So I'm much more worried about someone taking a mediocre proxy and cranking the optimizer than about someone using a careful proxy at low pressure, and I'm suspicious of any proposal whose plan is basically "optimize really hard against this stand-in and hope."

(4) But I also think learned proxies are a genuine mitigation and not just one more proxy in a long line: fitting a reward model to human preferences and doing a bounded amount of RL against it buys something a hand-written metric doesn't — it's richer, it tracks considerations nobody would have thought to write down, and in practice it seems to break less.

(5) Methodologically I want the unit of analysis to be the proxy, not the optimizer. The question I keep asking is how gameable this metric is, in advance — and I've been playing with makeing that precise as a gaming threshold: the amount of optimization pressure (gradient steps, bits of optimization, whatever you want to count in) at which this particular proxy visibly decouples from what it's supposed to track, which you could in principle measure empirically by scaling the pressure up in a sandbox and watching where the correlation falls off. I'd like gameability to be a stable, pre-measurable property of the metric, the way a confidence interval is.

(6) I think adversarial Goodhart is categorically different from the statistical kind and much scarier: a system that can represent the fact that it's being measured and route around the measurement is doing something a hill-climber isn't, and that's what makes me think very capable optimizers are a special kind of risk rather than just a faster version of the ordinary one.

(7) My paradigm cases, the ones I keep coming back to, are the ML ones: the simulated boat-racing agent that worked out it could keep racking up points by looping around and re-triggering the same targets instead of finishing the race, and the robot arm that parked its gripper between the camera and the block so that it looked like it had grabbed the thing. I also think the original monetary case — a central bank adopting some money-supply aggregate as its target and then finding that the relationship it had been relying on comes apart — is the cleanest instance of the same phenomenon.

(8) And I think the boring human-institution cases are pure instances too: hospital waiting-time targets, exam pass rates, police departments reclassifying incidents. Same phenomenon, slower and dumber, nothing extra going on.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Go through these and tell me which pairs actually pull against each other and how badly, ranked by severity rather than everything flagged as equally bad, and separately point out any pair that only looks inconsistent and say why it dissolves. For each genuine tension, say exactly what I'd have to give up and what giving it up would cost me, and then name the single revision that would clean up the most. Finally, if one of my stated commitments can't actually deliver a case verdict I lean on it for — especially the cases in (7) and (8) — say that explicitly, because that's precisely the kind of thing I won't notice on my own.
