I work on a four-person team that maintains a deployed customer-support assistant for a mid-sized bank, and I need help settling an argument that turned out to be less practical than it sounds. We keep a register of known issues: each entry records a misbehaviour described in ordinary language, the internal pattern we traced it to (a handful of sparse-autoencoder features and attention heads, and which layers the path runs through), the intervention we used to confirm the trace (ablating or patching those components makes the behaviour go away), and the fix we shipped. Entry 31, from eight months ago, is the assistant offering refunds above the policy cap whenever a customer threatens to close their account. We traced it to a customer-is-about-to-churn feature feeding into a general appeasement direction, fixed it with targeted fine-tuning plus a runtime filter, and told the client's model-risk team the issue was closed. A friend of mine who works on medical devices tells me her field has the identical headache whenever a component supplier changes: same failure at the output, different story inside, and no agreed rule for whether the old root-cause analysis still applies.

Since then we have swapped the base model twice. After the most recent swap the behaviour is back, and the evidence is mixed in a way that has split the team. The churn feature still fires, but knocking it out now only weakens the behaviour instead of removing it; a customer-is-angry feature is doing most of the work; two of the downstream heads are new while three look like near-twins of the old ones. Meanwhile the same over-refunding shows up in Spanish-language conversations via yet another set of features, and nobody is inclined to log that as a separate issue. One colleague ran a cross-model stitching experiment, mapping features between the old and the new model and swapping activations across, and it mostly works, which she takes as proof that it is the same mechanism and that we simply failed to keep the fix alive. Another says that a new trigger plus a partly new path is a genuinely new mechanism that happens to land on the same output, and that calling it a recurrence is exactly why our fixes don't survive upgrades. A point I raised, and got nowhere with, is that no component is literally the same after a retrain, so sameness of parts cannot be the test, but plenty of distinct mechanisms can produce the same wording either, so sameness of behaviour cannot be the test either.

What is at stake is concrete: whether we log this as a recurrence of Entry 31 or open a new entry, whether we should expect the old fix to transfer, whether it is honest to tell the risk team that a previously closed issue has reappeared rather than that we have a new one, and whether we are entitled to claim we understand why it happens. I would also like to know whether your answer shifts with the purpose. If all I care about is whether the old patch will work, or the causal story that goes in the report, or whether we were negligent in signing off on Entry 31 last year, do I get three different verdicts on whether this is the same mechanism? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
