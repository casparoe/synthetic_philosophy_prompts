I'm assembling a packet of documents for a first-year seminar I teach on the prehistory of machine learning. The readings run from the 1950 *Mind* paper on computing machinery and intelligence through to the modern sparse-reward literature, and the students persistently read the passage about building a child machine and then educating it as a charming aside rather than as a research programme with a hiring pipeline attached to it. Rather than assign another commentary essay, I want to dislodge that reading with a forged primary document, and I'd like you to write it.

The document is a "Situations Vacant" notice, as it might have appeared in a Cambridge or London newspaper in 1951, placed by a university computing laboratory that has taken that proposal perfectly seriously: one should not attempt to write a programme simulating the adult mind, because the adult mind is far too large to specify by hand; instead one writes a programme simple enough to be specified by hand that resembles a child's mind, and then subjects it to an appropriate course of education, driven by rewards and punishments, until it can rival human beings in purely intellectual work. Assume the laboratory also accepts, and considers unproblematic, three further things the paper asserts in that connection: that the teacher will largely not know what is going on inside the pupil, though she may to some extent predict its behaviour; that the search for the right adult organisation is too large to conduct directly, which is precisely why education is being substituted for programming; and that the pupil's initial organisation must be small enough to be written out by hand. Format: a headline, a five- to six-sentence preamble in the voice of the laboratory's Director, six to eight numbered "Conditions of Engagement" clauses, and a one-line postscript. Total length about 550 to 650 words. The voice throughout is deadpan 1951 British bureaucratic earnestness; there must be no winking, no aside to the reader, and — since I cannot verify exact wording offline — no verbatim quotation from the paper at all, only paraphrase in the laboratory's own idiom. Do not name Turing or any other person inside the document; the irony should be aimed at the argument, not at the man.

The comedy has to come from exaggerating real features of that argument, not invented ones, so I want the clauses built around the following, each of which is a genuine consequence of the proposal rather than a strawman:

1. *The initial organisation.* The applicant must supply, by hand, the small amount of structure with which the pupil is born — a few thousand binary digits, say — and the advertisement should treat this as a clerical afterthought, listed below the duties about moral instruction. The joke is that the programming was never eliminated, only relocated into the least visible and least legible part of the job.
2. *Credit assignment and sparse reward.* The pupil arrives at correct behaviour only occasionally, after long stretches of trial and error, and the teacher is told she will not be able to inspect which internal state produced the correct answer. The reward therefore cannot in general be aimed at what deserves it. The advertisement should present this impossibility as a matter of the governess's "tact" or "instinct."
3. *No external examination.* The test of whether the pupil has been educated is the governess's own judgement that it has been; no outside examiner is contemplated, since anyone qualified to examine would herself be a product of the same arrangement. Let the advertisement state the circularity as though it were a guarantee of standards.
4. *The regress of certification.* Applicants must supply references from their own governess, and those references must be vouched for in turn, with the notice deadpan about the paperwork involved and unconcerned about where it terminates.
5. *Payment and status.* The wage should be conspicuously small against the duties, the difficulty of the post described in terms of patience, character and a quiet temperament rather than expertise, and rewards and punishments administered at the governess's own expense. I want it visible that the hard technical problem of specifying intelligence has been reclassified as underpaid domestic teaching work — this is the point of the whole exercise, so let it land without ever being stated.
6. *The punishment schedule.* Punishment, if too severe or too frequent, teaches the avoidance of punishment rather than the lesson; the notice should say something earnestly moderate about this while making it the governess's liability.

You may smuggle in anything the 1951 diction can carry — sparse rewards, an unexamined hand-specified prior, the pupil's behaviour collapsing once it leaves the nursery for a less forgiving drawing room — but nothing that breaks the frame: no "reinforcement learning," no gradients, no twentieth-century terminology the Director could not have written. After the advertisement, add a memorandum of no more than 150 words in your own voice, mapping each clause to the feature of the child-machine proposal it exaggerates, and flagging honestly any place where you had to attribute to that proposal something it does not actually say.
