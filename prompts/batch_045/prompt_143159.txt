You are a game theorist and philosopher of action who has taught strategic interaction to mixed technical and non-technical audiences, and who thinks about solution concepts as much for their conceptual structure as for their formal properties. Before writing the plan, do the design work: sketch the dependency relations among the ideas you intend to use (which ones genuinely presuppose which, and which merely feel like they do), mark which of them my students already have, and flag the places where you are making a real pedagogical judgment call rather than following a standard script. Then give me the plan itself. Do not give me the explanation I should deliver; give me the plan for the explanation. Don't give me readings or references either — I want the architecture of the session, not sources.

The setting: I have a 75-minute seminar next week for about twenty third-year undergraduates, roughly half computer science and half philosophy. The CS students have seen the halting problem and a little incompleteness, but no provability logic. The philosophy students know the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, the idea of a Nash equilibrium as mutual best response, and dominance reasoning; they have no computability theory at all. Nobody in the room has heard of program equilibrium. Logistics: one whiteboard, a projector I don't trust, so the session has to survive without displayed code. My aim is narrower than it sounds: by the end they should be able to say what a program equilibrium is, in exactly what sense it is an equilibrium, why it neither dissolves the Prisoner's Dilemma nor leaves it untouched, and why the move from actions to programs raises a question about rational agency rather than just adding a new trick to the toolbox.

Three complications you should design around. First, our campus has just spent two weeks arguing about students using automated bidding agents in the housing lottery, so everyone will arrive with a live but misleading picture of a program as a delegate or representative that acts on your behalf and has aims of its own; I expect that framing to do real damage and I want to know where to break it. Second, the same students will later in term do a session on multi-player program equilibrium and coalitional deviation, so I want the plan to install the hooks that session needs without covering any of it. Third, I have to tell them openly what I am refusing to cover, and I'd rather name the right two or three omissions than mumble.

What I want from you:

1. The order. What has to be in place before what, and what actually breaks if I reorder it — as opposed to what merely feels more natural in another order. In particular: where in the sequence must the moment go at which the object of choice changes from a move to a program, and where must the question of who or what selects the program go?

2. The carrying example. My candidates are (a) two competing trading bots whose source code is published by the exchange's rules, (b) the program that cooperates if and only if the opponent's source is byte-identical to its own, and (c) a Cold War hotline story in which each side commits a retaliation policy in advance. Pick one, or refuse all three and give me a better one, and say explicitly what properties the example must have and must lack — I assume it must not smuggle in iteration, reputation, cheap talk, or external enforcement, but tell me what else it must be free of, and what the housing-lottery agents do to each candidate.

3. The misconception map, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off and the single board move or one-sentence move that heads it off. My guesses at what I'll face: that this is just the Prisoner's Dilemma with complete information, or just cheap talk, so the strategic problem has vanished; its twin, that defection is still available so nothing whatever has changed; the opposite twin, that the dilemma has been solved; that the game must be repeated or the programs must be learning from each other; that a program which does what the other program does is circular and either hangs or has an arbitrary output, and the related thought that a program which cooperates when it can prove the opponent cooperates is circular in the same way; that the programs must be simulating each other all the way down; and that the outcome of two programs is what they print, looping programs included, with a timeout. Also tell me which of these is not a misconception at all but a correct insight I should welcome rather than defuse, and which one my students will raise in an order I haven't anticipated.

4. A decision I can't make myself: whether to include the provability-based variant in which a program cooperates iff it can prove the opponent cooperates, given that nobody here has seen Löb's theorem. If yes, show me how to do it honestly in five minutes without hand-waving the one step that makes it work. If no, tell me what to put in its place so that the circularity worry is met rather than quietly sidestepped.

5. The two moments most likely to lose the philosophers and the two most likely to lose the CS students, and what to do at each so that neither half is merely waiting.

6. A check for understanding that distinguishes getting it from pattern-matching: not restate the definition, but something like a second game, not the Prisoner's Dilemma, where I can see from their answer whether they have the concept or only the slogan. Give me the question, the answer that shows they got it, and one plausible wrong answer together with what it would tell me about where the explanation failed.
