I sit on the programme committee of a foundation that funds pilot projects in economic democracy, and at Tuesday's meeting we have to decide on the largest application in this round: roughly two and a half million euro over twenty-four months, from a consortium made up of a federation of about forty worker cooperatives (metalworking, food processing, warehouse logistics — they already trade a few hundred distinct intermediate goods among themselves, and roughly a third of their final output is sold to outside customers at world market prices, the rest being consumed internally) together with an operations-research group at a regional university. The proposal is to stop negotiating transfer prices between member firms and instead have a central planning office run a weekly linear-programming solver over data the firms report — input requirements, machine and labour capacities, inventories — and then publish a vector of shadow prices for every internally traded good. Firms would be instructed to treat those prices as parametric: choose whichever production technique has the lowest social cost at the announced prices, book internal transfers at exactly those prices, and report shortfalls and surpluses back, with the office nudging each price down when a good piles up and up when it is scarce, iterating toward clearance. Two further features matter for us. First, the application tries to answer the objection that a central office cannot know what the shop floor knows: each firm sends weekly qualitative notes about local conditions, which the office is supposed to encode as extra constraints on the programme. Second, member managers are to be evaluated on hitting their output quotas and on the cost reductions documented in their own reports.

The committee is split in a way that I suspect just replays the old calculation debate without resolving it. One member says that without markets in the means of production there is no way to compare heterogeneous inputs and outputs on a common scale, so the application is a very expensive way to rediscover a settled result. Another says that objection was formulated long before anyone had a solver and a stream of weekly telemetry, and that the trial-and-error procedure the neoclassical socialists described is now practically computable, so the interesting question is empirical and we should pay for the experiment. A third says the real difficulty was never computational but epistemic: the relevant knowledge is dispersed, partly tacit, and brought into existence by the bargaining process itself, so that a planning office fed by reports is not being denied a calculation it could perform but is being denied the inputs, and — worse — that this pilot as designed could not tell us whether it succeeded, since its own success criteria are internal to the plan. I find myself genuinely torn between funding it as proposed and funding a redesigned version, and I have one worry of my own that I would like tested rather than flattered: the shadow prices come out of an objective function defined over reported capacities and requirements, so the whole apparatus is only as good as managers' willingness to report honestly, and the application rewards managers for reporting cost reductions, which looks like an incentive to understate capacity and overstate needs.

What I need from you is the decision memo I will circulate, about nine hundred words, addressed to the committee and written so that the consortium could also read it and respond. It should open with a single recommendation — fund as submitted, decline, or fund a specified redesign — with a time frame and, if there is a pilot, an explicit go/no-go gate. It should then give the reasons in a form that engages the substantive arguments rather than summarising the literature: no bibliography and no citations, since our committee has all read the standard accounts and what we lack is a decision. Two things need particular care. The federation's partial exposure to outside prices is the only external yardstick available, and I want the memo to say concretely how a pilot should be instrumented so that we could actually learn something from it — which firms, which segment of output, whether the solver should run alongside real prices before it replaces them, and what result at the gate would count as evidence against funding the next phase. And I want the memo to be honest about the strongest thing that can be said for whichever option it does not choose: I will be asked to defend it by the members who voted the other way, and a memo that only rehearses the winning case will not survive Tuesday's discussion.
