guy in my local rationality meetup is drafting a pop evo-bio blog series and posted this in the group chat as his "steelman of the anti-adaptationist case". I've read a lot of blog-level Gould discourse over the years but never the actual 1979 paper, so I can't tell how much of this is Gould and how much is my friend. Here's the paragraph:

"In 1979 Gould and Lewontin published a devastating critique of adaptationism. Their claim was an empirical one: most traits of most organisms are not adaptations at all, they're spandrels — byproducts of selection on other traits, like the roughly triangular bits of masonry between the arches under the dome of St Mark's in Venice, which are there only because you can't set a dome on arches without leaving those gaps. The adaptationist programme they were attacking is the assumption that every trait was built by selection for the job it now does; they called it the Panglossian paradigm, after Voltaire's Dr Pangloss. And they gave a test: before you accept any adaptationist explanation, ask whether the trait could have arisen as a side effect of something else. If it could, it's a spandrel until someone proves otherwise. This is the reason to be extremely suspicious of just-so stories in ev psych: if a trait could be a byproduct, the burden of proof is on whoever claims it's an adaptation."

Three things make me twitchy but I can't cash them out. (1) The "most traits" line feels like it's converting a complaint about method into a headcount, and I don't know why that matters or whether it matters at all. (2) His test looks like it would classify basically everything as a spandrel, which would make it useless — and yet I've definately seen "spandrel" used online to just mean "trait that isn't for anything", so maybe that shift is already baked into the word. (3) Someone in the thread said the features they were pointing at in the basilica are technically pendentives, not spandrels, and that Dennett made a big deal of this somewhere; is that pedantry, or does it actually bite on the argument? I also half-remember a related term, "exaptation", that Gould supposedly introduced later with someone else — is that the piece his paragraph is missing?

What I actually care about is the downstream use. In comment sections I constantly see someone propose that X (status competition, disgust at contamination, whatever) is an adaptation for Y, and someone else reply "or spandrel", and that's treated as a refutation or at least as having flipped the burden of proof. Is that a fair moral of the paper, or is it the exact failure mode the paper was warning about with the sign flipped?
