Got a talk in three weeks and I need help prepping the Q&A, badly.

Setting: half-hour slot at a health law and policy workshop. The room is maybe 25 people. Two disability-studies scholars who've both written against legalisation and who everyone else in the room defers to. A health-law professor who helped draft our assisted-dying statute and is deeply invested in eligibility criteria and safeguards as basically the whole point of the exercise. A palliative-care physician who thinks most requests are untreated symptoms plus fear plus people not being told what's coming. A consultation psychiatrist who does capacity assessments and thinks any single-shot assessment is a fiction. And a health-services researcher who'll want a number for everything. Assume a fairly standard background regime — two physicians, waiting period, roughly a six-month prognosis criterion — I keep the jurisdiction nameless in the talk.

My thesis, which is deliberately not the usual one: assisted dying shouldn't be grounded in autonomy at all. The real phenomenon, I want to say, is that people ask to die when they have lost confidence that anyone will do anything about their suffering. A request is an appeal. What makes assisted dying the right thing to offer is the failure of the *response*, not the content of the request. So the trigger for eligibility should be documented response-failure — a written plan, attempted, failed, reviewed — not prognosis and not diagnosis.

Second part, the one I know will get me in trouble: the offer should not have to be patient-initiated. Under current norms the patient has to raise it, which means the people least likely to ask — people who don't want to be a burden, people whose clinicians have already quietly given up on them — are exactly the ones who never hear about it. So I want clinicians required to raise it once response-failure is on the record.

Three arguments in the talk: (1) the phenomenology of requests — they track abandonment and loss of confidence more than they track pain scores; (2) autonomy arguments can't explain why we restrict this to the terminally ill in the first place — if it's my life my choice, the six-month rule is arbitrary, and yet nobody who argues from autonomy ever proposes repealing it, so autonomy isn't doing the real work; (3) unsolicited *offers* aren't the danger, unsolicited *pressure* is, and the two come apart — this is the argument I'm shakiest on.

What I want from you: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, not the ones I'd get from a general bioethics audience. Order them by how much damage they'd do. Phrase each one the way that particular person would phrase it — the disability-studies objection should sound like it comes from disability studies, not from a textbook. For each: a reply I can give in about two minutes, and a straight note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes rather than have dragged out of me, which single reply has to land or the whole proposal is dead, what I say to the health-services researcher when she asks for data I don't have, and — please — the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
