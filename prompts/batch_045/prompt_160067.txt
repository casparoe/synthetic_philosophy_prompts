I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar: fourteen students, one ninety-minute session, no equipment beyond a projector, index cards and data I generate in advance, and no real money for stakes. They have already done a week each on confirmation and on the theory-ladenness of observation, and their working instinct is that evidence "adds up" toward the truth of a theory — that with enough of it, the theory is eventually forced on you. Next week is underdetermination, and I want them to run into the problem rather than hear about it: no framing lecture up front, and I would rather the distinctions get their names only in the debrief.

Here is the shape I have been toying with, which you should either make work or replace with something better. Stage one: small groups interrogate a hidden rule or mechanism under a strictly limited budget of probes they choose themselves — they design the experiments — and end up with two live candidate theories that fit everything they have been able to observe, then bet, for points, on which one is right. The rule should be such that collecting more data of the same kind does not help, so the frustration is the point. Then a beat I am less sure about: hand one group a different background assumption, an auxiliary they did not know they were relying on, and let them discover that the tie between their rivals was contingent on it and can break without any new evidence arriving. Stage two, after the bets are locked: reveal that the actual rule was never in any group's hypothesis space, so the failure was not between the rivals they compared but in the list of rivals they were able to compare at all. My worry is that the whole thing lands as a trick and the takeaway becomes that anything goes, when what I want them to feel is something narrower and harder; and I am genuinely unsure whether stage two belongs in this session or a later one.

What I need is the mechanism itself, something I can actually build and run with what I have, plus the rules, the point-payoff scheme, a timeline with minute marks, and a debrief that maps the moments they actually lived through onto the distinctions I need them to hold apart: underdetermination by the evidence available to us versus by all the evidence there could be; the dependence of any apparent equivalence on auxiliary assumptions; and the status of the tiebreaker they will have reached for — simplicity, generality, elegance — including whether that tiebreaker is doing evidential work or some other kind of work entirely. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it in the moment, and be frank about what a ninety-minute game cannot stage and that I will therefore have to say myself: my guess is that it cannot reproduce the difference between transient and permanent underdetermination, since my students only ever get finite data out of a fixed budget. Structure it under the headings Setup and materials, Rules and timeline, Debrief map, Failure modes, What you must supply; keep the whole thing under 800 words; and close with a short note on how confident you are in the design overall and which single moment or claim you are least sure of.
