I write about medicine and ideas for a national magazine, and I have spent the last two years reporting on organ donation. A large academic hospital has invited me to give the clinical ethics grand rounds that opens its committee's deliberations on whether to adopt a protocol for normothermic regional perfusion: the technique used when donation proceeds after death has been declared by circulatory criteria, in which a circuit restores warm, oxygenated blood flow to the abdominal organs within minutes of the declaration, while the vessels supplying the brain are clamped so that the brain is not reperfused.

The thesis I am defending is that the technique is ethically defensible, but only if the hospital stops telling itself that the neurological and the circulatory criteria are two ways of detecting one and the same biological event. My sketch of the argument, for you to attack: the two tests do not measure the same thing — whole-brain death is declared on the basis of absent function of the entire brain including the brain stem, whereas circulatory death is declared after a short no-touch period following asystole, at a point when the body's tissues, and much of the brain, are nowhere near destroyed. What bridges them is the move from *irreversible* to *permanent*: the cessation counts as death because it will not be reversed, and part of why it will not be reversed is that a decision has been made not to reverse it, so permanence is partly constituted by our intention rather than discovered in the body. The perfusion technique makes this explicit, because the circulation declared permanently ceased is restarted by us minutes later, and the clamping of the cerebral vessels concedes that reperfusing the brain would matter — which is precisely what the permanence claim denies. My constructive proposal: treat the determination of death as an institutional status with a biological rationale rather than as a biological fact we detect; require what I call criterion disclosure, so that what families are told matches the criterion actually being used and what it does and does not test; and defend the protocol on that basis.

The room will be: a neurocritical care physician who wrote the hospital's brain-death protocol and regards the whole-brain criterion as the only principled one; an abdominal transplant surgeon who wants the technique for organ quality and does not want procurement redescribed in any way that alarms families or lawyers; an intensivist and an ICU nurse who actually conduct the withdrawal and the no-touch period; the hospital's general counsel, who is worried about the statutory language requiring irreversible cessation, about state law, and about exposure; a clinical ethicist who holds that death is the loss of the organism as an integrated whole and thinks both criteria are imperfect proxies for that; a palliative care physician worried about conflicts of interest at the bedside; and a donor-family liaison who is herself a donor family member and cares about what families are told and whether they can trust it.

Give me my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones I would get from philosophers at a conference, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way its asker would phrase it — statutory language, graft viability, the dead donor rule, organismic integration, what the family witnesses at the bedside — with a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes before anyone asks anything, which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive the committee, and what the question I am hoping nobody asks really is. Lead with the bottom line in one sentence — the objection most likely to sink the session and the concession I have to make — then give me the sheet; no preamble and no restating of my question.
