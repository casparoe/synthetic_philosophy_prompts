I work in mechanistic interpretability on transformer language models. The vocabulary I actually think in: features and circuits, superposition, sparse autoencoders, the residual stream, attention heads, activation patching and resampling ablations, pruning and distillation, fine-tuning and continued pretraining, training loss versus downstream eval, in-distribution versus out-of-distribution behaviour, shortcut or spurious features, phase changes and grokking. I have no training in philosophy of biology, but I keep running into a distinction I cannot make precisely in my own terms: the difference between what a component of a network is *for*, what it merely happens to do, and what it is supposed to do and fails to do.

I want that philosophical dispute translated into my vocabulary, as faithfully as possible, and I want to know what does not survive the translation.

Start by restating, in my terms, the main accounts of what it is for a part of a system to have a biological function: the selected-effects or etiological family, the causal-role or organisational/dispositional family, and the variant that ties function to what a designer intended rather than to a history of selection. For each, give the criterion stated in my vocabulary, and give the experiment or quantity in my vocabulary that the account would treat as the evidence for a function attribution. Use one concrete running example, say an induction head in a small transformer: what would each account say that head's function is, why, and what would each count as an accident rather than a function.

Then run the accounts against cases I actually face, since I think these are where the accounts come apart:

- A circuit that was load-bearing early in training, was later bypassed, and still fires on the training distribution but no longer affects the output.
- A pruned or distilled model that reproduces the behaviour of the original without containing the circuit at all.
- A circuit found by a lucky initialization or by brute-force search over random seeds, with no reinforcement history behind it, that does exactly what a trained circuit does.
- A feature that lowers training loss but was never intended by anyone and is useless or harmful at deployment, for instance a detector that keys on a spurious correlate of the label.
- A model deployed for a use nobody optimised for, such as a language model used as a knowledge store, so that trainer objective, selection history, and actual use come apart in three different directions.

Report the residue honestly. I expect at least these trouble spots, but find others: my vocabulary has a loss function, which already presupposes a designer's goal in a way the biological case was supposed not to; optimisation by gradient descent is not obviously the same thing as selection, since there is no population, no reproduction, and no heritable variation in the relevant sense; and I have no term for malfunction that is not just a performance drop on some distribution I chose. Say, for each, whether the gap is a defect in my vocabulary, something interpretability needs and lacks, or a sign that the philosophical distinction was doing less work than it appears to.

Finally, answer the question I actually care about: does the machine-learning case give anyone a new argument in that debate? In particular, does the possibility of systems that were designed but never selected, or selected but never intended, or that acquire new uses after their selection history ends, favour one of the accounts over the others, or does it merely show that function attributions are always relativised to something the attributor picks?

Two constraints. Do not give me citations, a bibliography, or reading suggestions; I will add those myself if I want them, and I would rather have the arguments. And flag explicitly any place where your translation smuggles in an assumption from my framework rather than being neutral between the accounts, especially where the analogy between training and natural selection breaks in a way that would matter to the philosophical question.
