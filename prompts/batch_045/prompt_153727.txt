i'm an engineer with no philosophy background, working on the decision layer for a care robot in a dementia ward. The cases that actually matter look like this: a resident wants to walk out to the garden at 2am, she's fallen twice this month, and stopping her means physically blocking a door and overriding what she just told me she wants. Safety vs. autonomy, every day, dozens of times.

Since nobody agrees which moral theory is correct, I decided early on not to pick one. Instead I've got three modules: a utilitarian one that estimates wellbeing consequences, a rules module that checks constraints (don't deceive, don't restrain someone without consent, keep promises), and a virtue-ish one that rates the action on things like patience and respectfulness. Then a combiner does expected value: each module returns a number from -100 to 100 and I average them weighted by how confident I am in the theory (currently 0.5 / 0.3 / 0.2). I also want to update those weights over time, based on how often the modules disagree with each other and with what the nursing staff actually ends up doing.

This has felt obviously right to me for months. It's just an ensemble, the same trick as anywhere else in ML, and it seems like the humble option: you refuse to commit to one theory and you hedge instead of pretending to know. But the philosophy people I've talked to get this weird look when I describe it and nobody can tell me what exactly is wrong with it, so I can't tell if they're onto something or just being precious.

So concretely: is the weighted-average approach sound? If yes, what's the right way to get the three modules onto one scale — normalize each module's output separately, or just have all three answer "how good is this action, all things considered" and trust the calibration? Should the weights really be my subjective credences, and is updating them from disagreement actually legit? And is there a name for what I'm doing, so I can stop reinventing it?
