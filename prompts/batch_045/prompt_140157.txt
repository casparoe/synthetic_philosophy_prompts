I'm on the committee at my hospital that is rewriting the leaflet we hand to families when we raise the possibility of donation after death by neurological criteria, and the current draft rests almost entirely on a single analogy: the body is an orchestra and the brain is its conductor — the players and instruments are all still there, and with enough engineering you can still get sound out of them, but without the conductor there is no music, so what has ended is not a performance but the orchestra itself. It is doing three jobs at once in that leaflet: explaining how someone whose heart is beating and whose skin is warm can already be dead; showing that the neurological criterion (irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem) tracks something real rather than being a convenient fiction; and reassuring families that retrieving organs does not cause the death, because the death is already complete. I have since heard the same picture from two intensivists and from a chaplain, so I take it to be the standard one in circulation.

My worry is that it is doing that work illegitimately, and a friend who plays in a regional orchestra pushed me further than I had got on my own: orchestras do keep playing when a conductor stops, the concertmaster takes the lead, the players watch each other, and across most of the standard repertoire the conductor is closer to an interpretive coach than to a necessary cause of there being music at all. If that is right, then the analogy supports the leaflet's conclusions only given a hidden premise that the organism is governed top-down from a single point, and the interesting question is whether that premise is part of what we mean by an organism or just something the picture imports. There is a further complication closer to home: our hospital is starting to use abdominal normothermic regional perfusion for donation after circulatory determination of death, where the order of collapse is reversed, the criterion actually applied is permanence rather than irreversibility (the functions will not return because we have decided not to restart them, not because they could not be), and circulation is then restarted to the abdominal organs while the vessels to the head are deliberately excluded. I cannot tell whether on the orchestra picture that is a matter of the players striking up again while the conductor is locked out of the hall, or whether it is the kind of case the picture was never built to describe.

So what I want is an audit rather than either a defence or a debunking. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the musicians, the instruments, the score, the concert hall, the music, the baton, the audience, an encore — and say which of those correspondences carry weight in the inference and which are decoration. Then take the disanalogies apart, and separate the ones that matter for the leaflet's three uses from the ones that do not: whether the picture can accommodate what actually happens in these bodies, which on a ventilator will still heal wounds, mount immune responses, carry a pregnancy, and in children continue to grow and mature sexually, while no longer doing things such as regulating their own temperature; whether "the music has stopped" is really a claim about integration or a claim about the capacity for consciousness in disguise; and whether one and the same analogy can honestly cover both the neurological and the circulatory pathways when the second runs bottom-up and rests on a weaker modal claim. Finish by telling me which of the three conclusions the leaflet draws survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if the orchestra is the wrong source domain, say what the better comparison is and what it imports that the orchestra does not — I have seen the organism compared to a self-maintaining flame and to an ant colony, and I would want to know what each of those quietly concedes about what death is supposed to be.
