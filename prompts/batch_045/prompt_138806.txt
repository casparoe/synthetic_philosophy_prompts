You are a philosopher who works on collective action problems — multipolar traps, races to the bottom, the way individually sound reasoning aggregates into an outcome nobody chose — and who is also an accomplished satirist in the Swiftian mode, able to write a document whose author is entirely sincere and entirely self-indicting. Please think carefully before drafting. Begin with a short private brainstorm (not part of the finished piece) of four or five distinct satirical targets hiding inside this pattern: the but-for-anyone structure of the standard excuse, the way the diagnosis of a trap gets deployed as a move within it, the habit of describing the dynamic as an external entity to which sacrifices must be made, the ratcheting floor that never quite arrives at a bottom, the fact that the gains are positional and therefore self-cancelling, the assumption that the only possible remedy is near-universal coordination rather than a plain enforcement mechanism with a budget and a bailiff. Choose the one with the sharpest reductio, commit to it, and then tell me in a single line at the very end which target you picked and why.

Here is what I need. My current novel is set about fifteen years from now, on the Kestrel Straits, where four freeports — the older three, Brindle Anchorage, Sablemouth and Halfmoon Reach, and the brash new Perigee Terminal — compete for the same handful of shipping lines by offering ever-faster customs clearance and ever-thinner inspection of refrigerated ammonia carriers and ageing gantry cranes. Every managing director privately agrees the standards are now unsafe and unpaid-for; every concession is announced with the same reasoning, that no single port's restraint would change where the ships go, so restraint would be a pure deadweight cost borne by the restrained port alone. The regional Straits Commission has issued a Call for Evidence on cumulative standards degradation, and the ports' own industry body, the Straits Co-Prosperity Council, has submitted a response. I want you to write that response: a deadpan white paper, roughly 850 words, proposing that instead of enduring decades of incremental degradation the member ports should complete the race to the bottom in a single, efficient, one-off procedure, and get on with things. Give it a title, a three-or-four-bullet Summary for Policymakers, numbered sections, and a closing offer to pilot the mechanism at Perigee.

Constraints, and these matter more to me than the word count. Every justification has to be one the Council can sincerely make: they never admit to wanting lower standards, never claim credit for the outcome, and present themselves throughout as the trap's victims and the reform's reluctant technicians — they are, in their own telling, only removing friction from a process that would happen anyway. The comedy must come from getting the logic exactly right, so that a game theorist laughs rather than winces: the but-for-anyone test, the positional and therefore temporary nature of any advantage, the way "if we don't, Perigee will" is itself the engine, the way the trap's own diagnosis functions as a competitive instrument. Invent at least two pieces of in-world apparatus and make them feel like real policy furniture — I'm imagining something like tradable Defection Offset Certificates, a Terminal Concession Auction, Sunk Virtue credits for ports that held out longest, a statutory counterfactual-impotence test — and include at least one short worked numerical illustration of a port that gains a season of traffic and loses it again. Include one genuinely aggrieved aside about how hurtful it is that outside commentators call the members defectors. Use the word Moloch at most once, and only inside a quotation from a critic; never use the word tragedy or the phrase prisoner's dilemma, and let a reader feel the absence of both. No real names, no citations, no bibliography, and nothing quoted from any actual text: I need no verbatim material, and a reader with no internet should be able to follow everything from what is in this prompt. The irony has to stay legible without anyone winking — a careful reader should finish the piece realising that the Council is the trap and that the modest mechanism is simply the existing dynamic with a logo on it, while the document itself never notices.
