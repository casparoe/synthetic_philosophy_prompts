For the final problem set of my graduate seminar in applied ethics I need a model answer, so that I can calibrate the rubric before I hand it to my teaching assistant. The last question asks students to rank six diets by their expected effect on the suffering of wild animals. I would like you to produce the answer I would ideally circulate, showing the reasoning at the level I would expect from a strong PhD student holding fixed a year's calories and protein for one person, the six options are:

A. Grass-finished beef on land that would otherwise be unmanaged grassland; no grain or soy feed; low stocking density.
B. Pasture-raised, feedlot-finished beef: grazing for most of the animal's life, then maize and soy; far less land per kilogram than A, but with cropland in the supply chain.
C. Broiler chicken fed on maize and soy.
D. Conventional arable vegan diet: annual grains and pulses, tilled, with synthetic insecticides and herbicides.
E. Vegan diet based on perennial grain cultivars: no tillage, no insecticides, weeds controlled by autonomous machines making three passes per season, with yields per hectare about 30 percent below D.
F. Farmed insect protein (crickets) raised on crop residues and food waste in a heated indoor facility, supplemented by D-type staple crops.

The following stipulations are deliberately simplified and are not empirically accurate in every detail; treat them as given for the purposes of the exercise rather than as claims to be checked. (i) For D, about four fifths of the animal deaths attributable to cultivation occur at harvest and in post-harvest operations rather than from pesticide application; insecticides suppress insect numbers on the treated field for roughly eight weeks, but the effect on landscape-level populations is negligible once immigration from field margins is accounted for. (ii) For E, each weeding pass kills about a quarter as many animals per hectare as a harvest pass under D. (iii) Land taken out of production reverts within about a decade to secondary growth that supports a density of small vertebrates and invertebrates comparable to the crop it replaces, but with a different composition: more ground-nesting insects and rodents, far fewer sap-feeding insects. (iv) Grazing under A displaces native herbivores roughly one for one and halves ground-nesting bird numbers on the parcel. (v) Heating dominates the energy footprint of the facility in F, and its emissions are small compared with the land-use differences above. Assume throughout that the experiences of invertebrates matter morally and can be net negative; ignore farmed-animal welfare, human health, climate and other considerations except where they feed back into how many wild animals exist.

Please give me:

1. A tier list of the six options, each with a rating from −10 to +10 keyed to the average of the six, and one sentence stating why it lands where it does.
2. The ranking twice: once under the assumption that the lives of the animals in question are on balance worse than non-existence, once under the assumption that they are on balance better than non-existence. If the ordering does not shift, explain why it is robust to that parameter; if it collapses or inverts, say exactly where.
3. A clear separation of what is settled from what is contested among people who actually work on this. Flag every ordering claim that depends on a genuinely contested normative or empirical assumption — the relative moral weight of invertebrate as against vertebrate suffering, whether premature death matters independently of the suffering it involves, whether effects on population size can be aggregated at all, and whether long-run ecological feedback swamp the differences between options, the cluelessness worry. Do not present a contested assumption as the state of the art, and do not let a contested assumption do load-bearing work silently. Finally, give me a single number between 0 and 1 for your confidence in the top-versus-bottom comparison only, and name the two findings that would most change that number.
