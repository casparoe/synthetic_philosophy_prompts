i sit on my county's heritage commission, and we keep getting cases where what a community remembers and what the archive says don't match. I think our past rulings fit more than one rule and i can't tell wich one we've been running:

(1) A Black neighborhood's long-held story that the interstate was routed through it on purpose; the engineers' memos show the route was chosen on cost grounds years earlier. We kept the neighborhood's framing on the marker.
(2) A family's claim that their grandfather invented a farm implement alone; company records list a team of six. We made them change the plaque.
(3) A village's story of a massacre at a bridge; no massacre in the record, but there was a forced relocation there that same year. We paid for a memorial to the relocation and asked them to stop using the word massacre.
(4) A tribe's account of where a treaty was signed; the record says elsewhere and nothing settles it. We marked both sites.

New one: a town has reenacted, every year since 1931, the 1887 night its people dug a stranded train out of a snowdrift, and both the plaque and the schoolkids' script end with "and so the railroad gave us our depot" — that's the story of why the town exists. Our researcher found in the railroad's own ledgers that the depot was already scheduled in 1886, and the stranded-train night looks like it drifted in from a town forty miles away; but payroll records do show locals spent three days digging out a train in 1887. Two wrinkles: state heritage money now requires sites to be "documented," and the reenactment isn't an assertion anyone states out loud — the kids just pull a train with ropes.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which of the four is the closest analog, what principle would we be adopting with each option, and what further case would actually split the rules that fit our record so far?
