I am applying for a one-year postdoctoral fellowship in political theory and need a two-page proposal (about 1,200 words) on constitutional monarchy in contemporary democracies. I will supply the references myself, so please write the proposal without citations or a bibliography: refer to positions and arguments rather than to authors, titles, or literatures, except where a proper name is genuinely unavoidable.

My half-formed idea is this. The familiar defences of a hereditary head of state — that it separates national solidarity from partisan contest, that it supplies a non-partisan arbiter, that it is cheap, that it guarantees continuity across electoral cycles — all seem to fail at the same point: none of them explains why the office should be filled by inheritance. A figurehead elected for a long non-renewable term, a life-tenure office filled by supermajority, or a head of state selected by lot could apparently do the symbolic and unifying work just as well. What heredity might uniquely supply, I suspect, is not symbolism but a particular structure of incentives around the powers the monarch formally retains and never uses: assent to legislation, appointment and dismissal of governments, dissolution of the legislature. Those powers do their work, if they do any work at all, by not being exercised; but a power that is never exercised deters only if someone is credibly prepared to exercise it, and in a mature constitutional monarchy exercising it would very likely precipitate the crisis that ends the monarchy. The power is thus self-consuming, in roughly the way a nuclear threat is: available only to an agent willing to destroy the standing that makes it available. I want the proposal to turn this into an argument rather than a metaphor, and to centre it on the question whether heredity specifically solves, or merely cosmetically conceals, that credibility problem.

Please build the proposal so that it does the following.

First, separate three questions I keep running together — the design question of whether such an office should exist and be hereditary, the convention question of what the operative rules about exercising the reserved powers should be, and the legitimacy question of whether a democracy may contain an office filled by birth — and state the project's own question so that it takes one of these as primary and admits of a determinate answer.

Second, explain why the existing answers leave that question open, including why the self-consuming-power framing is not already settled by the standard debates about deterrence or about the legitimation of unelected institutions. A referee on an earlier draft said my idea reduces to the question whether any unaccountable power can be justified and has nothing specifically to do with monarchy; answer that objection in the proposal rather than ignoring it.

Third, confront a wrinkle that I think is fatal if it goes unhandled: heredity secures an interest in the continuation of a dynasty, not in the constitution. A monarch could use the reserved powers to protect the monarchy itself — against legislation abolishing it, for instance — which would make dynastic self-interest a corrupting rather than a stabilising motive. Say whether the account should treat this as a defect, a virtue, or an unavoidable cost, and what follows for the argument either way.

Fourth, set out a method, month by month, for one year. Address the methodological crux directly: by hypothesis, the behaviour that matters is almost entirely absent from the historical record of stable monarchies, because the powers are never in fact exercised. So what kind of argument could settle the question at all? Recommend an approach — comparative institutional analysis of non-monarchical offices with analogous reserve powers, equilibrium or credibility modelling, close analysis of the rare cases in which vice-regal representatives did act, or conceptual work on the difference between a legal power and one that convention has disabled — and explain why it is better here than the obvious alternatives, and how much of the year each stage should take.

Fifth, state the possible conclusions, say what considerations would decide between them, and give an honest account of what would count as the project failing. I would rather read a proposal that says the project fails if the credibility argument can be reproduced identically for a life-tenure elected figurehead, so that heredity turns out to do no justificatory work, than one that describes only success. Also say what would make the project too large for a single year.

Two working assumptions, which you should flag in a single sentence if you take them to be wrong but which need not be researched: in the United Kingdom and the Scandinavian monarchies the head of state formally retains powers that are in practice exercised only on ministerial advice, whereas in Japan the emperor's role is constitutionally defined as symbolic and without powers of government; and vice-regal representatives in the Commonwealth realms have on rare occasions dismissed governments or refused dissolutions. Do not rest the argument on the details of any particular episode.

Write the finished document in continuous prose, as something I could send to a fellowship committee: no bullet points or headings in the proposal itself, no invented references, and no appeal to empirical findings you cannot specify.
