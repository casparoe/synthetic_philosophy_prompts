I spent forty years as an experimental physicist and have lately been sitting in on a graduate reading group in epistemology. I tried to restate the Gettier problem in the vocabulary I actually trust — measurement, error budgets, systematic uncertainties — and the result seems to refute itself. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

The case: a graduate student of mine estimates the activity of a sample by dividing a measured count rate by a detector efficiency she has miscalibrated, quite badly. Unknown to her, the sample's mass is also recorded wrongly, in the opposite direction, and the two errors cancel almost exactly. Her reported value is correct to within the precision she claims, her reasoning is impeccable by the lab's own standards, and nothing available to her would reveal either error. Call this the compensating-errors case. My group agrees that it has the smell of a Gettier case. I then argued:

(1) What Gettier cases show is that knowledge requires more than justified true belief: the truth of the belief must not be a matter of luck relative to the reasons or the method that produced it.
(2) The standard way to make not-a-matter-of-luck precise is modal, via safety: a subject knows that p only if, in the nearby possible worlds in which she forms her belief that p by the same method, p is true.
(3) The method in (2) has to be individuated at some level of generality, and I can see only two candidates: the procedure as she actually runs it, in the circumstances she actually runs it in, or the procedure as she understands and describes it.
(4) On the first reading, the compensating-errors case is safe: the nearby worlds differ only in which decays happen to occur and which noise realization the electronics drew, and there the two errors are still present and still cancel. So safety certifies as knowledge a belief whose truth depends on a coincidence the subject knows nothing about — which is exactly what Gettier cases are supposed to be.
(5) On the second reading, the compensating-errors case is unsafe, and so is ordinary measurement: every real measurement rests on calibrations and corrections whose residual errors we do not know, and a procedure can be accurate only because such errors happen to cancel. So no measurement result is knowledge.
(6) (5) is absurd, since measurement is the paradigm of empirical knowledge, and (4) abandons the point of the exercise.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? I would sooner give up the modal formulation in (2) than accept (5), but I would like to know what an anti-luck condition looks like once nearby worlds are dropped, and what it would have to say about my student.
