Hey — I've got to run a 50-minute session next week on pain asymbolia and whether it shows that pain is only contingently bad, and I need help planning the hour rather than an explanation of the topic itself. The room is a genuinely motley group: palliative care nurses, two residents, a chaplain, and a few people from the hospital ethics committee. Nobody's done philosophy. Most of them will walk in with some version of the picture of a frontal-lobe patient who grins while you stick a pin in him, and with the assumption that this is a niche curiosity with no bearing on their work.

What I want them to leave able to do is say which of three things they'd give up: that badness is built into the sensation itself; that pain is bad because of the subject's relation to it — we're averse to it, we want it gone; and that pain is bad because of what it does to a life, grabbing attention, wrecking concentration, closing off options. Asymbolia looks like a clean counterexample to the first, something odd and partial for the second, and I genuinely can't tell whether it's silent on the third.

So, concretely, the things I'm stuck on:

One, the order. My instinct is that I have to get them to feel the pull of "pain just is bad, that's what the word means" before any case appears at all, or the cases won't surprise anyone. But should the three-way split between the sensory, the motivational and the evaluative aspects of pain come before the first case, or should they arrive at that split themselves by comparing two cases? I keep going back and forth and I can't tell what depends on what.

Two, the case that carries the session. I've been toying with inventing one instead of using the standard clinical description: a patient with the inverse profile — the pain is awful, she wants it stopped now, it ruins her afternoon, but she can't locate it, can't rate it, can't say whether it's burning or stabbing, because as far as she can tell there's no sensory thing there to describe. Is that a better or worse centrepiece than a straight asymbolia case? What does the carrying case have to have for the detachability point to land, and what would quietly wreck it? My worry about my invented one is that the missing sensory bit does all the work and the lesson degrades into "pain needs a location" rather than anything about value.

Three, the misconceptions, and where in the hour to head each off. The ones I'm expecting: that this is just Stoicism or mind over matter; that the patients are lying or putting on a brave face; that if it doesn't hurt then by definition it isn't pain; and I'd bet good money someone says either "so don't give them analgesia" or the really bad one, "so we can ignore what the patient reports." The one I can't place is the slide from "pain isn't intrinsically bad" to "nothing is intrinsically bad, suffering is just a preference." If that one lands I've lost the room, and I don't know what needs to already be on the whiteboard beforehand so it doesn't.

Four, how I'll know it worked. I need one closing question that distinguishes people who've actually got the distinction from people who've just picked up the habit of saying "it's complicated." And I'd like to know what I should openly refuse to get into — I was going to rule out, up front, settling whether these patients are really in pain, and any discussion of analgesia policy — but tell me if there's something more important I should be ruling out instead.

Whiteboard, no slides, and I'd rather plan for forty-five minutes of material than fifty so there's room for it to go wrong.
