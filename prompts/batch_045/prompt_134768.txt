I came to all of this from an argument about whether the worst thing in the world is pain, and I should say up front that I have no philosophy training whatsoever — I plan delivery routes for a freight company, and four of us argue about these things in a pub on Sunday afternoons. I've tried reading the material people point me at and I always get lost a few paragraphs in, so please don't send me to anything: instead I've written down, as exactly as I can, the things I actually believe, and what I want is an audit of the set. Which of these claims genuinely can't live with which of the others, which pairs only look like they clash, ranked by how badly they really collide — and for each genuine collision, what the cheapest revision is and what that revision would cost me somewhere else in the set.

To keep it concrete I use an imaginary device: a dial you strap on that produces an exactly specified experience, calibrated 0 to 100, where I'd put a mild sunburn around 8, a bad toothache around 40, and passing a kidney stone somewhere in the high 70s or low 80s. With that in hand, here's where I've landed. (1) One currency: pleasures and pains are the same kind of stuff with a plus or minus sign, and they trade off continuously — for any unpleasant setting on the dial there's some amount or duration of pleasant setting I'd take as an even swap, and I take my own willingness to make that swap as real evidence of a common scale. (2) What I call the lock-in point, my own label: past some intensity, an experience stops being compensable at all, so that one person's hour at 80 is worse than any number of people having any length of wonderful experience. (3) But there is no sharp line: discomfort shades into suffering the way a heap shades into a non-heap, and I don't think there's a fact of the matter about the exact setting at which the lock-in kicks in — and I don't think that's just ignorance that better measurement would fix. (4) Below the line, ordinary arithmetic: a million mild headaches really is a million times as bad as one, whether they're in one head or a million heads, and they can be weighed against ordinary pleasures in the normal way. (5) But nothing adds up to the line from below: however many mild headaches you pile up, you never arrive at something that outweighs a single hour at 80. I've started calling this the pocket problem, because it feels like mild pain is stuck in a separate pocket from severe pain even though I want to say they're made of the same stuff — and it's the item on this list I'm least comfortable with, though I can't say why. (6) An empty world has value zero, not more than zero: a world full of contented people with nothing above the line in it is straightforwardly better than an empty one, bringing such people into existence is fine, and painlessly ending everyone alive right now would be monstrous. (7) I'm not a fanatic about risk — I drive a car, and I think accepting a small chance of something above the line in exchange for a lot of ordinary good is fine, though I can't tell you what rule I'm using when I say that. (8) The line should be a fact about the experiences themselves, not a fact about what anyone would put up with; I'd want it to stay put even if it turned out that my own willingness to accept pain slides around depending on the time of day.

Two of my pub friends have taken swings at this and I don't know whether either lands. One says (2) and (7) flatly contradict each other. Another says that once I say pain lexically dominates pleasure, I'm committed to wanting the world emptied of everyone, which is the one conclusion I can't accept. My own suspicion is that the trouble is somewhere else entirely, in the region of (3) and (5), but I can't locate it, and I don't trust my suspicion. So: go through the set pair by pair, tell me which of these are real tensions and which dissolve when you look properly, order the real ones by severity, and say for each what would have to give. One last constraint: (6) is the item I'm most certain of, so if some revision would force me to abandon it, flag that loudly, because I'd probably reject the revision instead.
