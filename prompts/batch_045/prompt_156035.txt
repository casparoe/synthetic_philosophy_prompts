I'm a third-year in an epistemology seminar, and I've been asked to lead a thirty-minute session for the undergraduate reading group that runs alongside it on luminosity and the KK principle. The group has done Gettier and the justified-true-belief analysis and nothing beyond that: no formal or modal epistemology, no logic past truth tables.

Please don't explain the material to me; plan how I should explain it. What I need is:

- The order the ideas have to arrive in and what depends on what. My instinct is to get luminosity on the table first and then present the KK principle as what falls out of luminosity once you treat knowing that p as itself a state of the knower, so that the margin-for-error argument against luminosity removes both at one stroke. Tell me whether that dependency is real or whether I have it backwards, where the step from "in a position to know" to "knows" has to be handled (I can't see whether it is free), and what has to be in place before the argument can even be stated.
- The one example that carries the whole session, and what it must have and must lack. Two constraints: the condition being monitored should not be a sensation, so they can't file the conclusion as "introspection of feelings is unreliable," and the case must not hand the subject any external readout to consult. I'm inclined toward a driver judging, hour by hour on a long night drive, whether she has now become too drowsy to continue; argue me out of it if it smuggles something in.
- The misconceptions, in the order they'll form, and the exact point in the sequence at which each has to be headed off. The three I most fear: reading the argument as the sorites paradox in costume; concluding that we never know our own minds; and taking the moral to be that knowledge requires certainty, or that KK fails because people are overconfident.
- What I should openly decline to explain at this level, and one question at the end that tells me they grasped the argument rather than its mood. I'd like the check to be a classification task: hand them a candidate condition and ask whether it is luminous and why.

Format: an ordered sequence of steps, one short paragraph each, at most eight steps, under 600 words in total, closing with the check question as a single sentence.
