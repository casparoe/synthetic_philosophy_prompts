I sit, as the token psychiatrist among mostly health-service planners, on the working group that maintains the diagnostic classification a mid-sized national health system uses for three jobs at once: gating access to funded care, matching patients to treatments, and defining research cohorts. We revise it roughly once a decade, and the revision will be published as plain open text — machine-readable, quotable, and certain to end up in screening questionnaires, school guidance, insurance forms, and the short-form video content that patients now consume before they ever meet a clinician. People will arrive at the clinic having already applied one of our labels to themselves, and in some cases having organized friendships, work, and a sense of their own limits around it. I have recently been rereading Ian Hacking on what he called looping effects — the way classifying people feeds back into how the classified understand themselves, present themselves, and are treated, so that the kind and the classification move in relation to each other — along with his historical case of the fugue diagnosis of the 1890s, which appeared, spread through a population of patients who then presented in its terms, and eventually faded. It has made me suspicious of how my colleagues argue: much of what we demand of a category now looks to me like the methodological preference of whichever faction is in the room — the neuroscientists want mechanism, the trialists want a variable that responds to an intervention, the clinicians want a decision rule applicable in fifteen minutes, patient advocates want recognition and access, finance wants an auditable code, and the psychometricians want internal consistency.

So before we begin trading amendments, I want the specification. Write the desiderata that any classification of mental disorders has to meet given that it will loop, each with the case or consideration that motivates it and an explicit verdict on whether it is non-negotiable for a classification doing our three jobs or merely a preference that a system with different jobs could drop. Please cover at least the distinctions a classification must not collapse: being useful for a decision versus tracking something real; reliability across users versus what I have started calling loop-stability, the property of a category's continuing to pick out roughly the same phenomenon once the people it describes know the criteria and reorganize around them; and accuracy at one moment versus stability across a decade of feedback. Say also what kind of evidence should be permitted to count as validation, given that a category can acquire impressive predictive validity partly because being labeled changes whom you subsequently meet, what they are offered, and how they describe themselves — I want to know which validation findings a loop-aware classification should discount, and roughly by how much. Then the part I care about most: which subsets of the desiderata on your own list cannot be jointly satisfied once looping is taken seriously — where tightening operationalized criteria to raise reliability seems to make a category easier to game and quicker to drift, where publishing criteria in plain language appears to erode the very validity publication was meant to serve, where tracking a mechanism conflicts with being usable at the point of care next Tuesday — and which single requirement, if my committee insisted on it, would eliminate the largest share of the options actually on the table. If any desideratum turns out to be incoherent or self-defeating under looping, say so and strike it.

Close with a deliberately compressed scorecard, at most two sentences each, for four candidate architectures: operationalized categorical criteria with thresholds and exclusions, in the style of the current DSM and ICD; a dimensional, mechanism-first framework built on neurobehavioral systems rather than on diagnoses, in the style of the NIMH Research Domain Criteria; a hierarchical dimensional taxonomy derived from how symptoms covary; and a formulation- or problem-list-based alternative that abandons discrete kinds in favor of describing individual cases. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question, and keep the scorecard shorter than the criteria. Please write in American English.
