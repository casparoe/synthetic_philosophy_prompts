Write a poem — three stanzas of nine, six, and three lines, followed by a closing couplet — about a chess-playing computer's choice of one particular move, a knight fork.

The first stanza should reach the move in the vocabulary of the physical stance: voltages, transistor states, clock cycles, nothing that mentions the game. The second should reach it in the vocabulary of the design stance: the machine was built to win, and this is what optimal play requires. The third should reach it in the vocabulary of the intentional stance: it sees the fork, it wants the piece.

Every stanza must end on exactly the same line — the move itself, given in algebraic notation, worded identically each time — and the stanzas should get visibly cheaper as they go, shorter lines as well as fewer, so that the shrinking of the verse enacts the compression that is supposed to make the pattern real: on Dennett's account a pattern is real if there is a description of the data more efficient than the bit map, and the intentional stance earns its keep by being the cheapest route to a prediction the physical stance also reaches. Let each stanza also betray, in half a line, what its own stance characteristically fails at.

The couplet should turn on the worry that compressibility is not the objective test it looks like, since whether a description counts as more efficient depends on the description language and the baseline you measure against — so the refrain may have been discovered or may have been imposed. Leave that open rather than settling it.

It should be accurate enough to hand out in a seminar on the three stances, and it should still work as a poem for someone who has never heard of them.
