Last night I argued for three hours with a friend about Parfit's hitchhiker, and I came away unable to reconstruct what she actually thinks. I would like you to do that reconstruction and then apply it to a case we never got to.

To fix the case: a driver is stranded in the desert; a hitchhiker will drive her to town only if he is confident she will hand over $100 on arrival, a sum worth far less to her than her life; he is an excellent judge of character and of intentions. Once in town the rescue is over and the money has not yet changed hands.

Her verdicts, which I think I recorded correctly:

(1) Ordinary case, where the driver forms the intention to pay while still in the desert: she said the driver in town should pay, and that the driver who refuses is not merely ungrateful but irrational.

(2) I pressed her on whether the driver in town, at that moment, has any reason to hand over the money, given that the rescue is already done. She said no, and that this is the whole point: rationality can require you to do what, at the moment of acting, you have no reason to do. Her view, as I understood it, was that rationality's real work happens at the earlier node, by making yourself in advance into a payer.

(3) Pre-commitment variant: the driver knows the route and the hitchhiker's habits, and before leaving can install a device that transfers the money automatically on arrival. She said installing it is rational but only a crutch: what rationality requires is being a payer, and the device guarantees the behaviour without the disposition.

(4) No-dependence control: a hitchhiker who rescues anyone he finds, or who decides by coin. She said paying here is generous and perhaps decent, but not required, and not what the word rational marks out.

(5) Bad-reason variant: a driver who pays because he falsely believes that handing over the money now will cause the hitchhiker to have rescued him. She said this is the right act for the wrong reason, and that she would not call him rational.

(6) Self-modification variant: before ever setting out, a driver can cheaply and irreversibly take a pill making her reliably disposed to pay. She said taking it is rationally required if available, and that there is no contradiction in rationality's demanding that you become someone who will later act against your own interests.

The new case. A driver is found unconscious in the desert. The hitchhiker reconstructs her decision procedure from a brain scan, runs a perfect simulation of it on the desert situation, sees that it outputs that she would pay, and drives her to town. She wakes in town, is told all of this, has the money, and has never promised anything, adopted any policy, or cultivated any disposition: there was no earlier node at which she could have made herself a payer. The encounter is genuinely one-shot: no reputation, no recurrence, no one she will ever meet like him again.

What I want from you: the two or three candidate rules that fit verdicts (1) through (6) equally well — I can think of one built on dispositions adopted in advance and one built on counterfactual dependence between what her decision procedure outputs now and what the simulation produced, the sort of view sometimes called functional decision theory, but tell me if there are others. I also tried an evidential version, on which you act so that your act is good news about whether you were rescued, but it seems to misfire once the rescue is already known, and I cannot tell whether that refutes it or merely relocates all the work to the earlier node. Then: what each rule predicts about the unconscious driver; which single verdict you would bet my friend gives, with your reasons for preferring it; and the further variant you would put to her to force the rules apart. I had been planning to ask whether her answer changes if this is the first of ten such journeys rather than a one-shot — tell me whether that question really discriminates between the rules or whether they collapse on it.

Please answer in under 500 words, in this order: the candidate rules, their predictions about the new case, your best guess with reasons, and the discriminating variant.
