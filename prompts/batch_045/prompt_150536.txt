I'm writing a novel — third one, no, second — about a city that picks its governing council by lot, and I've discovered I have very strong gut feelings about which institutions should use lotteries and which shouldn't, and they don't add up. I need them to add up, or at least I need to know which one I'm supposed to give up, because my protagonist has to argue for something, and right now she's just asserting things.

Here's where I land, case by case.

- Trial jury, twelve people pulled off the electoral roll, one case, then dispersed: obviously fine. Not even a question.
- Jury duty as an obligation — you get summoned, you can't say no: fine, and I think partly *because* you can't say no.
- Citizen assembly: a hundred people drawn at random, six weekends, binding decision on one issue (abortion law, electoral reform, whatever): I love this in the abstract and I get a sinking feeling in the concrete. I want to say fine and I don't quite.
- Drawing the head of government by lot from the whole citizenry: insane. No.
- One chamber of a legislature chosen by lot, one-year terms, staggered: nervous-making but not insane. Closer to fine than the last one.
- Term limits on a mayor, two terms and out: fine, good even.
- Career legislators, thirty years in office, a decade chairing the same comittee: not fine. This is the thing the book is against.
- Career civil servants — unelected, appointed at twenty-five, still there at sixty, actually writing the rules: fine. Good, even. I can't see the problem.
- Judges appointed for life, no election, no term limit: fine, and I'd fight you on it.
- Elected judges on twelve-year terms: not fine. Worst of both, somehow, and I can't tell you why.
- Heredity, throne passes to the eldest child: obviously not fine, but my objection here feels different from my objection to the lottery president and I can't say how.
- Sortition with quotas: the draw is stratified so the chamber mirrors the population on income, education, region, ethnicity. My instinct is that this is cheating, and also that it's obviously necessary, and those can't both be right.
- Random tax audits, random screening at airports, random assignment of cases to judges: fine, all three.
- Conscription by lottery in a war I think is just: fine. Conscription by lottery in a war I think is unjust: not fine — but that's not about the lottery.
- Literary prize: choosing the *jury* by lot from the pool of published novelists — genuinely great. Choosing the *winner* by lot from the entries — vile, an insult to the art.
- Tied election broken by drawing a name out of a hat: fine?? I don't love it, but I don't hate it the way I hate the prize-by-lot.

So here's my tentative principle, and I can see the hole in it myself: lotteries are for selecting people, not for selecting outcomes. Jury: people. Prize jury: people. Prize winner: outcome. President: outcome, sort of. That handles a lot of it. But a tied election settled by lot is an outcome selected by lot and I don't hate it; a chamber chosen by lot is people selected by lot who then produce outcomes by voting, and I'm only half-sure about it; and the citizen assembly is the worst case for me because it's doing both at once. So maybe the real principle is: lot is fine when what you want is a sample and not fine when what you want is a judgment, plus a clause letting lot in as a fallback once judgment is genuinely exhausted. But then the prize shortlist is arguably exactly the case where judgment is exhausted, and I still find the prize-by-lot vile.

One more thing nagging at me. I have a hazy memory — don't trust it, it's the sort of thing I half-remember from a documentary — that Venice used lotteries *inside* an election, cutting a big pool down to a small one before the real voting started. If that's right, it suggests a third use of lot I don't have a handle on: not as a sample and not as a decision, but as a way of stopping factions from organizing around a candidate. And if lot-as-antifaction is a real thing, then my verdict on stratified quotas might just be wrong, because quotas put back exactly the group identities the lot was supposed to dissolve. Or maybe not.

What I want:

1. The best principle you can state precisely that captures as many of my verdicts as it can, with the case that motivates each clause. If it's really two principles — one about who gets picked and one about what sort of decision is being made, or one about distributing burdens and one about distributing power — say so, and say where the two conflict.
2. Where you'd overturn me. I'd much rather you told me flatly that the prize-jury/prize-winner pair, or the judges/civil-servants/career-legislators trio, can't all survive, and which one goes, than that you built me some baroque machine that saves everything.
3. Verdicts from the principle — not from your own politics — on cases I haven't listed: a standing second chamber drawn by lot, staggered three-year terms, able to block and delay legislation but never initiate it; a chamber drawn by lot whose proposals go to a referendum and nowhere else; lot used inside an election to cut forty candidates to four before voters vote; sortition for a school board, six-year terms, binding curriculum decisions; a publisher breaking a genuine editorial deadlock by drawing lots among three shortlisted manuscripts; and a lottery restricted to people who scored in the top decile on some exam rather than to everyone.
4. Whether my city is even coherent. If a place runs its council by lot, what does it have to do about the permanent officials underneath — the ones who stay while the councillors rotate out? Does the principle say anything about that, or is it silent there? I have a feeling the interesting part of the book isn't the lottery at all but the bureaucracy the lottery sits on top of, and I want to know whether that's a real insight or just a novelists' instinct for a good setting.

Keep it concrete, please, and skip the Athens preamble. I've read it.
