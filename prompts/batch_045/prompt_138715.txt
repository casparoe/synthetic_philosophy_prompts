Posting in the right place I hope.

Something came up at a meeting last week and I can't stop turning it over. I moved here eleven years ago for a job, got my citizenship in the spring, and I'm now on a small volunteer committee in my town that decides what to do with a leftover budget of about forty thousand pounds. Half the room wants it to go to the local hospice. Me and one other person want to send it to a charity that hands out malaria nets, which by the usual cost-effectiveness numbers helps vastly more people per pound.

An older guy on the committee, who has lived here his whole life, more or less ended the discussion by saying that a country is just a family with more people in it, that you feed your own kids before you feed strangers, and that if you'd hand the money to people on the other side of the world before your own neighbours then you haven't understood what a country is. Everyone went quiet and we adjourned. It did something to me in the room, but I also think it might be cheating.

Two things complicate it for me personally. First, I didn't choose my neighbours here and I didn't choose my family back home; the difference is just that I filled in a form about one of them. Second, the border near where I grew up was drawn by people who never went there. My grandmother's village and the next village over used to be in the same district, and now they're in different countries. If a country really is a family, did those two villages stop owing each other on the day the line moved?

Can you audit the analogy properly? What maps onto what — who's the parent, who's the child, what's the household budget, what plays the role of the forty thousand — and where does the mapping fail? Which features of a family is it quietly importing that a country doesn't have, and which of those imports are the ones actually doing the work in his argument?

I'd also like it split up by what a special claim is supposed to mean, because I think thats where the room got confused. Does it just mean my town's claim wins when everything else is equal? Does it mean it wins even when the need on the other side is much bigger? Does it mean my neighbours are the ones owed an explanation and strangers aren't? Does it mean the group can make me pay up? I have a feeling the family picture is quite good for some of those and terrible for others — siblings can't exactly tax each other, and if the state is the parent then who voted for the parents — but I can't get it straight.

Then the bit I actually care about: does any of it survive? Even if compatriots do have some special claim, does it get him to spend it locally, and does it get him anywhere near the stronger thing he seemed to want — that sending it abroad would be wrong rather than merely optional, or that a state shouldn't be allowed to spend tax money on outsiders at all? Or was the analogy carrying the whole conclusion?

And if the family one is bad, what's better? I keep coming back to something like co-signers on a loan: people who didn't choose each other, are on the hook for each other, and have to decide things jointly whether they like it or not. Does that do more work, and where does it break?
