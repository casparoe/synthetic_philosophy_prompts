I'm taking a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of time, and I came away from this week's discussion of the sea-battle argument in Aristotle with a complaint I can't quite turn into an argument. As I understand the dialectic: the worry is that if some present claim about a contingent future event — that there will be a naval battle tomorrow — is already either true or false, then the outcome looks necessary in a way that seems to swallow the very contingency we wanted to preserve. The reply I was taught distinguishes necessity of the consequence from necessity of the consequent: the necessity is supposed to attach to the conditional linking yesterday's truth to tomorrow's outcome, not to the outcome itself. My complaint is that this answers a different question from the one that generated the worry. The worry wasn't about whether the outcome follows from the earlier truth; it was about what there is in the world *now* in virtue of which that earlier claim was true rather than false. Distinguishing the two kinds of necessity looks to me like a bookkeeping move that relocates the mystery rather than dissolving it.

Here is the three-way distinction I'd like to work with, and I'd like you to use it even if you would draw the lines differently. Say a future event is **inevitable** relative to now when nothing anyone could now do would prevent it. Say it is **predictively settled** when there is evidence or a reliable procedure available now that indicates which way it will go. Say it is **determinate** when there is already a fact of the matter about whether it will happen — when one of the candidate futures is as of now the actual one, whether or not we can tell which and whether or not anyone could change it. These come apart in cases I can construct: an outcome can be inevitable without being predictively settled, as with a deterministic process whose result no evidence available to us discriminates; and it can be predictively settled without being inevitable, as with a reliably forecast event that the forecasters could still prevent — or that they prevent precisely because it was forecast.

My suspicion is that the sea-battle argument and the standard replies to it trade on treating these three as a single package. The example is introduced as a paradigm of the contingent, but the intuitive pull of *surely it isn't true yet* seems to come from the event's not being predictively settled; the threat to contingency that the argument poses seems to concern determinateness; and the practical sting — the thought that deliberation would be pointless — seems to concern inevitability. Please explain, as precisely as you can, why the argument is seductive given that conflation, and what becomes of each of the main options once the three notions are pulled apart. In particular: if one denies determinateness for future contingents, does one thereby owe an account of what makes a present-tensed claim about the future true or false, and does that account quietly collapse into one of the other two notions? And if one instead asserts determinateness, in what sense is the contingency the example was meant to illustrate actually preserved? I don't need names, dates, or citations — I have the reading list and am not asking you to reconstruct it; I want the shape of the argumentative space and where the burden of proof falls. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. Around 700 words.
