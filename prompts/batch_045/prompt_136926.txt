I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy working on a dissertation about disagreement, and I need to turn a half-formed idea into a two-page proposal for a nine-month dissertation fellowship. The topic is the common prior assumption in game theory and formal epistemology — the Harsanyi-style doctrine that differences in belief between agents should be modeled as differences in information about a shared world rather than as differences in prior probability, and the results that ride on it: agreement theorems, no-trade results, and the modeling conventions of information economics generally. My half-formed idea is that the debate has been arguing about the wrong component. The common prior assumption is always stated against a fixed state space that is already shared, and that shared space already does an enormous amount of work — it settles which distinctions are relevant, which propositions are the objects of belief, and what it would mean to receive a given piece of evidence. My suspicion is that the real content of the assumption, and the real objection to it, both live in the requirement that the space be shared rather than in the requirement that the prior be shared, and that the standard framing hides this because it takes the space as given. A related worry I can't shake: a weakened version of the assumption, on which each agent's probabilities only have to be recoverable as marginals or conditionals of one prior over some common refinement, looks close to trivially satisfiable, so if the assumption is to have any content at all, something has to be held fixed — and part of the project is to say precisely what. I also want a taxonomy out of this: disagreement that is purely informational (different partitions over the same space), disagreement at the level of the prior over a shared space, disagreement about which distinctions belong in the space at all, and disagreement about what a given piece of evidence would indicate even when the space is shared. My guess is that the cases everyone reaches for when they say people have different priors — political and ideological disagreement, cross-paradigm scientific disputes — are really cases of the third or fourth kind, which would mean the machinery has been addressing a narrower phenomenon than its participants advertise.

Here is what I need the proposal to do, beyond stating the question precisely. First, explain why the existing positions leave it open: defenders typically treat the assumption as a methodological or as-if device that costs nothing because any disagreement can be traced to some information structure, while critics treat it as a substantive and ideological claim that rules out fundamental disagreement by fiat — and, on my reading, both camps are arguing about the prior while silently relying on the space. Second, argue for my approach over the obvious alternatives: I do not want to run an empirical study of whether people actually share priors, I do not want to build a general logic of unawareness from scratch, and I do not want to survey the literature. The approach should be conceptual plus a modest amount of formal spadework: an audit of what exactly has to be pinned down for the assumption to have testable content; an account of what sharing a space requires and when a common refinement exists; and two worked case studies where disagreement looks prior-like but may be space-like or evidence-likelihood-like. Third, tell me honestly what the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them — in particular, if it turns out that any pattern of observed betting or trading behavior can be rationalized by some common-prior model on a rich enough space, then either the space-first reframing rescues the assumption's content by locating it in the choice of space, or the whole apparatus collapses into a modeling convention, and I want the proposal to present both outcomes as genuine results rather than as a threat to be hidden. Give me a month-by-month plan for the nine months, a statement of what would count as the project failing (not just being hard), and a realistic scope judgment: tell me if this is really two projects and which half to cut, since my advisor thinks the case-study material is the part I'd have to drop.
