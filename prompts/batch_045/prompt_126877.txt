I teach an undergraduate module on the ethics of military service and want to close it with a screening of Paul Verhoeven's *Starship Troopers* (1997) rather than another paper. Please read the film through one specific argument rather than as satire of militarism in general.

The argument: an army raised by universal obligation rather than by wage is supposed to act as a brake on foreign policy, because those who authorise a war are the same people — or the parents of the same people — who would have to fight it. This is part of the usual explanation for why militia-based territorial defence of the kind historically associated with Switzerland, Sweden and Finland sits so awkwardly with expeditionary war. The United Citizen Federation in Verhoeven's film appears to instantiate that republican ideal and then behaves exactly as its critics say a professional army will: enlistment is voluntary and front-loaded, service is a credential that buys the franchise (and other rights withheld from civilians) rather than a continuing obligation shared by the whole citizen body, the war is fought on other planets against a population that will never be a neighbour of anyone voting on it, and mobilisation is sold to teenagers as self-realisation.

So the question I want put to the students: does the film refute the claim that universal service disciplines war-making, or only a version of it — voluntary, careerist, expeditionary — that the claim was never about? Give the argument at its strongest, as its ablest defender would state it; then the scenes you would screen and what each dramatises about it; then both directions of the film: where its irony cuts against the citizen-soldier ideal, and where it quietly depends on or even delivers that ideal's attractions (the integrated, comradely, meritocratic unit is the most appealing thing in it). Be honest about where the reading strains: the film is a satire with an axe to grind, so say plainly what a student would be misled by. If you think my historical framing is wrong, correct it rather than working around it.

Deliverable: a critical reading of about 600 words, addressed to a seminar of twenty-year-olds, followed by a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "central_claim": one sentence, the most you would want a student to take away;
- "scenes": an array of objects, each with "scene", "what_it_dramatises", "why_screen_it";
- "argument_at_its_strongest": the case for universal service as a restraint on war, in the voice of someone who believes it;
- "film_supports": what the film gives that argument;
- "film_undermines": what it takes away, and from which version of the argument;
- "satire_as_evidence": where the film's hostility to its subject distorts the philosophical question and would mislead a class;
- "verdict": does the film refute the argument, refute only a version of it, or change the subject, and why;
- "historical_caveats": anything in my framing of Switzerland, Sweden and Finland you would not say in front of students.
