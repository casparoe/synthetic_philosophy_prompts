I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the Judy Benjamin problem, and I want someone to tell me how exposed my side actually is. The case, as you'll know it: an officer is dropped by parachute into a territory divided into four quadrants — two held by friendly forces and two by hostile forces, and within each side, two companies' areas, Easy and Headquarters. Her prior is uniform over the four cells. Then a radio message comes through that bears only on theEasy half: roughly, if you are in Easy territory, the chances are three to one that you are in friendly territory. I hold what I'll call the conservatism rule: because she has learned no proposition with certainty, conditionalization has nothing to bite on, and the rational posterior is the one that satisfies the new constraint while changing nothing the message didn't bear on — so the probability of Easy stays at one half, the conditional odds within Headquarters stay at one to one, and the answer comes out 3/8, 3/8, 1/8, 1/8 (friendly-and-Headquarters and hostile-and-Headquarters untouched; the Easy mass split three to one). I don't hold this because it feels conservative; I hold it because I think "the probability of B given A is x" simply is not information about what happens when A is false, and I take the alternative distance-minimizing approaches to be answering a different, merely mathematical question.

The part of the argument I couldn't finish is a variant we invented on the spot, and I've since become attached to it. Suppose the same transmission carries two constraints at once: the probability of friendly given Easy is 3/4, and the probability of Easy given friendly is 3/4. Now conservatism is over-determined. Keeping the probability of Easy at one half (which automatically keeps the probability of friendly at one half as well) forces the hostile-and-Headquarters cell up to 3/8, so the odds within Headquarters move from one to one to one to three. Keeping the odds within Headquarters fixed instead pushes the probability of Easy up to two thirds, giving 1/2, 1/6, 1/6, 1/6. So something has to move that nothing was said about, and there is no fact of the matter about which message arrived first, because there was only one transmission. Minimizing relative information from a uniform prior isn't better: I make it roughly 0.43, 0.14, 0.14, 0.28, which moves both quantities, by amounts that depend on the distance measure, and to numbers with no intuitive content at all. My friend's further charges were that my rule, applied to constraints arriving one at a time, is order-dependent in a way no rational update should be; that it can't say what was learned, and so can't say why updating at all is mandatory rather than optional; and that any rule that refuses to conditionalize is exposed to a diachronic Dutch book.

What I want is a map of the exposure, not a verdict that my rule wins. Please structure the reply as follows: "The view, sharpened" (about 150 words), restating what I am committed to in its strongest and least evasive form, including what I must say about the single-constraint case; "What it predicts, and where it diverges from its rivals" (about 250 words), especially the minimum-information and pure-conditionalization treatments; "What would be decisive against it" (about 400 words); "What would only be awkward" (about 300 words); "What its defenders concede versus what they dismiss" (about 250 words) — I want the concessions from inside the view, not only the objections people make from outside it; "Has it been qualified into safety?" (about 200 words), since I suspect the standard escape, that the right update depends on the causal and informational story of how the message reached you, may be a way of making the view unfalsifiable, and I want to know whether that is legitimate here or a punt; and "Bottom line" (about 150 words). Please run the two-constraint variant explicitly through each rival rule, show the arithmetic wherever it decides something, and tell me if you think the variant is ill-posed rather than just hard. For what it's worth, I would personally be moved most by an accuracy-dominance or Dutch-book style argument, considerably less by further invented cases, and I would treat a convergence result — anything showing that the reasonable rules agree as constraints accumulate — as genuinely mitigating.
