I want to work out what I actually believe about whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible — situations in which an agent cannot do everything she morally must, and in which there is no morally adequate way out — and I would rather do it from my own verdicts on cases than by adjudicating between the familiar theoretical options. Below are my verdicts, including the ones I am unsure about, followed by the principle I have been using to make sense of them. What I want from you is the principle, repaired or replaced.

The cases

(a) Two weighty promises that cannot both be kept. Months ago I promised my closest friend I would sit with her through a medical appointment she has been dreading. Later I promised my sister I would be at my niece's school play, which falls at the same hour and matters enormously to my niece. Verdict: a genuine dilemma. Whichever I do I have wronged someone to whom I owed something, and I owe an apology that no explanation discharges.

(b) The same structure with one promise trivial: I promised a colleague a lift to the airport and promised my niece I would help her move. Verdict: not a dilemma. I break the airport promise, make it up, and do not feel I have done anything I could not do.

(c) Two strangers drowning, equidistant, in equal need, and I can reach only one. Verdict: not a dilemma. I pick arbitrarily; asked why that one, I have no answer; but I feel no guilt and owe no one an apology.

(d) My own two children drowning, equidistant, alike in every respect I can discern. Verdict: a genuine dilemma, and worse than (a), even though nothing distinguishes them and no reason can be given for the choice.

(e) Uncertainty: two hikers lost on opposite ridges, I can search one, and I have no evidence about which. Verdict: not a dilemma. One search is in fact the right one; my problem is ignorance, not conflict. If I search the wrong ridge I feel regret, not guilt.

(f) A conflict I created: yesterday, carelessly and against my own better judgment, I agreed both to chair a hiring committee and to be at my brother's wedding, and the two cannot both happen. Verdict: still a genuine dilemma. That I made the mess does not seem to release me from either commitment, though I know many people deny this.

(g) A conflict that help could have dissolved: I promised to drive my mother home from hospital and to collect my sister; my brother could have collected my sister, but he is unreliable and asking him would itself have been an imposition. Verdict: I oscillate. Sometimes I think it was never a dilemma because I should simply have asked; sometimes I think it was one and the asking was beside the point.

(h) The forced choice: a guard tells a mother that she must name which of her two children will be killed, and that if she refuses both will be. Verdict: my gut says this is the paradigm dilemma, yet on my own account the outcomes are not equal — one death is better than two — so my principle seems to classify it as tragic but resolvable.

(i) No permissible option: an official can avert one serious harm only by lying under oath, and can avert a distinct but equal harm only by disclosing a source she explicitly pledged to protect. Silence is not available: it guarantees both harms and breaches her duty of office. Both live options are forbidden. Verdict: this feels like the strongest candidate of all, and I am not sure the notion of weighing even applies here.

(j) Whistleblowing: reporting my mentor's fraud betrays a relationship and ruins him; staying silent lets patients be harmed. Verdict: not a dilemma — I should report. Yet I carry more residue here than in (b) or (e), and I do not take the residue to show that I was in a dilemma.

(k) A large life choice: take the job abroad or stay to care for my ageing father. Both permissible, both costly, neither forbidden. Verdict: obviously not a dilemma, though the hardest decision I have made.

(l) The case I go back and forth on: a friend tells me something in confidence whose disclosure would stop another friend from making a serious mistake. Keeping the confidence is a minor wrong to the second friend; breaking it is a betrayal of the first. My verdict flips with how I describe it, and I want to know which description is doing the work.

(m) A wrinkle about perspective: advising my sister in case (a)'s position, I would tell her to choose either and stop agonising, and I would mean it. First-personally I cannot see it that way at all. I do not know whether that asymmetry is a feature of dilemmas or a defect in my introspection.

My tentative principle

A situation is a genuine moral dilemma for an agent just in case: (1) the conflict is not epistemic — the agent's uncertainty is not what makes it hard; (2) the conflict is not resolvable by weighing, because the competing considerations are either exactly balanced or incomparable; and (3) each competing consideration is a directed claim — something owed to someone, such that the person let down is owed an apology. Clauses (1) and (3) are meant to separate (a) and (d) from (c) and (e); clause (2) plus the permissibility condition is meant to separate (a) from (b) and (k).

Where I want your help

Test that principle against these cases and repair it where it fails. I can already see three points of pressure. (h) seems to satisfy (1) and (3) but fails (2), and I am unwilling to give up the verdict. (i) is not clearly covered by any clause: nothing is permissible, so there is nothing to weigh, and it is not obvious that any claim is directed at the official. (f) shows the principle is silent about the history of the conflict, which is just where I would have expected it to speak, and (g) suggests that the availability of an escape route matters in a way the principle does not capture.

Concretely, I would like: a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself, with each clause tagged to the case that motivates it; an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which you would overturn; and its verdicts on cases I have not listed — say, a hospital committee allocating one ventilator between two patients with equal claims, a conflict where the person owed the duty has recently wronged the agent badly enough that the claim may be forfeited, and a conflict between a duty to a living person and a duty concerning people who do not yet exist. I would also like to know whether residue and "ought implies can" do any independent work in your account or are epiphenomena, whether the notion of a dilemma should be first-personal in a way that vindicates the asymmetry in (m), and what your account says about (l). Finally, say whether the distinction that matters is really between dilemmas, tragic-but-resolvable conflicts, and merely hard choices, or whether some other cut does the work. Please argue from the cases rather than from what any theorist has argued, and tell me plainly if you think a verdict of mine is simply contaminated by the theory I have been reading.
