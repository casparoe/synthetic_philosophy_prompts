I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training and I got nerd-sniped by this 'Death in Damascus' thing a friend sent me. I keep giving different gut answers to versions that look structurally the same, and I want to know whether there's one principle behind my verdicts or whether some of them have to go. Please skip reading lists and citations, I only want the principle.

Setup in every case: I'm in Damascus, Death tells me which city it will be in tomorrow, I can stay in Damascus or go to Aleppo, and I die iff we end up in the same city.

1. Death is a perfect predictor and says 'Aleppo'. Verdict: nothing I do makes a difference, so I'll stay in Damascus because the hotel is cheaper — but staying is not smarter than going.
2. Death is 90% accurate and says 'Aleppo'. Verdict: stay in Damascus, obvously. 90% beats 50%.
3. Death isn't predicting anything, it just read my booking confirmation, so it's 100% accurate because my plan is already fixed. Verdict: change the booking and stay in Damascus, and I feel no temptation at all to think it would have read the changed one. This feels like a totally different situation from 1, not a variant of it.
4. Death ran a full simulation of my brain yesterday and the printout says 'Aleppo'. Verdict: I feel like taking the other option is a real move worth making — which contradicts what I said in 1, and I can't say why.
5. I can pay a stranger at the bus station to pick my destination for me; Death's simulation only covered me, not him. Verdict: I pay him and feel good about it, but it feels like cheating in a way that 4 doesn't.

The case I go back and forth on: Death is perfect, but I can pay to have the prediction erased from my memory before I decide (I'd choose the way I normally would, just without knowing). Half of me says obviously pay, half says that's just 1 with extra steps.

Wrinkle I made up because none of the versions I found online had it: the accuracy isn't uniform — when I'm the sort of person who flees, the oracle gets it right 90% of the time, but when I'm the sort who stays it's right only 60%. I feel like that asymmetry definately has to matter, but I can't tell which way it pushes.

So: is there a principle that captures most of these, stated precisely enough that I could implement it as a function of (accuracy profile, what the predictor had access to, what I know)? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what does it say about a version where the prediction is published in the morning paper, everyone reads it and heads the other way, and Death's simulation included the fact that everyone reads the paper?
