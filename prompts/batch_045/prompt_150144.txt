Hi — I'm on the four-person methods committee at a mid-sized psychology journal (we're the people who get handed the awkward cases after normal peer review), and this has suddenly gotten personal: the multi-lab replication of a finding from my own department — the one our textbook chapter, our undergrad lecture and two grant proposals are built on — came back with an effect estimate of basically zero and a confidence interval that rules out anything close to the original effect size. Our editor-in-chief wants a written recommendation from us by the end of the month, and the committee is split between people who think we're obliged to say something public and people who think a single replication never settles anything and that we'd be trashing a decent paper on the basis of one study. I'm the one who has to draft it, and I definitly don't want to write something that a quantitatively literate reader will laugh at.

What I need is a procedure, not an essay. Realistically: we meet for 90 minutes a month, only one of us is comfortable with anything past t-tests and regression, and whatever we write has to be readable by the original authors (who get a right of reply) and defensible to the EIC, who is a substantive researcher and pushes back hard on anything that sounds like statistical jargon. So please give me an ordered list of questions we can run on any replication that lands on our desk, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where the procedure stops and says "no verdict, leave the literature as it is", where it just kills the case outright, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. A few things we keep going in circles on and that I'd want the procedure to actually settle: (1) my stats person insists that comparing the replication's estimate to the original's confidence interval is the wrong test and that we should compare it to a prediction interval that inflates for the original's own sampling error, but everyone else in the field seems to use the CI comparison — is that distinction worth carrying into a simplified procedure, and which way does it cut? (2) whether a non-significant replication that was powered only to detect, say, 80% of the original effect counts as evidence of absence or merely absence of evidence, and whether framing the same case in Bayesian terms (something like "how much should this move a reasonable prior?") would change the answer we give; (3) the hidden-moderator escape hatch — how much drift in population, materials or procedure has to pile up before we say the replication isn't testing the same hypothesis, and how we stop that becoming a rubber stamp where every failed replication gets blamed on a moderator nobody pre-registered; (4) how the fact that the original estimate was probably inflated by publication bias and analytic flexibility should enter — does it change what we conclude about the replication, or only about the original? And (5) our chair's proposed shortcut, which we've started calling the "two-and-a-half rule": treat a replication as decisive against the original if its sample is at least 2.5x the original's and its estimate comes in under a quarter of the original effect. I'd like to know whether that's a defensible compression of the real reasoning or a trap that will misfire on us.

Then please run the finished procedure on three cases so I can see it work. Case one: the multi-lab replication of my department's finding — pre-registered, about 2,000 participants across a dozen labs, original authors consulted on the materials, estimate essentially zero, interval excluding anything near the original. Case two: a replication done by the original authors themselves, 4x the sample, effect at about 60% of the original size, p around .02. Case three should be one where the honest answer is one our committee won't like: there's a flashy study from our own back catalogue (n = 40, from about fifteen years ago, still cited in the popular press) that most of us privately think is noise, and a rushed replication of it came back non-significant — but the replication was only powered to detect an effect 1.5x the original, so I suspect the procedure is going to tell us we can't say the thing we actually want to say. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: which of our real questions it's simply silent on (whether to attach an expression of concern, whether to change our editorial policy on publishing replications at all, whether a "failed" replication ever licenses saying the effect is zero rather than small or context-bound). If it helps, assume we're willing to be told that the right output is a two-page note that mostly says "we can't tell" — I just need to be able to defend why.
