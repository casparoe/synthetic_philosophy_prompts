hey — I'm the undergrad rep on our university's Animal Care & Research Ethics Committee (they let one of us on, mostly to take minutes), and we've got a case nobody knows what to do with, and of course it landed the same week as my midterm for PHIL 240 (minds & machines, we're on moral status right now). I have to circulate a draft written opinion before Tuesday's meeting and I genuinely don't know what to recommend, so: be the person who drafts it.

The case: the Vollmer lab submitted what they call an "amendment for determination of scope." They aren't asking us to approve anything; they want us to rule whether their project is our jurisdiction at all. They run a digital emulation of a nematode nervous system — 302 neurons, full connectome, simulated body, simulated chemical gradient. Open-source worm sims have been around for years; this is their own tuned version. They use it to study learning: the thing habituates, it does aversive conditioning, they can lesion individual neurons. What they want to do next is where it gets messy:

(1) run it at roughly 400x real time, in 500 parallel copies, for statistics;
(2) run a "valence-off" variant where they knock out the neuromodulatory system that does approach/avoidance in the real animal, on the grounds that without it there's nothing the emulation is doing that could matter to it;
(3) run a "table mode" variant: instead of integrating the dynamics at runtime they precompute the whole input–output function into a lookup table, so at runtime it's just a giant memory read. Their argument is: if what matters morally is the computation in the abstract, table mode is the same computation and has to be treated identically; if we want to treat it differently, we owe them an account of what we think we're protecting, and "causal oomph" isn't an answer until we say why the oomph matters.

Our policy covers "non-human animals," defined by reference to vertebrates and cephalopods — so strictly speaking none of this is covered. But there's a clause about novel preparations, and the chair wants a real answer rather than a definitional dodge, because the PIs have said (very politely) that if we rule it out on the taxonomic definition they'll just do it anyway, and we'll have taught everyone that our jurisdiction is a technicality.

Two more things from the last meeting that I think are actually the crux:

- Prof. Adeyemi raised the incentive problem: the better their emulation is — the more it learns, the more wormlike it behaves — the more likely we are to say it's covered. So our ruling hands them a reason to keep the model bad, or at least to not probe it very hard. Half the room says that's a publication-integrity problem and not ours. The other half says a rule that rewards making your model worse is a bad rule.
- The counting problem. If it is covered, what's the unit? One subject per emulation? Per copy? Per hour of simulated time (in which case 400x speed is 400x the animal-hours and their protocol is enormous)? Someone said per-copy, but also that running the identical emulation twice might be the same subject twice over rather than two subjects, and I can't tell if that makes it better or worse. Related: two members disagreed about whether we should go by how it behaves or by what's going on structurally inside it, and I don't think they noticed how much rides on which one we pick.

What I need: a short written opinion I can circulate. Recommend one of — (a) decline jurisdiction, with a short explanation that isn't just the definition; (b) assert jurisdiction and require a full protocol with named conditions; (c) assert jurisdiction over some variants and not others, saying which — and give a timeline (what has to happen by when, and what happens if it doesn't). It has to give the Vollmer lab something they can actually respond to rather than "it depends on your metaphysics." And it should take the strongest argument against your own recommendation seriously: I'd guess for (b) that's the "you're regulating a spreadsheet" line, and for (a) it's the incentive thing, but say whichever it actually is.

Last thing: I'm supposed to flag where I think the committee is posing the question badly. If "is it sentient" is the wrong frame — if it should be something like how much moral risk there is and who carries the burden of proof under uncertainty — say so and reframe it. But definitely don't just reframe it, I still need a bottom line by Tuesday.
