I've been reading up on the experimental philosophy of action and came across a summary on the episode page of a popular philosophy podcast. I'd like to know how much of it to trust before I repeat it to anyone. Here is the passage, quoted in full:

"Joshua Knobe ran a simple experiment. People read about a company chairman who was told that a new program would harm the environment, and who replied that he didn't care about the environment at all and only cared about profits. Asked whether the chairman harmed the environment intentionally, most people said yes. But when the same story was told with 'help' in place of 'harm,' most people said he did not help the environment intentionally — even though in both versions he plainly foresaw the side effect and was indifferent to it. The same asymmetry appears for 'deliberately' and 'on purpose,' and it appears no matter what order people read the two stories in. The moral valence of the outcome is therefore driving a judgment that ought to be purely factual: people's moral verdicts distort their view of the facts. But the distortion is not merely a bias, because Knobe's actual claim is stronger — our very concept of intentional action is partly constituted by moral considerations, so that when the outcome is bad, foresight plus indifference really is enough to make the bringing-about of it intentional, and when it is good it is not. That is why the result is said to refute the classical philosophical project of analysing intentional action in terms of belief, desire and causation: 'intentional' has no stable content to analyse, and the old dispute between simple and complex views of foreseen side effects is settled. The broader upshot is methodological: what people say in surveys about cases just is what the concept means, so experimental philosophy can replace armchair conceptual analysis rather than merely supplement it. And practically, anything that leans on the intended/merely-foreseen distinction — the doctrine of double effect, for instance, or the legal notion of intent — is resting on a distinction that is really just a moral judgment wearing a factual disguise, and should be abandoned or replaced by an explicitly normative test."

Where I stand: I find the basic result plausible and the last paragraph suspicious, but I can't say why. I'd like a claim-by-claim audit rather than a verdict on the whole.

Please separate, for each claim in the passage, (i) what an experimental philosopher working on this literature would accept as a fair statement of the finding, (ii) what is a common simplification or a slide between two things the literature keeps apart, and (iii) what a defender of the view would reject outright. In particular I want you to be explicit about the difference between the empirical finding, the competing explanations of it, and the philosophical conclusions people have tried to draw — and to flag places where the passage helps itself to more than one of these at once, or asserts two things that cannot both be true (it seems to me to say both that moral judgment distorts a factual judgment and that moral judgment partly constitutes the concept, but I may be misreading it).

Two things I made up, which I'd like you to use as probes rather than as data. I am not asking whether anyone has run them; tell me what the main competing explanations would each predict about them, and whether the result would discriminate between those explanations.

Probe one — the compliance engine. A firm's automated scheduling system is built to minimise cost, and its designers know, and the firm accepts, that running it will contaminate a local water supply; in a matched version it will instead improve the water supply. There is no question of the system caring, valuing, endorsing, or having a "deep self," and no question of its having reasons. Would the valence-sensitive, reason-based, deep-self/valuational, and pragmatic-or-expressivist ("intentionally" as a vehicle for blame) explanations of the asymmetry each predict that the asymmetry survives here, and with what, if anything, as the target of the attribution?

Probe two — the pointless rule. Same chairman vignette, but the foreseen side effect is neither beneficial nor harmful: in one version the program will cause staff to breach an arbitrary, pointless office convention, and in the matched version it will cause them to comply with it. Does the driver of the asymmetry have to be moral valence, or would a norm-violation account or a general affect/valence account predict the same pattern? What would it mean for the "moral" in "moral considerations are constitutive" if the pattern held here?

Finally: does the practical conclusion in the last sentence follow from the parts of the summary that survive correction? I care about two cases especially — the doctrine of double effect's intended-versus-foreseen distinction, and legal or institutional uses of "intent." Say what would follow for each if the finding is robust but the explanation is unsettled, and what would follow only on some explanations and not others.
