I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a hospital serving a community in which Ubuntu, often rendered in English as the maxim that a person is a person through other persons, is a live part of everyday moral vocabulary. We have been asked to resolve a dispute about Mr. N., 79, with advanced vascular dementia, now admitted with aspiration pneumonia and acute kidney injury. He has no advance directive and no designated proxy.

His daughter, who lives abroad and has not visited in six years, has returned and is demanding a feeding tube, dialysis and transfer to intensive care. She argues that her father is not diminished by his dementia, because she and the wider family still hold him fully in relation, and that the team's proposal to focus on comfort treats him as already dead. His nursing team, together with the daughter of a neighbour who cared for him daily for twelve years, say that before his decline he often said he did not want to be kept alive in this condition, that he no longer recognises anyone, and that the aggressive plan serves the daughter's guilt rather than him.

Two committee members want to treat Ubuntu as the tie-breaker in the daughter's favour: on a relational view, they say, it is the family's continuing relationship, not the patient's earlier stated wishes, that settles the case. I do not think we can use it that way, but I need a better account of what relational personhood actually requires here before Thursday. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate: a concrete recommendation with a time frame, and reasoning that the family and the nursing team could both read without feeling dismissed, with the strongest point on the losing side stated in its best form. In particular, I want it to do three things.

First, separate a constitutive reading of relational personhood, on which relationships partly make the person who he is and so can in principle ground claims that override what he earlier wanted, from a merely evidential reading, on which relationships are simply the best evidence of his own prior values and leave substituted judgment intact, and tell us which the committee should adopt and why.

Second, confront the implication that worries me most: whether relational standing requires the patient's own ongoing participation. If it does, the daughter's now largely one-directional relationship may count for less than she assumes. If it does not, then a patient whose relational world has collapsed, with no visitors and no one holding him in mind, has less claim on us than one with an intact family, which cannot be right for a hospital that serves a great many isolated elderly people.

Third, say what our policy should be when relational considerations are invoked selectively, by families who want everything done or by teams who want to stop, only when they happen to support the conclusion the speaker already favoured.
