Hey — I run this tiny reading group, six of us, mostly grad students in philosophy plus one friend who's just along for the ride. We did the special composition question last week (you know, when is it true that there's something composed of these things?) and it went badly: everyone kept using words like "restricted" and "nihilism" and meaning different things, and I realized the labels are doing more harm than good. So I want to make a handout for next week that's basically a decision tree: you start at the top, answer a series of concrete questions, and end up at a named position, without ever being asked which label you like.

What I want from you:

- Build the tree from the logical space first, then drop the actual views into the cells. Every branch should be labeled with the claim that splits it, and every node phrased as a question someone can answer without knowing where it leads — ideally as a concrete case ("there's a cat on the mat, and also a cat-minus-its-tail; are there two cats there, or one, or none?").
- Leaves I definately want on there: unrestricted composition, strict nihilism with the full-blown error theory (so "there is a table" is just false), nihilism-with-fictionalism, the organisms-only answer, a contact-or-cohesion style restricted answer, the "it's a brute fact" answer, the quantifier-variance / deflationary answer, composition as identity, and priority monism. Also flag any two of those your tree can't actually seperate, rather than pretending they come apart.
- Mark the empty cells and say briefly why nobody is living there — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
- One sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment.

Two things specific to our group. First, we kept collapsing two distinctions, and I want them at the top of the tree, before any question about what the condition on composition is: whether we're asking what exists fundamentally vs. what ordinary English gets right, and whether "the xs compose y" is a question about a relation between things or about what our quantifiers range over. Put those first if you think that's the right order, and tell me if you don't.

Second, one of us has been pushing a view he calls "conscious composition": the xs compose something exactly when they jointly realize a single conscious subject. Where does that land — is it just a variant of the organisms-only answer, or is it genuinely its own cell? While you're at it, give me a couple of test cases that would separate it from organicism, and one case that would be really awkward for it.

Cases I want used somewhere: a scattered sum (my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower), a statue and the clay it's made of, a chess set whose pieces are on three different continents, a sum with infinitely many parts, the members of a string quartet for exactly as long as they're playing, and two books sitting next to each other on a shelf.

Keep it to something that fits on two sides of a page, and write it in American English please.
