I'm in the final year of a joint philosophy and computer science programme, and this term I'm taking a seminar on levels of explanation: the cranes-and-skyhooks vocabulary we got from Dennett, the complaint about greedy reductionism, and the general question of when a lower-level story genuinely explains something and when it quietly explains it away. Our last study-group session is on Thursday and I've been asked to close it with a poem — something that can be read aloud in about a minute — so I want a fixed form rather than free verse, and I keep coming back to the sestina. The six rotating end-words could just be our own seminar vocabulary: crane, skyhook, design, greed, ground, and debt. That last one is our coinage, so let me define it: every time you discharge an explanatory obligation at one level, you incur a matching one at the level below — explain the fit of an organism to its niche by selection and you now owe an account of where the variation and the copying machinery came from; explain a decision by the firing of some neurons and you now owe an account of why those firings are about what they're about. On our way of talking, greedy reductionism is the move that pays the first debt and then behaves as though the second one had never been contracted.

The reason I think the form fits is that the rotation makes the terms change jobs. No end-word is allowed to stay where it was: a word that began as the thing to be explained shows up later as the thing doing the explaining, and then as the thing that turned out to be an illusion, and then again as the name for a pattern you can't do without. That is more or less the shape of the debate — design starts out as the target, gets demoted, and then comes back in through a side door because the lower-level story keeps needing to talk about the very pattern it dissolved. And the envoi has to compress all six words into three lines, which seems to me to be the actual moral: you don't get to drop any of them, you only get to fit them together at the end.

So: a sestina, six six-line stanzas plus the three-line envoi, unrhymed is fine, and morphological variants of the end-words (cranes, designing, grounded) are fine as long as the stem is audible. Two more constraints. First, don't work with abstractions alone — pick one running concrete example and let it carry the poem: the fit between a cheetah's speed and a gazelle's, the aboutness of a road sign or a hand-drawn map, or the value of a banknote would all work. I want the crane story visibly lifting in some stanzas and I want the greedy move to be visible when someone tries to reduce that example to its substrate and loses the very feature that made it worth explaining. Second, please be accurate rather than merely allusive, since there may be a philosopher in the room. The target shouldn't be reduction as such — most of the group is sympathetic to good reductionism — but the greedy kind that explains away the phenomenon; and the skyhook shouldn't be a cartoon deity but the thing it is in the literature, a top-down lift that smuggles in the design it was supposed to account for. No proper names in the poem itself, please. End with the tension intact rather than with a tidy moral, and if there's a turn anywhere, let it be the moment the reader notices that reaching for a skyhook and refusing to pay the debt are the same failure seen from opposite directions.
