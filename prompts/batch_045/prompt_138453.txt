Okay so I want you to analyze those recent-ish American lawsuits where an animal-rights group tried to get courts to treat some captive chimpanzees—and then later a zoo elephant—as legal persons with a right to bodily liberty, mostly by filing habeas corpus style petitions. I dont need the full procedural history; just take these as the given facts: the lawyers argued the animals are autonomous, self-aware beings who shouldn’t be locked up; the courts mostly rejected the personhood claim; but a couple of judges sounded uneasy, and at least one said something like even if the animal isn’t a person the writ could still be used to ask whether its confinement is lawful. Now analyze this through Kant’s views on animals.

As I get the standard Kantian picture: moral status in the serious sense tracks rationality or being an end in itself, so animals aren’t ends in themselves and we dont have direct duties to them; our real duties are indirect duties to humanity, e.g. we shouldn’t be cruel because it corrupts our own character and can make us worse to other humans. Assuming that’s close enough, do two things. First, do a disciplined mapping. Who are the players in the moral/legal situation—the animal, the captor or owner, the state, the lawyers, the public? What are the actual options—release to a sanctuary, keep in the zoo, transfer somewhere else, declare legal personhood, use a different legal tool like an anti-cruelty or unlawful-confinement law? And what is supposed to count as evidence—self-awareness, autonomy, suffering, effects on human character, something else? Say clearly where Kant’s theory has an answer and where it is silent.

Second, put some pressure on both sides. I want you to use a distinction I’ll make up for this: call it displaced wrongness versus proxy standing. Displaced wrongness is the Kantian thought that the genuine wrong in mistreating an animal is really a wrong to the agent’s own humanity or to the moral community, even though it shows up as harm to the animal. Proxy standing is the legal move where a human is allowed to challenge another human’s treatment of an animal without the court having to say the animal is a person. Sort the petitioners’ arguments, the courts’ reasons, and the sympathetic seperate opinions into those two buckets, and tell me which bucket is actually doing the heavy lifting in the case. Notice that part of the difficulty is procedural: a court has to recieve a claim brought on behalf of a being that cannot bring it itself, and it has to decide whether that requires pretending the animal is a person or merely appointing a human voice for it.

Then flip it around: what does the episode show about Kant’s view? Does it make the indirect-duty theory look cold, because it can’t say the animal is wronged, only that we degrade ourselves? Or does it show the theory’s strength, because it explains why we care about the animals without pretending a chimpanzee is a moral agent, and it points to legal protections that don’t require calling the animal a legal person? Is the whole personhood strategy conceptually confused from a Kantian angle, or can it be reconstructed as an indirect-duty argument wearing legal costume? And does the judges’ move of separating unlawful detention from moral or legal status make Kant’s own distinction between moral duty and legal or political obligation look more sensible than the activists probably want, or less? Be honest about where the framework just doesn’t apply, and don’t force Kant to speak where he has nothing to say.
