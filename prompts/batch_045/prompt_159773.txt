I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the extended mind thesis, and I've hit a case I can't place. The parity principle and the trust-and-glue conditions usually attached to it look designed for cooperative artifacts — notebooks, diaries, orderly filing systems — but most of the external resources that now perform my cognitive work are built by parties whose interests partly diverge from mine and are tuned to shape what I retrieve: a recommendation feed, an autocomplete, a ranked result list, a calendar that nudges me into meetings. Call this adversarial coupling. Does an adversarially coupled resource literally constitute part of a mind, or is it merely coupled? Two lines compete: (a) the automatic-endorsement condition fails when the content is selected for someone else's ends, so the coupling-versus-constitution distinction settles it and the thesis stays limited to resources transparently under the subject's control; (b) the condition does not fail, because automatic endorsement is a claim about how the subject treats what comes back rather than about who engineered the vehicle, so adversarial coupling reveals a mind with parts that are not on its side — the boundary of the mind coming apart from the boundary of the agent's interests. Which extension of the thesis is better, and why? Please be explicit about what the constitutive claim is supposed to earn: if it stops tracking what we should protect, assist, or hold agents responsible for, is it doing any work at all? And give the strongest objection to whichever line you favor. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then reason it out with no preamble and no restatement of my question, in American English.
