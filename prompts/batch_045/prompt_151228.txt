I write literary fiction, and I am midway through a novel in which two characters are locked in an argument I cannot adjudicate: a retired meteorologist who believes the world is a deterministic machine we happen to be too small to read, and her daughter, who works in quantum computing and believes the openness at the bottom of physics is what leaves the future genuinely unwritten. I have started keeping a list of concrete cases with my gut verdicts, because I want the argument in Chapter 11 to be about something real rather than about two people talking past each other, and I have roughed out a principle of my own to explain the pattern. What I call *horizon elasticity* is this: what matters is how much extra forecast horizon you buy per extra digit of precision in the initial conditions; where errors grow at a fixed exponential rate, the horizon lengthens only logarithmically as you sharpen the input, so no precision that a physically situated creature could obtain buys you a meaningful extension — and that, I want to say, is what it is to be unpredictable in the way that matters. Notice that the principle is deliberately about knowers with budgets, not about the Laplacean demon, because my meteorologist's claim is supposed to be compatible with the demon and I don't want to beg the question against her daughter.

Here are the verdicts I find myself giving. A double pendulum hanging in the foyer of the science museum in Chapter 3: unpredictable, and I am entirely comfortable saying so. Whether it rains on a particular afternoon in a particular city twenty days from now: unpredictable, same kind of thing as the pendulum. A tossed coin: unpredictable, but not the same thing at all — I bristle at calling it chaotic, even though by my own criterion it ought to come out exactly like the pendulum. The shuffle function on a music app: random-looking but not unpredictable in the way that matters, and I feel its randomness is somehow fake where the pendulum's is real, even though in both cases the unpredictability is just相对 to what I happen to know. A plume of smoke: chaotic, and yet I want to say predictable — I know the shape of the plume, the rate of spread, the statistics — even though I cannot say where any particular fleck goes; and yet for the coin I also know the statistics and still want to call it random, which I cannot explain. Whether Earth stays inside the habitable zone over the next few hundred million years: I have been told that long integrations of the planets diverge exponentially from one another, and my verdict is still that we basically know, which by my own principle should come out unpredictable and doesn't feel that way. Which of two detectors clicks when a single atom decays: unpredictable in the deepest sense, and more so than anything above — except that my principle is about deterministic systems with sensitive dependence, and this is not one, so the principle doesn't even apply to the case I care about most. A billiard ball rattling around a table with a circular obstacle bolted in the middle: technically chaotic, technically unpredictable, and my verdict is that I don't care, which is not the verdict I gave the pendulum despite the mathematics being the same shape. Whether my narrator's sister takes the job in Chapter 12: unpredictable, and I recoil from chaotic. Whether a given first novel becomes a bestseller: unpredictable, but I feel it fails for a different reason than the weather does — not that the needed precision is unobtainable, but that I don't have the equations in the first place.

What I would like from you is this: one principle, or a short list of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could paraphrase it in a character's mouth, with each clause tied to the case that forces it, and an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and therefore asks me to drop. Test horizon elasticity against the list rather than starting from scratch, and repair it or replace it as the cases demand — I suspect it needs to be relativised to a target quantity rather than to a system (individual trajectory versus aggregate or distribution), and to a horizon and a precision budget, but I can't tell whether that repair saves my coin verdict or quietly destroys it, and I especially can't tell whether one criterion can cover both the case where precision is unobtainable and the case where no initial condition settles the matter at all. Then tell me what the repaired principle says about cases I haven't listed: the timing of the next large earthquake on a well-instrumented fault; the average July temperature of a named city in 2100; a chaotic circuit used to encrypt a message, where the receiver knows the equations exactly but not the initial voltage to many digits; and whether the output of a deterministic AI system can be predicted before it is produced. Finally, and this is the part I actually need for the book: on the resulting account, is there any substantive difference left between the meteorologist's deterministic-but-unknowable future and the daughter's genuinely open one, or does the distinction I have been building the novel around turn out to be a matter of whether you happen to want the trajectory or the statistics?
