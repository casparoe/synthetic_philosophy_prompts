I'm writing a short paper for a grad seminar on whether philosophy makes progress, and I want to use Borges's "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" as an actual example rather than a cute epigraph. Recap in case you dont know it: Menard is a minor French man of letters who decides to write Don Quixote. Not to copy it, not to translate it, not even to become Cervantes (he considers and rejects that route) — but to arrive at it, the same words, through the experiences of a twentieth-century Frenchman. He finishes a couple of chapters and a fragment. The story is written as a fake literary note on the complete works of a man who barely published anything, and the narrator insists that Menard's Quixote is almost infinitely richer than Cervantes's, despite being word for word identical.

Here's the work I want it to do. The pessimistic side of the progress debate scores things by whether philosophers converge on answers to the big questions, and by that measure philosophy looks bad. But Menard's case looks like an obvious achievement that consists in nothing but the route: same sentences, different set of alternatives already seen and given up, different objections already answered, different cost of holding the view now. Ive started calling that positional as opposed to propositional progress — a view can be more of an achievement without being a different view. I keep hitting this in my own reading, where a position my teachers presented as refuted turns up twenty years later defended by people who are clearly not idiots (presentism, sort of, on cosmological grounds; sense-datum-ish accounts of perception under new names), and it feels like something has been gained even though what anybody believes hasn't moved.

So: is that a real kind of progress, or am I just relabeling "we understand the options better now"? Does the story support it, or quietly undercut it? The obvious objection is that if Menard's identical words mean something different, then he never asserted the same propositions at all, and my case of "same content, new contribution" never arises. Can the reading be repaired by moving to something like a context-independent core of the claim, or does that collapse the whole point? And a bigger worry: Menard's achievement leaves no trace in the text, so nobody can build on it. A discipline whose advances are invisible in its content can't accumulate — which sounds like it helps the pessimists more than me.

Two more things. Menard does this on purpose and knows exactly which text he's aiming at; the recurring positions in philosophy get rediscovered by accident, and the fact that so-and-so already said it in 1934 usually turns up in a referee report. Is that disanalogy fatal, or does it just change what the example shows? And last: what does the story add to the debate that the straight arguments dont — is it only a metaphor, or does it say something about credit, inheritance, and how a discipline keeps score that the papers leave out? If you think the example does zero work for my paper, say that too and tell me why.
