I'm one of five voting members of the Eastvale Housing Review Board, and I've been asked to draft the memo that will frame our vote next week. Six months ago the city adopted middle housing by right: three- and four-unit buildings permitted on any residential lot, no discretionary hearing and no design review. Since then roughly two-thirds of the applications have landed in the East Ward, the lowest-rent and most historically disinvested part of the city, where lots are cheap and where about 40 percent of the city's unsubsidized low-rent apartments sit in small, old, fully occupied buildings. Most of the East Ward applications are demolish-and-rebuild.

The petition in front of us comes from the East Ward Tenant Assembly. It is not a homeowners' group: its members are mostly low-income renters, and they want us to recommend a twenty-four-month anti-displacement overlay for the ward, under which a by-right project could proceed only if it were net-additive, meaning no demolition of occupied units unless they are replaced one-for-one at rents the sitting tenants could pay, with relocation assistance in the interim. Our planning staff estimate the overlay would cut new units in the East Ward by about a third over those two years, and that most of that would be deferred or foregone rather than built elsewhere in the city; they also think the reform is already moderating rent growth at city scale.

Two things make this harder than it looks. First, our enabling ordinance tells us to weigh the housing needs of present residents and of households who would move to the city, and nobody from the second group is in the room or able to testify. Second, the East Ward is the only ward whose residents have organized at all, and two high-cost, high-opportunity wards successfully exempted themselves from the by-right reform, so the costs of the reform are concentrating in the ward with the least capacity to resist while its benefits concentrate in the wards that opted out.

Please draft the memo, about two pages: recommend approve, deny, or approve with conditions, with a specific time frame and any conditions stated tightly enough that staff could administer them. It should say plainly what kind of claim each side is making, a claim to remain where one already lives versus a claim to be admitted to somewhere one is not yet, and why the board should weight those as it does, without leaning on the right to the city as a slogan. Do not simply rehearse the case for building more housing; the board already accepts it, and the live question is what follows when the costs of acting on it fall on the people least able to refuse them. Before you close, give the strongest version of the argument against whatever you recommend, in your own words.
