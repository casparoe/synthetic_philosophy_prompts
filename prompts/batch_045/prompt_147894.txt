I'm giving a job-market talk in three weeks at a philosophy department that is hiring in social epistemology, and I want to walk into the question period knowing what is coming.

The room: two formal epistemologists (both Bayesian, one of them works on disagreement and merging of opinion, the other on graphical models of testimony); three social epistemologists of science who work on how the structure of a community's communication network affects whether it converges on the truth and on the trade-off between a group's diversity and its average reliability; a visiting behavioral economist who runs laboratory experiments on sequential decision-making; and two political philosophers who will say nothing for fifty minutes and then ask one question each about blame. There are thirty minutes of Q&A after the talk and then a lunch with four of them.

The paper defends two claims, one negative and one positive.

First, negative: the familiar indictment of herding in sequential observational learning — that an agent who sees enough predecessors choose the same way does better to ignore her own private signal, so that everyone from some point on copies, and the group can lock onto the worse option with a probability that does not go away — is an artifact of a modeling choice. Agents in the standard setup have exactly two actions and no option to stay out, so the only thing an action can transmit is its direction: one bit.

Second, positive: give each agent a third option, wait, and give her a privately known idiosyncratic cost of acting. Now the decision to act at all is a threshold-crossing event, and so is the decision to act *now* rather than later. The public record therefore carries more than direction: the number of people who acted, and how early they acted, leaks something about the strength of the private information behind those actions. I call this the turnout channel. Once it is in the model, what looks like a cascade is leaky: later agents never fully stop attending to their own signals, and individual deference is not merely excusable but informationally load-bearing. The normative upshot I want to defend is that the proper object of criticism is the design of the situation — whether abstention is cheap, whether abstentions are visible, whether early movers are rewarded for speed — and not the agents who defer.

What I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Please give me:

Eight or so objections, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by how likely they are, and phrased as the sentences these people would actually say — I want to hear the wording, not a paraphrase of the position. Attribute each to the person or subgroup most likely to raise it. For each: a reply I could give in about two minutes; a note on what I should concede in the course of giving it; and a note on where the reply is soft or where I would be bluffing.

Then, separately: what I should concede in the first ninety seconds of the talk, before anyone can accuse me of hiding it; the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the visit; and the question I am privately hoping nobody asks. Assume I know the literature, so do not spend any of your answer summarizing it or pointing me to readings — spend it on the dialectical moves.
