For my epistemology reading group I have to hand in a one-page "where I stand" on accuracy-first epistemology before the roundtable next week, and I'd much rather find out now that my package doesn't hang together than find out in front of eight people. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Epistemic rationality for credences is entirely a matter of accuracy, in the sense of closeness to the truth, and evidence alone fixes what it's rational to credence. Stakes, costs of error, and how much is riding on being right never move the rational credence itself. I'm firmly anti-pragmatic-encroachment.

(2) The thing that convinced me of probabilism is the accuracy-dominance argument: an incoherent credence function is accuracy-dominated, so there's a coherent alternative that beats it whichever way the world turns out. I also want to insist this is not a prudential or pragmatic argument — nothing about my interests enters, it's just that dominated options are irrational. My supervisor says this makes me a pragmatist; I don't think it does.

(3) I have a pet inaccuracy measure I keep using in examples and I don't want to give it up. Take a forecaster's whole season: N yes/no questions, each assigned a number in [0,1]. Bucket the questions by the number forecast (round to the nearest tenth). For bucket k, let p_k be the number forecast, n_k how many questions landed in it, and f_k the fraction of those questions whose answer was actually yes. Calibration = (1/N)·Σ_k n_k(p_k − f_k)². Refinement = (1/N)·Σ_k n_k·f_k(1 − f_k). Then Long-Run score = 10·Calibration + 1·Refinement, lower is better. I weight calibration heavily because I think "getting the frequencies right" is the core epistemic virtue and sharpness is optional extra credit. I know this isn't the usual squared-error measure, but I've never seen a good argument that the usual one is compulsary.

(4) I'm untroubled by there being no uniquely correct inaccuracy measure: as far as I can tell, all the reasonable candidates agree on the big verdicts (probabilism, updating by conditionalization), so you pick your favourite and get on with it, and the Long-Run score is mine.

(5) I'm a permissivist about credence: same evidence, different reasonable priors, both fully rational.

(6) In the bank-style cases I say her evidence and rational credence are exactly the same in the low- and high-stakes versions; I handle the pull of the high-stakes denial by being a contextualist about "knows" (the denial is true in that context), and I also think knowledge is the norm for acting on and asserting p, so I'm happy to say that in the high-stakes case she's not in a position to act on p even though nothing epistemic has changed.

I'll admit I've noticed that on my measure a forecaster who always says the base rate does suspiciously well — sometimes better than a sharp forecaster who's only slightly miscalibrated — and I've always filed that as a feature rather than a bug, since she really is perfectly calibrated and he isn't.
