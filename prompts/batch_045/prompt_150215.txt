So I got into a three hour argument last night with a friend who works on paraconsistent logic — she holds that some contradictions are genuinely true, not that we're confused, not that our language is broken, but that a statement and its negation can both be true at once. I think it's nonsense but I kept losing the thread, and I think part of why is that everything she said I immediately translated into my own job, and the translation came out wierd in ways she didn't seem to think were a problem. So I want to do that on purpose.

Me: I'm a data infrastructure engineer, about ten years, no formal logic past a discrete math course. My system ingests records about the same entities from a dozen upstream services and they constantly disagree — two services will both have a row saying a customer's address is X and another row saying it isn't X. I never repair that at write time, I keep both and handle it at query time: a query only returns an answer if the answer comes out the same no matter which way you'd repair the conflicting rows, and otherwise it returns nothing. I also live in SQL, so I know the three valued stuff in my sleep — a comparison against a missing value isn't true or false, it's the third thing, and negating the third thing just gives you the third thing back.

Four things I need translated into that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, without quietly swapping in assumptions I didn't have:

One, the positive case for the view: both the liar-sentence style case and the self-referential set case, which she said are the same shape of argument — some totality that seems to outrun itself — and where she says the right move is to accept the contradiction instead of patching the definitions.
Two, the thing everyone throws at her: that if you allow one contradiction, everything follows. What exactly is the step she's refusing, and what is it in my terms — is it the difference between a system that rolls back the whole transaction on a constraint violation and one that quarantines the bad rows?
Three, whatever it is that lets her still use completely normal reasoning in domains that turn out to be consistent, and how she's supposed to know which domains those are.
Four, the objection I half-formed at the pub and couldn't finish: it looked to me like she can say a claim is true, and can say it's true and false, but has no way to say it's true *only* — and in my world the inability to say "definately not" is exactly what you get when negation refuses to give you back a clean answer.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which of those four translate cleanly into constraints, repairs, and three-valued columns; which only translate if you squint; and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all. Three specific things to press on while you're there. First, my architecture is two tier: a tolerant store that keeps conflicting rows, plus a strict fail-fast supervisor that checks invariants and shuts things down when they're broken — she said that's cheating and that I'd given her a classical metalanguage, and I said it's just good engineering, so tell me which of us is describing her actual position. Second, my system needs two distinct states, missing data and conflicting data, and it looked to me like her framework only has room for one of them, so I want to know whether that's my misreading or a real feature of the view. Third, in my system a conflict is always local — I can point at the exact rows — and I want to know whether she can say which contradictions are the true ones or whether once a theory is inconsistent the glut spreads.

Where the translation fails, I want your view on the cause: is my vocabulary missing something, or is the philosophical thing less clear than it sounded at the time?
