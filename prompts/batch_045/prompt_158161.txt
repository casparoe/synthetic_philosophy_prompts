I'm in a final-year seminar on moral responsibility, and last week's session on the standing to blame has left me stuck inside an argument I built for my response paper and can no longer see the exit from. I want to hold on to two claims: that a hypocrite really is missing something when they blame, and that a great deal of ordinary blame is entirely in order. The trouble is the route from the first to the denial of the second:

(1) If A blames B for a wrong of kind K, and A has herself committed a wrong of kind K without acknowledging it or making amends, then A lacks standing to blame B.
(2) Standing is a matter of A's relation to the norm B is accused of violating, not of whether B actually violated it; standing is what makes the blame A's to give.
(3) Blame issued without standing is not merely offensive but defective: it fails to do whatever blame is supposed to do, and B may properly set it aside by pointing out that A has done the same.
(4) Nearly every adult has done something morally wrong that they have neither acknowledged nor fully made amends for.
(5) Kinds of wrong are classifiable at multiple levels of generality: any two wrongs fall under some shared description (carelessness; disregard for someone else's interests; acting on a maxim one would not universalize; treating a person as a means), while also being distinguishable under some finer-grained description.
(6) So for any wrong of B's there is some description K under which it and some unacknowledged wrong of A's both fall, and by (1) A lacks standing.
(7) Since A was arbitrary, no one ever has standing to blame anyone for anything, and by (3) blame never succeeds at what it is for.
(8) That cannot be right: some blame plainly is in order, and a practice that could never once succeed is not a practice we could ever have acquired.

I tried escaping by narrowing (1) to cases where A's own conduct shows she does not genuinely accept the norm she invokes, but the ladder seems to rebuild itself, since for any norm A invokes there will be some other norm she has flouted. So please tell me which step you would reject, why that step looked airtight, and what each of the alternatives costs — including what each of the three accounts we read (blame as second-personal moral address, blame as a modification of the blamer's relationship to the target, and blame as a judgment about the target's quality of will) would have to pay to reject (6), to reject (3), or to reject (1). I would rather keep (1) and (8) than (4) or (5), but I want to know the price before I choose. Answer in American English, in no more than 450 words, and lead with a single sentence naming the step you reject before turning to the diagnosis and the pricing.
