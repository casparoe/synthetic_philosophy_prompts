Epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness has been charged with self-stultification for well over a century: if experiences cause nothing, then my experience of pain does not cause my judgment that I am in pain, so that judgment is not grounded in the very thing it is about, and the epiphenomenalist who sincerely avows her own phenomenology appears to be claiming knowledge of a realm with which she has no commerce. The reply is equally old and equally stable: the avowal is caused not by the phenomenal property but by its neural correlate (or by some other non-phenomenal state that reliably tracks it), and reliable tracking plus truth is all that knowledge was ever supposed to require. I have now watched this exchange run in place across decades of papers and several reading groups: the moves on both sides are entirely predictable, nobody shifts, and each side treats the other as having simply failed to see what is at issue. So I don't want another round. I want a diagnosis of what kind of stalemate this is.

Here is the state of play as I see it, and please engage these positions rather than a stylized version of them. The objector says the epiphenomenalist's avowal is unjustified, or accidentally true, or — in the stronger version — not really about her phenomenology at all; pressed on what exactly is missing, objectors split three ways, into those who want the experience to cause the judgment, those who only want it to explain or metaphysically ground it, and those who want the experience itself to be a constituent of the subject's reason for the judgment. The epiphenomenalist replies that reports issue from a monitoring system whose deliverances covary with phenomenal states, so the beliefs are safe and reliably produced by anything an externalist epistemology could ask for; that the objector's principle would generalize disastrously to the unobservable past, to other minds and to abstracta, where the causal condition is idle; and that the demand for phenomenal causation smuggles in an acquaintance requirement nobody has independently motivated. Note also that the target moves: on the older steam-whistle image experiences have effects that are functionally irrelevant, while on the stricter version they have no effects at all, and the two versions do not meet the objection in the same place.

Two probes I want your diagnosis to be tested against. First, the shadow-channel stipulation: suppose every phenomenal state P is accompanied by a physically specified state S which is not caused by P, which covaries with P as a matter of natural law, and which causes, through a dedicated monitoring system, the subject's judgment that she is in P — with no further explanatory route from P to the judgment beyond this common-cause-plus-covariance structure. This is, I take it, the best case the epiphenomenalist can construct. Does the objector's complaint survive it? If it does, the dispute was never about causation or reliability but about a further requirement that ought to be stated in its own terms; if it does not, the objector was running an ordinary epistemic complaint under a metaphysical-sounding label. Second, the displacement test: run the same argument with phenomenal properties replaced by epiphenomenal moral properties, by facts about a region of spacetime no signal from which can reach us, and by another person's mental states known only through behavior. If the self-stultification argument runs equally in those cases, the live disagreement is about a general principle about knowledge or reference — some causal or explanatory access condition — and the phenomenal case contributes only intuition-pumping. If it does not run, then something special about phenomenality is doing the work, and the diagnosis has to say what it is: self-intimation, the constitutive tie between a phenomenology and its seeming, or the fact that here the property in question is the very thing one would otherwise have counted as one's epistemic contact with the world.

My own hypothesis, stated as one, is that at least three different questions are being argued under a single heading — is the avowal justified, does it refer, and what makes it true — and that the apparent depth of the paradox is an artefact of sliding between them. But I want that weighed against the alternatives: a verbal dispute about grounded in and because of; a genuine clash of epistemic standards; a disagreement about which cases are data, with the epiphenomenalist's own avowal treated as a datum on one side and as theory-contaminated output on the other; or bedrock intuitions about the conceivability of causally inert properties that neither side can argue for. Give me the diagnosis with reasons drawn from what each side actually says, and then tell me, given that diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, the displacement test, a formal result about reliability without a causal chain, a change of question — or why no progress should be expected at all.
