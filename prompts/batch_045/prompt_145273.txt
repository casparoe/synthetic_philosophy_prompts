Hi — hoping you can do me a solid. I've got a talk in a fortnight at a one-day workshop on fanaticism and small probabilities, and I want to walk in with the question period already rehearsed.

The room is about thirty people and the mix is the problem. Roughly half are formal epistemologists of the imprecise-probability persuasion: they think the mugging is what you get for pretending to have a sharp prior over "the mugger is telling the truh", several of them are permissivists about rational credence, and they'd all much rather smear or bound the prior than bound utility. Then maybe five decision theorists, mostly of a causalist bent, who will want to know exactly what I take myself to be conditionalizing on and whether the "evidence" I'm leaning on is doing news-value work or genuine causal work. And three or four longtermist types who think unbounded expected value is basically in order and that a bit of fanaticism is a feature — they're the ones I'm most likely to loose, because my view, if it works, is a general-purpose defuser and not just a patch for the parking-lot case. Oh, and one emeritus philosopher of religion who came because of the "Pascal" in the title and who is going to be quietly furious that we've spent the morning on a man in a hoodie. Assume nobody there has read anything of mine.

The thesis: the bite of Pascal's mugging doesn't come from small probabilities, or from large payoffs, or from unbounded utility. It comes from a hidden assumption that the magnitude of somebody's demand carries no information about whether the demand is credible. What I'm defending — I've been calling it the counterfactual-cost constraint, though I may rename it before the handout — is that a demand is credible only up to the cost the claimant would have had to bear to secure the thing demanded by some other route. Any agent who can really produce or destroy N units of value has, for large enough N, vastly cheaper ways of getting your five dollars than asking you for it: hire someone, take it, mint it, or simply not care about five dollars. So the probability that the threat is real, given that this utterance occurred, has to fall at least as fast as something in the neighbourhood of 1/N, and once you conditionalize on the speech act the expected value collapses on its own — before anyone has to bound utility, go imprecise, or impose a probability threshold. The bit I actually care about defending is that this isn't an ad hoc patch: utterances are actions, actions have opportunity costs, speakers are responsive to those costs, so the content of the ask is evidence about the world in the boring way that any cheap signal is.

I have replies to the three I can see coming — (a) the ask is nearly costless, so the mugger can spam a billion people and the opportunity-cost argument evaporates; (b) there are agentless versions, a natural process or a physics that permits astronomical amounts of value, where there's no utterance to condition on and the constraint does nothing at all; (c) the permissivists will say my constraint is just a smuggled prior and that I've swapped one arbitrary number for another. I want the ones I haven't thought of, and I want those three ranked by how much damage they'd actually do me in that room.

So: a prep sheet, not a defence. Order the objections by how likely they are and how much trouble they cause me, phrase each one the way that subgroup would actually say it (I want to hear the permissivist's version of the "smuggled prior" point in permissivist vocabulary, and the CDT person's version of the news-value point in theirs), give me a two-minute reply to each — I've got maybe twelve minutes of questions, so realistically four or five — and tell me where each reply is soft. I also want: what I should concede in the first slide before anyone asks, what I hold come hell or high water, the single reply that has to land or the whole thing is dead, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks. If you think one of my three replies above is the weakest, say so and say why. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I know the literature.
