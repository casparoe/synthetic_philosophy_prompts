I'm in a reading group — two philosophers, one econ theorist, and an ML person — that's spent about a year going in circles on revealed preference: does "utility" as it's used there mean anything more than a compressed description of choice behavior, or is it supposed to be doing real work, like telling you whether a policy made someone better off or what their well-being actually is? Nobody has moved an inch. My own hypothesis is that it isn't a verbal fight about the word "preference" and it isn't really about psychologism either: the two sides want the formalism to do different jobs (one wants a representation for prediction, the other wants a currency for evaluation), and each keeps scoring points with examples that only look decisive given their own job description.

But the ML person threw in something I think might be doing more work than my diagnosis: in the settings we actually care about now you often dont get repeated choice from a whole family of budgets, you get one more or less fixed policy, one action per situation — and then the utility function isn't even pinned down, since a reward that's zero everywhere (and continuum-many others) rationalizes whatever the agent did. So maybe the argument feels stuck because both sides are quietly assuming identification is fine and arguing about what to call the thing once you've got it.

Is that right — is a hidden underdetermination question the load bearing issue here, or is that a red herring and the real disagreement is about whether utility has to be a causal, psychologically real state? And given whichever diagnosis you give, what would actually count as progress — a theorem, a distinction, a different question, or nothing at all? I want the diagnosis with reasons, not a tour of the positions.
