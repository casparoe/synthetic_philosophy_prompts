I'm in a 4th-year seminar right now on measurement and machine learning (cross-listed between philosophy and the i-school), we spent most of last week on Goodhart's law, and I've realised the thing I keep noticing isn't quite it and I don't know what to call it.

The pattern: you pick a proxy, you optimise against it, the proxy goes up and up, and the thing you actually cared about really does improve — genuinely, not just on paper. But the proxy quietly stops being able to tell apart the cases you now actually have. It hasn't stopped tracking the target in the coarse sense: if you plotted the whole history including the bad old days, it would still correlate fine. It's just that everything you now see is crammed into a narrow band near the top where the proxy has no resolution left, so a real improvement and a real collapse look identical, and the number you were relying on as an early-warning system has gone deaf.

Three cases I keep coming back to:

1. My sleep tracker's "sleep score". I actually fixed my sleep — same bedtime, dark room, nothing to drink after nine. Score went from around 70 to a steady 92–95. And now it's useless: it gives me 93 whether I wake up fine or wake up wrecked, and if something new started ruining my sleep I genuinely don't think the number would budge.
2. My university's counselling service started being measured on time-to-first-response for intake. They got fast, the average dropped under an hour, good. Then the bottleneck moved to the second appointment, a real backlog built up, and the response-time number just sat there. Nobody gamed anything, and the number still measures exactly what it says it measures.
3. Our course project: we fine-tuned a summariser against a learned reward model. Rewards climbed smoothly. The outputs weren't doing the obvious reward-hack stuff we'd been warned about — no all caps, no weird padding — they just stopped getting better in any way any of us could detect, while the number crept up. And we had no way to tell whether we were still improving or had quietly broken something.

So: is there an established concept for this? So far everyone just says "that's Goodhart", but the flavors we went through in class don't seem to fit — nobody is adversarially gaming the measure, it isn't regression toward the mean from selecting on a noisy proxy, it isn't the extreme-tails version, and the causal link between proxy and target hasnt been broken by the optimisation. Someone said Campbell's law, someone said restriction of range, someone said it's just the base-rate problem (the outcome got rare, so the measure got noisy). All feel close, none feel right, and I also can't tell whether case 2 is even the same phenomenon as 1 and 3 or a different one where the bottleneck just moved downstream.

I don't want a label on its own — I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, how it differs from each of the nearby things, whether it's really a special case of one of them, and where its edges are. Is it still the same thing if the proxy lost resolution because you *selected* on it rather than optimised against it? What if the target hasn't changed at all? What if the proxy still has plenty of variance but the variance has stopped meaning anything? If there's no name, coin one and define it, including the cases where you'd say no, that's not an instance. And is there any way to tell from the numbers alone, before it bites you, that you've slid into it?
