I'm a philosophy PhD student working in bioethics, and I'm drafting a chapter on how a public payer should decide what to cover — specifically, whether a scheme that insures treatments but not enhancements could be made to work, and what would have to be true of the therapy/enhancement distinction for it to bear that kind of institutional weight. Before I go further into the existing accounts I want the specification. So: write the desiderata that a theory of the therapy/enhancement distinction would have to satisfy in order to be adequate for this purpose — good enough to underwrite a coverage rule that is administrable, not arbitrary in the cases everyone antecedently agrees about, and not trivially gameable — and give the case or consideration behind each requirement. Two constraints on the list itself: it must not collapse restoring into improving, i.e. must not let the distinction degenerate into a difference in the magnitude of benefit, and it must not quietly redefine it as medical need, since that is the notion the coverage rule was supposed to be built out of. Where two requirements pull apart, state that as a result rather than papering over it: tell me which subset you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive theories. Please also mark which requirements you take to be genuinely non-negotiable and which are really commitments of one camp dressed up as adequacy conditions. I have a suspicion that both the demand that the concept be value-free and descriptive, so that clinicians can apply it without moral contestation, and the demand that the distinction do real justificatory work in allocating resources, are such pseudo-desiderata, but argue it either way. A table is fine.

The cases I keep tripping over are the familiar ones plus several I have not seen handled. Prevention — vaccines, statins, prophylactic surgery in people who would never have developed the condition — looks like enhancement on any species-typical-function view and is nevertheless the paradigm of reimbursable medicine. Aging presses from the other side, since senescence is statistically normal for the species, so such views seem to entail that sarcopenia or presbyopia are not medical matters at all. Continuous dimensions with no principled break (growth hormone for idiopathic short stature; cochlear implants that first restore and then exceed the typical range) raise the cutoff problem. There is also environment-relativity: stimulants for inattention that is partly an artifact of how a school day is structured, and the historical pathologizing of traits that were costly only within a particular social order. The cases I have not seen discussed are these. First, the set-point ratchet: suppose an intervention works by raising the level around which a homeostatic system regulates, so that after withdrawal the person sits below their pre-intervention baseline and the maintenance dose only returns them to the new set point. Is that dose therapy, and which baseline is authoritative — the pre-intervention individual, the current individual, or a population reference? Second, second-order interventions, which do little for you directly but change how well later interventions work on you, something along the lines of a plasticity or learning-rate enhancer; their value is entirely deferred and derivative, and I do not know what a coverage rule should say about them. Third, delegated-benefit interventions, where the benefit accrues to third parties rather than the patient — a drug that makes a parent less dispositionally short-tempered, say — which have a strong claim on a public payer but fit badly under restoring function in this patient. Fourth, prevalence inversion: if a deficient condition becomes nearly universal, statistical views appear to reclassify its treatment as enhancement, which looks like a reductio. So I want a list that tells me, for each requirement, which of these it is there to settle, and I especially want to know whether an adequate theory can be baseline-invariant, in the sense that its verdicts do not shift with the choice of reference class among species, age-and-sex-adjusted subpopulation, and the individual's own history, or whether instead it must argue for one of those as the correct baseline.
