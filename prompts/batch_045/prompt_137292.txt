I've been asked to give the opening remarks at a one-day festschrift for my doctoral supervisor, Professor Vera Lindqvist — six minutes, roughly eight hundred words, before her two sons speak — and I'm stuck, because Vera is alive and will be in the front row. Three years ago a bilateral stroke left her in what her clinicians classify as a minimally conscious state: she tracks objects, she has an intermittent and unreliable yes/no, she has not held a reliable conversation since. Her rehabilitation physician has told the family that she can probably hear them and that they should keep talking to her, and they have, most evenings for three years. The room will be about half her former students and colleagues — comparative cognition, computational neuroscience, two people who now work on digital minds, a few philosophers who know the literature on whether computational descriptions are observer-relative — and about half people who are not: her sons, one of whom is a born-again Christian convinced that his mother is in there and frightened and that her career was partly to blame for his inability to say so, one of whom is estranged and hostile to the whole enterprise; a niece; her physician; two of the nurses from her care home; and a scattering of undergraduates who came because their seminar was cancelled.

The problem is that Vera's own view, which I more or less hold, makes almost every available line unavailable. She spent the last two decades arguing that for ethical purposes we should stop asking whether a system is conscious and ask instead whether it keeps what she called a ledger: a single scalar quantity, one currency, into which aversive and appetitive signals are integrated and then used by the system itself to rank candidate actions against counterfactual futures. On that view a creature matters in proportion to the width of its ledger, how many distinguishable things can be at stake for it at once, and not in proportion to its neuron count or its behavioural repertoire; a system with no ledger has nothing at stake in it, however much information it moves; and whether a given piece of processing is inside the ledger at all is not settled by the physics alone but by which decomposition you take to be the system's own, the thing she used to call the missing bookkeeper. That last claim is exactly why I cannot stand up and say that Vera hears us and is glad we came. Intact auditory processing with no one to record it is the case her view says is empty, and the half of the room that has read her will hear me betray her if I blur it; her sons will hear me say that three years of talking at her bedside was addressed to nobody. I will not tell them instead that she has an intact soul following the proceedings, because I don't believe that and she didn't. And I can't split the difference with a pious we simply cannot know, because on her own account that is where the work starts rather than ends.

What I want is a short speech that gets through this honestly. It should honour her as the person who made the argument without pronouncing on whether she is still the sort of thing the argument protects; it should be honest about our ignorance without treating ignorance as a place to stop; it should land for the neuroscientists and the AI-welfare people without a single term the undergraduates and her niece can't follow; and it should end in something concrete, because I am going to walk up to her afterwards and say something and I would prefer it not to be a lie. Please do not give me the rationalist-blogosphere version of this: I have been reading those blogs and forums for about fifteen years and I can produce the speech about how many subjects might be in the room and how we should shut up and multiply under moral uncertainty myself, and it would be grotesque in front of her sons. No scope-sensitivity applause lines, no substrate-independence hand-waving, no nematode jokes, no condescension toward the son who believes in a soul. And please don't put anything crude in my mouth about one physical system implementing the same computation as another: about a third of the room will notice, and they are the third I have to face afterwards. After the speech, add a short note of a couple of hundred words flagging any line that is a strategic fiction, something I say for the room's sake that I don't think is strictly true, with the honest version next to it; and tell me what would make you write me a different speech, both evidentially, what clinical finding about Vera would do it, and philosophically, what argument against the ledger view would do it, and where you think the reasoning in what you have written is most likely to fail.
