ok so I'm about 400 pages into a novel set right after a war my made-up coalition (Varenne, Ostmark, the Sene Republic) has just won, and there's a tribunal sitting in the occupied capital trying what's left of the defeated government. I modelled it loosely on Nuremberg and Tokyo: three counts (crimes against peace, war crimes, crimes against humanity), one judge from each victor on the bench. I've read enough about the real ones to know the standard complaints — retroactive crimes against peace, tu quoque, the emperor nobody indicted, the dissent that called the whole thing victors' justice. Don't explain that back to me and don't tell me what the real tribunals did in specific cases. What I need is for my judges to be consistent with themselves, because my readers will definately notice if they're just doing whatever the scene needs.

Here's the track record I've already written them into:

- Chancellor Aurev, who ordered the invasion. Convicted on the peace and humanity counts, life. His lawyers showed (correctly) that aggressive war wasn't a crime in Varenne's statute book and that the treaty which would have made it one never came into force. The presiding judge's line: some acts are crimes against the peace of the world whatever any statute says, and a man who could have not invaded doesn't get to hide behind the silence of the law.
- Air Marshal Tell, incendiary raids on Ostmark cities, around ninety thousand civilians dead. Acquitted on that count, though he got fifteen years on a forced-labour count. The opinion gives two reasons and never says which one carries the weight: (a) the coalition's own air commands did the same thing on a larger scale, and the bench will not invent a rule for the vanquished that it declines to apply to the victors; (b) the law of aerial warfare in 1941 was genuinely unsettled, and where the law is unclear we do not convict.
- Commandant Sera, who ran the Wreyland camp. Convicted on the humanity count with the heaviest sentence given to anyone below cabinet rank. She selected prisoners for transports she knew ended at a killing ground, and she devised punishments the regulations didn't require. Orders are no defence, and a defendant who improvises cruelties her orders didn't ask for stands in the position of an author.
- Second Lieutenant Baas, nineteen, conscripted, who guarded a siding where people were loaded, and who once, on a corporal's order, fired into a crowd breaking out of a wagon; two died. Convicted, three years, the lightest sentence the tribunal has handed down. There was no exit a reasonable person would have taken, and the sentence marks the distance between the hand that gave the order and the hand that carried it out.
- Manufacturer Kel, whose firm built the camp crematoria to the camp administration's specification. Convicted, seven years: a man who supplies the instrument knowing the use is not a bystander.
- And a refused count: the Sene prosecutor moved to add the peacetime Relocation Decrees, the regime's persecution of the Shem minority inside its own borders, 1931 to 1935, before any war, aimed at nobody outside the country. Refused in one line: this tribunal's jurisdiction is the war. (The Sene Republic has a minority of its own and nobody wants that looked at.)

Now the defendant I have to write the ruling for next week. Major Ansel Vey, northern district rail office, 1941 to 1944:

- He scheduled and sealed the transports to Wreyland. He was never in a policy meeting, never met Aurev, had no hand in designing the programme.
- He knew where they went: he initialled a stationmaster's written report describing the ramp.
- He asked for a legal opinion, and the Judge Advocate General put it in writing in 1942 that the resettlement transports were lawful under the emergency decrees.
- From 1943 he forged manifests and routed something like four thousand people to a farm-labour scheme instead, and arranged mechanical failures that delayed two trains. He was nearly caught once. He went on scheduling everything else.
- Unlike Baas he had an exit that wasn't heroic: a transfer request to a line regiment, routinely granted, no penalty for asking. He never filed one.
- What my defence lawyer digs up in chapter twelve: article 47 of Varenne's own Military Penal Code, in force since 1908, makes it an offence for a serviceman to withhold the means of subsistence from a civilian population under his charge. So on one reading the thing he did was already a crime in his own army's law, and whatever saved Tell can't save him. The defence answer is that under his charge means people actually in his custody, not people at the far end of a line he timetabled.
- And the bit the Sene judge is going to hate: the Sene food blockade of the defeated territory killed, on the in-world Red Cross numbers, roughly as many civilians as the transports Vey scheduled, no Sene officer has been charged, and the Sene judge has said on the record that the blockade was harsh but lawful.

So: what should the bench do with Vey if it wants to be consistent with itself — convict or acquit, and if convict, where does the sentence sit against Baas's three years and Kel's seven? Which precedent is the closest analogue, and if the honest answer is that two of them pull in opposite directions, say that. I can see three or four rules that fit the six rulings I've already written — something like no rule for the vanquished the victors won't apply to themselves, something like convict where the conduct was already criminal somewhere and otherwise only where it shocks the conscience, something like liability tracks the options the defendant actually had, something like knowing contribution is enough — and they come apart on Vey, so tell me which one I'd be committing the tribunal to with each verdict. Then give me the one further case that would actually tell the rules apart, because I want a lawyer on the page to make that point. And if you can, draft the couple of sentences the presiding judge has to say out loud, since that's the bit I really have to write.
