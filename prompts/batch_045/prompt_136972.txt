I convene a small graduate reading group in aesthetics and political philosophy; this term we have been working through W. E. B. Du Bois’s writings on art, especially his notorious claim that art cannot escape being propaganda, and I am trying to work part of the material into a seminar paper, which is why the stalemate keeps nagging at me. The dispute in our group has not moved for over a year. On one side is a Du Boisian view I’ll call the advocacy thesis: every artwork, simply by presenting a form, a style, or a way of seeing, inevitably advocates some vision of human life and relations, so the only serious critical question is whether that vision is true and just; a work that is formally accomplished but serves falsehood or degradation is, on this view, defective as art. On the other side is an aesthetic-purist view: a work’s artistic value is fixed by its sensible and formal properties, or by its success as an aesthetic object, and any political or moral advocacy it seems to have is either an accidental causal effect, a matter of how viewers happen to use it, or simply not part of its artistic identity; from this side, Du Bois’s claim looks like either a trivializing redefinition of ‘propaganda’ or an imperial demand that all art become polemic. To make the disagreement concrete, we tried a test case involving a text-to-image model: a user enters the bland prompt ‘a quiet street at dawn,’ and two differently fine-tuned systems return images that are respectively adopted by a gentrification campaign and by tenant organizers. Call the resulting puzzle the advocacy-supervenience question: is a work’s standing as propaganda fixed by its intrinsic aesthetic features alone, or does it also depend on relational facts about its training, distribution, audience, and uptake? My formalist friend says the propagandistic character is entirely downstream of use and therefore external to the image’s artistic content; my Du Boisian friend replies that the impossibility of a neutral uptake is precisely the point, because the model’s output is already saturated with stylistic and compositional choices that presuppose a social world. The dispute lately spilled into a hallway next to the student ceramics display after a room-booking mix-up, where a glazed bowl with a traditional pattern became yet another contested example: nobody could agree whether its pattern carried any advocacy at all.

My own hypothesis is that the deadlock has two layers, and I want you to tell me whether that is right and, more importantly, what kind of disagreement this really is. At the first layer, I suspect much of the argument is verbal: Du Bois seems to use ‘propaganda’ broadly, for any unavoidable advocacy of a vision of life, while the purists use it narrowly, for manipulative or partisan indoctrination, and if we stipulate one sense many blows glance off. But I do not think that is the whole story, because even after that stipulation there seems to be a substantive clash about whether a work’s political-ethical orientation is internal to its aesthetic value or external to it, and perhaps also a hidden empirical question about whether audiences in fact always read political vision into even the most formal works. There may also be two different questions under one heading: one about the semantics or metaphysics of ‘propaganda,’ another about the ethics of criticism, and maybe a third about the causal powers of images in digital platforms. Using what each side actually says — not a survey of the literature, and with no need for citations or a reading list — diagnose the structure of this impasse: is it verbal, substantive, a clash of bedrock intuitions about artistic autonomy that neither side can argue for, a hidden factual or formal question, or several debates collapsing into one? Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction between broad and narrow senses of propaganda, a formal result about whether propaganda-content supervenes on aesthetic properties, an empirical finding about audience uptake, a reframing of the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the issue is ill-posed. I would especially like you to say whether the AI test case clarifies the dispute or merely restages it, and to be blunt if you think the only honest answer is that the debate should be dissolved rather than resolved.
