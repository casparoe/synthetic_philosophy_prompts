so, quick context: I write historical fiction. Two novels set in and around a Mainz printing house in the decades after the movable-type thing got going, one set in a Lancashire mill town in the 1820s, and a fourth I keep failing to finish that's about the people who built the first bomb. Somehow this has made me the person who gets invited onto panels about AI. And every single panel, without fail, somebody says "this is just like the printing press," or "it's more like the industrial revolution," or "it's the Manhattan Project, obviously," and everybody nods, and I have never once heard anybody say what would make one of those analogies *bad*. They're unfalsifiable in a really specific way: they're all offered after we know how the story ended.

So before I say yes to another one of these — write me the spec. Not a list of analogies. The requirements: what a historical analogy for a transformative technology has to do in order to count as any good at all, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Adequacy is relative to purpose, so here's mine, and it's a weird one, which is sort of the point: I want an analogy good enough to (a) tell me something true about what it's like to be a person inside the change — my unit of interest is a life, not a GDP curve — and also (b) survive being said out loud to someone who funds things or regulates them. Please flag anywhere those two purposes pull apart, because I suspect they do, hard.

Things I already have opinions about, which you should feel free to demolish or promote into real desiderata:

- I think an analogy has to come with a *mechanism*, not a resemblance. "Both made information cheap" is a resemblance. What's the causal thing being transferred, and what were its preconditions — and can I check whether the preconditions hold here, now, before the fact? If the only way to check is to wait thirty years, say that, because it matters.
- I have a private test I use on my own material and I want to know if it's fair: the year-five test. Run the analogy as if you were living five years in, knowing only what was knowable then, not how it turned out. If the analogy only works once you know the ending, it's a narrative device, not evidence. But I worry this test is too strong, since the entire epistemic value of an analogy seems to be that we *do* know the ending — strip that out and maybe there's nothing left. Is that a real tension or am I being stupid?
- Reference-class shopping. Printing press, steam, electricity, the automobile, the bomb, the stirrup. There are enough big transitions that you can always find one that supports what you already wanted to do, and n is tiny and each case is overdetermined. Does a good theory need to fix the reference class in advance, and if so how, and what does it do about the fact that the sample is small and the surviving record was written by the people who won?
- Timescale compression. These analogies smuggle in a duration. Printing didn't reorganize European life in a decade; the mill town took generations to produce whatever it produced, and there's a famous argument about whether ordinary workers' material conditions actually improved for the first several decades or got worse first. If the analogy is silent about how long each stage takes and about whether there's a bad part before any good part, it can't be used for anything.
- The protagonist requirement, which nobody on these panels ever mentions and which I care about most: an analogy should be able to name the person whose life it's about and say what happens to their skill, their income, their standing, and their sense of what they're for. I notice the individual-level story and the aggregate-level story often point opposite directions — the person is ruined for a while and the aggregate is eventually fine, or vice versa. Can one analogy do both, or is that switch forbidden?

Deliver me:
1. The requirements, with the motivating case behind each, and for each one a verdict: non-negotiable, or a preference that only one camp insists on. I especially want the ones people *think* are requirements but which are really just taste, and the ones nobody asks for but which are load-bearing.
2. Any pair (or triple) of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied. If the honest conclusion is that no analogy can be both genuinely predictive and genuinely checkable-in-advance, I want that stated flatly.
3. Which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the analogies people actually make. One requirement, and the argument for it.
4. Then — and keep this short, it's not the part I care about — a table scoring the printing press, the industrial revolution, and nuclear weapons against your list. Three rows. No citations, no further reading, I'm not writing a paper.
