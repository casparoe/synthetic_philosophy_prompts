I drive a delivery van and have no legal training, but for the past year I've been going to the evening meetings where a group of towns in my valley is drafting a Compact to federate, and I can't stop turning over an argument I built after one of the sessions. A member of the drafting committee proposed what he calls the lockbox clause: the rest of the Compact can be amended by a simple majority, but this sentence can be amended only with the unanimous consent of every member, including anyone who joins later, and no amendment to this sentence takes effect unless it keeps that unanimity requirement in place. He says this is just an extreme version of what ordinary supermajority rules already do, and that if we accept those we should accept this. I went home and wrote out why I think his clause breaks the whole idea of a written constitution, and the argument has since grown a conclusion I don't believe.

Here it is as I wrote it. (1) A body of rules is law of a self-governing order only if, for every rule in it, the order itself lays down some procedure by which that rule can be changed; a rule that no such procedure can reach is not part of the order at all, it is a limit imposed on the order from outside, like a colonial governor's power to strike down local ordinances. (2) If an order can amend its own amendment procedure, then its founders can entrench: they can pass a rule that makes some later rule harder to change, and they can even attach to it a condition that any amendment to it must preserve the very condition that makes it hard to amend. (3) The lockbox clause is such a rule. (4) Once it is in force, no procedure the Compact lays down can ever remove it: a repeal would need unanimous consent, but even a repeal with unanimous consent would fail, because a repeal does not preserve the unanimity requirement. (5) So by (1) the lockbox clause is not really law of the Compact but an external constraint on it — yet it was made by the Compact's own procedure and binds the members, so it is both inside and outside the order at once, which is impossible. (6) And the problem isn't the clause: any order that can amend its own amendment rule can always do this to itself, so the power to amend the rules of amendment cannot be separated from the power to extinguish one's own legal order. (7) Therefore the idea of a self-amending legal order is incoherent, and the honest conclusion is that no founding document binds anyone who comes after it: a bare majority can always legitimately rewrite the whole thing, and every unamendable clause in every real constitution is either a bluff or a rule imposed by people who are dead.

I can't accept (7), partly because it would make most of our drafting pointless and partly because it seems to prove far more than the argument earns. But everyone I've shown this to rejects a different step, which makes me think the trouble may be somewhere I haven't looked. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? In particular, is there a reading on which the founders can genuinely bind a later majority without their clause thereby ceasing to be law of the order at all? And if I give up whatever the offending step is, do I also have to say something strange about the ordinary case, where a supermajority rule merely makes amendment hard rather than making the clause preserve itself? Of the claims in play, the one I'd most like to keep is that the founders can bind us, and the one I'd least like to keep is that the lockbox clause is just words on paper with no legal force — but I'd rather know what each option costs than keep the conclusion I started out with.
