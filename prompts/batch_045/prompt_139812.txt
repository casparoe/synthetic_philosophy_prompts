**Question 3 — Conceivability and possibility.**
(a) Two distinctions among kinds of conceivability are standardly drawn in this literature. State both, and explain why they matter for the inference from conceivability to possibility.
(b) The Kripkean cases of the necessary a posteriori are often said to break that inference. Present the resulting objection to conceivability arguments in its strongest form, and state exactly what the objector must establish.
(c) Explain how a defender of the conceivability argument against physicalism should respond, and assess whether the response succeeds. Be explicit about any further premise the response requires.

The answer I want you to look at is reproduced in full:

"Conceivability arguments stand or fall on distinctions that are routinely blurred, so I will begin with them. The first is between prima facie and ideal conceivability. A proposition is prima facie conceivable when it strikes us as coherent on our current reflection; it is ideally conceivable when it survives ideal rational reflection, understood as reflection extended without limit rather than reflection by some omniscient being. Only ideal conceivability could be a guide to possibility, since prima facie conceivability fails whenever we are ignorant, confused, or simply tired. The second is between negative and positive conceivability: to negatively conceive that p is to fail to rule p out a priori; to positively conceive that p is to form some rich, detailed, quasi-perceptual conception of a scenario in which p. The zombie argument needs the negative and ideal notion, not the positive one. It is not the claim that I can picture a zombie in the way I can picture a teapot; it is the claim that nothing in the complete physical truth about the world rules out a priori the absence of consciousness. Critics who say the argument rests on a failure of imagination have simply picked the wrong notion.

The strongest version of the objection from the necessary a posteriori is Kripke's own. Kripke showed that some truths are necessary but knowable only a posteriori: Hesperus is Phosphorus, water is H2O. In each case we can apparently conceive the denial, and what we are conceiving is impossible. Kripke concluded from this that conceiving is a guide only to how things appear to us, never to how they could be, and hence that no argument of the form conceivability therefore possibility has any force. Applied to the zombie case, the objector's point is that our inability to derive the phenomenal truths from the physical ones reveals only our ignorance of an identity we could discover empirically, just as the ancients' inability to derive Hesperus is Phosphorus from what they knew revealed only their ignorance. Conceivability arguments are then, in the end, inferences from ignorance to possibility, and the necessary a posteriori is the standing refutation of that inference.

The defender has a good reply. In the Kripkean cases the apparent conceiving is not a genuine conceiving at all. When someone takes herself to conceive that water is not H2O, what she really conceives is a scenario in which the watery stuff of our acquaintance has some other microstructure, and that scenario is perfectly possible; she misdescribes it using a sentence whose terms are rigid. The appearance of contingency is an illusion produced by not knowing the identity, and an illusion is not a case of conceiving, so these are not counterexamples to the link between conceiving and possibility. The materialist cannot run the same diagnosis in the zombie case. For her to explain away our conceiving of zombies, she must say that consciousness is in fact identical to some physical process, and that this identity, like the water one, is knowable only a posteriori. But that is to concede that the physical truths do not entail the phenomenal ones by a priori reasoning, and a priori ruling-out is the only route we have to possibility in this region. So the materialist faces a choice: produce the a priori entailment, which nobody has ever produced, or concede that the zombie world is not a priori excluded, which is to concede that it is ideally negatively conceivable and therefore possible. Since the possibility of zombies is incompatible with physicalism, physicalism is false."
