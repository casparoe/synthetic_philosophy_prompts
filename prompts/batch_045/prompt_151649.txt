I'd like your help predicting a verdict, and I would rather have a careful answer than a confident one.

My closest friend from graduate school — she left philosophy for forecasting and machine learning, and I defer to her on these questions more than I do to myself — has, over about a year of arguments, given me the following verdicts:

- Standard Newcomb case, predictor right about 90% of the time, a million in the opaque box and a thousand in the transparent one: take one box.
- The same, except that the prediction was made and sealed before you were born, the predictor has been dead for thirty years, and nobody believes in backwards causation: still one box.
- The same, except that the predictor is only 60% accurate: still one box.
- Transparent-box Newcomb, where you see whether the million is there before choosing: if it is there, take one box; if it is not, take both.
- Newcomb where a reliable assistant truthfully tells you, before you choose, what was predicted: one box if told one box, both if told both.
- One-shot prisoner's dilemma against someone who is, as far as you know, running exactly your decision procedure, with no causal interaction between you, no reputation and no aftermath: cooperate.
- Newcomb where the predictor's accuracy comes entirely from a marker that causes the choice rather than from any simulation of it: she refused to answer, and said the case needed to say more about how the marker does its work before it was well enough posed to judge.

When I pressed her once on why she one-boxes, she said that the thing doing the deciding and the thing that was simulated are the same thing. In another mood she said that she would rather be the sort of person the machine predicts will one-box. I read the first remark as functionalist and the second as evidentialist, which is why I cannot predict her.

Two months ago my cardiologist started me on a preventive medication. Reading the evidence myself, the effect in patients like me looks like part genuine mechanism and part a marker of being the kind of person who takes medication as prescribed — the ordinary confounding story. Whether the confounded half of that evidence should carry any weight for me is therefore no longer academic, and as far as I can tell it is the same question as the one below. I am not asking for medical advice; I will take whatever you say to my doctor. I want to know what the views commit me to.

The case I need a verdict on — call it the hybrid machine. A committee puts a million in an opaque box if and only if its machine outputs one box, and puts four hundred thousand in a transparent box either way. The four hundred thousand rather than the usual thousand is deliberate: I want the candidate rules to have room to come apart. A weighted lottery has already placed you in one of two conditions; you know the weights but not the outcome, and nothing you do can affect the lottery, the machine, or the past:

- With probability 0.3, the machine is a bit-level simulation of your decision procedure on this very problem, run to completion before you choose, and it outputs whatever the simulation outputs.
- With probability 0.7, the machine reads a single genetic marker from you and outputs one box if and only if you carry it. Carriers of the marker one-box about 90% of the time, non-carriers about 10%, about half the population carries it, the marker does nothing else, and your deliberating does not change whether you have it.

One box or both?

What I want from you:

1. The rules that could generate her track record, and how each handles it. I take plain causal decision theory to be off the table, and I have tried and failed to keep a ratifiability or stability version of it alive, but check me on both. Also consider whether she is really running a rule about acts at all rather than one about which disposition it would have been best to have.
2. What each surviving rule says about the hybrid machine, with the arithmetic shown. As I do it, the evidential rule's verdict does not depend on the 0.3 at all, while the functional rule's verdict flips at 0.4 — please redo this, because I do not trust it.
3. Your best guess at her verdict, and the reasons for preferring it. If the track record genuinely underdetermines it, say so, tell me which verdict she should give in order to be consistent with herself, identify the earlier verdict that is the closest analogue and say how much it constrains her, and tell me what she would be committing to either way.
4. The further case that would settle which rule she is running.
5. If you think she would refuse this case as underdescribed, tell me what would have to be added and what she would then say.

Finally: tell me how much confidence to attach to each part of what you say, and if any of this rests on a mistake — if the two rules do not really come apart here, if my arithmetic does not do the work I think it does, if the case is ill-posed in a way that makes prediction impossible, or if some rule I have not listed fits her track record better and predicts something else — say so plainly rather than picking one politely.
