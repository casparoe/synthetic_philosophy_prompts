Longtime lurker here — been chewing on this cluster of stuff since the old Slate Star Codex days, and I finally want someone to make an argument I've only ever been able to half-make.

The topic is the higher-order evidence / epistemic akrasia thing: your evidence supports P, then evidence about how you processed that evidence says you botched it, and nobody agrees whether you're supposed to end up believing P while also thinking that belief is untrustworth, or suspend, or split the difference. Please don't build anything out of the drunk reasoner or the belief-inducing drug; I've read those a hundred times and they've stopped doing any work for me.

The distinction I want the argument to turn on, which I basically never see drawn: call a piece of higher-order evidence *self-implicating* when the only way to assess the debunking story is to use the very capacity it says is impaired. Contrast:

(a) Clean: you act on a temperature reading, then get a calibration report saying that thermometer's readings were offset by a random amount. Judging the report dosen't require the broken thermometer.

(b) Self-implicating: you read a careful 9,000-word essay arguing that people who read a lot of 9,000-word essays online steadily lose the ability to tell a good argument from a bad one — and deciding whether *this* essay is any good requires exactly the capacity it says is degrading.

The claim I want argued as hard as you can: for self-implicating higher-order evidence, the correct response is much closer to "keep the first-order belief at full strength and let it go on guiding action" than to "moderate, suspend, or akratically hedge." Not for Moorean-stubbornness reasons, but because the expected evidential force of a self-implicating debunking argument, given that you have to process it with the faculty it impugns, is near zero — or at any rate can't underwrite the specific calibrated adjustment the standard views demand.

The part I can't do myself, and the part that would make this worth reading: a non-question-begging line between self-implicating debunking you're allowed to discount (the essay case) and structurally similar cases where you obviously should update anyway — the guy who's been awake forty hours, or somebody mid-mania reading a pamphlet about how mania wrecks judgement. If there's no principled line, say so and tell me what it costs the argument.

Format: no citations or name-dropping, 700-900 words, state the load-bearing premise as one bolded sentence, and close with the single empirical assumption that, if false, sinks the whole thing. If you think the claim is wrong, argue it anyway as well as you can and give me your objection in two sentences at the end.
