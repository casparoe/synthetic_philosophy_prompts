I've been going in circles in the usual LessWrong-adjacent comment threads about whether moral patienthood comes in degrees or switches on at some threshold, and I want a scoring instrument rather than another round of intuition-mongering. Suppose the only thing you know about a system is that it exhibits one behavioral or architectural marker. Score each marker below 0–100 for how much that single fact should raise your credence that the system has valenced experience — that things can go better or worse for it — and, since I want an ordering rather than a vague gesture, no two markers may receive the same score.

1. A withdrawal reflex to a noxious stimulus, nothing else known.
2. That reflex being modulated by pairing, in the way trace conditioning would produce, as opposed to mere habituation or sensitization.
3. A motivational trade-off: the system pays a cost it would otherwise refuse in order to escape the stimulus, and the exchange rate shifts as its other needs shift.
4. My own coinage, so pin it down as you use it: cross-contextual valence integration, meaning a single internal state fed by several unrelated sources of good and bad news that re-prioritizes action selection across all of them at once, rather than each source having its own local controller.
5. A broadcast architecture: that state is made globally available to many downstream consumer systems through a bottleneck, roughly as a global workspace is supposed to work.
6. Self-report: the system says, in the ordinary way, that it is in pain.

Two constraints. For each marker, add one sentence naming the cheapest insentient lookalike — the way a system with no valence at all could still show that marker — since that lookalike is what should be capping your number. Then score a domestic thermostat with negative feedback as a seventh case on the same scale; if it does not land at or near zero, say whether that is a reductio of the instrument or a finding about thermostats.

Close with a few sentences on two questions: where a honeybee lands on your own scorecard, given that it plausibly shows markers 2 and 3 but few would bet on 5; and whether constructing the scorecard moved any credence you held beforehand, or only reconstructed beliefs you walked in with.
