I have been walked into a corner in a reading group on epistemic game theory and I cannot find the exit. The argument was offered as a reductio of the very idea of reasonable disagreement:

(1) [the standard agreement theorem] If two agents share a common prior over a finite set of possible worlds, and at the actual world it is common knowledge what their respective posterior probabilities for an event E are, then those posteriors are equal.

(2) In any serious disagreement each side has publicly stated its probability for E and heard the other's; a public statement heard by both, each seeing that the other heard it, is common knowledge.

(3) So two people who have publicly disagreed about E were never really disagreeing about E: their posteriors coincide.

(4) Hence every persistent disagreement — between hydrologists reading the same core samples, between physicians reading the same scans, between jurors who have heard the same testimony — must have a non-evidential explanation: the parties started from different priors, or one of them is being dishonest, or one of them has processed the shared evidence badly.

(5) And since nothing in the reasoning turns on how much has been said so far, the verdict is stable under further discussion: no amount of subsequent exchange can turn a genuine disagreement into agreement, because at whatever stage the posteriors differ, the same trilemma reapplies.

(6) But that is absurd. It convicts essentially every real disputant of bad faith, error, or idiosyncratic priors; it seems to abolish the possibility of rational peer disagreement, which is the central case that the epistemology of disagreement is about; and it does so without ever telling either party which of the three failings is theirs.

My first escape was to reject (2): a public announcement gives mutual knowledge of some finite order, not common knowledge, and in any case hearing someone's number is not the same as knowing the information they conditioned on. But someone in the group replied that on a finite state space this is repairable — that if the parties iterate the public announcement of their posteriors, honest Bayesian updating from a common prior drives the announcements together in finitely many rounds, so (2) can be strengthened rather than abandoned, at the cost of inserting some rounds of talk.

I want to keep (6), and I am willing to give up the common prior assumption in (1) if that is the cheapest exit, but I am worried that this makes the framework vacuous — that any observed disagreement could then be absorbed by a suitable choice of priors, and that the no-trade results and the equilibrium predictions that lean on common priors lose their bite.

Tell me which step you reject, why that step looked safe, and what rejecting it actually costs; price the alternatives as well, including the common-prior escape and the option of saying that most real disputes are not about events in a shared space of worlds at all. One further wrinkle: the case that started this was a disagreement about whether a levee will fail in the next storm, where failure depends partly on how many residents evacuate, which depends on what the residents are told, which depends on what the disputants conclude. Say whether that feature of the case stops the argument before it starts, or whether it is a red herring.

Lead with your verdict — which step you reject — in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of the question.
