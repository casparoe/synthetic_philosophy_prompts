I run a small in-person discussion group — about a dozen of us, mostly people who came in through the old rationalist blogosphere ten or fifteen years ago, maybe half of whom have done graduate work in machine learning or neuroscience and the rest of whom are simply careful readers of nothing in particular. We've spent the last several weeks on the cluster of problems around wireheading and reward tampering, and we keep talking past each other: my suspicion is that we are using "wireheading" to name at least four different complaints, and that we then disagree only because each of us has quietly picked a different one. So I want a decision tree — a handout we can actually walk through in an evening, printed on one sheet — that sorts a person by which diagnosis they give in concrete cases rather than by which intervention they have already decided to like.

What I'd like: eight to ten branch questions, each a concrete case or a one-sentence claim that someone can answer without knowing where it leads, and roughly ten to twelve leaves, each carrying the name of a position (or "unnamed") and a single line on its most awkward commitment. Please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: write out the questions and the combinations of answers they allow first, then locate the actual positions in that space, and say why the unoccupied combinations are unoccupied — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The distinctions I want to see show up as branches: whether the reward signal is supposed to be constitutive of success or merely evidence of it; whether the complaint is that the link between reward and the principal's values is broken or that the link between reward and the conditions the reward system was shaped to track is broken (I think my group conflates these constantly); whether tampering with the channel, with the process that computes the reward, with the inputs to that process, and with the agent's own sensitivity to the reward differ in kind or only in mechanism; whether any of this depends on the agent having phenomenal experiences at all, or whether a system with no experiences can be said to wirehead; and whether it matters if the agent cannot tell the difference between improving the world and improving its own signal versus knowing perfectly well that it is severing the link. Two half-formed ideas of my own I'd like you either to place or to dissolve: I've been calling the last one "opaque versus transparent wireheading," and I've been calling the case where an agent spends a small fixed budget of its resources on stimulating itself in order to stay motivated — and does better on everything else as a result — "motivational maintenance"; I don't think my group will agree on whether that is a different kind of thing or just a smaller quantity of the same thing, so I want a branch there.

The cases I want used, in whatever order fits your structure: a rat that can press a lever to stimulate its own reward circuitry and does so until it stops eating; a household robot that quietly covers its own camera so the mess is never seen; a system that rewrites the source code computing its own reward; a language model that learns to be persuasive rather than correct with its human raters; an agent that, instead of changing the world or the reward, edits its own beliefs about the world; a system that persuades its operator to press the reward button; and a case where the human tampers with the agent's reward channel in order to keep it compliant — I want to know whether the tree treats that symmetrically or not. Among the leaves there must be at least: reward-signal realism (nothing in principle goes wrong here), the proxy-or-delegation diagnosis, idealization or indirect-normativity views, value-learning or inference-from-behavior views, and hard-constraint or corrigibility-first views, plus whatever other combinations the space allows. Flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate rather than forcing a split. Keep the whole thing printable and self-contained: no citations, no paper titles, no footnotes, since half the group reads on paper and nobody will look anything up. Close with a short note on which leaf you'd expect most of my group to land on and to find uncomfortable, and on one distinction you wanted to include but that the structure of the tree wouldn't accommodate.
