I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and over the last three years I've moderated something like forty panels, podcast episodes and conference Q&As on whether large language models 'really' believe, know or want anything — and every single one of them ends at the same wall.

The shape of it is always this. One side says the intentional stance is a tool we pick up, not a fact we discover: nothing in the world settles whether it's the right one to adopt, and for these systems it's actively misleading, because unlike with a person we've got the weights and the training objective right there, so we should be giving the mechanistic story instead. The other side says: a pattern is real if there's a description of the data that buys you prediction more cheaply than the raw bit map does, and if attributing a goal to the model lets you predict what it will say next better than anything else you can do in the time you've got, then that goal is as real as anything in the special sciences — and besides, we mostly can't read the mechanism off the weights, so the intentional description is doing genuine work. Then the first side says that just sounds like instrumentalism with extra steps, because on that criterion a pattern could be real for you and not for me, depending on our computational budgets. And the second side says yes, obviously, all patterns are interest-relative, what did you expect, chess is full of patterns that only exist for players. And round we go. Nobody has moved an inch in three years, and I say that as someone who has watched the same people have this argument.

Two things I keep chewing on. First, engineers I talk to have quietly started using what I've started calling the training stance: don't ask what the system believes or how the circuit works, ask what the training process would have selected for here, and predict from that. It's neither of Dennett's top two stances and it sometimes beats both. Second, I can't shake the feeling that the argument is stalled because 'real' is doing three different jobs — a metaphysical one (can a pattern be real if it's indexed to a predictor's capacities), an epistemic one (which stance is cheapest for whom, for which system), and a public-facing one (are we licensed to talk this way to readers without misleading them).

So: diagnose the thing, not adjudicate it. Is it verbal, a fight over the word 'real'? Three questions under one heading? A hidden empirical question about which stance actually buys more compression on real systems, which somebody could in principle go and measure? Or bedrock intuitions about observer-dependence that neither camp can argue for? My hunch is the third-jobs bit, but I want it tested against what each side actually says, and I don't want a tour of the literature. And tell me honestly what would count as progress — a distinction, a compression shoot-out between stances on a fixed budget, a formal result, a change of question — or whether I should just conclude the question is ill-posed and write the piece about why it won't close. About 2000 words' worth of thinking, ideally, definately not a survey of the positions.
