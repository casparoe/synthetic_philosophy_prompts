I'm running a 75-minute session next week for a mixed grad / upper-level-undergrad seminar on concepts and analogy — 18 people, so five or six groups of three. We've been circling the idea that "the same" is never just given, that the relations and categories involved have to bend a bit before an analogy is even visible. I want them to feel that rather than hear it, so: design me an activity. No lecture up front, and nobody should hear words like "slippage" or "fluid concept" until after.

Here's the setup I've been toying with, please fix it up and fill it in properly. Instead of the usual a–z letter-string puzzles — where everyone just runs the alphabet song in their head and nothing actually slips — give them a tiny artificial microdomain. The one I've been playing with: five symbols, say ◆ ▲ ■ ● ★, arranged on a wheel with an arrow giving a direction, so every symbol has a successor and there's no first symbol, no last symbol, and no edge to fall off. Strings are short sequences like ◆◆▲ or ■●★. Change the symbols or the size if you have something better, but keep those features, and make sure the set of moves the domain invites includes: successor / predecessor; repeating a symbol vs. moving to the next one; leftmost vs. rightmost as the thing that changes; treating a whole run (◆◆) as the unit instead of a single symbol; growing a run instead of travelling along the wheel; and the gap between "the wheel" and "the symbols that happen to occur in this particular string."

The thing I actually want them to run into: the felt obviousness of an answer should turn out to depend on what they saw immediately before it, not on the problem itself. So my idea is that each group quietly gets a different warm-up set — one set only ever needs successor moves, one keeps forcing run-length moves, one keeps forcing "stay inside the symbols already in this string" moves — and then everyone gets the same handful of genuinely ambiguous target problems. They find out in the debreif that the groups disagreed because of the warm-ups, not because anyone reasoned badly. After that I'd like something where they have to bet on what a group with a different warm-up will answer, and then rank answers for naturalness rather than just concluding that anything goes.

Give me: the actual problem sets (warm-ups plus targets, with the answers you'd predict and which ones are meant to be contested), rules and materials (a wheel handout, cards, sticky notes — nothing I have to buy), a payoff scheme that needs no money, a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes with five groups, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the idea — especially that a slip isn't free, that some are cheap and some expensive, and that context and competing pressure are what make one reading win. Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm mainly worried about the relativist collapse where they decide it's all arbitrary, and about the loudest group steamrolling the room), and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce — I assume it can't show the graded, sub-symbolic, many-attempts-at-once character of an actual mechanism that does this, but tell me what else I'll have to say in my own voice.

Keep every problem answerable in the room: nobody should need any theory, any reading, or anything outside the wheel handout.
