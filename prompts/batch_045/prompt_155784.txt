Someone in my maths-and-philosophy reading group keeps wheeling out Hilbert's line "No one shall expel us from the paradise that Cantor has created" as a conversation-stopper on actual infinities. Most recently it got used to close down a discussion of whether an infinite past — no first event, infinitely many days before today — is even coherent: the idea being that Cantor sorted all this out, the hotel-with-infinitely-many-rooms stuff is only suprising because our intuitions are parochial, and finitism is a dead end. To me that one sentence is carrying several very different claims at once, and I suspect only one or two of them could actually do the work he wants.

Can you lay the readings out as a short numbered menu — the trivial or platitudinous ones, the substantive and defensible ones, and the ones that are just false — covering things like: a methodological claim about what mathematicians may help themselves to, a sociological claim about who gets to decide, an epistemic claim about consistency or truth, an ontological claim about completed infinite totalities, and a claim about physical reality as opposed to mathematical objects. For each, say whether it would really settle the hotel/infinite-past worry or just relabel it. One reading I definitely want in the list: the line comes from someone whose own programme tried to justify infinitary mathematics by finitary consistency proofs, treating infinite totalities as ideal additions rather than real objects — tell me whether that reading helps him or undermines the way he's using the quote. British English please, six readings at most, two or three sentences each, whole answer under 400 words.
