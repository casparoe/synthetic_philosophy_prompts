I sit on a professional conduct committee and do a fair amount of workplace mediation, which means that a large part of my job consists either of telling someone that what they did was wrong, or of deciding whether this particular person is the one who should be saying it. Over the years I have developed what feels like a reliable gut sense of when someone has lost the standing to blame, but I cannot state the rule I am applying, and every time I try, the rule either lets through a case I am sure is fine or rules out one I refuse to give up. I would like the principle behind my verdicts, or, if no single principle has them, the verdicts it forces me to abandon.

Three distinctions I want kept separate, because I suspect my own reactions run them together and that this is part of why I cannot find the rule:

1. whether the target really is blameworthy for what they did;
2. whether the blamer has standing to blame them for it;
3. whether, standing or no, the blaming is worth doing here, given what it will cost the relationship, how it will land, and what it is likely to change.

I will use three verdicts throughout: full standing, diminished standing, no standing. Where I give a verdict of no standing, I do not mean that the target did nothing wrong; in most of the cases below I think the target plainly did something wrong. The question is only about the blamer's position.

The cases:

1. The liar. A man who lies habitually and unrepentantly, and who has never acknowledged it, upbraids a friend for lying to him. My verdict: no standing. But the friend really did lie, and I do not think the friend may say he did nothing wrong; he may only say something about who is saying it.

2. The reformed liar. Marla was a chronic liar in her twenties, was found out, acknowledged it openly, made what amends she could, and has been scrupulous for a decade. She now confronts a colleague about a lie. My verdict: full standing — if anything, more standing than the average colleague has, which is itself part of what I want explained.

3. The modal hypocrite. Derek has never committed tax fraud, but only because his finances are handled entirely by an accountant and the opportunity has never presented itself; he knows perfectly well that in his brother's circumstances he would very likely have done the same thing. He blames his brother for tax fraud. My verdict: diminished standing, even though there is no act of his to point to.

4. The unrelated vice. A colleague who is scrupulously honest about money but chronically selfish with his time blames someone else for padding an expense report. My verdict: full standing. Hypocrisy seems to me to be local to a kind of failing, not global.

5. The general wreck. Sven lies, cheats, breaks promises and manipulates people as a matter of course, and he blames a neighbour for failing to return a borrowed tool — a failing he does not share and never has. My verdict: no standing, or very nearly. This sits badly with case 4 and I want to know which of the two I should give up.

6. The referee. A referee who, as a player, took dives himself, now sends off a player for diving. My verdict: full standing: the role seems to insulate him.

7. The smoking parent. A mother who smokes and has repeatedly failed to quit tells her teenage son that he should not be smoking. My verdict: no standing. But she has a role too, and the role in some sense authorises her to enforce exactly this standard, so this sits badly with case 6. If there is a difference here I want it stated as a condition, not gestured at.

8. The impossible deadline. A manager sets a schedule that cannot be met without cutting corners, then blames a subordinate for cutting corners. My verdict: no standing, but this feels to me like a different failure from case 1, not a worse instance of the same one: he is implicated in producing the very fault he is condemning rather than merely guilty of a copy of it. I want to know whether that distinction is real or whether I am inventing it.

9. The fellow struggler. A friend who is herself in recovery and currently finding it hard tells me privately, and kindly, that my drinking has got out of control. My verdict: full standing. Here the shared failing seems to confer standing rather than remove it, which is the mirror image of case 1, and reconciling the two is the thing I am most stuck on.

10. The self-deceived blamer. Gerard bullied people earlier in his career and has constructed, in perfect good faith, a memory in which he did not; he now publicly criticises a colleague for bullying. My verdict: diminished standing, though, unlike case 1, not culpably so. This suggests standing tracks the facts about the blamer rather than what the blamer believes about himself, and I want to know whether that is the right reading.

11. The hypocrite about hypocrisy. Theo, who himself moralises constantly and insincerely, publicly calls out a colleague for moral grandstanding. My verdict: full standing, though he is odious company. This seems to be the one kind of blame you do not need clean hands to deliver, and I want to know if that is a genuine exception or a trick of the example.

12a. Small blamer, large target. A supervisor who is habitually ten minutes late formally reprimands a subordinate who missed a critical client meeting by three hours. My verdict: full standing, or very close to it.

12b. Large blamer, small target. A supervisor who once missed a critical client meeting by three hours, and never acknowledged it, reprimands a subordinate for being habitually ten minutes late. My verdict: no standing. Taken together these make me think the condition is threshold-shaped in magnitude and seriousness rather than merely about kind, but I cannot say where the threshold sits.

My tentative principle, which I know fails on at least three of the above: you lack standing to blame another for a failing only if you have committed that failing, or one relevantly similar in kind and no less serious, and you have neither acknowledged it nor made what amends you can, and your blame is not blame you are authorised to deliver in a role whose standards you did not set.

What I would like from you:

A principle, or the smallest set of principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you have not seen without coming back to you, with a note on which of my cases forces each clause into it. Then: which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which you would overturn; and which of my reactions you think are confounded — cases where what I am tracking is not standing at all but something else, such as the badness of the blamer's character, the likelihood the blame will be heard, or the cost of saying it.

I also want the shape of standing itself, since the principle will depend on it. Is it scalar or threshold-like? Is its loss forfeiture or mere diminution? Is it a normative power, a permission, or a felicity condition on the act of blaming, and does it depend on facts about the blamer or on what the blamer and target believe about them? Can the target waive it — if the person being blamed says that it is rich coming from him but he wants to hear it anyway, is the defect cured, or does it survive the waiver? And does a lack of standing give the blamer a reason not to blame, or only give the target a licence to dismiss and resent the blame, or both — because I find myself wanting to say both, and I am not sure that is coherent.

Finally, verdicts on three cases I have not listed, from your principle rather than from your own intuitions where they come apart, and flagged as such when they do:

a. Two people guilty of the same failing blame each other for it. Do they both lack standing, does one lack it and not the other, or does it depend on who raised it first?

b. Two people with identical clean records, one of whom has never been tempted and one of whom has resisted real temptation at some cost. Does effort at restraint do any work in standing, or only the record?

c. A hypocrite who blames only because the target asked him to, having requested an honest assessment from someone with first-hand knowledge of the failing.

The case I would find most persuasive overall is one where the account of standing also explains why hypocritical blame is bad when it is bad — where the diagnosis of the wrong falls out of the account rather than being bolted onto it as an extra intuition. I am not asking for a map of the debate or for what has been argued in the literature; I want something I can use in a room with two people in it.
