I write literary fiction — two novels so far, both of them about institutions making decisions they can't quite justify to themselves, a university search committee in one, a cathedral chapter in the other — and I take it that this is why I was asked, somewhat against my better judgment, to join the nine-person jury for a prize in translated fiction. Six books, all of us reading them in English, one long meeting day in June, and a chair who has already sent round the procedure she used last year: open discussion, then a show of hands, then a second round of discussion if the vote is close. Two of the jurors read one of the source languages; the rest of us, including me, are reading translations and taking the translators more or less on trust. I also happen to be the person who championed one of the six books during the longlist meeting, which means that anything I say about procedure now will be heard as special pleading, and I need to decide whether the cost of that is worth paying.

One of the other jurors — a former weather forecaster, a man who talks about calibration at breakfast — has been quietly lobbying me to propose something different: that we each fill in a secret written ranking before anyone says a word, that the aggregate be treated as the panel's actual judgment, and that discussion be reserved for the cases where the ballots are already closely split. He cites something he called the Condorcet jury theorem, and he worked the arithmetic out for me on the back of a programme: if each of us independently picks the better book maybe sixty per cent of the time, then nine of us voting will pick it something like seventy-three per cent of the time, and a panel of fifty would be right more than nine times in ten. So, he says, aggregation beats expertise, and the chair's open discussion is exactly what breaks the theorem, because it converts nine independent judgments into one herd following whoever spoke first and most confidently. He is also insistent that if we ballot at all it must be simultaneous and secret rather than a straw poll taken around the table, on the ground that the second person to state a preference is no longer reporting a judgment but reacting to one.

The arithmetic I can check, and the advice about simultaneous ballots strikes me as plainly right. What I can't settle is whether any of it applies to us. Two worries keep circling. The first is that I don't think we have a determinate thing to be right about: we could be estimating which book is best, which will still be read in thirty years, which choice we could defend in public without embarrassment, or which the two jurors who read the originals would prefer, and I suspect our competence differs enormously across those and is not obviously above chance on any of them. The second comes from an acquaintance I met at a residency who studies how groups form judgments. She separates what she calls vertical independence — my judgment is not contaminated by yours — from horizontal independence — our errors do not share a common cause — and her view was that deliberation destroys the first but that the second was already gone before we entered the room, since most of us have read the same handful of reviews, absorbed the same house style, and been trained to admire the same things; and she added, half joking, that where a shared cause pushes a whole field below chance on the question it cares about, the theorem doesn't counsel caution, it counsels something no committee will ever agree to do. I didn't understand that last remark and she had to go.

So: I have one afternoon in which to decide whether to put my name to a procedural proposal, and if so, exactly what to propose. Please don't just lay out the considerations for me — tell me what I should actually do, whether the forecaster's argument carries the weight he thinks it does, what becomes of it once the books have no fact of the matter and the jurors share a formation, and what my acquaintance meant by that last remark. Say plainly where you are reasoning from a contested assumption rather than from something the theorem establishes, but give me a recommendation and the reasons for it, not a menu.
