You are a professor of legal philosophy who has published on statutory interpretation, and you are unusually good at taking a slogan apart into the distinct precise theses it might be expressing. Please think carefully before you write: first brainstorm the full range of readings you can think of, then keep only the ones that do real work, and present those as a menu rather than as a single favoured analysis.

I have no legal training and got pulled into an argument on a local-politics forum that I now can't get out of. Our council passed an ordinance saying that no person may operate a motorised vehicle in Riverside Park. It was passed after a long run of complaints about dirt bikes on the footpaths. Someone then dug up the committee minutes, where a councillor said something like we're only talking about things with petrol engines and a seat, and where an amendment to say so explicitly was proposed and then dropped. Since then the forum has been arguing over four cases: an e-bike ridden slowly along the path; a mobility scooter used by a woman who cannot walk far; a camera drone flown over the park; and a car with a dead battery being pushed across the path by two people. Every time I said the council surely can't have meant to ban the mobility scooter, the same person shut me down with: the text is the law, full stop. If we start asking what they really meant, then judges are just rewriting the rule. He also dismissed the old line about the letter killing and the spirit giving life as nothing but an excuse for activism.

What I want is a disambiguation. What are the distinct precise claims that the text is the law could be making here? Please lay them out as an ordered list from the most trivial to the most radical, and for each one say what exactly it commits you to, whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or something false, and — most importantly — whether it would actually settle any of my four cases or would get my opponent the conclusion he wants. I'd like you to include the awkward versions: a claim about what statutory words mean; a claim about what materials a judge is permitted to consult; a claim about democratic legitimacy, i.e. that only the enacted words went through the process that makes something law; and a claim about which unit counts as the text, given that the words sit inside a section with a heading, inside a larger ordinance about parks, used by speakers of English at a particular date. Please also say what happens when the slogan is applied to itself, since it too is a sentence that has to be interpreted. Finally, tell me which of the readings is doing the real work in his argument, and which of them he would have to abandon if that reading turned out to be false.
