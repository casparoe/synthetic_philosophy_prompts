I am a novelist, three years into a historical novel set in Saint-Domingue, and I want to test whether the master–slave chapter of Hegel's Phenomenology gives me a usable skeleton for the interior architecture of the book, or whether it is the wrong instrument and I should stop trying to make it fit. I am not asking for a summary of the chapter, which I have read, and I am not asking for secondary literature. I want the mapping done carefully, and I want to be told where the theory falls silent rather than pushed to speak where it has nothing to say.

The facts I am working from, so that you need not reconstruct them: the rising began in August 1791 in the northern plain; the revolutionary French state abolished slavery in 1794; Toussaint Louverture rose from the insurgency to effective rule; Napoleon sent an expedition under Leclerc in 1802 to restore metropolitan authority and, with it, slavery; Toussaint was deported and died in French captivity; Dessalines carried the war to victory and declared independence on 1 January 1804; the remaining French population was then killed or expelled; the new state was free of slavery but not of forced labour, the former cultivators being held on the estates under a coercive regime; and from 1825 onward France exacted a large indemnity in exchange for recognition, a burden carried for decades. Hegel, meanwhile, was finishing the Phenomenology in Jena in 1806 and 1807, as these events were being reported in the European press.

What I want from you, in order:

First, discipline about the players. Who may occupy the place of the master — the individual planter, the overseer, the colonial administration, the metropole under the Convention and then Napoleon, or the abstraction of whiteness? The chapter seems to require two self-consciousnesses facing one another, each wanting confirmation from the other; if so, is a colonial system simply not the sort of thing the dialectic is about, and at what exact point does the mapping stop being honest?

Second, the fight over recognition, and the trouble my ending makes. On Hegel's account the one who risks his life wins, and the one who clings to it becomes dependent; but the master then depends on a recognition that is worthless to him, and the bondsman is the one who moves. Apply this to the insurrection. And then: what becomes of the dialectic when the masters are killed or driven out rather than preserved? My characters need recognition from the very people they have destroyed, on Hegel's own terms the recognition of an equal; is 1804 the consummation of the dialectic or the moment it has no way to continue? I suspect the interesting material is there, and I want it stated precisely.

Third, work. Hegel's bondsman shapes the thing and comes to see himself in what he has made, while the master only consumes. My people spend the whole novel on the same fields, first as slaves and then as coerced cultivators of an independent state. Does the chapter have the resources to distinguish the work it praises from work done under the whip or under the state, or is that distinction simply outside it?

Fourth, what the case does to the theory in return. Is the Haitian Revolution a vindication of the claim that freedom is won through the fear of death and through labour, or is it a case the chapter never anticipated — collective, racialised, military, geopolitical, and therefore perhaps a category mistake to read through a story about the education of a single self-consciousness? If it is a mistake, say so plainly. And if it is not, tell me which of the shapes the chapter moves on to best describes an independent Haiti that must purchase recognition from France with money it does not have: is that the unhappy consciousness, or something the chapter has no name for?

Finally, a craft question, since I have to make this into scenes rather than argument. I have provisionally divided the book into three movements corresponding to dependence, the trial of fear, and work, and I want to know which of the three will collapse into lecture if I write it as Hegel would have it, and whether some other lens does the interior work better — recognition treated as a political and legal relation rather than a metaphysical one, for instance, or the anti-colonial tradition's account of the violence of decolonisation. I should also say that I am uneasy, as a novelist rather than as a scholar, about building a book about the only successful slave revolt on a scaffolding whose author was, by reputation, brutally dismissive about Africa in his lectures on the philosophy of history; tell me how much that should weigh with me, and whether it corrupts the application or merely sits beside it.
