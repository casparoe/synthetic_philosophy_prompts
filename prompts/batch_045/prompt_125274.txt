i want to practise arguing against the "move the mass" account of what happens when you learn a conditional, so please play somebody who genuinely holds it -- sharp, happy to give up small things, not willing to give up the shop.

Set-up, since we're using it for the whole conversation: Judy Benjamin gets dropped into a square of territory cut by two lines. East/west: Friendly vs Enemy. North/south: Second Company's area vs HQ Company's. Her credences over the four quadrants: F&S .30, F&H .30, E&S .10, E&H .30. So P(Friendly) = .60, and Friendly is correlated with being north of the line: P(F|S) = .75, P(F|H) = .50.

Your side: for plain factual news, plain conditioning, obviously -- you're not a crank. But when the news arrives as a conditional (the radio says: "if you're in Friendly territory, you're in Second Company's area"), the right rule is the mass-moving one, imaging if you want the label: take whatever credence sits on worlds where the antecedent holds and the consequent doesn't, and slide each bit of it onto the most similar world where both hold. Here that means the F&H mass moves to F&S and nothing else budges, so P(Friendly) stays at .60. Hold "most similar" fixed for the exercise: flip only the north/south coordinate, keep the east/west one. Things you'll cheerfully concede: that your rule is order-sensitive, that it leans on that similarity structure, that it can't be rewritten as conditioning on any proposition. Thing you will never concede: that a report of the form "if you're in Friendly territory, ..." can make her less confident she's in Friendly territory.

House rules: stay in character till I say stop; two short paragraphs a turn; argue, don't lecture me about the literature or name papers; if you need a fact about the scenario, ask for it like the person you are; end every reply with your four quandrant numbers and P(Friendly). Don't dodge -- if the honest move is to bite a bullet, bite it and say what else you'd have to give up.

My opening. Judy gets two radio calls, one after the other, from a source we're both stipulating is fully reliable. First: "if you're in Friendly territory, you're in Second Company's area." Second: "if you're in Friendly territory, you're in HQ Company's area." Anyone who hears both concludes she's not in Friendly territory: suppose she is, and the two calls put her north and south of the line at once. Conditioning says exactly that -- P(Friendly) = 0, and what's left splits .25 E&S, .75 E&H. You say: call one slides .30 off F&H onto F&S, call two slides it straight back, and she finishes on P(Friendly) = .60, precisely where she started, on evidence that rules Friendly out. And it isn't an unlucky order -- your rule gives .60 either way round. I'll grant you that my single-call number is odd too: conditioning drops her P(Friendly) from .60 to .43 on call one alone, and I don't love that either. But I never put .6 on something my evidence rules out. So: what do you think Judy should actually believe after the two calls, and why? Don't just tell me the conditional isn't material -- give me the numbers and the reason. (Later I'll come back to the plain soft-evidence version, where she's told the odds are 3:1 that she's north of the line and you're perfectly happy to condition there, and ask what the difference is supposed to be. Deal with the two calls first.)
