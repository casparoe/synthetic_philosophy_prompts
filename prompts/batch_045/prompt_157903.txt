I'm a senior in high school, and in a government seminar this term we've been arguing about whether a voter is ever morally required to support the lesser of two bad candidates. The discussion kept circling back to one line, which a classmate used to close it: if you don't vote, you can't complain. I'd like to know what, precisely, that sentence could be asserting, because it seemed to be doing several jobs at once. I took it to be saying both that the abstainer has forfeited something, and that staying home does not keep you untainted by a bad outcome. But when I try to restate it as a claim whose conditions I could actually test, it dissolves.

Could you give me a menu of the distinct precise theses the slogan might be expressing, ordered roughly from trivial to substantive to radical, and for each one: what exactly it says, which word is carrying the weight (vote, complain, can't, you), whether it is plausible, and whether it is a platitude, a contentious moral claim, or straightforwardly false?

Some distinctions I've been trying to work with, which you should feel free to correct, reorganize, or replace:

- "Complain" might cover several different acts: saying that an outcome is bad, blaming particular people for it, publicly registering a grievance and demanding a remedy, or merely predicting bad consequences. The slogan may apply to some of these and not to others.
- "Can't" might mean lacks standing or has waived a right, in something like the procedural sense of having failed to file a complaint through the proper channel; or that the complaint is insincere or hypocritical; or that the complainer is partly causally responsible for the outcome; or that the complainer is in no epistemic position to judge it.
- "Vote" might mean cast a ballot for a candidate who could win, or cast any ballot at all including a blank or deliberately spoiled one, or merely be registered and eligible. The slogan also seems silent about people who cannot vote at all: minors, noncitizens, the disenfranchised, the ill, people in districts where the result is not in doubt, and people in elections that are rigged.

The part I actually care about: which of these readings, if true, would support the use the line was put to in my seminar — that is, which would show that a principled abstainer is not morally cleaner than someone who holds their nose and votes for the lesser evil? If none of them would, please say so. It would also help to know whether the readings are independent of one another or whether accepting one commits you to the others, and whether any version of the slogan applies with the same force to someone who did vote, but for a losing minor party.

On confidence: mark which verdicts you would defend firmly and which are genuinely contested or depend on distinctions that philosophers disagree about, and don't flatten real disagreement in order to hand me a tidy list. And if my question is muddled — if the slogan is better understood as etiquette rather than a moral claim, or if "what does it really mean" is the wrong question because the sentence has no determinate content — say that plainly instead of producing a menu anyway.
