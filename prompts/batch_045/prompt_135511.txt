I'm putting together a talk for a philosophy of mind reading group (meets Thursday, I've got three slides left to fill and about fifteen minutes for this bit) on Dennett's ladder of kinds of minds: Darwinian creatures that are just hard-wired by selection to do the right thing, Skinnerian ones that get shaped by reward and punishment out in the world over their own lifetime, Popperian ones that can try candidate actions out in an inner environment so their hypotheses die in their stead, and Gregorian ones that import thinking tools — words, numbers, diagrams, rules of thumb — from the culture around them (I think the last grade is named after Richard Gregory, but I might be misremebering that, so don't lean on it). My flatmate, who does operant conditioning work with pigeons, insists the ladder is monotonic: add a grade and the creature does better in every situation, end of story. I think that's wrong, and I want exactly one case to break it — a scenario where the Gregorian creature does measurably worse than the Popperian one, i.e. the tool-user loses to the creature that only does generate-and-test inside its own head. So: design me a task in which that's what happens, and where the Popperian creature wins by actually using Popperian machinery (simulating candidate actions and rejecting the bad ones before acting), not by getting lucky and not because the answer was already baked into its wiring.

What the case has to satisfy, which is where my own attempts keep falling apart: (a) one paragraph, and something a grad student could plausibly set up for real — a box, a maze, a screen with two shapes, a kid in a classroom, someone in an office — no aliens, no uploads, no philosophy-of-sci-fi furniture. (b) A measurable verdict: solved versus not solved, trials to criterion, pellets or money or effort spent — not just "the creature is confused". (c) The two creatures get identical sense data and identical feedback; the Gregorian's problem can't be that it's misinformed about the task, undertrained, missing the needed action from its repertoire, or too dull to notice it's failing. (d) The tool has to be a genuinely good one — tell me which environment it's well adapted to and why it earns its keep there — so the failure has to fall out of a mismatch between the tool's domain of validity and this particular task, not from me stipulating that the tool misfires. (e) The hard one, and the bit I keep getting stuck on: the grades are cumulative, so the Gregorian creature still has its Popperian machinery and can in principle notice the tool isn't working and fall back on trial and error in its inner environment. Your case needs a principled reason why it doesn't — or why falling back is too slow, too costly, or already infected by the tool. Dennett says somewhere that importing tools restructures the inner environment itself, so that might be your lever, but I'd rather you make the call than have me hand it to you.

After the scenario, do the check: for each of the four grades, say what the creature does in your task and what verdict it gets — I need all four placed, even if two of them fail in the same boring way. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the result if you remove or reverse that feature (does the Gregorian creature win again, or does the Popperian one collapse too?), and how my flatmate would attack the case if he wanted to save the monotonic ladder — I assume he'd say something like "that isn't really a Gregorian creature" or "that just shows the tool is a bad tool here", so give me your strongest version of that objection plus your reply. The thesis of the talk is that the ladder is a ladder of power and flexibility rather than of reliability, so anything that works by defining the tool as useless in this scenario is cheating even if it technically satisfies the spec. And if you don't think (a) through (e) can all be met at once, say which constraint has to bend and what we lose by bending it — a near miss with an honest note beats a clean-looking case that smuggles in the answer.
