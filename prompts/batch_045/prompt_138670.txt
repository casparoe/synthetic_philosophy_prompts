I work in a semantics reading group, and for the past few weeks our supervisor has been handling indicative and counterfactual conditionals the way she handles everything else: case by case, recording whether she takes a sentence to be assertable in a stipulated context, and — where she distinguishes them — whether she takes it to be true, false, or not a truth-evaluable claim at all. I have her verdicts on seven setups and I want to know what she will say about an eighth, and, more usefully, what rule she is running. Her verdicts so far, with the contexts as she stipulated them:

(1) Everyone in the conversation is certain that the night watchman started the fire. Indicative: *If the watchman didn't start the fire, someone else did.* She says this is perfectly assertable and informative — it says the fire had a human cause — and that the antecedent need not be compatible with what we take for granted; we add it and revise around it. Counterfactual twin: *If the watchman hadn't started the fire, someone else would have.* She says this is not assertable in that context and is not equivalent to the indicative: it says something about how the night would have gone, and the nearest candidates are ones in which no one starts a fire at all. Pressed on whether it is false or merely unassertable, she said false.

(2) Dry match in a dry room, unstruck. *If the match had been struck, it would have lit* — acceptable. *If the match had been struck after lying in water all night, it would have lit* — not acceptable. She does not think accepting the first commits her to the second, and she does not treat the escalation as a fallacy.

(3) Same match, but the speaker does not know whether it was struck. *If the match was struck, it lit* — acceptable. *If the match was soaked overnight and struck, it lit* — not acceptable. So the failure of strengthening the antecedent is not what separates the two moods.

(4) A patient's symptoms are on the screen; nobody knows the cause. *If the patient had taken arsenic, he would be showing exactly these symptoms* — acceptable, and she insists explicitly that counterfactual morphology does not require a false, or even a doubted, antecedent. *If the patient were showing exactly these symptoms, he would have taken arsenic* — not acceptable as a counterfactual; the causal arrow runs the wrong way. *If the patient is showing exactly these symptoms, he took arsenic* — acceptable, as an inference from evidence to explanation.

(5) *If it rains tomorrow, the match will be cancelled* versus *If it rained tomorrow, the match would be cancelled.* She hears the second as adding a note of doubt about rain that the first lacks, but she denies that this remoteness is the whole difference between the moods, citing (1) and (4).

(6) *Most of the time, if the alarm sounds it is a false alarm* and *If the alarm sounds, it is usually a false alarm.* She says the *if*-clause is restricting the quantifier here rather than combining with the consequent to make a single proposition, and she thinks the same machinery covers *If the patient is showing these symptoms, he must have taken arsenic.*

(7) She has never, in any of this, assigned a conditional a probability, and she has said nothing about whether a conditional has a truth value at all.

As far as I can tell, the record excludes a strict-conditional analysis of the counterfactual, the rule that counterfactual morphology requires a false antecedent, and the rule that the mood contrast is only stylistic or only a matter of speaker confidence. What it does not settle is whether she is running two semantics — something epistemic and context-set-relative for the indicative, something similarity- or circumstance-relative for the counterfactual — or one semantics with the mood difference located in a modal base and ordering source; whether she thinks conditionals are truth-evaluable at all; and how she understands the probability of a conditional. It also seems to me compatible with everything so far that she holds a truth-functional view of the indicative with assertability handled separately by a robustness condition, though I would be surprised.

Here is the new case. At noon, in a sealed room that no one can now enter and from which no signal escapes, a machine tossed a fair coin. If and only if it landed heads, the machine immediately rolled a fair die and paid out a prize iff the die showed six; if it landed tails, it rolled nothing and no prize was paid. All records were then destroyed. So the coin's landing and the paying of the prize are both epistemically open for us, with prior probabilities of a half and one twelfth, and we have no evidence either way. The coin's landing is causally independent of everything else in the story; the payout, if there was one, is causally downstream of it. Note that the probability of a payout given heads is one sixth, while the probability of the corresponding material conditional is seven twelfths. The sentences I want verdicts on:

(i) *If the coin landed heads, the prize was paid.*
(ii) *It is more likely than not that, if the coin landed heads, the prize was paid.*
(iii) *If the coin had landed heads, the prize would have been paid.*
(iv) *If the coin had landed heads, the prize might have been paid.*
(v) *Either, if the coin had landed heads, the prize would have been paid, or, if the coin had landed heads, the prize would not have been paid.*

What I want from you: first, your prediction of her verdict on each of (i) to (v) — assertable or not, and where she draws the distinction, true, false, or not a truth-evaluable claim — with the reason in each case. Second, the two or three rules that fit the entire record, old cases and new, stated explicitly enough that I can see what each one predicts for each of the five sentences and where they come apart. Third, which of them you would bet on, and why, including what you think she will say about whether the counterfactual in (iii) has a determinate truth value that we simply cannot discover. Fourth, the single further case you would put to her to separate the survivors, with each rule's prediction for it.

My own hypothesis, for what it is worth: that she is running one restrictor-style semantics in which the mood shifts the modal base, and that the (1) pair is explained by what gets held fixed rather than by two different selection functions — but I cannot make that hypothesis predict anything distinctive about (iii) and (v), which is why I am asking. Please answer with the predictions and the diagnosis rather than with a survey of theories of conditionals; I can find the survey myself.
