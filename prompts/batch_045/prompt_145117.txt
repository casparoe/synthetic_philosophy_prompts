And here's the script, anonymised but otherwise verbatim:

**(a)** The Best System Account says that a law is a regularity that shows up as an axiom or theorem of the deductive system that best balances two competing virtues. Simplicity: how few and how short the axioms are. Strength: how much of what happens the system manages to entail. You take the whole mosaic of particular matters of fact, and the winning system is the one that says the most about it with the least machinery. Accidental generalizations drop out automatically. "Every coin in my pocket on 4 March was silver" would have to go in as a separate line in the axioms, and it doesn't entail enough else to be worth the cost in simplicity, so no system we would choose contains it. That is the advantage over the naive regularity theory, which has to count the accident as a law too. Notice also that on the BSA lawhood is contingent: whether a generalization earns its place depends on what the rest of the mosaic is like, so the very same generalization could be a law in one world and an accident in another.

Humean supervenience, as I understand it, is the same doctrine in metaphysical dress: the laws are the regularities that make it into the best system, and everything else — causation, counterfactuals, chance, modality — supervenes on the laws together with the particular facts. The two labels name one view, and it descends from Hume's own denial of necessary connections between distinct existences.

The nomic necessitarian denies all of this. On Armstrong's version, what makes it a law that all Fs are Gs is not the regularity but a relation N(F,G) holding between the universals F-ness and G-ness themselves — a relation between universals, not between their instances. And because it holds between the universals, the law is necessary: given that F-ness and G-ness are so related, every F must be G, and that is true in every possible world in which those universals are instantiated. Accidental regularities are no trouble here either, since "coin in my pocket" doesn't name a universal at all (Armstrong's universals are sparse), so there is nothing for the N relation to relate.

**(b)** The objection is that a description has no oomph. A list of what happens, however elegantly compressed, only says what *does* happen. It doesn't say why it happens, it doesn't tell us what would have happened if things had been different, and it gives us no reason to expect the next instance. The anti-Humean thinks laws have to *govern*: to be something in the world in virtue of which the instances hold. Only then can they explain, support counterfactuals, and license induction.

The Humean can answer all three. Explanation: the best system just is the most unifying description of the world, and explanation is unification; there is no further sense in which one thing explains another, so laws do explain their instances. Counterfactuals: since the system is selected partly for strength, a system that only covered the actual distribution would lose out to one that also handles merely possible arrangements — so the winning axioms tell us what would have happened if this match had been struck. Induction: the BSA in effect solves Hume's problem of induction, because it tells us which regularities are projectible, namely the ones in the axioms.

The one worry I can't see an answer to is simplicity. It looks as though what counts as simple depends on us — our notation, our psychlogy, what we find neat. If lawhood depends on that, it isn't a fact about the world at all, and the anti-Humean's point stands. So on balance I think the argument succeeds, and some form of anti-Humeanism is true.
