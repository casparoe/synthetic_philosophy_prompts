I teach (well, TA for) an upper-level seminar on Wittgenstein and rule-following — 16 students, one 90-minute session, whiteboard, index cards, nothing I'd have to buy or prepare in advance. We've just worked through the chapters where Kripke's skeptic raises the challenge about plus and the quus-like rival, and the room's reaction has been almost uniformly that the whole thing is a parlor trick: of course I meant addition when I said add, and the fact that I have only ever actually added pairs below some threshold is neither here nor there. I want them to feel the pull of the challenge rather than be told it, and in particular I want them to feel why the three escape hatches they keep proposing — that meaning is just a disposition to go on in a certain way, that the right rule is simply the simplest one compatible with the evidence, and that meaning is something I grasp in a flash and then consult — each lose their grip once pressed.

So I'd like you to design a runnable activity for the session in which the students generate the underdetermining evidence themselves, collide with it before it has a name, and only afterwards get the vocabulary (the skeptical argument, the straight solution, the skeptical solution, truth conditions versus assertability conditions). Give me rules, materials, a timeline with minute counts, and stakes or payoffs that don't involve money. A few constraints that matter to me. First, run it on an invented operation rather than on addition — I've been playing with one I call flink, defined over ordered pairs of small numbers, but I'm happy to use yours — so that nobody can lean on prior arithmetic competence; the point is that a finite training sequence is compatible with indefinitely many continuations. Second, build in at least one rival rule gerrymandered onto the class's own data: it agrees with the natural rule on every pair anyone in the room has actually computed and diverges only on pairs nobody has touched, so that no amount of re-checking the record settles anything. Third, also include a rival that is not gerrymandered but equally cheap to state — for instance a rule keyed to how many times the operation has been applied so far in the session, or to who is being asked — so that the take-the-simplest-rule reflex doesn't quietly rescue them.

Fourth, I want a stage where the room has to adjudicate someone else's continuation: I can plant a confederate, or split the room and train the two halves on different example sets, whichever you think works better. The class should be forced either to correct a person who seems to be going on perfectly naturally, or to accept a continuation that looks wrong to them, and then to notice that their own agreement is the only court of appeal left — which is exactly what the skeptical solution offers, and exactly why it initially feels like a swindle. Then the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the skeptical argument, onto why the dispositional and simplicity responses fail, and onto the shift from truth conditions to assertability conditions within a community. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect at least one student to announce that on this view nothing means anything, and at least one to propose a platonic or meaning-grasping answer, and I want to know how to handle both without turning the last twenty minutes into a lecture — and be frank about which part of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and I will simply have to supply in words. I'm thinking specifically of the regress-of-interpretation point (that any further formulation or rule I add, including a written one, would itself stand in need of interpretation) and the normative dimension (that what is in question is what I ought to say next, not what I am likely to say), but tell me if you think something else is the real gap.
