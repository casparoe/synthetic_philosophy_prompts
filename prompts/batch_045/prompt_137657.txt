I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for many years, and I try to treat contested policy opinions as explicit forecasts rather than as tribal markers. The position I currently hold about sex-work policy is more specific than the usual shouting match, and I want it stress-tested.

Here is the view. Start from the observation that the regimes people compare — the Nordic model of asymmetric criminalization, German legalization, Dutch licensed legalization, and New Zealand decriminalization — differ along a dimension I will call the Legibility Mechanism. By that I mean the degree to which a commercial sex transaction becomes a third-party-verifiable record: an enforceable contract, a documented refusal, an employment or tax relationship, an occupational-health interaction, or a licensing inspection. My claim is that most of the real protective effect of these regimes comes from legal legibility rather than from de-stigmatization or from deterring clients. Legibility helps in three ways: it gives workers credible outside options and bargaining power, it creates evidence that makes coercion and theft easier to prosecute, and it opens access to ordinary labor and safety institutions.

On this view, New Zealand decriminalization scores best because it removes criminal penalties from sex workers, brothel-keeping, and living off earnings without requiring sex workers themselves to register or obtain a license as a condition of lawful work. German legalization started well by making contracts enforceable and workers insurable, but partly undermined itself by adding registration and stricter conditions that many marginal workers cannot or will not satisfy. Dutch licensing produces an excellent licensed sector but leaves unlicensed and migrant workers outside its protections. The Nordic model, whatever its intentions, makes transactions less legible by pushing clients and third parties to avoid records, which I think weakens the evidentiary base for finding coercion. My ranking is therefore decriminalization, then legalization-with-licensing, then the Nordic model, with the important scope condition that the benefits concentrate among workers who can actually enter the formal sector — indoor, online, and documented workers — and thin out for street-based or undocumented workers, where formal legibility often does not reach.

I only want the shape of the evidence and the arguments. Tell me what observations, data patterns, or conceptual objections should move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Distinguish defeaters that would falsify the Legibility Mechanism itself, defeaters that would preserve the mechanism but overturn my ranking of the regimes, and awkward results that a defender could absorb by narrowing the scope condition without real loss.

I also want you to be explicit about which counterarguments a committed defender of my position should concede as genuinely counting against it, as opposed to objections that rely on conflating correlation with causation or on comparing formal-sector outcomes while ignoring the marginal workers the formal sector excludes. Finally, audit the position for insulation: has my scope condition — the benefits concentrate among workers who can enter the formal sector — been gerrymandered so broadly that almost any outcome can be accommodated? If so, say whether that is a reasonable feature of a mid-level policy mechanism or a sign that the view has degenerated into unfalsifiability.
