I'm doing a piece for the mag on why smart people make bad long-term calls, and I keep tripping over what "irrational" even means once time gets involved. Going on gut:

1. Al prefers Rome to Lisbon, Lisbon to Reykjavik, and Reykjavik to Rome, and will pay $10 each time to swap. He gets walked around the circle and ends up poorer. Irrational, obviously.
2. Same guy, same cycle, but nobody ever offers him a swap — he books Rome and has a lovely time. Still irrational. The ordering itself is broken; whether some bookie shows up shouldn't matter.
3. Bea in March would rather have a June trip than $500; come June she'd rather have the $500 and pays to cancel. She's coherent at every single moment, her tastes just moved. Not irrational. That's just life.
4. Carla prefers $5 today to $10 tomorrow, but when both are a month out she prefers the $10. Every evening she resolves to wait, every morning she takes the $5, and she can see it coming and still does it. Irrational.
5. Dee has a dial with a thousand settings; each click up is imperceptably worse and comes with $10,000. Every click is, at the moment, her own favourite option. She clicks to the top and wrecks her life. Irrational, and I don't care that each individual step was locally defensible.
6. And here's the one I can't place: same cycle as Al's, except a benefactor pays him $10 for every swap, so he finishes with Rome and more cash than he started. Not exploited, enriched. Still feels off to me.

My stab: it isn't about the money at all, it's about whether there's a plan you could adopt in advance and not later want to deviate from. But I can't make that do all the work. Is there a principle that gets 1 through 5 and settles 6? And what does it say about someone who goes halfway round the pump, cottons on, and then refuses every remaining swap — irrational for the first five hundred trades and rational for the rest, or what? Please also say plainly which of my verdicts you'd overturn.
