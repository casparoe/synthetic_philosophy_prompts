I am setting the take-home final for a final-year seminar on causation, necessity and divine power in classical Islamic philosophy, and I want to build one essay question out of an argument I have just spent the better part of an evening having with an old friend — a physical chemist, unsentimental about theology, but annoyingly good at locating the exact place where a philosophical dispute stops making a difference.

His thesis was that al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, whatever its merits as polemic against the philosophers, is a thesis that no possible course of experience could distinguish from the necessitarianism it attacks, and that the apparent depth of the quarrel is therefore displaced: it is either a covert disagreement about what grounds the counterfactual robustness of the sequences we observe, or it is a verbal dispute wearing metaphysical costume. Two concessions were extracted before we gave up. He granted that the habit or custom of God cannot be a merely observed regularity if it is to do the work Ghazālī needs it to do — namely, underwriting the reliability of the natural order and the sanity of inductively grounded practice. I granted that once the habit is stronger than a bare regularity, it becomes hard to say what work is left for the claim that God creates each effect directly: robustness, wherever it comes from, looks as though it reintroduces under a theological name exactly the necessity that was supposed to have been denied.

I would like to hand that deadlock to the students rather than my own resolution of it. Please write, for a 2,500-word take-home essay:

1. The question itself. It must put the deadlock at the centre and be unanswerable by reciting the three things every student in the room can already recite: the attack on necessary connection in the discussion of causality in the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, the appeal to the habit of God, and Ibn Rushd's rejoinder that knowledge is knowledge through causes, so that denying causes destroys science. Those should be presupposed as background, not rewarded. A student should have to (a) disambiguate the habit into at least two readings and say which one al-Ghazālī is committed to and at what cost; (b) say what the position implies about counterfactuals — whether a divinely guaranteed sequence supports them differently from a naturally necessary one, and whether that difference is observable, explanatory, or merely modal; (c) meet the merely verbal charge head on, by stating what would have to be the case for the dispute to be a genuine one; (d) handle the epistemic version separately from the metaphysical one, i.e. whether the occasionalist can distinguish induction from expectation without created causal powers; and (e) state what would count as evidence against the reading they have chosen to defend. The question should be phrased so that a candidate cannot score well simply by choosing occasionalism or necessitarianism and defending it. If it helps, my friend floated this case, which you may use, sharpen, or replace: a world exactly like ours except that one contact in every million between fire and cotton is not followed by burning, with no further pattern to say which. Is that world possible on each view, and does either view have a principled rather than an ad hoc answer?

2. A model answer of roughly 800 words, written as what a genuinely first-class candidate would produce. It should depend on nothing beyond the primary texts in translation: no secondary literature, no Arabic terminology beyond one or two words glossed in English.

3. A marking scheme out of 20, built around five must-hit points of four marks each. For each point give the move a top answer must make, the one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong, and a separate line on what distinguishes a first-class treatment from an upper-second one — the difference should be in the moves made, not in fluency or length.
