I keep returning to Glaucon's challenge in Book II of the *Republic* — not the scholarly question of what Plato was up to, but the practical one: if I had a ring of Gyges, what exactly would I refrain from doing, and what does the fact that I would refrain tell me? I have been using the ring as an intuition pump rather than as an argument, and my gut turns out to be far less uniform than either the "nothing, if you are rational" answer or the "everything, if you are honest" answer predicts. I would like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to.

Four ring variants, so the cases can be stated precisely:

- **R1, the standard ring:** perfect invisibility, no trace, reusable, and only I have it.
- **R2, the logged ring:** identical, except that an unfalsifiable record of everything done under it is published fifty years later, when I am dead. No punishment, no reputational cost to me, no consequences for anyone I care about. The only thing that changes is that it becomes known.
- **R3, the universal ring:** everyone has one and everyone knows everyone has one.
- **R4, the reversed ring:** I am invisible by default and must actively choose to become visible, and the choice to appear is itself visible to everyone.

My verdicts, under R1 unless stated:

1. Invisible, I pull a child out of the path of a car. Obviously fine. I include it only because a principle should say why this is the easy case.
2. I take small amounts from a large chain: fare evasion, a few items from a supermarket. My gut says not fine, but barely — I would have to be fairly bored. Under R3 it feels no worse, and this bothers me, because every rule I can formulate says it should.
3. I read my partner's private messages because I suspect they are cheating. Not fine — and clearly worse than (2), which I cannot justify by the size of the harm.
4. I copy documents showing that my employer is defrauding customers and leak them anonymously. Not merely permissible; close to obligatory.
5. I follow a public official for a week, learn something private and irrelevant to their office, and use it to pressure them into a vote I am confident is correct. Not fine — and I hold this even though the outcome is better than in (4) and the information-gathering is the same kind of act.
6. I read the answer key the night before a professional exam I would otherwise fail. Not fine. There is no coercion here and nobody wronged in the way (5) has a victim, so whatever drives (5) cannot be what drives (6) — or so it seems to me.
7. I sit in on the confidential deliberations of the committee deciding whether to promote me. Not fine, but I go back and forth, and I do not know why: the subject is me, and I feel an entitlement I do not feel in (3), yet the promise of confidentiality was made to the same kind of people.
8. The case I am least sure of: a friend has refused my help and I think they may be hurting themselves. Invisible, I follow them for a week to check. My gut says this is fine, or at least far more defensible than (3) — but the structure is nearly identical to (3): covert surveillance of someone who would refuse if asked, undertaken for my own reasons.

Two of the variants do real work. R2 changes almost nothing about (2), but moves (3) and (5) decisively toward not fine, and leaves (7) exactly where it was. R4 makes (2) distinctly worse — under the reversed ring even ordinary visible courtesy starts to feel like a claim about me — while leaving (3) and (8) untouched.

My tentative principle, which I do not think survives: a ring use is wrong when it exploits someone's vulnerability in a way they would refuse if asked and knew the facts, and the consequences are irrelevant. Against it: (4) is exactly such an exploitation and I will not give it up; (6) has nobody who refuses in the relevant sense; and in (8) the person might consent if they knew why, in a way that makes my verdict unstable for reasons the principle does not predict.

What I would like from you:

- One or two principles stated precisely enough that I could apply them to a case you have not seen, with each clause tied to the specific case that forces it.
- Verdicts on three cases I have not listed: covertly watching how a babysitter treats my child when they think nobody is looking; using invisibility to run a matched-pair audit of whether landlords or employers discriminate; sitting in on the private session of a committee allocating a scarce resource I am competing for, where the only thing I would learn is how much they like me.
- An honest statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate. I would rather drop a verdict than add an ad hoc clause, so say which one you would overturn and why.
- Some account of how much weight these verdicts deserve in the first place. I am aware that I generated them under a description I wrote myself, that framing effects are real, and that the version of the argument that circulates in my corner of the internet — act as though you are choosing the output of the procedure that is you, so that a good predictor or a simulation of you gets it right — is extensionally identical to just being moral without being remotely motivating. A veil-of-ignorance test gives me different answers from my gut in at least two of the cases above. Which should I trust, and is there a version of this method that is less likely to be an artifact of how I described the cases?

And finally: does any of this touch Glaucon's actual question? My worry is that the ring strips consequences and nothing else, so that a principle arrived at by asking what I would do without them is a description of my tastes rather than an answer to him. If the honest conclusion is that the ring was never doing the work Glaucon thought it did, I would rather hear that than a tidy principle.
