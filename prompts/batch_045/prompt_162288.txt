Question 3. (a) State the principle of the identity of indiscernibles and distinguish it from the principle of the indiscernibility of identicals. (b) Set out Max Black's two-sphere universe as a counterexample and explain what work the example is meant to do. (c) Canvass the main replies available to a defender of the principle and say which one you think is strongest, and why.

Student's answer:

PII says that no two distinct things can have exactly the same properties: if x and y share every property, then x = y. It is the converse of the indiscernibility of identicals (often called Leibniz's Law), which says that if x = y then x and y share every property. The two directions are independent — one can fail while the other holds — and it is only the identity-of-indiscernibles direction that is controversial.

One wrinkle: the principle comes out trivially true if every predicate counts. In a universe containing just a and b, a has the property being identical to a and b does not, so any two things are always discerned somehow. So to get a substantive principle we have to restrict it to qualitative or "pure" properties, the ones that don't mention or involve any particular individual. That's the version I'll discuss.

Black's counterexample asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing at all except two iron spheres, a mile in diameter, two miles apart, exactly similar in every respect, with nothing else anywhere and no asymmetry of any kind. Then every pure property of one is a property of the other: same size, shape and composition, and the same pure relational properties as well — each is two miles from another sphere, each is the only other object in the universe, and so on. So if that universe is possible, the qualitative PII is false. Note that the argument is modal: PII is offered as a necessary truth, so one possible counterexample is enough, and Black doesn't need the spheres to actually exist.

Two things might save the principle. First, one might say the spheres occupy different places, and location is a property, so they were discernible all along. I think that is fine as far as it goes, but it assumes locations are individuals in their own right rather than just relations among things, which is itself contested, so I won't rely on it. The more interesting move is the recent one: even in a perfectly symmetric universe the spheres are weakly discerned, since the relation is two miles from is irreflexive and symmetric, holding between the two spheres but not between a sphere and itself. So each sphere does have a property the other lacks, and PII survives after all.

That leaves the reply that the scenario is not really possible. I don't think this works. We can clearly conceive of the two-sphere universe — I have just described it, and there is no hidden contradiction in the description — and conceivability entails possibility. So the honest conclusion is that Black's argument succeeds and the qualitative PII is false. A weaker principle may survive: perhaps distinct things must always at least be weakly discerned, which is the version that matters in quantum mechanics anyway.
