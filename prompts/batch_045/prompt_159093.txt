I'm a TA for an undergrad course in the philosophy department called something like "Chance, Evidence and Uncertainty" — mostly second-years, about 18 in my section, and the maths background is all over the place: half of them have had one stats course, the other half run away from anything with a sigma in it. I've got one 80-minute discussion section next week and I want to build the entire session around that old chestnut where you draw a chord of a circle "at random" and ask how often it's longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle: three different ways of doing it each look perfectly uniform and they give you 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4. I've been arguing with a friend about this for days and I want the students to hit the same wall he and I hit. He says: fine, that just means "at random" is incomplete, so go up a level — be indifferent between the three procedures and report the average, about 0.361. I said that's just sweeping the bump in the carpet one level up rather than removing it, and he said "ok smart guy, what's the right number then," and I honestly didn't have an answer, which is why I want the session to end there rather than resolve it. The thing I want them to feel is that the move up a level doesn't fix anything either: there's a whole continuum of procedures, not three (you can pick the chord's midpoint at distance d from the centre with a radial density proportional to some power of d, and the radius method and the uniform-in-the-disc method are just two settings of that dial; the endpoints method isn't even a setting of it), and being indifferent over where the dial sits vs. being indifferent over the number the dial spits out give you different answers, and nothing in "choose a chord at random" tells you which coordinate you're supposed to be indifferent in.

Concretely: I need a runnable activity, not a handout. Four tables of 4-5, 80 minutes, seminar room with a projector and a laptop for me but no computers for them. Materials I can definitely get: A3 sheets with a circle of radius 10 cm printed on them, a bag of rice, a box of long wooden matches, 30 cm rulers, protractors, dice, a board-game spinner, and cardboard strips cut to 17.3 cm to use as a go/no-go gauge so nobody has to compute anything. I want them to *do* the three procedures and collide — each station generates its own chords by its own physical routine (rice dropped onto the disc for the midpoint method, two independently randomised points on the rim for the endpoints method, etc.), each station tallies long-vs-short, and then the groups come back to the room with something like 12/40, 20/40 and 10/40 and every one of them is convinced they did it at random. No lecture up front, and please don't have anyone say "Bertrand" or "the principle of indifference" until the debrief — I want the words to arrive after the experience. Then the second half: I want a stage where they're forced to commit to a single number. Our course already runs a weekly prediction ledger scored with a log score for bonus points, so they're used to being graded on calibrated probabilities rather than on being right, and I'd like to exploit that — some payoff structure where picking your favourite answer is punished and hedging is rewarded, so they personally experience the pull toward ~0.361, and then discover the dial and realise the hedge itself was arbitrary. I was toying with a fourth station where they throw matches at a *blank* sheet and only afterwards drop a circular template anywhere on it, so the procedure never gets to "see" where the circle is — I think that's the invariance argument made physical and that it should land on 1/2, but tell me honestly if that works or if it's a distraction. I also want a timeline with minute marks, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific parts of the idea, says what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (rice bouncing off the page, students re-rolling until the chord "looks random", 40 samples not actually separating 1/3 from 0.36, the one student who has heard of this and spoils it), and is frank about what the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud myself.
