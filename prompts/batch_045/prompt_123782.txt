Public-information design has converged, over the last century, on a single conceit: that the things people most need to know can be told to them without words. Transit hubs and airports are signed almost entirely in pictograms; hazard communication on chemical packaging and in workplace safety law is built around a small set of pictograms; pharmaceutical leaflets, appliance manuals and voting instructions are illustrated for readers assumed not to read the language of the country they are in; software ships icon-only toolbars. Set against this, three features of the same practice look hard to reconcile with it. First, the pictogram is very often not permitted to stand alone: the labelling regimes that mandate pictograms also mandate words beside them, a signal word and a hazard statement, or a sentence in the local language, and icon-only interfaces routinely restore the label on hover or long press. Second, the people who design these symbols do not in fact trust them to be understood: they are tested on samples of users, and a symbol that misses the comprehension threshold is withdrawn or redesigned, which treats understanding as an empirical, learnable, culture-dependent achievement rather than a natural transparency. Third, the range of content pictures are asked to carry is strikingly narrow: objects, actions, quantities, directions, and prohibitions of a stereotyped kind. Nobody asks a pictogram to express a conditional, a comparison of reliabilities, a scope distinction, a degree of confidence, or a negation other than the one carried by a diagonal stroke.

The historical ancestor of all this is the picture notation developed by Otto Neurath and his collaborators for popular education and social statistics, in which quantity is shown by repeating identical unit signs rather than by enlarging a single one, perspective and incidental detail are eliminated, numbers are aggressively rounded, and every chart is printed together with a short verbal caption. What interests me is that Neurath himself, as I understand him, held a considerably more austere view than the practice descended from him now expresses. He denied that the notation was a language in the full sense; he held that pictures can never be more than a helper to words; he allowed at most that a limited stock of recurring signs might function as a kind of international jargon, with no grammar; and he insisted that the notation has to be taught, so that what makes a chart legible is visual education rather than immediacy. The same man is of course the author of the well-known anti-foundationalist line about knowledge: no privileged observation language, no given, every statement revisable, inquirers like sailors who must repair their ship at sea and can never rebuild it from the bottom.

I would like the implicit theory of this practice reconstructed. Concretely:

1. Does the practice strictly presuppose a substantive thesis about pictorial content — that what a picture conveys is not translatable without remainder into sentences, and perhaps is not inferentially articulate, so that one can take it in without being able to argue from it — or does it merely fit well with that thesis while being equally well served by the deflationary view that a chart is a set of claims in a compact notation?

2. Which parts of the practice carry which commitments? The comprehension-testing and redesign parts look like they presuppose that picture meaning is learned and conventional; the icon-only signage looks like it presupposes the opposite. Is the practice simply incoherent across its parts, or is there one theory of meaning and understanding under which testing, teaching, and word-free deployment are all what one should do?

3. Does the narrowness of the content — no conditionals, no modality, no uncertainty, no scope, no negation beyond the stroke — show anything about whether a syntax-free notation could ever be a language? And does the practice's reliance on a caption commit it to the claim that pictures are semantically dependent on language, in the way Neurath's own austere remarks suggest?

4. Is there a real tension between the pedagogical aim, which is to make a social fact evident to the eye so that the reader sees a comparison rather than infers it, and the anti-foundationalist epistemology: does letting the eye deliver the fact reintroduce a given, a non-inferential contact with how things are? Or is the tension only apparent, on the grounds that a pedagogical aid is not an evidence-base and nothing is meant to rest on what the picture shows?

5. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories of pictorial meaning, or a purely psychological story about memory and attention, would license exactly the same design rules? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates.

6. Finally, what would have to change if the implicit theory were replaced? If designers accepted the deflationary view that a unit chart is a bundle of assertions and should be answerable to the standards we hold assertions to — exact magnitudes, stated uncertainty, sources, the possibility of being wrong — which features of current pictogram practice would have to be given up? And if instead they accepted that pictures carry irreducibly non-propositional content, what would they have to stop pretending, for instance about the interchangeability of a chart and a table?

Please answer in the form of a reconstruction: state the implicit theory as numbered claims, mark each claim as strictly presupposed, presupposed by a particular part of the practice only, or merely compatible, and then take up the Neurathian tension and the two replacement scenarios.
