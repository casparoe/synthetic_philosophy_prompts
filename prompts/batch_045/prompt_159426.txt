Something keeps coming up in long argument threads in the rationalist-adjacent corners of the internet, and I have never once seen anyone attach a name to it, so I want to know whether the philosophical literature has one.

The pattern: someone proposes an attitude — believe that P, hope for Q, trust this person — and defends it by pointing to what having the attitude would *do*. Sometimes the move is transparently corrupt. Offer me a million dollars to believe that the sky is green, or credibly threaten me until I do, and you have given me an excellent reason to end up with that belief and no reason at all for it; the reasons there are for believing something are supposed to come from the thing believed, not from the payoffs of believing. Fine.

But the same move sometimes looks not merely acceptable but exemplary. If I say that I trust my graduate student with the lab because my trusting her is part of what makes her rise to the occasion, nobody hears that as a corrupting reason. On the contrary, the person who withholds trust on those grounds is the one whose attitude looks defective. Hoping seems similar: "hoping will make you work harder, so hope" is not heard as a corrupt reason for hope — and, tellingly, the reverse is also heard as a real consideration: "hoping will make you complacent, so don't hope." Belief looks sealed against considerations running in either direction: neither the bribe nor the prospect of a devastating truth seems to touch whether the belief is the one to hold. Trust and hope look porous in both directions. That contrast is the thing I want a handle on.

More than a label, I want a carving, and these are the distinctions I keep needing and cannot state cleanly:

- Trusting her does not literally manufacture her reliability; at best it gives a capacity she already has the chance to manifest. My believing that I will recover from the placebo can help produce my recovery, with no prior capacity to elicit. Those two feel different, and I suspect the difference is doing the real work in the cases above, but I cannot say what the difference is.
- Some cases are collective rather than individual: my believing that the bank is solvent does not make it solvent, though enough people's doing so would. Does that belong to the same phenomenon or a different one?
- Intending to do something makes it likelier almost by definition. So if "the effects of the attitude" were admissible in general, intention would come out as trivially self-supporting. Something must exclude that, and I want to know what.
- And there is a looping worry: if an attitude brings about the very property it is supposed to be a response to, then it looks as though it cannot be a response to anything independent of itself, and so cannot be merited at all. That conclusion seems right for the bribe and wrong for the trust case, and I cannot say why.

Please don't give me a reading list; I want the map. Is this one phenomenon with a settled name, or several that I am conflating? Which established concepts are the near neighbours, and how does what I have described differ from each of them? Where are the edges — which attitudes are porous to their own effects, which are sealed, and what predicts the difference? If some part of it genuinely has no name, coin one, define it, and tell me which of my cases fall inside it and which just outside.
