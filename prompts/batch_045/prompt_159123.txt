I'm the screenwriter of a biographical feature, *Rent Strike*, about Hallie Brant — a tenants'-union organizer and legal-aid lawyer in Baltimore who died in 2021. The library that holds her papers (letters, case files, cassette recordings of meetings) has agreed to take the film's production archive alongside them, and next month there is a small ceremony to mark the transfer: her daughter Naomi will be in the front row, the film's producers and principal funders will be there, the librarians and archivists will be there, and so will a room of documentary-studies students. I've been asked to give the eight-minute remarks, and I find that I'm stuck on something that isn't really a writer's problem. The film does three things I can't undo: it invents a composite character, Del, who stands in for three real people; it builds a public speech for Brant out of sentences she wrote in private letters; and it includes a deathbed reconciliation with her estranged brother, which Naomi says never happened and which breaks an undertaking we gave the estate — a "silence rule" — that Brant would never be given a line she is not on record as having said or written.

I've been arguing about this with a friend who makes documentaries, and she put the difficulty in a way that I think is right but that I can't convert into a speech. She said the wrong we might be doing isn't a harm to Brant, since there is nobody left to be harmed, but something more like a betrayal: a misuse of a voice that can't answer back, and a kind of unfairness in the record that is worse than the ordinary kind because it can never be repaired by the person it was done to. Then she said the opposite thing — that a dramatization can be true to someone in a sense that isn't the same as being accurate, and that a scene built out of her own private sentences can sometimes say something about her that the documentary record alone doesn't say. Both halves feel true to me, and I can't see how to hold them at once in eight minutes in front of four audiences who want different things. What I do know is what I'm not entitled to say: I don't want to say she would have loved the film, or that she is somehow present in it, because I don't know either thing and because she is the one person in the room who cannot correct me; I don't want to apologize in a way that turns Naomi's objection into the evening's scandal; and I don't want to flatter the archivists by pretending a fiction belongs in a box of letters for the same reasons the letters do.

Please write the remarks: roughly 1,000–1,100 words, in American English, first person, to be read aloud by me before the film is screened in that room for the first time, so nothing in them can spoil the ending. They need to be genuinely honest about the composite character, about the borrowed sentences, and about the one invented line, without becoming either a confession or a defense brief. They should give the archivists a real answer to what it means to file a fiction beside the papers it was built from. They should be something Naomi can sit through without being made into a prop for my conscience, and something the documentary students will still be arguing about afterward. And they should find some way of honoring Brant that doesn't depend on claiming to know what she would have thought of any of this. I'd like the underlying philosophical problem — what, if anything, we can owe someone who no longer exists, and what a fiction asserts when it puts words in a real person's mouth — to be doing structural work, deciding what I am and am not allowed to say, rather than decorating a speech I'd have written anyway.
