I'm writing the final paper for a graduate seminar on the evolution of communication, and I've been trying to write down what I actually think an honest signal is. We worked through Zahavi's handicap principle, Spence's job-market model, and the distinction between handicaps and index signals, and I came out of it with what seemed like a clean criterion: a display is honest when its price is systematically lower for the individuals who have the trait being advertised, so that nobody for whom the message is false can profit from sending it. Then I started checking the criterion against cases, and my verdicts won't sit still. I'd like help extracting the principle I'm implicitly using — or being told which verdicts to give up.

Here are the cases and my gut verdicts.

1. A peacock's train. Genuine, and the paradigm: the cost of growing and carrying it falls harder on a male in poor condition, so a weak male can't fake it.

2. A mountain guide who, on the first day of a hiring tryout, hoists an unusually heavy pack to the summit. Genuine, and I trust it *more* than the peacock's tail. Nothing is wasted here — carrying heavy loads uphill just is the job — and the absence of waste is part of why I trust it.

3. A young analyst who orders a five-thousand-dollar bottle in a club to signal wealth. Genuine, and here the waste looks load-bearing: plenty of people could spend five thousand dollars on something they actually want, and only a genuinely wealthy person can set fire to it. Remove the waste and the signal dies.

4. The same five thousand dollars spent on a machine that pays for itself within a year. Not a signal of wealth at all: anyone who can borrow against next year's income can buy it.

5. A gazelle stotting in front of a cheetah. Genuine, and nearly costless — a few joules, and the animal would be running anyway. What does the work isn't expense but the fact that a gazelle in poor condition couldn't do it: the display is a sample of the very capacity being advertised rather than a surcharge on it.

6. Two people who spend the same total amount on cars over five years: one rents a supercar for a single weekend, the other drives an old car and keeps it in perfect repair for five years. The second is far better evidence of being reliably well-off, even though the totals match. I've been calling this the amortization clause: the price has to be paid along a path that can't be financed out of the very benefit the signal is meant to secure. A one-shot display can be bought on credit against the reputation it earns; a stream of small payments can't.

7. A professional certification that everyone in the field believes takes two years to earn and that in fact takes a weekend. Not honest — even though it works on employers, and even though many of its holders really are skilled. Compare a genuinely brutal qualifying exam at an institution nobody outside the subfield has heard of, whose difficulty the audience systematically underestimates: honest, but it fails to signal anything to the audience it actually has. I don't like that honesty and effectiveness come apart here, but they seem to.

8. Three cases where someone other than the signaler pays. (a) A manager whose elite MBA is funded by the firm that also benefits from his promotion: I distrust it. (b) A student at a fully funded program where tuition is zero and admission is fiercely competitive: I trust it completely. So it isn't who writes the check that matters, but whether a real differential barrier sits somewhere in the chain. (c) But then: a leader whose reputation for resolve is built out of a confrontation whose costs fall almost entirely on constituents. There's a real barrier here, borne by real people, and I still distrust it — the barrier constrains the wrong person. Call this the no-externalization clause: the price can't be displaced onto parties who don't bear the downside of the message being false.

9. The case I can't fit at all: costs that fall on the audience rather than the signaler. A job candidate who submits a two-hundred-page writing sample to a committee that everyone knows reads ten pages; a theorist whose proof is written so densely that checking it takes a week. I learn something from both. The willingness to impose that burden on a gatekeeper reads as real evidence of confidence, and it's evidence I can't get any other way. But every formulation I've been handed puts the cost on the signaler, and I can't see how to move it to the receiver without breaking the account.

10. A musician who practises six hours a day and whose recording sounds effortless. No signal at all: the cost is real and differential, and nothing in the recording carries it. Contrast the degree from a school known to be brutal: nobody observed the studying in that case either, and yet the credential tells me something. So the requirement seems to be that the audience can get from the display to the cost — not that anyone witnessed the paying.

My tentative principle: a display is an honest signal of a trait to an audience only if (i) its price is systematically lower for individuals who have the trait, (ii) that price can't be displaced onto parties whose interests don't track whether the message is true, (iii) it's paid along a path that can't be financed out of the benefit the signal is meant to secure, and (iv) the audience's estimate of the price is roughly correct. Clause (i) is meant to cover 1–5, (iii) covers 6, (ii) covers 8, (iv) covers 7 and 10. My worries: (ii) and (iii) feel bolted on rather than derived, (iv) makes honesty depend on the audience's beliefs in a way that bothers me, cases 2 and 3 pull in opposite directions about whether waste is required, and nothing in the principle says what to do with 9.

The case I keep going back and forth on: a fashion house that burns its unsold stock rather than discounting it. It's pure waste, it's expensive, and it's differentially expensive for a house that can't move its goods at full price — by my own criteria it should be an excellent signal of scarcity and quality. Sometimes it reads that way to me, and sometimes it reads as nothing but a symptom of a pricing mistake.

What I'd like from you: a principle stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking anyone, with a note on which clause each of my cases motivates; an honest list of the verdicts above that the principle can't keep and which ones you'd overturn; and its verdicts on two cases I haven't listed. I'd be especially interested in what it says about willingness to be arrested at a protest as evidence of how strongly someone cares, and about a journal's punishing review process as evidence that the articles it publishes are good — but choose your own cases if those turn out to be unhelpful.
