For roughly fifteen years I've been reading the rationalist end of the blogosphere on probability, and the local orthodoxy about Bertrand's chord example is that it was dissolved decades ago: the paradox arises only because the question is ill-posed, you supply the missing symmetry, the transformation-group machinery returns a unique answer, and anyone still puzzled is re-litigating a nineteenth-century muddle. I've come to think that's too fast, and I'm deciding how to spend about a year of full-time independent work on the cluster of problems around indifference over continuous parameters. I have no collaborators: solid measure theory, some group theory, enough physics to read the maximum-entropy literature, and no empirical data of any kind.

What I want from you is an agenda, not a survey and not a reading list — I'll do my own literature work, so please don't point me at papers, and don't spend words on who said what.

The candidate lines I'm currently weighing:

1. The residual-mesh problem, as I've started calling it. One natural discretization of the chord problem — pick two vertices independently from a fine finite mesh on the circumference and let the mesh refine — appears to converge to one of the three textbook answers. I want to know how much of that is an artifact of the mesh. If other mesh families (non-uniform, randomized, refined in a different coordinate, refined at different rates in different regions) converge to different limits, then "discretize and pass to the limit" only relocates the original arbitrariness one level down, and the discretization-based defense of a unique answer is circular.

2. The scoring version. Stop asking which answer is correct and ask which distribution one should announce. You must commit to a density over chord length, and then a mechanism is selected from some class — the three textbook set-ups, or something broader like all pushforwards of a uniform measure under a smooth map — and you are graded by logarithmic score. Is there a distinguished minimax distribution? Does it coincide with any classical answer, and what follows if it does not? And if the mechanism is itself drawn from a hyperprior, does the original arbitrariness reappear one level up?

3. The analogy with description-language relativity for universal priors. Bertrand's paradox looks like the continuous-parameter analogue of the fact that a Solomonoff-style prior depends on the choice of reference machine, and the invariance fix looks like it works because continua carry Lie groups of measurable symmetries. Does the fix generalize to discrete hypothesis spaces at all, or is the continuum case misleadingly special? I care because indifference over a scale parameter seems to get smuggled into fine-tuning arguments and anthropic-style reference-class reasoning in exactly the places my crowd takes most seriously.

4. The dissolve option: that "uninformative prior" was never coherent, that the right output is methodological (always elicit a weakly informative prior, treat the symmetry requirement as a demand that you state the physical procedure, stop there), and that the residual attraction of a principled unique answer is a leftover from a bad picture of probability.

Please give me a dependency-aware prioritization. Which of these — or of the ones I'm failing to consider — is upstream of the others, in the sense that most of the remaining disagreement would look different once it were settled? Which is genuinely tractable for one person with my background in a year, as opposed to requiring a literature I can't reconstruct alone or machinery that needs a collaborator? Which is the most overrated, i.e. would consume the year and leave the structure of the debate unchanged? And for each, what concrete evidence I could obtain in that time — a computation I could actually run, a known theorem I could check against, a case that would refute the framing — would move it up or down your list? Fix your criteria explicitly, tell me where they trade off against each other, and say what would make you reorder. I'd rather end the year having settled or cleanly dissolved one thing than having contributed a fifth proposal to the pile.
