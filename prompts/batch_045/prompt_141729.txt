I'm running a 90-minute workshop for first-year PhD students on why we shouldn't just read our intuitions off cases -- the sort of thing Dennett calls an intuition pump, where the set-up nudges you to a verdict before you've noticed you've been nudged. I want a live demo they can feel rather than one they're told about, so I need you to build me the case for it.

What I want is what I've started calling a Janus pair: two short passages, each under about 120 words, describing exactly the same situation, written so that someone who reads only one of them gets pushed toward one verdict on some philosophical question and someone who reads only the other gets pushed toward the opposite verdict. Half the room gets A, half gets B, hands up, and then I put both on the screen.

The hard bit, and the bit I care most about: neither passage may contain a single evaluative, thick or theory-laden word -- no "lied", "negligent", "reckless", "manipulated", "risked"; no loaded verb were a neutral one would do. The flip has to come entirely from structure. The levers you're allowed are: the order the clauses come in; which of two symmetric agents gets to be the grammatical subject; how finely or coarsely the action is described; what you mention at all, and therefore what contrast class you make salient; and the units you give for a rate (per week against per year, say). If you need something else, tell me why.

The question I'd like the pair to bear on is whether a particular outcome was caused by the agent's action or by the background condition already in place -- actual causation, the kind of judgement people hand out in a second and then treat as data. If you think a different question makes for a better pair, say so in a line and use that instead. What I don't want is a moral verdict: the whole point is that this sort of steering happens to questions that feel purely descriptive.

Constraints: something mundane -- a hospital rota, a housing office, a school, a lab, a warehouse, whatever. No sci-fi, no gadgets, no trolleys, no fake barns, no stopped clocks, and not the CEO who harms or helps the environment as a side effect, which half of them will have seen already and which gets its effect from one obviously loaded word.

Because both passages go up side by side at the end, the pair has to survive being seen together: a careful reader comparing them should be able to check they're factually the same and shouldn't feel conned. That's the line between a good demo and a cheap trick.

After the two passages, give me: the question exactly as the students will see it; a point-by-point match between A and B showing which lever is doing the work and why it changes no fact; the verdict each version is meant to pump, and the mechanism -- why that bit of structure pulls that way; your predicted split in a room of about twenty, written down in advance so I can hold you to it; a flat third version with the lever removed and the verdict it pumps, since if it still pumps one of them then the lever isn't doing what you said it was; what to expect from the wash-out check when I show both together, and what result would mean the intuition was tracking the facts rather than the framing; and the strongest thing someone who thinks case intuitions are decent evidence could say in reply, plus whether it actually works.

Start with passage A on the first line -- no preamble, no "here's a case about causation", nothing before it. B straight after it. Then the question, then the rest. I'm running this Thursday morning, so if anything here is underspecified just pick whichever option makes the cleanest demo and note the choice; and definitely keep the apparatus after the passages under about 900 words.
