Last spring my husband got a kidney from a living donor — not from me (I'm not a match, long story), but from someone he climbs with, who just... offered. So I ended up wierdly deep in this world, and somehow that got me a seat on the transplant program's patient-and-family advisory council. Last week the council got handed a proposal to comment on, and trying to explain to the other members why it made me uncomfortable is how I discovered I have no idea what the rule against paying donors is actually *for*.

The proposal, in brief. Nobody is suggesting writing a donor a check. Instead: (1) anyone who registers as an organ donor gets a block of priority points if they ever need a transplant themselves, and can name one other person who gets half those points; (2) you could also buy the same priority points with a large tax-deductible gift to the program's "patient support fund," the one that already pays for donors' travel, lodging, childcare and lost wages; (3) the points would be inheritable by your kids. It's not a market in kidneys, exactly. It's a market in queue position, with a reciprocity story stapled to it.

So here's what I want. Take the existing practice — we forbid paying someone for an organ, and we treat that as one of the few things that's just not up for negotiation — and tell me what it presupposes. Concretely, the parts I keep tripping over: we *reimburse* donors for lost wages and travel and childcare; we pay people for plasma, for eggs, for sperm, for taking real medical risks in early-phase drug trials; in a lot of places we pay surrogates to carry a pregnancy but not to hand over the baby; paired-exchange programs let you donate now and bank a voucher for a friend years later; the recipient's insurer gets billed for organ procurement, so somewhere in the paperwork the organ has a price on it; and our coordinator told us you can register at more than one transplant center and get worked up wherever the queue is shortest, which is a shorter wait you can basically buy if you can afford the flights and a hotel.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of that whole package. Does the ban presuppose that some things are inalienable — that a body part can be given but not sold, and that the sale would be a different act and not just the same act with money attached? Does it presuppose that money corrupts the meaning or the motivation, so paying would drive givers away rather than bring them in — in which case the ban is an empirical bet we could in principle lose? Does it presuppose that money damages consent, so a poor seller isn't really choosing? Or something about what a fair distribution of access to a scarce lifesaving resource has to look like?

And then the parts I actually care about: which of those does the practice *strictly* presuppose, versus which just happens to sit nicely alongside it? Where are the parts pulling in different directions — the inalienability story seems hard to square with paid plasma and paid egg donation, and the "money destroys consent" story seems hard to square with reimbursing lost wages, which is exactly the part that makes donation attractive to someone with a low income. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that two or three quite different theories would all license what we currently do? And what would have to change if we swapped the presupposed view for one of its rivals — say we concluded the only honest worry is that the poor end up as the sellers, and not that commodification is bad in itself: what would our policy have to look like then, and would it look more or less like the proposal I just described? Does that proposal slip past every theory that would block a cash payment, or does some version of the theory catch it?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give me the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question, no recap at the end. I don't need papers or names or citations, I need the argument; I can look up who said what later. And if some part of my description of current practice is wrong, say so in passing and keep going.
