I'm retired now — spent most of my working life as a field ecologist, lots of time fitting models to messy count data, and I've only started reading philosophy seriously in the last couple of years through a little reading group that meets at the library. We wandered into the algorithmic fairness literature, and I ended up defending a position that the rest of the group thinks overshoots badly, so I'd like it stress-tested. The position: the well-known impossibility results — roughly, that you can't in general have a risk score that is well calibrated in every group and also equalizes false positive and false negative rates across groups unless the groups have the same underlying base rate or the predictor is perfect, and likewise that you can't equalize error rates and predictive values at the same time — are doing genuine philosophical work and not just bookkeeping. On my reading they show that a family of fairness demands, each of which sounds like an independent moral requirement when you state it on its own, are jointly unsatisfiable, so that any deployment involves a real moral loss and not merely a technical compromise. My friend in the group says this is a category error: the theorems are arithmetical facts about populations with different base rates, no more morally loaded than the fact that a diagnostic test can't be maximally sensitive and maximally specific at once, and the moral question of what we owe people is untouched by them. I've been holding out that the dilemma survives even if you go causal or counterfactual, because the causal turn just relocates the choice — you now have to say which intervention, which comparison world, which path through the system counts as the fair baseline — and that choice is exactly as contested as the one you started with.

So: what would count against the position I've stated? I mean defeaters rather than objections I can shrug off, and I'd like them ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Some of my own hunches that I want you to grade as real defeaters or as cheap escapes: (1) the impossibility results all assume a fixed action space, and once you let the system defer, abstain, gather more information, or treat people differently rather than just thresholding a score, some of the conflict reportedly dissolves — does that undercut me, or is the conflict just reappearing one level up, in who gets deferred? (2) the criteria are defined relative to a partition of people into groups, and I notice that if you refine the partition far enough the constraints become jointly satisfiable again while the coarse version of them stays violated, which smells like the impossibility is partly an artifact of how the groups are drawn rather than a fact about justice; (3) in a lot of real deployments the outcome label is itself contaminated or partly produced by the decision process — recidivism recorded only for people who were released or policed, a loan repaid only because it was granted — so maybe the whole framework is measuring the wrong thing and the trade-off is an artifact of a corrupt target variable. For each of those, tell me whether a committed defender of my reading would concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it, and say what the view predicts that the deflationary reading doesn't. Also: is my position in danger of being insulated into unfalsifiability, given that any theorem is conditional on assumptions and I can always escape by rejecting one? If it is insulated, is that a problem for a claim of this kind, or is it fine because the claim was never meant to be empirical? The thing that would move me most personally is a small concrete worked case — three groups, an actual-looking risk distribution, thresholds and error rates written out — where the trade-off bites hard and none of my three escape routes helps; if you can't construct one, tell me what that failure means.
