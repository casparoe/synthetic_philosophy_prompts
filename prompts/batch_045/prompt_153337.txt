Last night I had a long argument with a friend — she works in computational linguistics and is the most consistent skeptic about machine understanding I know — and I have been turning it over ever since. She set out the following chain of steps. I think it is in trouble because it seems to prove far more than she wants, but every exit I try either costs me something I would rather not pay, or turns out to be one she has already blocked. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

(1) Anchoring. A symbol-token in a system has content — is about something, refers, can be true or false — only if the system's use of that symbol is anchored in something that is not itself a symbol: the system must be able to pick out its referent non-symbolically, whether perceptually or through some causal relation that is not merely a matter of symbol-to-symbol rules. This is the grounding requirement as it is usually stated.

(2) No proxies. Anchoring cannot be inherited. If A's token for dogs is anchored, and A's activity causes a corresponding token in a system S whose handling of that token is fixed purely by relations among symbols, then S's token is unanchored all the same: causal descent from an anchored token is not anchoring. She says this is exactly what separates grounding from reference-borrowing.

(3) The machines. A large language model is trained on text. Every element of its training signal is a symbol-vehicle, and its objective is to predict symbol-vehicles from symbol-vehicles. Nothing in the training loop puts it into non-symbolic contact with anything beyond its own internal states. So none of its symbols are anchored.

(4) Therefore its outputs are not about anything. When it emits the string "the capital of France is Paris," that string no more refers to Paris, or is true, than the digit on a calculator's display is about the number five.

(5) But the tracking is real. It produces sentences it has never seen; those sentences can be checked against the world and come out true; it can be corrected and it revises; and its failures are failures about the world rather than about strings — it mixes up two cities, not two symbol-types. Something with no content at all cannot be systematically right or wrong about France.

(6) So one of (1)–(3) is false. Give up (1) and the grounding requirement was never a requirement on meaning in the first place. Give up (2) and content can be inherited through a chain, in which case the model inherits whatever content the authors of its corpus had and (4) collapses. Give up (3) and you owe an account of what non-symbolic contact a text-trained model actually has.

(7) And here is where I think the argument overreaches. (1) and (2) are not restricted to machines. Most of my own vocabulary is acquired from testimony: I have never had non-symbolic contact with a quark, with Byzantium, with the interior of a star, or with the heat death of the universe. By (1) my tokens for these are unanchored; by (2) I cannot borrow anchoring from the physicists and historians who did have the relevant contact. So a large part of what I say — everything unobservable, abstract, merely possible, or future — comes out contentless and not truth-evaluable. That cannot be right. So (1) or (2) has to go; and whichever one goes, (4) goes with it, and I no longer see any formulation of the grounding requirement that bites on the model and spares me.

Her replies, briefly, so you know which ground is already occupied. On (5): a barometer tracks storms without being about them, so reliable covariation with the world is not yet content — the model is a very elaborate barometer. On (7): she half accepts the consequence and calls it the right result, saying that my talk of quarks gets its content only by deference to people who do have contact, and that deference is a social relation a model does not stand in. I find the second reply stronger than the first, but I cannot see what it costs her, or whether it survives being stated precisely.

There is one further wrinkle she raised that I could not settle for myself. If you train the same kind of architecture on text together with images, does anything change? She says no — to the model a picture is just another symbol-vehicle. I want to say that the causal route from the world into a photograph is different in kind from the route from the world into a sentence. How this gets settled seems to matter for how (1) should even be worded, before we argue about whether it is true.

What I want from you: which step do you reject, and if you reject none, which premise is quietly doing more work than its statement suggests? My own guess is that there is an equivocation somewhere between (2) and (3), between "the inputs are symbol-vehicles" and "the causal chain to the world is broken," but when I try to write out the repair, my version lets in far too much — books, dictionaries, thermostats, and map legends all start to look like they mean things. So please say why the step you reject looked fine, and price the alternatives: what exactly do I lose if I keep (1) and drop (2), as opposed to keeping (2) and restricting (1) to some subclass of symbols, observational ones say? If holding (1) and dropping (2) commits me to inherited content, I also want to know whether inherited content comes in degrees or is all-or-nothing, and whether it commits me to saying something about artefacts.

If it helps to know my sympathies: of everything above, I would most like to keep the thought behind (1) — that there is a real difference between a system that has met the world and one that has only read about it — and I would rather give up (2) than that. But I want to be told what I am paying.

One last thing about the shape of the answer. Please keep separate what is settled among people who work on these questions from what is a live controversy, and do not present a contested position as the received view. Where you rely on a distinction that has a standard label, name the label; but I am not asking for reading recommendations, and I would rather have your reasoning than a survey.
