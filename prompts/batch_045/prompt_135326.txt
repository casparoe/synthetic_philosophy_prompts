I write novels — the kind that get shelved near the literary section but keep sneaking in a physics argument — and I'm stuck on a scene between two characters. One is a physicist who, after her mother dies, starts talking like a mystic: she says the world isn't made of separate things, that we are literally one object, and that physics proved it. The other is her old PhD supervisor, a very dry, very hard-SF type, who snaps back that it proved nothing of the sort, that all it did was kill off local hidden variables and says nothing whatsoever about whether the universe is "one thing." I honestly can't tell which of them I'm meant to agree with, and I don't want the scene to pick a winner by fiat.

The line at the centre of it — "Bell's theorem proves that nature is nonlocal" — is one I've heard a million times, including from a novelist at a reading who used it to justify a whole book about interconnectedness, and I've realised I've been repeating it myself without knowing what I meant. So: what are the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making? I want a menu, not a verdict on the best one. Some readings I can half-guess at and want placed and graded: that influences travel faster than light; that no theory which keeps causes local to their effects can recover the predictions; that you can't build a machine that sends a message; that the *state* of a composite system isn't fixed by the states of its parts plus their locations, so separability rather than locality is what goes; that the whole thing only bites if you assume the experimenters' settings are statistically independent of the hidden variables, so a theory could buy back locality by paying for it somewhere else; and that the correlations don't need a causal story at all and asking for one is the mistake. For each, say whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, contested, or false — and importantly, which ones would actually let my mystic's claim survive her supervisor's objection, and which would make her just wrong, or make *him* the sloppy one.

One more thing, because this is the part I care about: a friend in my writers' group, trying to help, drew a distinction between what he called *handle* nonlocality and *bookkeeping* nonlocality — with a handle, you can grab something here and wiggle something there, whereas bookkeeping means the weirdness only shows up when the two labs bring their notebooks together and compare, and nothing either side does locally changes anything anyone can detect locally. He claims the supervisor's "you can't signal" point only kills the handle version and leaves my mystic's version standing. I don't trust it, but I also can't tell whether it's a real distinction in the physics, whether it collapses into something physicists already have a name for, or whether it's two hats on one idea. Also, does any of this change if a character is the sort who thinks the wavefunction never collapses? I've been told the experiments are settled now and I don't need the history, and please don't send me papers — I won't be able to find them and I'd just be guessing at titles. Mark, next to each reading, how confident you are that it's what people actually mean when they say the line versus a reading you're reconstructing for completeness, and how confident you are in the verdict, since I'm going to put some of this in a character's mouth and don't want her sounding certain about something that's genuinely up in the air. And if the framing is off — if "nonlocal" isn't doing the work I think it's doing, or if my friend's two kinds of nonlocality are really one — say so plainly instead of handing me a tidy menu for a distinction that isn't there.
