I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so apologies in advance if I mangle the vocabulary — I have about four chapters of a draft that depend on an idea I can't tell whether I'm getting right or badly wrong. My protagonist is a woman in her late thirties who walked out of a small town in the Eastern Cape eighteen years ago and never came back, and now has to return because her mother is dying. What I want is for the town's coldness toward her to be something more than small-town resentment: I want it to be a moral logic in which leaving isn't just rude, it's a kind of damage to who she is, maybe something she has to be re-made into a person again. I don't want to write the version where African communitarianism means everyone obeys the chief, which is the version I'd have written by instinct and which a reader could smell a mile off. So I picked up a secondhand introduction to global ethics and read its chapter on ubuntu. Here's roughly what it says: ubuntu (a Nguni word, the slogan means something like a person is a person through other people) is the African mirror image of Western individualism. Western ethics starts from the isolated rational agent and then has the awkward job of explaining why that agent owes anyone anything; ubuntu reverses this and holds that personhood isn't something you have at birth but something the community confers on you as you're drawn into it, and that it comes in degrees. A newborn is therefore, strictly speaking, an it rather than a person; and someone who goes on violating the norms of the community can lose the standing they had earned — the author calls this the recognition veto — remaining biologically alive while no longer counting as a person. He pushes this further: since there is no individual prior to the group, ubuntu has no space for human rights, which are precisely claims an individual makes against the collective, and in their place it offers a straightforwardly consequentialist test, maximize the cohesion and wellbeing of the community as a whole, which is why it slides so easily into the African socialism of Nyerere and Kaunda and why post-apartheid South Africa preferred truth and reconciliation to trials. He isn't hostile to it, and ends by calling it a serious rival to rights-based liberalism. But he also says the fatal flaw doesn't take much digging to find: if personhood just is full membership of your own community, then ubuntu has nothing to appeal to when the community is wrong, and the best-integrated member of a village that hates its neighbours is, on this view, the most fully a person. There's a quick throwaway reductio too — taken literally, he says, the graded view would make killing a newborn no worse than killing a goat, which shows it can't be right.

So: how much of that would someone who actually works on this accept, where is it the pop version, and where is it just wrong? I'd like a sentence-by-sentence-ish audit, because I can't tell which parts to build a plot on. Concretely, the things I care about: does the harsh part really work that way — is personhood in the literature genuinely revocable, or is that the author stretching a claim about moral failure into a claim about other people's say-so (and is recognition veto a real thing anyone calls it, or something he made up)? Is ubuntu really a maximizing, aggregate-welfare view, or is that a category error about what sort of principle it is? Is the no-room-for-rights bit a refutation or just one well-known objection that people have answered — and does the consequentialist reading even belong to the same writers as the personhood stuff, or has he stitched two different positions together? Then the two that matter for the book specifically: would a defender of ubuntu say my protagonist has lost something real, or that the community has failed her; and does the framework have any way of saying a community can be wrong, given that a lot of the criticism I've half-heard is about exactly that, about it being used as a shibboleth to enforce conformity and shut up women and dissenters? I'll admit I half want the harsh version to be right, because it gives me a much better third act, and I'd rather know if I'm wishing it into the source. Also, if it helps: my friend who grew up in Soweto read the page over my shoulder and said it sounded like it was written for foreigners but couldn't say why, and I have a vague memory of a line in the epilogue of South Africa's 1993 interim constitution about needing ubuntu rather than victimisation, plus two names in my notes, Menkiti and Gyekye, listed as the strict one and the soft one, which may be nonsense. Please don't give me a reading list or papers to chase, I only need to know whether the moral logic I'm putting in these characters' mouths is one anybody would recognise, and where I should quietly take the author's confidence down a notch.
