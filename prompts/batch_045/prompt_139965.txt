Six weeks ago I took a job as the only person on a small research project building a tool that does something I now think may be impossible in principle. Users describe a scenario in ordinary language; the system is supposed to decide whether the scenario describes a genuine way the world could have been, and if it does, compile it into a world that can be simulated and explored. My first deliverable is the acceptance criteria: the rule that says which submitted scenarios count as possible. A colleague on the project thinks the rule is trivial — anything you can describe without contradicting yourself is possible. I have found that I disagree with him, and that my own disagreements with myself are worse.

Here are my verdicts on scenarios people have already submitted to our test set. For each I can picture the scenario; the question is which of them that settles anything.

1. A world in which the bright thing in the west just after sunset and the bright thing in the east just before sunrise are two different objects rather than one. My verdict: not a real possibility. This is a description of someone being mistaken, not of a way things could be.

2. A world in which the clear, drinkable liquid that fills the lakes and comes out of the taps has an entirely different chemistry from H2O, and in which everyone uses their word for water for that liquid and means by it whatever plays that role in their environment. My verdict: a real possibility. But I cannot say what makes it different from (1), and that bothers me more than any of the others.

3. A world that is particle-for-particle and field-for-field identical to ours, in which the same words get spoken, the same injuries get flinched away from — and nobody is conscious of anything. My verdict: I am inclined to call this possible, and it is the verdict I trust least, because I suspect I only got there by leaving something out of my description.

4. A world in which the Goldbach conjecture is false. My verdict: my being able to describe this is not evidence of anything. I have no idea whether the conjecture is true.

5. A world in which a man travels back in time and kills his own grandfather before his own parent is conceived. My verdict: not a possibility, even though I can tell the story fluently. It feels different from the others — as if the story cannot be finished rather than as if it describes something ruled out.

6. A world that came into existence five minutes ago, containing all of our memories, all of our fossils, all of our photographs, and all of our half-finished meals. My verdict: I refuse to treat this as a real possibility. But I also cannot find a single inconsistency in it, and every rule I have tried so far says it passes.

7. A colour that is not on our spectrum and is not a blend of two colours that are. My verdict: I genuinely do not know, and I go back and forth within a single conversation.

The only rule I have been able to formulate, and it comes from the tool rather than from anything I was taught: a scenario is a guide to possibility only if it is rendering-closed — that is, only if it can be specified completely enough that in principle a simulator could run it without ever having to ask a follow-up question. On that test, (1) fails, because to run it the simulator must be told which body is which and the request collapses into a different scenario; (2) passes; (3) passes, but only because the simulator is being asked to track physical quantities and nothing else, which I suspect is cheating; (4) and (5) fail; and (6) passes, which is the result that makes me think the test is wrong.

So my questions are these. Is there one principle about when imagining something is evidence that it could be so, which delivers my verdicts on (1) through (7), and in particular which treats (1) and (2) differently without ad hoc special pleading? If no such principle exists, which of my verdicts should I abandon? Is my rendering-closure test salvageable, or does the fact that it clears (6) and clears (3) only by tracking less than everything show that it is the wrong kind of test altogether? And whatever principle you end up with, what does it predict about two cases I have not put in the test set: a world physically identical to ours except that every object is shifted three feet to the left, and a world physically identical to ours in which my colour experiences are swapped relative to everyone else's?
