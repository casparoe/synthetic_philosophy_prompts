My friend and I got into a arguement last night that went on way past the point where either of us was enjoying it, and I want to settle it with a designed case instead of more hand-waving. Here is roughly where we ended up.

He says the exclusion argument only looks scary because we let the physical cause be the total, maximally specific state of the brain. Once you require that causes be *proportional* to their effects — a cause shouldn't specify more than the effect does — and once you remember that causal claims are really contrastive (why this rather than *that*?), the mental state and its neural realizer stop being rivals. The realizer is wildly disproportionate to something like reaching for a mug; the mental state is the right grain. No exclusion, no systematic overdetermination, no epiphenomenalism, done by lunchtime.

I said proportionality picks a grain, not a level. Turn the dial up and it doesn't politely stop at the folk-psychological description; it keeps going until you have some coarse state that loads of different experiences and loads of different neural configurations could all realize, and that state can be specified in either vocabulary. And the exclusionist can just rebuild the argument with a *proportionate* physical cause — the kind of state a working neuroscientist would actually cite, not the whole microstate and not some disjunction cooked up to win — and then the dissolution doesn't touch it. He said that's gerrymandering. Circles were gone in.

So build me the case that settles it. One paragraph, ordinary human setting — a ward, a kitchen, a warehouse floor, whatever — no sci-fi, no demons, no perfect predictors, nothing that needs a footnote. Constraints:

The physical cause that does the excluding has to be the state that *realizes* the mental state, not some upstream neural event the mental state causes. If you hand me a chain where the decision causes the motor preparation which causes the arm movement, I will just say: fine, no exclusion, everyone already agrees. The problem only starts when the excluder is the realizer.

Overdetermination and identity both have to be off the table. The mental state's only way into the story is by being realized by the physical goings-on, so there is no second independent route to the behaviour; and the mental state has to be multiply realized inside the case — two agents, or one agent at two times, or a nearby variant you spell out — so that the mental state just is that physical state is not available to either of us.

The isolating move, which is the bit I actually care about: hold the grain fixed and vary only the vocabulary. I want a mental candidate cause and a physical candidate cause at the same grain — equally specific, equally proportionate by his own test, answering the same contrastive question about the same behaviour — and then watch whether his criterion can tell them apart. If it cannot, say so plainly. If it can, say what is doing the discriminating, because I suspect it is something he has not admitted into the account.

The mental state has to be doing real explanatory work, so nobody can just shrug and take epiphenomenalism: the behaviour should show a pattern across the variations in your case — had the content been different, the behaviour would have been systematically different no matter how the state was realized — and the physical vocabulary should not itself track that pattern. That is the invariance-across-realizers test: a property earns causal keep by marking a pattern that holds across all its realizers and that the realizer-level description misses.

And his test must not simply hand him the win. Applied honestly and charitably to your case, the proportionate-cause-plus-contrastive-question test has to either (a) select a candidate that is specifiable without any content, meaning, or what-the-agent-takes-things-to-be vocabulary, or (b) select several equally proportionate candidates at once, in both vocabularies, so that it fails to uniquely select the mental one. Tell me which happens and why the winning candidate is not a gerrymander — he will pounce on anything disjunctive or cooked up, and he will also pounce on anything that smells like the total state of the universe, so keep the winner respectable.

Then run the three accounts through the case: the exclusion argument with each of its premises checked off against the specifics rather than asserted, his proportional difference-making account, and the invariance-across-realizers account. For each one I want what it says about the case and why, not just its verdict — and if you catch yourself writing and this is causally sufficient without showing why, you have cheated.

Finally: which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the three verdicts if you remove it, the sharpest thing my friend could say against the case (be mean, give the strong version, not the straw man), and whether the case shows his dissolution is sound-but-not-enough, secretly an identity theory in disguise, or just question-begging about which levels get to be causal. I would like to know whether I won last night. If two of the constraints pull against each other, say where the tension is, pick the version you think is most honest, and tell me what you gave up.
