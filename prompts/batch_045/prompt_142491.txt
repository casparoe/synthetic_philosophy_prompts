I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy writing on collective harm, and I've been asked to convene a small adversarial collaboration at our department's ethics workshop between two camps that have spent a decade talking past each other, and to produce the joint document they would sign. The first camp I'll call the difference-makers: they hold that the reason against an individual contribution to a collective harm is a function of the expected difference that contribution makes to the outcome, so that the familiar inefficacy thought is answered by insisting that what matters is expectation rather than certainty, and that in threshold cases your uncertainty about whether this is the pivotal act does the real work. The second camp I'll call the participation camp, and I suspect it is really a coalition of at least two wings — one that grounds the reason in membership of, or complicity in, a practice that is collectively harmful, and one that grounds it in something like universalisation or the expressive character of the act — so I want to know whether the coalition survives contact with the cases. To keep us off well-worn ground I've stipulated a set of institutional details rather than relying on slogans. First, the binned-purchase case: a regional processor orders birds in blocks of 500, rounds up, destroys the residual each quarter, and resets its counter, so that a single skipped chicken changes only how much is wasted unless it happens to be the five-hundredth. Second, the market-incidence case: in a large competitive market my abstention lowers demand infinitesimally, the price falls infinitesimally, and other consumers absorb almost all of it, so that the difference I make depends on whether we hold others' behaviour fixed or evaluate against the new equilibrium. Third, the public-counter case: the processor publishes its counter in real time, so I can know that I sit at position 3 of 500 and that 497 others would have to abstain with me. Fourth, the substitution case: the portion I decline is certain to be bought by somebody else. Fifth, the offset-lottery case: having to fly for a family emergency, I can either pay for the verified removal of one tonne with certainty, or buy a ticket costing one thousandth as much that has a one-in-a-thousand chance of triggering the removal of a thousand tonnes, with the same expected removal and money left over for other causes. Sixth, the resolution problem: the expected difference my flight makes to global mean temperature is around a trillionth of a degree, which is below the resolution of any climate model, any policy threshold I can identify, and arguably any probability estimate I can make with precision. And seventh, the shrinking-practice case: the same act performed when the practice has ten million participants and when it has ten, holding my own behaviour fixed.

What I want is the document itself, not a report on the literature — please don't pad it with citations, since both camps know the debate and we only have ninety minutes. Give me the claims both sides can sign, including about the cases above and about what would have to be true for the inefficacy thought to bite at all; then the disagreements, each stated twice, once in the wording the difference-making side would accept for itself and once in the wording the participation side would accept for itself, with no strawmen and nothing so thin that the other side could sign it too; then a verdict, for each disagreement, on whether it is substantive, verbal, or partly each, and if verbal, what the real question turns out to be once the words are discharged. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, and for each one the case, argument or empirical finding that both sides agree would bear on it — ideally one of mine or a sharpened variant of it — together with each side's prediction written down in advance and an honest note about how each side would update if the case came out against them. I especially want the cruxes to include at least these: whether the offset lottery is an acceptable substitute for restraint, and if not, whether that is risk-aversion about moral goods or something the difference-making framework cannot represent; whether the stringency of the reason scales with the expected difference and hence with the size of the practice, or stays roughly invariant; whether the reason survives known non-pivotality and certain substitution, and whether the participation camp gives the same answer to both; whether counterfactual difference is measured against others' behaviour fixed or against the new equilibrium; whether there is a threshold of moral significance below which tiny expected differences stop generating reasons at all; and whether, once the counter is revealed and I provably made no difference, there is any remainder — anything to regret, disown, or owe — or whether the ex post fact fully exculpates. Please also tell me plainly where the participation camp splits, if it does, and which of my cases pries the wings apart.
