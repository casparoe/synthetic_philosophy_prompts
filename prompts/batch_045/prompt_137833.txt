I'm a retired natural scientist (I spent my working life in experimental chemical physics, mostly spectroscopy) and I've gotten into philosophy only recently. Right now I'm stuck on Adam Smith's story about sympathy and the impartial spectator. The way I've understood it from a couple of popular summaries is this: when I judge my own conduct, I should imagine an actual neutral bystander who is watching me, feel whatever sympathetic reaction that bystander would feel, and then take that feeling as my moral verdict. Since I obviosly can't hire a neutral observer to follow me around, and since I like formal models, I've started building what I call a spectator function: feed in a description of an action, and get out the aproval rating of a perfectly informed, perfectly rational, perfectly sympathetic observer. Then I plan to decide by maximizing expected approval, integrating over the spectator's subjective probability distribution about consequences.

I've ran into specification problems. First, whose probabilities should the spectator use: my own uncertain personal credences, the true objective chances (assuming those exist), or the spectator's own perfectly rational posterior? Second, if the spectator is defined as perfectly sympathetic, doesn't its approval just reduce to some kind of utilitarian utility function? If so, which one: total happiness, average happiness, or maybe a priority-weighted sum? And third, should I treat sympathy as emotional contagion, so the spectator literally feels my victim's pain, or as something more cognitive? Am I on the right track here, and if so, how should I settle these parameters? I'm happy to treat the impartial spectator as a kind of Bayesian decision-theoretic agent; I just need to know what its utility function and priors should be.
