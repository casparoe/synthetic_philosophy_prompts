I'm in my last year of med school and I've been trying to write down my actual position on what a disease even is, because I keep saying things on the wards and in our ethics seminars that I suspect don't all fit together. Here's roughly where I've landed, and I'd like someone to audit it before I make a fool of myself presenting it.

(1) Disease is a value-free, factual matter: something is a disease just when some part or system of the body fails to perform its biological function, where I mean by "function" what that part actually contributes to survival and reproduction. I don't want the statistical-typicality version, because it seems to imply that if literally everyone in a population had a parasite or tooth decay, none of them would be diseased, which I think is backwards. I also don't want the "whatever evolution selected for" version — reconstructing historical selection pressures is mostly speculation and I don't want disease facts to depend on prehistory. (2) Pregnancy is obviously not a disease, and neither is being post-menopausal, being on long-term contraception for endometriosis, or being celibate — but my definition in (1) seems to make the last three count as reproductive-system diseases, since in each case the reproductive system isn't contributing to reproduction. Meanwhile blocked tubes and severe oligospermia are diseases, and IVF is treatment rather than enhancement, and I want to keep all of that. (3) Whether something is a disease shouldn't depend on what environment the person happens to be in: a friend's kid has congenital insensitivity to pain, the family lives in an extremely supervised setup, and she half-jokes that in that environment it isn't a disease at all — I think that's wrong, I want to say it's a disease full stop. But I also really don't want to say that every obese or type-2-diabetic patient has a disease just because our environment changed faster than our bodies did; my gut says those are problems with the environment, not with the people, and I notice my own account doesn't obviously let me say both. (4) Separately, I think classifications have consequences and that this is a legitimate reason to prefer one over another: it matters to me that gender incongruence is a diagnosable condition so people can get care covered, and I'm wary of child psychiatry categories like oppositional-defiant-type diagnoses because the label follows the kid around for years. I don't think homosexuality being in the DSM until the 70s was a values thing though — I think those doctors were just factually mistaken, since it isn't a malfunction of anything. (5) I don't think disease is a natural kind with an essence — cancer, depression, hypertension and a fractured femur have nothing interesting in common — I think it's more of a family-resemblance concept, and part of me worries that that hands the whole game to the normativists. (6) I think fibromyalgia and ME/CFS are real diseases even though we can't point to a lesion or a mechanism yet, and it annoys me when clinicians imply otherwise.

So: go through this and tell me which of the six actually conflict with each other and which only look like they do. Rank the real tensions by how severe they are, and for each one say exactly what I'd have to give up and what the cheapest repair is — I don't want "go hybrid" as an answer, I want to know what I'd be committing to. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please argue it out rather than giving me a reading list or telling me which philosopher holds which view; I can look that up later.
