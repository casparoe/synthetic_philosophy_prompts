hey — I need to borrow your brain on a teaching problem rather than on the substance itself, if that's a distinction you'll let me have.

I'm a PhD student in philosophy (rational choice / decision theory-ish) and I've been asked to run a 75-minute seminar for first-year public-policy masters students in their cost-benefit analysis course. They've had about two weeks: they can build a small decision tree, discount, do a basic NPV, and they've absorbed "sunk costs are irrelevant" as a slogan they can recite but have never seen stress-tested. The course director wants them to leave able to look at a real case of an agency continuing to fund a failing project and say something more useful than "fallacy" — without walking out thinking the slogan was a lie.

I've ran this twice and it went wrong in two opposite directions. First time I did the core fallacy properly and then bolted the exceptions on at the end; about half the room concluded the whole thing was sophistry dressed up. Second time I led with the interesting exceptions, and they could list three reasons honoring sunk costs can be rational but couldn't tell me which one, if any, applied to a case I put in front of them afterwards. So what I want is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation.

The substance I want them to end up able to handle — some of this is stuff from my own third chapter, so don't worry about whether it's standard, just take it as given:

- Whether a cost is sunk isn't a property of the money; it's a property of the cost relative to a decision and to what the feasible alternatives actually are. Ninety-five percent recoverable isn't the same animal as zero percent recoverable, and "the grant can only be spent on this" changes the alternative set rather than making the money free.
- A forward-looking theory of reasons can still have perfectly respectable things to say about past spending, because the past can be *evidence*: what my earlier self chose tells me something about what my earlier self knew, and I've lost access to most of that (the files are gone, the meetings weren't minuted, the gut feel is not reconstructible). On my "amnesia" argument, honoring the expenditure can be rational deference to a signal I can no longer read directly — and it's strongest exactly when my current evidence about the project is thin, not when it's thick. It should collapse into plain irrationality when the signal isn't there.
- Separately: for an agent who can't literally bind their future self, treating sunk costs as reasons can be a poor man's precommitment device — you get some of the benefits of a commitment strategy you can't otherwise implement.
- And the observability dial: whether anyone else sees the decision and updates on it (a state funder deciding about the next tranche, a developer waiting to build) is doing real work, and it's a different mechanism from either of the two above.

Don't explain any of that to me. Tell me how to get it into their heads in order:

What has to be in place before the exceptions even sound like answers to a question rather than like excuses — what depends on what, and why does that ordering specifically avoid both of the failure modes I described? I'm guessing the causal/evidential distinction has to be secured before the amnesia thing can land, but I'm not sure where to put "sunkness is relative to a decision."
2. The one case that has to carry the whole session. I've been using a city transit agency: $40m spent on a light-rail spur, $60m still needed, present value of benefits maybe $45m, and the person who signed off has left the agency and took their reasoning with them. Fix the numbers if the ratios matter. Tell me what this case must have and — more importantly, this is where I keep getting it wrong — what it must lack. My instinct is that it has to be stripped of anyone's job being on the line and of any suggestion that the project might come good on the numbers, but I'm not sure whether that strips out too much.
3. The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually form, and precisely where in the sequence to head each one off. The ones I expect: "sunk = already spent"; "if the past moves you at all you've erred"; conflating honoring a sunk cost with finishing the project; the idea that the reputational/observability story is cheating because the past spend is only a signal and not a reason; and then the rebound into "so it's all just evidence-talk, the fallacy doesn't exist." Give me the ones I haven't thought of too, especially any that the amnesia argument itself will manufacture.
4. One check at the end that distinguishes genuine understanding from being able to recite the four mechanisms. I'd rather ask them what would have to be *different* for a given mechanism to stop applying than ask them whether the agency should continue.
