So I stupidly volunteered to run a reading group at work next week, and the text is the Grand Inquisitor chapter from The Brothers Karamazov. It was my idea: we shipped a feed feature a couple of months ago that nobody on the team is proud of and every metric says users love — it reorders and pre-selects what people see based on a model of what they'll click, it measurably raises both time spent and self-reported satisfaction, and we deliberately didn't announce it or give it an obvious off switch, because when we tested that, the numbers collapsed. I said out loud in retro that this is basically the Grand Inquisitor in a product. Now I have to lead it.

What I need isn't an explanation of the chapter — I've read it — it's a plan for how I explain it, for about twelve engineers and two designers in forty-five minutes, most of whom have read it once in translation and two of whom have definately only read a summary.

- The order the ideas have to come in, and what depends on what. My hunch is that the burden bit — that freedom is something people actively want to hand over, that being given back the choice is the cruel thing — has to land before the threefold miracle / mystery / authority move, because otherwise they'll hear that trio as three flavors of propaganda rather than three ways of taking the decision off someone. Argue me out of that if it's wrong.
- One carrying example. I want it to be our feature rather than something from religion or Soviet history. Tell me what such an example has to have and what it has to lack: I think the benefit has to be real rather than fake (otherwise the Inquisitor is just a liar and there's no problem), and I think it has to be a benefit that would stop working if we told people about it — but I'm not sure about that second one, or whether it quietly smuggles in the conclusion I want them to reach. Also tell me why the two obvious alternatives are worse: the church-as-propaganda-machine / dictator framing, and a plain happiness-vs-autonomy slider where you just pick a point.
- The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually arrive in the room, and the exact beat at which I head each off. What I'm expecting: that the Inquisitor is a hypocrite who doesn't believe his own speech; that he thinks people are stupd and weak, when my sense is he thinks nearly the opposite — that they're capable of freedom and it wrecks them, which is why he pities them; that this is Dostoevsky going after atheism or totalitarianism; that Christ is offering suffering for its own sake; that Christ's silence or Alyosha's kiss settles it, so the chapter has an answer; and that Ivan just is Dostoevsky.
- How I keep the hour from becoming either group therapy about our own product or a pep talk about how engagement metrics are evil. I want them leaving able to say something precise about why the offer is hard to refuse, not just that it's bad.
- What I should openly decline to settle at this length, and say out loud that I'm declining it.
- One closing question that tells me whether they got the distinction I actually care about — that the fight isn't over how much happiness versus how much freedom, but over whether a benefit delivered to someone who never chose it is a benefit to *them* at all — as opposed to just having opinions.

Then a table at the end, one row per beat, with exactly these columns: beat (one line) | what_they_can_say_after_it | depends_on (which earlier beat) | misconception_killed_here | the_question_i_ask (the literal question I put to the room).

Don't give me a reading list and please don't summarize the chapter at me — I need the sequence, not the content.
