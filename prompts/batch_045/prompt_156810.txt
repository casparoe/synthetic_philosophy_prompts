hey — I'm in a 200-level aesthetics seminar and I've been trying to reverse-engineer our professor's account of what art is, because she has never actually stated one; she just rules on cases. Her verdicts so far, all from lecture: Duchamp's urinal — art. Warhol's Brillo boxes — art. A beautiful sunset and a bird's nest — not art. A six-year-old's drawing taped to a fridge — not art (I asked after class; she said it's a picture rather than a work, and that the kid wasn't doing what Duchamp was doing). The van Meegeren Vermeer forgeries — art, but dishonest art. Paintings made by elephants at a zoo fundraiser — not art. Chartres cathedral — art. The suspension bridge over the river in our city, which everyone agrees is gorgeous — not art, and she got a bit annoyed when someone pointed out the tension with the cathedral. The tourist facsimile of the Lascaux cave paintings, exact down to the brush strokes — a document, not a work. And a set of beach stones somebody arranged on their windowsill and privately regarded as an artwork, which literally no one else ever saw — she said probably not, because nothing was ever offered to anyone.

Here are the three new cases I want predictions on. (1) A retired engineer spends nine years building an enormous kinetic machine in his garage — gears, brass, moving figures — purely for his own amusement; he insists to everyone that it's a hobby and definately not art; a curator sees photos of it and a museum shows it over his written objections. (2) A woman who has never exhibited anywhere hangs one of her own paintings in a staff-only corridor of a museum overnight without permission; for a week the guards and cleaners assume the museum put it there; then it comes down, never accessioned, and the museum later denies it was ever on the wall. (3) An art student fine-tunes an image model on scans of her own old paintings so that every output looks like her earlier work, prints one, hangs it in the student show labelled untitled, and tells no one how it was made; the faculty jury accepts it believing it's hers and it wins the prize.

What I want is her verdict on each and, more than that, the rule she seems to be running. I can think of at least three that fit the first list about equally well — one about the artworld conferring status on an artifact, one about the maker's intention plus a historical chain back to earlier art, one about 'aboutness' and a work needing an interpretation — and they look like they come apart on these three, but I can't tell which one she'd fall back on. So: which rule fits her track record best and why, which single prior verdict is doing the most work in constraining the answer (my guess is the cathedral-versus-bridge pair, but maybe it's the fridge drawing), and — the part I actually care about — design me one further case, not in either of my lists, that would separate whichever two rules you think are closest, and say what each predicts for it. Two more things: someone in seminar raised the 'first art' problem, that if art is defined historically then what made the very first artworks art, and she waved it off, so does that tell us anything about which rule she's on? And if the honest answer is that her verdicts are just family-resemblance judgements and no rule predicts anything here, say that — but then tell me what would have to be true for the definitional project to be worth doing anyway.
