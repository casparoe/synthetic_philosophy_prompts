I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training and I keep arguing with myself about whether the whole "memes are replicators, culture evolves the way Darwinian evolution says" story actually cashes out when you look at concrete cases. I know the pop version — Dawkins coins the term, other people run with it, and there's a family of objections saying transmission is really reconstruction rather than copying. I dont want a recap of any of that. What I want is to know what principle I'm already using, because my gut verdicts on individual cases don't add up, and I can't tell which ones to throw away.

Here are the verdicts.

1. The email-validation regex from a 2010 Stack Overflow answer that's been pasted into a million codebases, subtly broken in the same way in nearly all of them. Definitely meme, definitely evolving. The selection pressure is "looks plausibly right when skimmed during code review", which is not the same as "is right".

2. hello world in every tutorial for every language. Copied billions of times, near-zero variation. I want to say: not interesting, and not really Darwinian — it isn't winning a competition, it's being handed down by an institution. But I can't say cleanly why that's different from 1.

3. Every team I've worked on independently converges on "just put a cache in front of it". Nobody copied anyone. I want to say: not a meme at all, there's no lineage, so there's nothing for selection to act on — even though the outcome looks like the spread of a trait.

4. A sound or a dance that only blows up because the recommendation algorithm pushes it. Feels meme-y to me, and I notice I'm happy to call it selection even though the thing doing the selecting is a ranking model with no brain and no preferences.

5. A column in our production database called something like legacy_flag_3, meaning unknown, which has survived four rewrites because nobody dares delete it. I want to call it a parasite, but the "pressure" keeping it alive is just that removal is risky and deletion is invisible. Is that selection, or is it only inertia wearing a lab coat?

6. Version control. Spread across the whole industry, and it spread because it works — teams that used it did better. I really do NOT want to call this a meme. It's knowledge, or tooling, or something with a different flavour.

7. The 10x engineer thing, and the move fast and break things slogan. Spread because they're fun to repeat and flattering to the speaker. Obviously memes, obviously junk. Which makes me suspect I'm secretly running "meme = spread for reasons other than being true", and that can't be right, because natural selection isn't supposed to care about truth either.

8. The standard proof that there are infinitely many primes. Copied, with variation, outcompeting other proofs, in every textbook, for centuries. I also don't want to call this a meme. But on the criteria I used in 1 it plainly is one. This is the one I keep flip-flopping on.

And 6 and 8 are the pair that worries me: structurally they look like 1 and 7, and I give them the opposite verdict, and I think the only thing driving that is that I approve of the content.

Three distinctions I half-invented while thinking about this, which I can't place:

- Cold carriers. The copy isn't in anyone's head, it's in an artefact — a repo, a config file, a form field, a UI. It still gets copied and it still has effects. Does a Darwinian account need a host who *knows* the variant, or is沉积 in a codebase enough? (Most of what we'd call culture in a software org is stored cold.)
- Source-opacity. You can copy the product but not the procedure: everyone imitates the shape of a well-designed API without ever seeing the design process that produced it. Can something be evolving if only the output is transmitted and the recipe has to be re-guessed each time?
- The graveyard condition. I notice I only feel comfortable saying "selection" when I can point at variants that actually died and see why. Where there's no visible graveyard — hello world, the database column — I start hedging. Is that a real requirement, or am I smuggling something in?

My tentative principle, which I half believe: a cultural thing is genuinely undergoing Darwinian evolution iff there's (a) a lineage of copies, (b) heritable variation, and (c) differential survival that is *systematic* — tied to features of the variant rather than to who happened to be standing nearby — plus (d) my graveyard condition. Test it against the eight cases, repair it or replace it.

Specifically: commit to a position. Give me one principle, or at most a couple of candidates you'd actually defend, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you haven't seen, with a note on which of my verdicts motivates each clause. Tell me which of my verdicts I have to drop, and be explicit about 6 and 8 rather than saying "it depends". Then run it on two cases I haven't listed: (i) a code assistant trained on public code that reproduces the same broken regex from case 1 in newly written code — is that a next generation of the same lineage, or a different mechanism wearing the same clothes; (ii) a team's private jargon word that everyone uses partly because using it signals you belong. And tell me which part of your own answer you'd bet least on.
