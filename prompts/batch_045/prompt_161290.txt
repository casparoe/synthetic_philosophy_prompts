Setup: in pain asymbolia (the old clinical picture, e.g. after insular or cingulate damage) patients still report pain — they can localise it, tell burning from stabbing, rate intensity much like anyone else — but it doesn't bother them. They say they hurt and yet they're largely immune to suffering from it: no distress, no demand for analgesics, sometimes they even laugh at the stimulus.

Now the case I actually can't find discussed anywhere, so I've made it up: call it Type-B asymbolia. Same absence of unpleasantness, but the motivational side is entirely intact. The person withdraws the hand, guards the limb, cancels their plans, takes the ibuprofen, books the appointment — and when you ask why, they're sincere that nothing feels bad or unwanted; "its not unpleasant, I just stop." So the affective 'this is awful' strand and the 'make it stop' strand come apart, and only the first one is missing.

Score, 0–10, how well each of these accounts of what pain's badness consists in copes with that pair of cases, then put them in tiers (survives fine / survives with an awkward story / needs real revision / sunk), one line each on why it lands where it does, and in British English please:

- the plain sensory view: the badness just is the distinctive hurtfulness of the pain quale, an intrinsic feature of the sensation
- evaluativism: pain essentially represents some bodily condition as bad, as to be got rid of
- imperativism: pain isn't a description at all but a command — protect this, stop that — and the badness lives in that command-like force
- the motivational view: to be in pain just is to be in a state that pushes you to end it, and that push is the badness
- a reasons-first view: what matters is what pain entitles you to do — complain, avoid, claim sympathy — regardless of how it feels or what it represents
- the deflationary cluster view: 'pain' names a bundle of sensory, affective and motivational strands that can dissociate, so there's no single thing that is the badness, only a list of strands that happen to travel together
