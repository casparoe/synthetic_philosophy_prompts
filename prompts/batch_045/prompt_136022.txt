I'm a retired geologist — thirty-eight years of mapping fault scarps in the Basin and Range, so I've spent a career thinking about processes that are easy to start and impossible to run backwards — and I've lately gotten talked into recording a little interview series for the lifelong-learning crowd at my old university. Half an hour, one guest, nothing technical. Next month's guest is a moral philosopher, Dr. Ada Kwon-Reinhardt, who's built a book out of the claim that Camus's 1957 essay *Reflections on the Guillotine* is basically right but nowhere near radical enough. Her reading is that what actually carries the argument isn't the deterrence statistics or the disgust at the machinery, but something she calls the revisability constraint: no punishment is legitimate unless the authority imposing it can in principle take it back, because nobody judging anybody has the absolute innocence or the certainty that an irrevocable sentence would demand. On that reading, Camus's own substitute — forced labour for life, which he himself described as harsher than death — isn't an alternative to execution but the same mistake running on a slower mechanism, and the constraint reaches life-without-parole, whole-life tariffs, maybe any sentence long enough that the person who comes out isn't the person who went in. I find it attractive and slightly alarming at the same time, and I'd rather press her than let her recite the book at me.

So: draft me eight to ten questions, in plain language my audience can follow (no name-dropping of journals or schools, and nothing that requires background reading), that make her go beyond restating the constraint. I want at least one each on: how much of Camus's case depends on his empirical claims about deterrence and on the fact that he wrote when executions had already moved behind prison walls — if the numbers had gone the other way, or if we got clean evidence tomorrow that executions prevent murders, does her view budge at all, or was that always scaffolding she can kick away, and if so is she left with a claim about punishment or only a claim about the limits of state authority?; the premeditation argument, i.e. Camus's comparison of execution to a criminal who warns his victim of the date and then holds him for months — is the wrong-making feature the state's deliberation itself, or just the procedural cruelties of a scheduled, delayed death, and if it's the latter, wouldn't a swift, unannounced, reliable execution be *less* objectionable on her own terms, which seems like the wrong answer; the secrecy point, since Camus approved of private executions while conceding that this removes whatever exemplary effect the punishment had and leaves only disposal — is a state that bites the bullet and makes executions public and genuinely exemplary better or worse by her lights, and what does it tell us if it's worse; and the footing of the whole thing, given that Camus is working inside a worldview where the universe is silent and no one has absolute innocence — is the ban on irrevocable punishment a moral fact or a posture of rebellion, and if it's the latter, why should a legislator who doesn't share the posture care?

A few more I'd like worked in: whether the fallibility argument proves too much, since if the real worry is unrecoverable error we could just raise the proof standard for capital cases rather than abolish, and every year of a wrongful prison term is also unrecoverable; what she does with prisoners who say they'd rather die, or who ask to be executed — if the condemned person rationally prefers death, is honouring that request more humane and less objectionable on her own constraint, and if not, isn't she being paternalistic about someone else's death; and whether revisability is satisfied by a rubber-stamp review every decade, or whether it requires a real prospect of release, because that's where I suspect the view either does serious work or quietly collapses into something everyone already accepts. She has two habits I'd like you to design around: she answers hard cases with 'that's a sentencing-policy question, not a question about legitimacy,' and she answers historical ones with 'that's a fact about 1957 France.' Give me, for each question, the follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give, flag the two questions where those deflections will be hardest to shake, and write a sharper re-ask for each. End with one question about what would actually count against her view — not what a critic would say, but what she'd concede as evidence she'd got it wrong.
