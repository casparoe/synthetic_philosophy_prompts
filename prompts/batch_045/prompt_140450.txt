THE FRAMEWORK, AS I UNDERSTAND IT. I've been reading about Daniel Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks and his notion of greedy reductionism, and here is my paraphrase; correct me if I've mangled it. A crane is a bottom-up, non-miraculous piece of explanatory machinery that does real work: you are allowed to explain a higher-level phenomenon by the lower-level machinery that produces it, so long as you actually show the machinery and don't smuggle in the very thing you were supposed to explain. A skyhook is a top-down gift: something that appears to lift a phenomenon into place without any underlying mechanism, so that the explanation is really just a restatement of the puzzle with a name attached. Greedy reductionism is the distinct error of wanting the lower level to pay out too fast — jumping straight from the smallest parts to the big phenomenon and skipping the intermediate levels where the actual explanation lives. In my own field the closest thing I have is the difference between explaining a bug by tracing through the layers (application logic, library, syscall, hardware) and either hand-waving it as an emergent property of complex systems or pretending you can read it straight off the machine code while ignoring everything in between.

THE EPISODE. The Human Genome Project and its aftermath. Treat the following as given, even if you would quibble with a number: the project was sold in the late 1990s, in fairly explicit terms, as reading the instruction book or blueprint for a human being; the received expectation was that humans would turn out to have on the order of a hundred thousand genes, and the actual protein-coding count came in around twenty thousand, fewer than some plants and comparable to some very simple animals; genome size varies wildly across species with no relation to how complex the organism looks. After the sequence arrived, the tidy gene-to-trait story largely dissolved into alternative splicing, regulation, non-coding RNA, epistasis, and developmental and environmental effects. Then in 2012 the ENCODE consortium reported that something like eighty percent of the genome shows biochemical activity — it gets transcribed, or bound by proteins, and so on — and a fight broke out about whether that amounts to function, with critics arguing that the notion of function being used was far too weak. One argument in that fight that I find appealing and want you to engage with: if eighty percent of our DNA is functional, why does an onion need roughly five times more of it?

TWO IDEAS OF MY OWN THAT I WANT YOU TO GRAPPLE WITH. First, I want to split greedy reductionism into two things I'll call level-skipping, where you jump from the bottom layer straight to the top and ignore the middle, and level-collapsing, where you insist that only one layer is real and the rest is mere bookkeeping. Is that a distinction worth having, does it carve the genome case at a joint, and does either half do independent work that greedy reductionism doesn't already do? Second, here is my worry about the framework as a whole: greedy reductionism seems much easier to diagnose after the fact than before it. Nobody announced in 1990 that they were about to be greedy. So give me a real answer to this — in advance, before you know whether a reductionist research program will succeed, what observable difference is there between a greedy reduction and a merely ambitious one? If there is none, tell me plainly whether that makes the concept a post-hoc labeling device rather than a predictive tool, and whether you think it is worth keeping anyway.
