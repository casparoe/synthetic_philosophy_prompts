I lead a small platform team, and I'm designing an internal oversight workflow, not writing philosophy — so please keep the machinery operational and skip the literature survey; I have no way to look anything up, and I'd rather you define any term of art you use in terms of what the system does than point me at a paper.

The setting. A strong model (the proposer) writes changes to a safety-critical service and supplies a justification. A second strong model (the critic) argues against. A reviewer adjudicates: either a human on call with roughly ten minutes and no knowledge of the particular subsystem, or a small model. The reviewer is deliberately weaker than either debater — that's the entire point, since if the reviewer could check the work directly we wouldn't need the protocol. The failure mode I care about is the obfuscated argument: the proposer buries one unsound step inside a long chain of individually plausible ones, so that the reviewer either rubber-stamps the whole chain or abandons the check. In our case a bad merge loses money and is detected within about a day, so false accepts cost more than false rejects, and a review that takes an hour is worthless even if it is accurate.

Three things I'll name so we can talk precisely, though please tell me if my definitions are the wrong ones to be working with.

Verification debt: the number of commitments the reviewer has accepted but not actually checked, where each new layer of justification can add several such commitments while resolving at most the one it was introduced for. My worry is that debt grows faster than the reviewer's attention budget, so the protocol's soundness degrades with argument length in a way no single step reveals.

The peel test: the reviewer may at any point demand that one claim be unpacked exactly one level, and may only hold the last few claims in view at once. A protocol passes the peel test only if an argument whose every layer looks fine locally can still be rejected on the basis of information the reviewer can actually see at some point.

The substituted-lemma test: take a lemma that the winning argument depends on and swap it for an unrelated falsehood of equal surface plausibility — same length, same register, same kind of evidence cited. If the reviewer's confidence doesn't move, the reviewer was tracking style rather than content, and the protocol has bought nothing.
