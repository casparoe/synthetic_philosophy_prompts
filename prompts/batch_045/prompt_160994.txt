Last night I got into a long argument with a friend — she's a GP, I'm not — about what makes a condition a disease, and it started from something concrete: a mutual friend was turned down for income-protection insurance after a scan incidentally picked up a rare anatomical variant that produces no symptoms and no increased risk of anything. That set us off listing cases and comparing gut reactions, and I came away with a set of verdicts I'm fairly confident about and no idea whether they're mutually consistent. Mine are: type 1 diabetes, cystic fibrosis and malaria are diseases, obviously; a broken leg from skiing is a medical problem but not a disease; male pattern baldness and a large, disfiguring but functionally inert facial birthmark are not diseases, even though both carry real distress and real social cost, and even though I'd support having treatment for them covered; the rare symptomless variant is not a disease, despite being statistically abnormal; the osteoporosis of an eighty-two-year-old woman, which is close to universal in her age group, is a disease; a bodybuilder whose steroid use has shut down his own testosterone production does have a disease, which means "not self-inflicted" can't be a necessary condition; a dog with hip dysplasia and a wheat plant with rust both have diseases, which makes me doubt that the concept is essentially about persons; a gene variant that leaves its bearer perfectly healthy but roughly halves their fertility is, I think, not a disease, though my friend insists it is; and when a guidelines committee lowers the threshold for high blood pressure and thereby turns a few million people into patients overnight while nothing about their bodies changes, I want to say that most of them did not acquire a disease, that the new line is to some degree arbitrary — but I also don't want to say the committee is merely stipulating, because the underlying gradient of risk is real.

The one I keep going back and forth on is a sedentary office worker with a mild, wholly symptomless reduction in lung capacity that would never matter to her unless she took up high-altitude mountaineering: part of me says there is a dysfunction there and her environment simply never probes it; another part says nothing is wrong with her and calling her diseased is a kind of idle counterfactualising. Relatedly, I can't decide about a harmless enzyme variant that causes severe illness only in combination with a food that happens to be a dietary staple where the person lives, nor about a "pure risk factor": a blood marker in the top decile that modestly raises lifetime heart-attack risk, causes nothing the person ever notices, and is nonetheless something doctors name and treat. My friend's own stab at a principle was that something is a disease just in case (a) it involves a reduction in a function that members of the species normally perform, where the function is one the mechanism in question was shaped to perform by natural selection, (b) the bearer is worse off for it in the environment they actually occupy, and (c) the thing that makes them worse off is not merely other people's reaction to a body that is functioning normally. I suspect clause (a) is doing no work once (b) and (c) are in place, but I can't quite show it. So: can you test that principle against my verdicts, repair it if it can be repaired, or replace it with the best candidates? I'd like each candidate stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause; its verdicts on cases I haven't listed — please at least say what it implies about the symptomless lung-capacity case, about conditions that are diseases in one environment and not another, and about a trait whose only effect is to raise the probability of later disease; and an honest account of which of my verdicts it forces me to give up and why. One last thing I'd like your view on: whether whatever principle you land on has to be the same one in the clinic, at the disability-benefits office, and in a research lab, or whether the argument only looked intractable because we were quietly asking three different questions.
