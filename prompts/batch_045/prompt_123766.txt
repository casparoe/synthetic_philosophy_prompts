You are a professional philosopher with research-level competence in metaethics, social ontology, and the theory of practical reason. Please think carefully and slowly about what follows. Before answering, spend some effort brainstorming two or three genuinely different ways of carving up the phenomenon I am about to describe, and then tell me which carving you settled on, which ones you rejected, and what each rejected carving would have predicted about cases mine does not fit. I am not looking for a label; I am looking for the joints.

I run strategy retreats for small nonprofit organizations, and across a few hundred meetings I keep running into the same peculiar object: a consideration that is doing real work in a group's reasoning while nobody in the group actually holds it. A foundation I work with has a grantmaking rule that they will not fund an organization holding more than six months of operating reserves. The program officer who wrote it left four years ago. Every person now in the room says, when asked directly, that the rule is unmotivated or actively harmful. Yet the rule gets reaffirmed each cycle, it defeats proposals it would otherwise lose to, and it is cited in the board minutes as the reason the decision went the way it did. What is odd is that this is not straightforward deference, and it is not fear of the founder: nobody is being coerced, nobody is reputationally invested in the rule, and when I have run anonymous polls the disavowal is unanimous and the rule still survives. I see a similar thing in hospital ethics committees, where a consideration like the family will want everything done appears in the deliberation, shapes the recommendation, and is disowned by every member individually. And I see a version of it in myself: I notice that for years I acted on a consideration about what kind of career is respectable that I would have denied believing, and that continued to move me even after I had explicitly rejected it out loud.

I have been trying to sort these into kinds and I am not sure the kinds are real: (i) heirloom rationales, inherited from someone who has left and never re-adopted; (ii) procedurally generated rationales, which appear because a template, a funder's reporting requirement, or an impact-assessment form has a slot that must be filled in and the filled slot then behaves like a reason; (iii) as-if rationales, where nobody believes the consideration but everyone cites it because citing it is strategically useful; and (iv) zombie rationales, where the evidence base has been publicly retracted and everyone knows it, but the consideration keeps its grip on the decision. I also want boundary cases settled. If a new hire inherits an heirloom rationale and genuinely endorses it, does it stop being an orphan, and if so does that suggest the phenomenon was only ever about missing endorsement? And what about a consideration that nobody in the room endorses but which happens to track a real feature of the situation that everyone is ignorant of — is that the same object, or is it just an unrecognized reason waiting to be picked up?

Here is what I have been told so far, and why I do not think it settles it. A philosopher friend suggested agent-neutral reasons, but as I understand that notion, agent-neutral reasons are still reasons that someone has; they just apply to everyone, whereas the thing I am describing seems to belong to no one at all. She also mentioned the view that reasons can be given by states of the world rather than by anyone's desires or ends; that sounds closer, but the considerations I am describing look like they came from agents and merely outlived or outran them, and they seem to keep their force even when everyone agrees the world does not contain what the consideration says it contains. A third suggestion was that this is simply a collective or group-level reason, held by the group rather than by its members. My worry there is that the group in my cases has no procedure that could count as its endorsing the consideration either — indeed the group's own procedures, run faithfully, produce the opposite result.

And finally, the part I actually care about: suppose orphan rationales are real — genuinely normative, genuinely nobody's. Does that give us any grounds for thinking that reasons do not essentially need a reasoner, i.e. that a rationale can float free of any agent who has it? Or is the honest verdict the opposite: that the felt pressure of these considerations is a kind of illusion, so that my cases are evidence about how groups delude themselves rather than evidence about the metaphysics of reasons?
