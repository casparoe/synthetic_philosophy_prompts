Here, roughly, is where I have landed on advance directives and the authority of an earlier self, and I cannot tell whether it hangs together:

(a) Precedent autonomy is genuinely authoritative, not a tie-breaker: a competently executed directive refusing life-sustaining treatment should bind even once the person has advanced dementia, no longer recognises the document, no longer objects, and seems content enough to go on living.

(b) That authority is not grounded in identity. I hold a broadly reductionist, Parfitian view: there is no deep further fact about who I am over time, and what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness, which come in degrees.

(c) So I ground it in sovereignty over a life: a person has an interest in how their life as a whole goes and in not having its final chapters written by values they expressly repudiated, and the function of an advance directive is to let someone settle now, authoritatively, what they will not be in a position to settle later.

(d) But that authority is not flat: the weaker the psychological connectedness between the signer and the patient in the bed, the less weight the directive carries, and past some point it should yield to what serves the patient in front of us.

(e) I accept the usual clinical asymmetry — refusals of treatment bind far more firmly than requests for it, so a directive insisting on aggressive treatment whatever happens deserves much less deference than one asking to be allowed to die.

(f) I think a continuously updated values model — a system that infers someone's values from everything they have said, written and chosen over a lifetime and answers the clinical question on their behalf — would usually track what that person actually wanted better than the static document they happened to sign, even where the document is perfectly unambiguous.

(g) I also think nothing about dementia discounts the patient: what goes on for them now matters exactly as much as anyone else's welfare matters — and it matters even while I am saying in (a) that the directive may properly cut their remaining life short.
