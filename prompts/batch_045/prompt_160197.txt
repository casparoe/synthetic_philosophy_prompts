I work on a team that runs a replicated state machine across a few hundred nodes in three datacenters, and my working vocabulary is the usual one: leaders and terms, commit indices and quorums, crash faults versus Byzantine faults, partial synchrony and the impossibility of asynchronous consensus with even one crash fault, gossip protocols that average neighbours' estimates, convergent replicated data types, and message adversaries. I am comfortable with all of that and with ordinary probability; I have no measure theory and no epistemic logic beyond what I have absorbed by osmosis. Aumann's agreement theorem keeps coming up in arguments I have with colleagues about whether a network of models that pool their evidence should end up agreeing, and I would like the whole thing rendered into my vocabulary as faithfully as possible, with an honest account afterwards of what the translation lost.

Concretely, please translate these one at a time: the common prior assumption; an information partition, that is, what a node can and cannot distinguish given its own history; common knowledge and the unbounded hierarchy of knowing that you know that they know; the act of conditioning on another party's reported posterior; and the conclusion that the posteriors must come out equal. For each, tell me what my vocabulary already has a word for, what has to be bent to fit, and what it cannot express at all. Three points I want handled explicitly rather than smoothed over. First, my instinct is to read the common prior as a shared random seed plus identical code, and a colleague says that is wrong because a prior is also a commitment about what you would have believed had you seen evidence you did not in fact see, which nothing in my systems represents. Who is closer to right, and what specifically breaks under my reading? Second, gossip averaging provably converges to the mean, and I have been told that iterated exchange of exact posteriors also converges to a common value; if both converge, is the agreement theorem just a consensus result with extra ceremony, or is the thing that converges different in kind — a probability attached to an event rather than a replicated value — and does that difference matter for anything I would actually build? Third, the case I actually care about: in our system nodes exchange a coarse bucketed confidence rather than a number, say high, medium, or low, and a node can crash mid-exchange so that its final message may or may not have landed. Tell me whether anything recognizably like the agreement result survives quantized messages and crash faults, whether the honest translation is that we can only ever get agreement to within a bucket and a may-have-been-delivered message, and how much resistance the result puts up before it gives way.

Then the residue report proper, which is the part I am really after. Give me your view on which assumptions are load-bearing and which are artifacts of the framing. Is the common-knowledge requirement the load-bearing one — given that in the two-generals style setting no finite run of acknowledgments over lossy links ever achieves common knowledge — so that the theorem turns out to be a claim about a limit my systems provably cannot reach? Is the assumption that reports are truthful and interpretable through the shared prior doing work that in my world has to be paid for with signatures, quorums, and Byzantine tolerance, and if so does the theorem quietly presuppose a fault model it never states? And above all, sort the residue into two piles: what my vocabulary is simply too poor to say, and what the original statement leaves less pinned down than its confidence suggests. Where a piece of the result cannot be expressed in terms of logs, quorums, and messages at all, I want to know whether that is a concept I should go and learn or a sign that the result is trading on an idealization. Please flag explicitly anywhere you catch yourself adding an assumption my framework does not have and calling it translation.
