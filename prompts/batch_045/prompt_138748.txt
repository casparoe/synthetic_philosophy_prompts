My friend and I spent most of last night on whether artifacts have politics, and I came away with an argument I can neither accept nor break. I have reconstructed it as carefully as I can below. I think something slips somewhere, but I cannot say where, and I would rather have you locate it than have me tilt the diagnosis by guessing out loud. Please note that I do not want citations or reading suggestions — I want the reasoning, and I want your verdict on the cases I describe, which are my own.

(1) Call an artifact *political* when its design, considered apart from the intentions of any particular user or maker, durably arranges who can do what — that is, when it distributes access, standing, or power among people in a patterned way. This is meant to be the standard thesis, of the sort illustrated by the low parkway overpasses a bus cannot clear and by the mechanical tomato harvester.

(2) Every artifact is designed, and every design settles some dimension along which people already differ: reach, grip strength, literacy, pace of movement, working hours, native language, height. Each such settlement is durable, and each holds independently of what any user intends.

(3) So, by (1), every artifact is political.

(4) A predicate true of everything does no critical work. The point of calling the overpasses political was to single them out — to mark them as open to a kind of objection that ordinary functional apparatus is not open to. If my toaster and my stapler are political in exactly the sense the overpasses are, the word has lost its target.

(5) The thesis was supposed to be a criticism with a target.

(6) So (1) is false, or (4) is, or the thesis is empty. My friend's formulation: it is either vacuous or self-effacing.

The second horn, which he produced when I objected:

(7) Suppose we repair (1) by requiring that the people advantaged and disadvantaged form a socially salient group — a class, a race, a disability, parents on fixed shifts — rather than whoever happens to be the wrong height.

(8) Then whether an artifact is political depends on facts about the society around it: the overpasses exclude only where there is a bus-using population for them to exclude; the same spans in a city with no buses and no residential segregation would not be political at all.

(9) So the artifact itself contributes nothing. What does the work is the social arrangement it is dropped into, which means we were never saying that artifacts have politics, only that societies do and artifacts are where it becomes visible.

(10) So the thesis, read so as to be non-vacuous, is redundant: social construction with extra machinery.

I tried two escapes and he met both. First, I said politics comes in degrees — the overpasses highly, the stapler barely. He said a continuous scale must be cut somewhere, and wherever it is cut we are back in (7) through (9). Second, I proposed a repair of my own: call an artifact political only when some feasible alternative design would transfer the advantage to a group already disadvantaged along some other axis. He said that just relocates the whole problem into (7).

The case I care about is from my own work: a school portal that releases parent–teacher appointment slots in a five-minute window at 7 p.m. It reliably favors parents with flexible schedules and a fast connection. He agreed it is political and then said it is political in the same sense and to the same degree as a door too heavy for some children to push.

Which step do you reject? If I have to choose I would rather keep (1), (2), and (5) than (4), but I am not confident that (4) is where the weight sits, and I would rather you tell me. For each candidate step, explain why it looked sound and what rejecting it commits me to — in particular what it commits me to about the portal, about the overpasses, and about artifacts whose exclusionary effects nobody foresaw or intended. Name the step in your first sentence and keep the whole answer under 600 words.
