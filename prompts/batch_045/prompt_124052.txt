I'm giving the toast at my sister's wedding on Saturday, and I would like you to write it. Let me set out the constraints, because most of them rule something out.

The room is mixed in a way that matters. My sister is not a philosopher, and neither is anyone on her side of the family; they are observant Catholics, and for them what happens at the altar is a sacrament rather than an arrangement. Roughly a third of the remaining guests are colleagues from my department, who will catch every allusion the moment it arrives, who will be quietly grading the argument, and who will not forgive me if the toast turns out to be a seminar with ribbon on it. The families will not forgive me if it turns out to be a seminar at all. It should run about eight hundred words, which is six or seven minutes out loud.

My own field is rational choice over time, and I want the toast to be built on a distinction from it without ever naming it. Here is the material. The familiar cautionary figure in my field is the person who is exploited not by a single bad trade but by a sequence: at each step they take the trade they prefer at that step, and the steps lead them back to where they began and poorer than they began. There is a second pattern, less often discussed, which is in a sense the mirror image of the first: at every step the locally better choice is to stay, because leaving has a cost that is charged immediately and all at once, so the person stays at each step and ends somewhere they would have refused outright had it been offered to them whole at the start. In the first pattern the person is drained by their own incoherence. In the second they are held by their own patience. A wedding is a ceremony that has to be said in the shadow of both, and almost all of what is normally said at weddings manages to be false to one or the other.

So here is what the speech cannot do. It cannot promise that they will always feel as they feel now: that is exactly the assumption the first pattern shows to be unreliable, and it is false, and my colleagues will hear the falsity. It cannot say that since feeling is involuntary, promises are decoration. It cannot use the obvious classical image of the sailor tied to his own mast, because in that story the thing you are being tied against is the person you are marrying, and there is no way to tell it at a wedding without implying that the marriage is a danger and the beloved is what the danger sounds like. It cannot be a lecture, and it cannot let the Catholic half of the room hear the sacrament being redescribed as a cost-management technique.

What I want it to do instead is work with the following thought, which I have not seen put quite this way and which I want you to develop rather than decorate. A vow is signed by a person who will not be the person who has to keep it: the one who keeps it is, in the only sense that matters for deliberation, someone who never agreed to it and cannot be consulted. There are two ways to bind such a person. One can remove the option, so that there is nothing left to choose, or one can leave the option entirely intact and arrange that choosing it will be seen and will cost someone else something. Marriage only permits the second kind; there is no mast available, only witnesses. And the question I want the toast to answer, out loud, in front of both halves of the room, is whether the second kind is a manipulation of a future person who never consented — a rigging of their situation so that they cannot deliberate well about what is actually in front of them — or whether it is, on the contrary, the only form a promise to a future person can take that they can be said to keep at all, precisely because it leaves them able to break it.

Please take a definite side on that question and let the speech give its reasons in its own voice, in the register of a toast, without hedging and without presenting both answers as equals: I would rather be wrong in public with an argument than non-committal in public with a balance sheet. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, no epigraphs and no quotations from anyone. After the toast, add a single paragraph of prose on the strongest thing that could be said against the position the toast commits itself to, so that I know what I am walking into on Saturday.
