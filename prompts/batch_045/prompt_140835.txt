I'm in a graduate seminar on naturalizing intentionality and we've just finished the stretch on teleosemantics — the etiological family of theories where a representation's content is fixed by what the mechanism carrying it was selected for doing, in the historical conditions in which its lineage was culled. My group presents next week and I'd like to close not with a summary slide but with a short poem, something I can read aloud in about ninety seconds.

The case I want it built on is the one our section argued about. Take three frogs that are, intrinsically, molecule-for-molecule identical: the same crude feature-detector in the visual system, the same snap mechanism, and all three of them snap this morning at the same small dark pellet dropped into their tank. What differs is only history. Frog A is the textbook case: her ancestors snapped at flies, the ones whose detectors fired at flies ate, the ones that didn't starved, and the mechanism was culled into being a fly-detector. Frog B's lineage has lived for a hundred generations in a sealed terrarium where the only small dark moving things are the food pellets the keepers drop in; the frogs that snapped at pellets ate and the ones that didn't starved, so by now the selection story for a mechanism indistinguishable from A's bottoms out in pellet rather than fly. Frog C is the swamp creature: a chance coalescence of molecules in a marsh that happens to be an intrinsic duplicate of the other two, with no lineage, no selection history, and no historical conditions of normal operation at all — and so, on the etiological verdict, nothing that its states are about.

I'd like the form to do the philosophical work. A villanelle, or a villanelle-shaped poem: the two refrains belong to A and to B, and each refrain should be a line whose reference is inherited rather than intrinsic — a phrase that picks out whatever the lineage's mechanism was selected for — so that the same string of words means fly in one mouth and pellet in the other. Someone who has done the reading should be able to tell which refrain is whose, and why two type-identical lines can differ in content. Frog C gets no refrain: she speaks only in lines that never return, since there is nothing in her past that would entitle any of her lines to come back, and in the closing stanza, when A's refrain and B's refrain both arrive, C's slot should be the one line that can't be filled by anything inherited.

Two things I'd rather the poem not do. I don't want it to resolve the indeterminacy worry — the objection that if the detector fires on any small dark moving thing, the content should be disjunctive, or just small dark moving thing, in all three frogs alike; I'd rather the ending leave that gap open, since that is where our discussion actually stopped. And I don't want it to resolve into either a slogan about history doing all the semantic work or a shrug about how nothing is really about anything.

Give each frog a distinct idiom — A's lines spacious and about open water and warm air, B's about glass, a keeper's hand, a schedule, C's clipped and slightly off, since on the theory she isn't quite saying anything — and keep every technical term out of the poem itself: no proper function, no normal condition, no teleosemantics, no content. Someone who hasn't read the literature should just hear a poem about three frogs. Somewhere around 25 to 40 lines.
