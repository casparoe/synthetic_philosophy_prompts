I cover science and ideas for a living, so I end up at a lot of dinners where two people who know far more than me go at it, and last month I got a really good one. I was tagging along with a reparations commission in a country I can't name — about 140 years ago the state seized a big chunk of land from one population and handed title to another, everyone directly involved is dead, and the commission is arguing about what, if anything, is owed now. Two members, a philosopher (Mara) and an economist (Deniz), went several rounds and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning, so I'm passing it to you.

(1) Mara opens with what she calls the but-for share rule: model what the descendants' slice of national wealth would be today if the seizure had never happened, and transfer the difference. She says this is the only way to make "repair" mean something rather than just a sympathetic handout.

(2) Deniz says the baseline is empty. Five or six generations back, had the seizure not occurred, different couples would have met, different children would have been conceived, and the specific people who would recieve the transfer would very likely not exist at all. So there is nobody whose but-for share you are computing — call it the no-plaintiff problem. He says that isn't a technicality; it eats the whole framework.

(3) Mara replies that you can run the counterfactual at the level of the lineage rather than the person, the way a university persists through total turnover of its students and staff. The group is diachronically the same thing; the instability of its membership doesn't matter.

(4) Deniz answers that a lineage isn't a university — it has no decision procedure, no legal personality, and its membership is itself partly an effect of the injustice, so using the group to fix the baseline is circular. Worse, you have to run the model on the beneficiary side too, and those people wouldn't exist either. And the counterfactual economy has a different total size (the seizure plausibly changed how rich the country is overall), so the computed gap can come out bigger than the entire GDP. Any number that can swallow the economy is, he says, a sign the method is broken.

(5) Mara bites part of the bullet: yes, use net advantage rather than gross, and yes, cap it — but she says a huge answer isn't a refutation, and adds that we use unstable group-level counterfactuals all the time (this cohort's lifetime earnings were depressed by that policy) without thinking they're void.

(6) Deniz distinguishes two kinds of group-level claim: descriptive ones about cohorts, which are fine, and normative ones about who owes what to whom, which need a pairing of a specific obligor with a specific obligee. He adds a second worry: even if there is a genuine group-level debt, paying it out of general taxation lands on people with no connection to the seizure, including recent arrivals, and he can't see why they should pay.

(7) Mara's last move is that obligations ride on positions rather than on causal chains: the injustice created a structure with advantaged slots and disadvantaged slots, newcomers step into the slots, so they do owe, and anyone who says otherwise has to explain why the current distribution is fine. She also says inaction is itself a distributive choice, so Deniz can't hide in the status quo. Deniz's reply, briefly: that concedes his point, because a position-based duty is forward-looking structural justice wearing a backward-looking costume, and if that's all that's going on then the history is doing no real work and we should just say so.
