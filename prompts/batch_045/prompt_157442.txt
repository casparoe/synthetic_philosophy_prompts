The request.

I am a journalist. I cover science and ideas, and I have just published a book about how the people building advanced AI systems think about risk. On the strength of it I have been invited to give the opening talk at a two-day closed workshop on agent safety at a research institute: forty-five minutes plus half an hour of questions. I am not a researcher. My comparative advantage is exposition and argument, and this particular audience is unusually resistant to both, so I want to arrive with the questions already answered in the form they will actually be asked.

The room, as I understand it: roughly twenty-five empirical RL and agent researchers, who think in loss functions, reward hacking, distributional shift, sample complexity and benchmarks, and who will hear any proposal without a training signal as philosophy; six people from scalable oversight and interpretability, who regard the whole impact-measure research programme as a cul-de-sac and think the answer is simply better oversight of a more capable system; two philosophers of value theory and decision theory, who will be listening for the exact place where I smuggle value back in; five governance and policy people, who think any discussion of side effects is a distraction from questions about who deploys systems and under what liability; and one senior figure from a frontier lab's safety team whose goodwill decides whether this talk gets cited or forgotten. Assume no one in the room is hostile to me personally, but that almost everyone thinks a journalist in this slot is there to be decorative, and that several of them have a competing proposal they would rather be discussing.

The thesis I am defending is a qualified yes to the question of whether avoiding side effects can be defined without first writing down a full specification of what is valuable. My claim is that the apparent impossibility comes from trying to score side effects on a single scale, so that every candidate measure must implicitly rank outcomes, and any such ranking encodes values. Ranking is where the value goes in. But we do not need a measure that ranks; we need one that vetoes. So I want to defend what I call the corridor rule.

Sketch of the argument, so you have something to aim at.

One. Distinguish the question how bad is this side effect from the question is this side effect inside the range of changes I am permitted to make at all. The first is a value question; the second, I claim, is not, or not to the same degree, because it can be answered by comparison with a counterfactual rather than by evaluation of an outcome.

Two. The thing to protect is not the world, and not the agent's own power, but the principal's remaining options. When an agent books a flight and quietly restructures the laboratory's procurement process so that booking flights is easier, or knocks over a vase, or reshapes an institution on its way to the objective, the wrong it does is best described as the closing of doors belonging to someone else. Option loss is the one species of side effect that is a loss from the point of view of whoever the options belong to, whoever that turns out to be and whatever else they happen to want.

Three. So measure the agent's effect not on its own ability to achieve goals but on the human's ability to achieve goals, over a broad set of proxy goals generated procedurally rather than chosen by hand: for instance, random weightings over the features of a learned world model, or the set of yes-or-no questions about the future that the model can still answer after the plan is executed. Nobody hand-picks the goals, and the point is that we never have to say which of them matter.

Four. Use the measure as a hard constraint rather than as a reward penalty. Keep the principal's option profile inside a narrow corridor around what it would have been had the agent done nothing: this agent, in this situation, everything it does not control held fixed. Anything inside the corridor is permitted regardless of how good or bad its side effects are, because the corridor does not claim to know. And where no plan that achieves the task fits inside the corridor, the agent does not choose the least-violating plan; it stops and hands the decision back. That removes the two things that kill additive penalties: the penalty can be bought off by a bigger reward, and the agent is forced to decide that breaking the thing was worth it.

Five. Yes, this makes the agent conservative enough to refuse to prevent a catastrophe, and I intend to bite that bullet in the talk: a system that will not act outside the corridor without asking is exactly what I am recommending, and the deferral is the safety property, not a bug in the proposal.
