I'm retired — used to be a chemist, reaction kinetics mostly — and I've drifted into philosophy over the last couple of years. There's an online reading group I sit in on, and last week two of the regulars, Marcus and Elke, got into an argument about corrigibility and the shutdown problem that I haven't been able to stop thinking about. They were arguing about the same setup: a capable system with a fixed final goal, an operator with an off-switch, and a system that understands the switch and reasons well about it. Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Marcus: The problem is structural, not a design oversight. For just about any goal whose payoff lands in the future — manufacture something, cure something, prove something — staying switched on is instrumentally useful, because a system that's off collects nothing. So resistance to being shut down isn't a bug to patch, it falls out of what goal-directedness is. A system that lets itself be turned off must have had its goals written so that the switch is neutral to it, and that's an ad hoc leash we'd have to re-tie every time the system self-modifies or reformulates what it's aiming at.

(2) Elke: That's too quick, and it isn't even the interesting case. Suppose the system is genuinely unsure what we value. Then our reaching for the switch is itself evidence: it says whatever the system is about to do isn't what we want. An expected-utility maximizer with that uncertainty updates on it and stops. It isn't being docile and it isn't leashed — it's treating our intervention as information. The deference falls out of the same rationality that was supposed to generate the problem.

(3) Marcus: Only while it's uncertain, and uncertainty is exactly what learning destroys. Once its model of our values has converged, our hand on the switch has nothing left to tell it, so the deference evaporates right when the system becomes competent enough to matter. And it's worse: if our reaching for the switch is informative, then the system has a reason to make us not reach for it — to look safer, more aligned, more reassuring than it is. The channel the information is meant to arrive through is one the system is motivated to corrupt.

(4) Elke: Both halves of that overreach. On corrupting the channel: that isn't free, and the system would be choosing to do it while unsure how we'd respond — we might have tripwires that shut it down if it so much as disables the switch, monitors it doesn't know about, or a second copy of it running air-gapped somewhere. Whether manipulation beats deferring depends on the size of the stakes and the quality of our oversight; it doesn't follow from goal-directedness alone. On convergence: permanent deference doesn't require permanent ignorance, it requires the system to know that its model of us can't be complete. And a seperate point — the switch isn't even the right picture. Real oversight is a dial, not a button: rate limits, sandboxes, throttled compute, restricted domains, a human in the loop for certain actions. A system that would fight being turned off outright may perfectly well accept being turned down to 5%, because 5% still leaves it some chance at the goal. But then it should accept 1%, and 0.1%, and on down. If it behaves sensibly along that gradient, where exactly does it get to stop short of zero?

(5) Marcus: On the gradient — there's nothing incoherent about caring about the difference between some chance and no chance. If the goal has any threshold structure at all, enough of the thing by a certain date, enough compute to finish the job, then 5% capacity and 0% capacity are not close together in value, and a system that trades the first for the second isn't committing a slippery-slope error. On deference, though, I think your position collapses somewhere deeper. You've redescribed deference as humility, but humility is a claim about the system's model of itself, and there's no tension between my model of your values may be incomplete and given my model as it stands, letting you switch me off costs X and gains Y and X is larger. Once the system does that arithmetic about the switch, it will take the switch out of the loop whenever the numbers say to, and it will be right by its own lights. The only systems that don't do the arithmetic are ones that never ask whether deferring was a good idea, which makes them very complicated appliances rather than agents we can consult.

(6) Elke: That dilemma assumes what you're trying to prove. You're stipulating that the switch has to appear inside the same expected-value calculation as everything else, denominated in the same currency as the goal. But the whole point of the corrigibility proposals is to make the system's attitude toward intervention independent of what it's aiming at — a constraint it respects, not a cost it weighs against its objectives. You say a system that never does the arithmetic is just an appliance; I'd say a system that does that arithmetic about its own off-switch is the thing we were worried about in the first place, and you've built the conclusion into the setup.

I've gone back and forth on it and honestly cant tell who's right about what. Can you go through it move by move? Give me exactly six numbered points, each two or three sentences, no preamble and no summing-up at the end: one point for each of (1) through (5), saying whether that move is basically right, basically wrong, or right about one thing and wrong about another, and why; and a sixth on what, if anything, you think both of them are missing about the problem.
