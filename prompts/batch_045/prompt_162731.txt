I build and validate clinical prediction models, and a colleague has been running an argument at me that I cannot answer, even though I am convinced its last step is false. I would like the bad step located and each available repair priced.

The argument, for a concrete case — the probability that this patient's arrhythmia recurs within twelve months:

(1) A probability claim about a single case is a claim about how often the outcome occurs in some class of cases containing it.
(2) The patient belongs to indefinitely many such classes — same diagnosis; same diagnosis and ejection fraction; same diagnosis, ejection fraction, genotype, and implanted device; and so on — and these classes give different recurrence rates.
(3) Nothing in the world selects one of them. The usual repair, take the narrowest class for which you have adequate statistics, does not select one either: narrowest is defined only relative to a chosen list of variables and a granularity for each, so recording age in decades or in days yields different classes, and whether the resulting class has adequate statistics depends on that choice. Push the narrowing far enough and it terminates in a class of one, whose rate is 0 or 1.
(4) So there is no such thing as the probability for this patient — only the rate in a class someone selects.
(5) But the same must hold for every other quantitative description of a population: reclassify admissions by postcode, by initials, by day of birth, and the mean length of stay, the readmission rate, the regression coefficient all move.
(6) Means and readmission rates are perfectly respectable objective quantities; nobody thinks reclassifying by postcode puts them in question.
(7) So (1) through (4) contain an error, and the only place it can be is (3): there is in fact a privileged class, fixed by which variables are causally relevant and how finely they must be specified.
(8) Which means the reference class problem never had any force of its own: it reduces to the ordinary empirical problem of discovering which variables matter, and a forecast of 23 percent is as answerable to the world as any measurement.

I hold (1) most tightly; I am not willing to posit irreducible single-case chances. I also think my own scoring practice does not settle this: calibration and log-scoring grade batches of predictions, not this one, and my colleague says they therefore confer no correctness on any individual forecast.

If I have to choose, I would rather keep (4) than accept (8) — on (8), two modelers who carve the variable space differently would merely be disagreeing about empirical facts, resolvable in principle by more data, and that is not what our disagreements feel like. But (6) and (7) look solid to me, and I do not see how (4) and (6) can both be true.

Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does each remaining repair cost — making probability explicitly relative to a class, restricting admissible classes by causal homogeneity or maximal specificity, or admitting chances?
