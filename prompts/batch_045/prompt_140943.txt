I'm a journalist — I do a weekly column on science and ideas, mostly the human and social stuff, and every so often I manage to smuggle in some actual philosphy. Right now I'm working on a piece about who gets counted as "the people" in a democracy, and I've been going round in circles on it, so I'd rather think out loud with you than be lectured at. Please don't send me a reading list or footnotes: I can't use citations in a newspaper piece anyway, and what I need is the argument, not the literature.

Here's the pattern that's bugging me. In most democracies the national vote is tied to citizenship, not residence, and it plays out in ways that look, on the face of it, like they can't all come from the same idea:

- People who have lived abroad for decades — sometimes born there, citizens purely by descent, never paid tax in the home country, never really lived under its day-to-day laws — keep the vote for life and usually pass it on to their children. Several countries even run dedicated overseas constituencies with reserved seats. (I know a few have time limits — Australia, I think, cuts you off after a while — but the general shape is what I mean.)
- Meanwhile long-term legal residents who've worked, paid taxes, and raised kids in the country for twenty or thirty years can't vote in national elections, and in a lot of places can still be deported.
- The bit that really gets me: in the US, as I understand it, non-citizen male residents are required to register for the draft. So the state can conscript you while refusing you any say in the decision to conscript you.
- But then flip to the local level, and residence suddenly seems to be enough: plenty of places let non-citizen residents vote in municipal or school-board elections, and within the EU a citizen of one member state living in another can vote in local elections where they actually live. So residency qualifies you down at the bottom and disqualifies you at the top.

So: what theory of legitimate inclusion does that package presuppose? I mean both "what has to be true for this to make sense" and "what does it quietly rule out."

I've been sorting the arguments I hear into three buckets and I suspect my buckets are wrong, so tell me if they are:
- Authorship claims: the law is issued in my name, so I have to be able to see myself as one of its authors; being pushed around by a collective I don't belong to is a form of domination even if the pushing is gentle.
- Exposure claims: I carry the costs of whatever gets decided, so I need a say in it.
- Stake or association claims: my life plan is bound up with this place over time, so I'm a member in a way a visitor or a passing investor isn't.

Which of those does the practice actually need, and which does it only sit comfortably next to? Does it maybe need a fourth thing I haven't got a bucket for — something about stability, like: the demos has to be a group whose membership you can't change by moving house, and which persists across generations so it can bind itself to long-term commitments? Is the nationality rule best explained by that kind of consideration, by the authorship idea, or by something less flattering (emigrants who send money home and lobby for the folks back home are useful to governments)? And can any single story hold the whole package together — the overseas voters, the voteless taxpayers, the draft-eligible non-voters, and the local elections where residence is suddenly fine — or is this just a settlement between theories that don't really fit?

Then the counterfactual I want: if we dropped the implicit theory and adopted something like a pure "everyone who's genuinely affected gets a say" rule, what would elections actually have to look like? Walk me through it concretely — do we end up enfranchising foreigners downstream of our emissions or our central bank, giving children or future people some kind of proxy, and disenfranchising emigrants? Is that a reductio of the affected-interests principle, or is it where the logic honestly goes and we should just get used to it?

And be clear on where the practice is underdetermined: which features would several rival theories all license, so that they tell us nothing about what we really believe, and which are load-bearing. I want to know which parts of this are philosophy and which are just historical accident.
