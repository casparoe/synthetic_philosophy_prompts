I've been reading about Frege's puzzle on my own for about two years now, with no academic background in philosophy (my day job is records and metadata work), and I've reached the point where I no longer want a verdict on who is right. I want a diagnosis of why nothing ever moves.

Here is how I understand the state of play, in my own words. The puzzle starts from cases like the morning star and the evening star: a statement of identity between two names for one and the same object can be informative and worth writing down, while the identity of that object with itself under one name is something nobody would bother to record. One camp says the difference has to be located in the semantics: the names contribute something more than the object to what is said, some way in which the object is presented, and that is why the two sentences differ in what a competent speaker learns from them. The other camp says names contribute nothing but the object; the sentence expresses a singular proposition about that object, and everything that looks semantic about the puzzle is psychology, pragmatics, or a fact about what the speaker happened to already believe. Both camps, as far as I can tell, know every argument the other camp has, and nobody has moved in decades.

My hypothesis is that this is not a dispute about facts at all, nor even about which cases count as data, but a dispute about what a semantic theory is supposed to be an account of: one side thinks it has to explain rational differences in what people learn, believe, and are surprised by; the other thinks it has to explain truth conditions and how meaning composes, and that cognition is someone else's department. If that's right, then the two sides are answering different questions while using the word meaning as if it picked out one thing. But I want that hypothesis tested against what each side actually argues and actually concedes, not just asserted back at me.

Three things I want folded into the diagnosis, because they're where my own thinking gets stuck.

First, a case I invented, which I find harder than the standard one. Suppose I am standing in front of a single statue in a storeroom. A curator tells me, pointing, that this object is the piece recovered from the harbour in 1908. An hour later, still pointing at the same object, she tells me that this object is the piece the museum bought at auction in 1923. So I know, of the very thing in front of me, that it was recovered from the harbour, and I know, of that same thing, that it was bought in 1923. Yet reading in the catalogue that the piece recovered from the harbour is the piece bought in 1923 is new information to me. I don't know whether this case is a known one or one I've muddled together; describe it in your own terms if mine are clumsy. My question is whether it discriminates between the two camps at all. If the direct-reference story is that the informativeness is just my ignorance of which object the two descriptions pick out, that story seems strained here, because I was perceptually confronted with the object both times. Does the Fregean camp, on the other hand, now need two ways of presenting an object that is already demonstratively fixed for me, and is that a cost or a feature?

Second, a variant that removes the two names entirely. I know someone only as a union representative named Dana Reyes, whom I think of as a blunt and unsentimental negotiator. Separately, I love a novel and learn that its author is also called Dana Reyes; I form a picture of a reclusive, bookish person. I never connect them. Asked casual questions, I would happily say that Dana Reyes has no taste for small talk and also that Dana Reyes spends her evenings in libraries, and I would resist the suggestion that these are claims about one person, even though there is exactly one name and exactly one person. Does a view that puts the explanatory work in names have the resources for this? And if the honest answer is that what's doing the work is not names but something like mental files, dossiers, or ways of thinking of an object, does that relocate the whole dispute to a place where the two camps could in principle agree, or does the same disagreement reappear one level down?

Third, whether my workplace version of this is the same dispute or only a costume for it. When we merge two database records about one real-world entity, the engineers say the entity is the entity and everything else is bookkeeping; the cataloguers insist that how each record came to us is part of what we are storing, and that this is not all there is to say is not a complete thing to say. I suspect this is a practical question with a right answer that philosophy has no bearing on, and that my suspicion itself tells me something: that the philosophical dispute only bites where rational cognition, not institutional bookkeeping, is the thing to be explained. Tell me if that's confused.

So: is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or two questions under one heading? Is a hidden empirical question about how human beings store and retrieve information about individuals quietly doing the work? Are the sides disagreeing about the data, about the standards a semantic theory has to meet, or about starting intuitions neither side can argue for? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a psychological finding, a change of question — or is there a good argument that no progress should be expected, and what would that argument be?
