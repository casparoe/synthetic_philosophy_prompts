Bit of context first, then the ask. I'm a retired analytical chemist — ran a mass spec lab for about thirty years — and since I stopped I've been sitting in on philosophy stuff at the lifelong learning center near me. I now run the weekly "Great Questions" group there: 9 or 10 people most weeks, all retired, two ex-engineers, a former city councilman who will definately say "well that's just politics" at some point, two who are hard of hearing, and we get a room with a whiteboard and bad coffee for 75 minutes. I keep thinking about this material the way I used to think about experiments: controls, conditions, and being honest about what three trials can't show you.

We've been circling around voting, and what I want is one session where they *feel* the thing I keep bumping into in the reading — the claim that any sensible ranked voting rule with three or more options can be gamed, that sincere voting isn't just naive but sometimes straightforwardly costly, and that this isn't a fact about bad people. I think there's a famous impossibility result about this (Gibbard-Satterthwaite? I may have the name mangled). I don't want them told it. I want them to walk into it.

So: design me a runnable game.

- Rules, materials, payoff scheme, and a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes. Cards, chips, a hat, whiteboard — nothing digital, no real money, candy or bragging rights as stakes is fine. Everyone gets a private "true preference" card they don't show, and the payout depends on where the winner lands on their hidden card.
- The key thing I want engineered in: a way for people to have *partial* information about how the others will vote — a straw poll, maybe deliberately noisy — so that gaming the system is tempting and sometimes works and sometimes visibly backfires. I want the group to see both outcomes, not just the clever-successful one.
- If you can fit it in, two conditions so we can compare, the way you'd compare against a control: one round where everyone votes blind and honesty does fine, one where they get the peek. The contrast between those two should be what does the teaching, not anything I say.
- My own pet hypothesis, which I want *tested* by the activity rather than argued about: I think the whole mess comes from throwing away intensity. In the lab we never threw away magnitude. So I've invented the "thermometer rule" — everybody marks a 0-10 feeling-dial for each option, you add the numbers up, highest total wins. Build in a round that uses my rule and lets people discover for themselves whether pumping your dial to the extremes pays. If it does, I want them to find the hole in it themselves, not me explaining why its broken afterwards.
- Nobody should hear "strategic voting," "tactical," or any theorem name until the last ten minutes, if at all. Ideally they spend the whole hour thinking they're just picking where the group's end-of-term outing goes.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto specific parts of the idea: what the blind round shows, what the peek round shows, what my thermometer round shows, why "it's just human nature" is the wrong lesson, and the gap between "this rule got gamed once" and "no rule escapes it." Be explicit about which of those two claims the activity actually gives evidence for and which one it doesn't and I'll just have to assert.
- What will predictably go wrong, and what I do about it: someone who votes insincerely out of pure contrarianism and wrecks the payoff data, someone who doesn't get the scoring, the guy who concludes democracy is hopeless, someone who tries to coordinate out loud.
- And level with me about the parts the activity can't reproduce at all. My guesses: the generality claim, the bit where preferences have to be completely unrestricted rather than lined up on some single dimension, and maybe the difference between one person gaming a rule and a bloc doing it. Tell me which of those I have to just say out loud at the end and in what words.
