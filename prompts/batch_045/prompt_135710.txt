Hey, I need help prepping for Q&A. I'm in a senior seminar on ethics and virtual worlds (it's cross-listed with the game design program, so half the room is design and CS people) and my presentation is this Thursday. What I want is a prep sheet, not a defense of the view — I've got the argument, I need to know what's coming at me.

The claim: when a player mistreats a persistent game character, the player can owe a duty that's directed at that character — not just a duty about how to conduct yourself, and not just a duty to other people downstream. I'm not saying the character has rights or can be harmed; the whole point is to say what directedness is left over once you drop those. My move is to split the target three ways: the character-as-process (the code actually running on the machine), the character-as-fiction (the abstract thing the writers made, the way a novel's protagonist is a thing), and the character-as-encounter (what shows up for this player, at this moment, with this history of play behind it). Only the third can carry the duty, and it's a duty with no matching right on the other end — closer to what you might owe a body, a place, or a story someone told you in confidence.

Two moves I keep leaning on. First a substitution test: if the same code ran on a server nobody ever logged into and whose output nobody ever looked at, nothing morally interesting is happening — so whatever is wrong isn't located in the process or in the fiction, it's in the encounter. Second, the save-file problem: you can reload, so nothing is lastingly damaged. My reply is that restoring a save isn't apologizing, and that easy repeatability makes it worse rather than better — a character can be made to suffer more times than any real victim can, and that repeatability is part of what's actually creepy here.

The room:

- The professor is an act-utilitarian who works on global priority and effective giving. He genuinely thinks concern for simulated creatures is misallocated attention. Expect opportunity-cost questions and something like: isn't a duty with no right-holder just our emotional machinery misfiring?
- Two or three game design majors who feel it — everyone in that program has felt bad about being cruel to a companion character — and they'll be annoyed if I explain the feeling away. But they also want to know whether I'm saying you should feel guilty mowing down faceless enemies in a shooter.
- One CS/ML person who will say the character is autocomplete and the "encounter" is a projection, and who'll demand an implementation-level criterion: how is your encounter-object different from an imaginary friend?
- The TA ran our session on fiction-directed emotion — the idea that what you do in a game is imagining, and the artifact prescribes what to imagine. Her objection is that my directed duty is really just a duty about what you're willing to imagine, with extra steps. I think this is the strongest objection in the room and my answer is soft.
- Someone from media studies is auditing and will ask who gets designed as killable, and whether "duty to the character" is a convenient way to dodge questions about who made the game and for whom.

What I want: about eight objections, ordered by how likely they are times how much damage they'd do if I handle them badly; each one phrased the way that particular person would actually put it (the professor in cost-benefit idiom, the CS person in implementation idiom, the design people in player idiom); and for each, what I should concede up front, what I should hold onto, a roughly 90-second reply (I've got about 20 minutes total), and where that reply is weakest. Also: which objection sinks the whole project if I fumble it and which one I can survive fumbling; the question I'm hoping nobody asks; and one thing I should volunteer in the first minute to take some heat off. My advisor thinks the three-way split is to diffuse and I'm not sure he's wrong, so feel free to tell me where it does real work and where it's just scaffolding.
