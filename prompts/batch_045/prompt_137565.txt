hey — I'm in a small reading group this term (about half philosophy people, half ML people) on measurement, incentives and optimization, and one of the ML folks brought in an argument that I'm pretty sure proves way too much, but I can't put my finger on the bad step. I've rewritten it below, lightly cleaned up, with a name I made up stuck into step (3).

(1) A proxy P tells you about a target T only because P and T co-vary in the kind of environment where P was validated. Call that: P is a measure of T.
(2) To optimize P is to select, from the options available to you, whichever scores highest on P; more optimization pressure means selecting from a thinner top slice of the P distribution.
(3) In that top slice, high P is disproportionately noise with respect to T: what gets into the slice is the stuff where the cheap routes to raising P without raising T were available — where the gap I've been calling the *gaming margin* (what you save by raising P the degenerate way rather than the honest way) is positive. So the harder you optimize, the further realized T falls short of what P advertised. This is the step I like most; it's basically the p-hacking story, or paying bounty hunters per rat tail.
(4) But if P is inflated exactly where the action is — among the options you'd actually pick — then P carries no information about T exactly there. And if *every* candidate in your top slice is inflated in the same way, P can't rank them against each other either. So maximizing P gives you no guidance about T at the point of decision.
(5) Every goal-directed system has to run on a proxy in the sense of (1): a planner, a firm, a person can't drag the target itself into deliberation, only some representation of it — a loss function, a KPI, a personal principle, a plan you wrote down on Sunday.
(6) So any goal-directed system that optimizes hard ends up holding a representation that carries no information about what it actually wants: sustained, single-minded pursuit of a goal destroys the agent's grip on the goal.
(7) Hence the sensible policy is to keep your objectives vague on purpose, refuse to quantify, optimize only weakly, and distrust anyone who hands you a metric — which is nuts (insulin dosing, bridge load tables, championship seasons), and which also eats itself, since "don't optimize too hard" is itself a rule you could go optimize.
