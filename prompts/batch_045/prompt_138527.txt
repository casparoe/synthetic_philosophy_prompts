I design metrics for a living — service-level objectives, A/B test guardrails, ranking-quality proxies, reward terms in a learning system — and I have been reading about Goodhart's law because it keeps describing my job back to me in unflattering terms. I want to test it on a case I think I understand, rather than on one of the sanitized examples (the Hanoi rat bounty, Soviet nail quotas) that circulate in blog posts. The case is the Wells Fargo cross-selling scandal.

The facts as I have them, which I do not want to lean on too hard and which I would like you to correct if I have them wrong: Wells Fargo's retail strategy, inherited from Norwest under Richard Kovacevich, treated banking products like supermarket goods and branch staff as salespeople, under a "Going for Gr-Eight" push toward eight products per customer. The headline internal measure was something like products, or "solutions," per household, and for years the bank was widely praised — by analysts and in its own investor communication — as the best cross-seller in American retail banking. Sales goals at branch level were described in a 2013 Los Angeles Times investigation as aggressive to the point of being arithmetically unreachable; employees under pressure opened deposit accounts and credit cards without customer consent, sometimes moving money out of real accounts to fund them, sometimes using a practice called "pinning" to keep control of the account; reports of the abuse went up the chain and, on the account I have, changed little. In September 2016 the CFPB and other regulators announced roughly $185 million in combined fines; the CEO resigned; thousands of rank-and-file employees were terminated; the count of unauthorized accounts was revised upward over the following years, into the low millions. Goodhart's own 1975 formulation, which I take to be the canonical statement, is: "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes."

What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions: the theory applied with discipline to the case, and the case used to interrogate the theory.

1. Set up the mapping carefully before drawing conclusions. What was the actual target the bank cared about (profitable durable relationships? deposits? share of wallet? fee income?) as distinct from the measure; who the agents with leverage were, from the teller to the district manager to the people who set the goals; what each of their real option sets and payoffs were, including the option of quitting or of escalating; what the information structure looked like — who could observe the decoupling, at what lag, and who had both the incentive and the authority to act on it; and what the timescales were. Then say what the theory predicts should have happened at each stage, and where the record matches or doesn't.

2. I don't think "Goodhart's law" names one phenomenon, and I want your help deciding whether that's a real insight or confusion on my part. Here is a provisional taxonomy to place the case against and to criticize or replace: (a) an equilibrium or selection effect, where the population under the metric changes so the old correlation no longer holds — the Lucas-critique flavor, with no one intending anything; (b) gaming proper, where agents optimize the measure while staying inside the rules; (c) fabrication, where the measure is simply falsified or produced by acts that are already illegal on their own terms; (d) constitutive drift, where the measure does not track a prior target at all but partly constitutes what the organization comes to mean by the goal, so that "divergence between measure and target" is not even well defined. Which of these is the Wells Fargo case, or is it several in sequence — did it begin as (a) or (b) and end as (c), and did the cross-sell number migrate from diagnostic to constitutive? Does the fact that much of the conduct was outright fraud put the case inside or outside the law's domain? If outside, that seems to me to be a serious limitation on how people invoke it.

3. Say something about the visibility of the collapse. My sense is that there are two different failure modes and they get conflated: a loud one, where the proxy visibly dissociates from the thing it was standing for and you can read the break off the numbers; and a silent one, where the proxy keeps rising while the composition or quality of what it counts degrades, so nothing inside the metric flags a problem and the only evidence is external. Which happened here, and what, in principle, would have been the canary — inactive-account rates, revenue per household, attrition, complaint volume, ethics-line reports (I have also read that employees who escalated were themselves penalized, but I am not confident of that and I'd like you to say whether the analysis depends on it). Is a canary that nobody is rewarded on the only real protection, and is such a read-only metric stable in an organization, or does it get discovered and consumed?

4. Be explicit about where the theory is silent. It does not obviously explain why this happened at this bank and not at peers with similar metrics; why the measure had such stickiness that it survived public reporting in 2011 and 2013, internal objections, and the fines; why the response fell so heavily on the lowest-paid participants; or how long decoupling can persist before something gives. Say which of those are genuine silences and which a better-specified version of the theory would actually cover.

5. Turn it back on the theory. Does the case confirm it, or does it show the law is nearly a tautology — any measure can be gamed, therefore no measure, therefore nothing actionable? Is it a claim about incentives, or is it at bottom an epistemological claim, namely that you cannot audit a proxy without an independent grip on the target, which is precisely what a proxy is adopted because you lack? Is there a coherent notion of how much optimization pressure a given measure can absorb before it breaks — a threshold one could in principle estimate by ramping pressure against a held-out measure — or is the idea of a tipping point an engineering fantasy? And is the analogy people draw to reward hacking in reinforcement learning apt, or does it mislead by importing a single optimizer with a fixed policy class?

6. Finally, the part I actually get to use. On the basis of the case, what does a person who sets metrics and objectives have license to do: hold out an unexposed measure, keep metrics plural and rotate them, randomize audits, separate the function that measures from the function that rewards, cap the stakes attached to any single number, keep some measures hidden? For each, tell me honestly whether it escapes the dynamic or simply relocates it one level up the measurement hierarchy, and what residual risk I would still be carrying.

Constraints: no citations or further reading, and nothing that depends on a figure I might have garbled — argue from the structure of the case, and correct my facts where they are wrong. Define terms as you go; I have no philosophy background. Where the theory can't speak, say so rather than stretching it. Around 1,200 words.
