For most of my working life I was an experimental condensed-matter physicist. Since retiring I have begun reading philosophy seriously for the first time, mostly in the philosophy of rational action, and I have let myself be talked into giving a forty-minute paper, with twenty minutes of questions afterwards, at a joint meeting of a university philosophy department's rationality seminar and the economics department's decision-theory group. I would like your help preparing for those twenty minutes.

The thesis I am defending is a correction to what I take to be the standard statement of Hofstadter's superrationality. In his Platonia dilemma, some number of people are each given the opportunity to send a single telegram, and a benefactor pays a large prize, to the sender, if and only if exactly one telegram arrives. Hofstadter's superrational player reasons that everyone will run the same argument and so each rolls an N-sided die, sending with probability 1/N. That, I claim, is a rule about actions: match your own probability of acting so that the expected number of senders is one. I argue that the object of choice should instead be the payoff-relevant event, and that the two come apart as soon as implementation is noisy.

My central example is what I call the Noisy Courier variant. As before there are N players and a prize paid if and only if exactly one telegram arrives, but each telegram independently fails to arrive with probability ε. The action-level rule still prescribes p = 1/N. The outcome-level rule I defend prescribes p = 1/(N(1−ε)): participation is scaled up so that the expected number of arriving telegrams, not the expected number sent, is one. As ε goes to zero the two coincide, so the standard case is a limiting special case of mine; for moderate noise the action-level rule discards a substantial fraction of the available expected value. I generalise as follows: choose the policy that maximises expected payoff conditional on being a randomly selected agent who follows it. I then suggest that the same principle governs over-participation in the Luring Lottery, where the prize shrinks as the number of entrants grows, and in volunteer's-dilemma situations with a threshold that must be reached but not overshot.

There will be about thirty people in the room. The ones I am anxious about are: two formal epistemologists, both causal decision theorists, who regard Newcomb-style reasoning as a disease and will want to know what causal structure I think connects my deliberation to that of the other players; a game theorist from the economics side whose first question will be whether my policy profile is an equilibrium at all, and who will want the deviation payoffs written on the board; an evolutionary game theorist who works on the evolution of cooperation and will ask what any of this has to do with real organisms; and a moral philosopher of broadly Kantian sympathies, who will want to know whether I have rediscovered universalisation and dressed it in dice, and whether maximising group expected value under the word rational is simply utilitarianism smuggled in. The rest are graduate students and a few retired scientists like myself, who will ask the plain questions about the arithmetic and about whether any of it has ever been tested.

Because I expect a hostile framing, I plan to concede at the outset that the superrational profile is not a Nash equilibrium, that a player who defects to sending with probability one does better by a factor of roughly N, and to argue that equilibrium is the wrong desideratum when the players are by stipulation running identical reasoning. That is the weakest joint in the paper, and I would rather be told so than reassured.

What I would like from you is a preparation sheet for the question period, not a defence of the thesis. Please give me the eight to ten objections I should actually expect, ordered by how likely they are to be raised combined with how much damage they would do, each phrased the way the person I have described would phrase it rather than the way I would. For each, tell me what I should grant immediately, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and note where that reply is soft or quietly assumes what is in question. Point out which one or two objections have enough force that I should concede them in the paper itself rather than wait to be asked, and which are misunderstandings I can dissolve in a sentence. Finally, give me the question I should hope nobody asks, the one that cannot be answered in two minutes or that I have no answer to.
