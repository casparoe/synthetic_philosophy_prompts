So I'm selling my car this week and a guy came and looked at it, asked me straight out "has it been in an accident?". I said "not with me", which is true — I've had it four years and nothing's happened — but I did see in the folder the dealer gave me that there was bodywork on the passenger side before I owned it, and I'm fairly sure that was a crash. He bought it, found the filler, called me furious and said I lied to him. I said I didn't lie, which is true and also feels like the single worst sentence available to me. My brother, who is enjoying this, told me "a half truth is a whole lie" and that I owe the guy his money back exactly as if I'd looked him in the eye and said "no".

I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real moral point or just something that sounds good. Can you lay out the different precise things that sentence could be claiming, from the boring to the strong? Like: is it saying a misleading truth literally *is* a lie, that the two are the same kind of act? Or only that they're equally blameworthy? Or equally bad *for the hearer*? Or that they come from the same attitude toward the truth? Or that the half truth is actually worse, because it buys you deniability? And for each one — is it trivially true in a way that doesn't get my brother anything, is it substantive and defensible, or is it false? Because I keep thinking there have to be cases where the distinction does real work: a stranger asking what I earn, someone I owe confidentiality to, a situation where I'm allowed to just not answer at all. But maybe I'm reaching for excuses.

I also tried splitting it myself and I don't know if it helps: there seems to be a difference between merely letting a wrong assumption stand (say he'd never asked and I'd said nothing) versus picking a true sentence *because* of the false thing it would make him believe. My instinct is the proverb is obviously right about the second and much less obviously right about the first, but my instinct is also the one with an interest here. And does it matter how easily one more question would have undone it? If he'd said "any accidents ever?" instead of "with you?" I'd have had to either lie out loud or come clean, and I sort of wish he had, because then I'd at least know what I did.

Oh and my brother's extra bit: that the fact I chose my words carefully shows I knew exactly what the full answer would do, so I'm more culpable than someone who panics and lies outright. Is that a separate claim or is it smuggled in by the slogan?

Give me it as a list I can argue with, and tell me which readings actually support his conclusion that I owe the money back.
