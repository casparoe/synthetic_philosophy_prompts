I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about how people who are actually trying to do good with money or with a career decide *what* to work on. It started with a dinner last month with three friends — one works at a big foundation, one runs a tiny nonprofit, one's an economist — where we tried to rank a list of causes on a napkin and ended up sort of shouting about why. Since then I've been keeping a spreadsheet of my own gut verdicts on stylized cases, and the pattern is embarassing: I keep saying yes to things that the usual importance/tractability/neglectedness scoring seems to rank low, and no to things it seems to rank high. My working formula is roughly impact ≈ importance × tractability × (1 − how crowded the *specific* slot is) + value-of-what-you-learn − credit-you'd-have-gotten-anyway, but I can't make it behave, and I don't trust it. So: here are my verdicts, on made-up-but-plausible cases (please don't worry about whether the numbers are real, I want the structure). Deworming-scale program, huge number of people affected, cheap, solid evidence, one small funder in the country: obvious yes. A therapy area with thousands of researchers where the actual binding constraint is that *nobody* is compiling the regulatory toxicology dossiers — zero people on it, cheap to fund, whole pipeline waiting: yes, top tier, even though the field is about as crowded as a field can be by headcount. A much-loved charity that will definately hit its funding target with or without me, so my 50k just frees up another donor's 50k: worth roughly nothing, no. A 0.5% shot at averting something catastrophic, where if it fails I learn a lot and can try a different approach next year: yes, fund it. A one-shot long shot with an even bigger nominal payoff — a durable artifact meant to shape some far-future civilization — tiny chance, enormous stakes, but if it fails I learn nothing and the window closes for good: no. (This d/e pair is the one I keep going back and forth on; I can't say what the difference is even supposed to *be* in a formula.) A pure diagnostic study that helps nobody directly but would redirect a large, named foundation's next 200M toward something probably-decent: yes. The same study, except the only funders in that space are locked into their priorities for a decade and won't move: meh, no — so learning on its own isn't enough. A field-building program that would create a community of people who will pick causes themselves fifteen years from now, in ways I can neither predict nor steer: suspicious, I'd rather fund the diagnostic study — even though that also works by changing what other people's money does later, which is my second uncomfortable pair. And finally: an intervention with genuinely good immediate benefits (big cash transfers, say) that is delivered through a patronage network and thereby entrenches that network, making a better reform harder for a generation: I want to subtract a lot, maybe flip it to a no.

So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or nearly? I'd like it stated precisely enough that I could actually put it in a spreadsheet, as a formula or as a short list of clauses, with the case that forces each clause into the picture. Then I want it to earn its keep by telling me what to think about cases I haven't listed — give me at least three of your own, ideally of different shapes from mine (something like: a cause only I personally could work on and nobody could be hired for; a cause whose importance hinges on one factual question I could resolve cheaply this month; a cause whose success would make the problem come back worse in twenty years). And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate and would have me overturn, and which verdict you'd drop if you had to choose between a messy principle that saves all of mine and a clean one that loses one or two.

Could you lay it out in four named sections, roughly: "The formula" (about 250 words, stated precisely, no hedging), "Clause by clause" (about 350 words, one motivating case per clause), "Three cases you haven't seen" (about 250 words), and "Verdicts you'd have me drop" (about 150 words, including the choice between messy-and-complete and clean-and-lossy).
