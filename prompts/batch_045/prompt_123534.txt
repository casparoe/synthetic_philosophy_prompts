I am a principal engineer with no background in philosophy, and I have recently taken over ownership of engineering standards for a product group of about seventy people, split between a London office and a smaller office in another country. Every month or so I have to decide, for one practice or another, whether to make it a hard rule enforced in CI or in code review, a default that people may depart from if they leave a note explaining why, or a matter left to individual judgement. The decisions are frequent, individually cheap to reverse but corrosive when churned, and each one has to be written up as a one-page recommendation that the engineering leadership team signs off and that a sceptical group of senior engineers will actually read.

The material I have been pointed to is the literature on tight and loose cultures: the claim that groups differ in how strong their norms are and how much deviance they tolerate, that this difference tracks how exposed the group is to threats and how costly miscoordination is for it, and that tightness buys predictability and coordination at the price of autonomy, experimentation, openness to novelty and tolerance of dissent. I do not want a summary of that literature. I want it turned into a procedure a tech lead can run in about twenty minutes, with no knowledge of the theory, on a single practice at a time.

One thing I would like tested rather than assumed. My suspicion is that tightness is not one dial but two: how wide a range of situations a rule claims to cover, and how much a departure from it is sanctioned when it happens. A convention can be broad and gently enforced, or narrow and strictly enforced, and the trade-offs look different in each case. If that distinction is worth keeping, build the procedure around it; if it collapses under scrutiny, say so and use whatever distinction does the work.

Please give me: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Then run it on three practices I am currently arguing about: requiring two approvals on every pull request; enforcing formatting and lint rules automatically in CI; and whether an on-call engineer during an incident must follow the runbook step by step or may improvise. I have a strong prior on the third one — I think improvisation should be allowed — so tell me plainly if the procedure contradicts me. Finally, be explicit about which of the theory's distinctions had to survive the simplification and which you dropped, and about what the procedure cannot tell me: I especially want to know what happens to it given that the theory was built to describe whole societies, while my engineers can leave, and given that some of our rules may simply be wrong.
