Hey — I run a tiny student podcast attached to my philosophy of psychiatry seminar (it's me and two other people from the reading group, we get maybe 40 downloads an episode, whatever). Next week's guest is a psychiatrist-philosopher with a new book defending what she calls proleptic nosology. Roughly: given Hacking's point that psychiatric kinds are looping kinds — the people classified get wind of the classification, change how they experience and describe themselves, and the kind becomes a moving target — she argues we should stop judging a diagnostic category by how well it carves nature at the joints and judge it by the loops it sets off. Her line in the book, which she repeats a lot: ask what a category does, not what it is. Her two concrete policy proposals are (1) sunset clauses — every category gets reauthorized or retired on a fixed cycle, because she thinks categories have a kind of half-life before feedback from self-narrative, insurance codes, disability paperwork and identity politics swamps whatever signal they had — and (2) what she calls stealth categories: research-only diagnoses deliberately kept out of clinical charts and out of public view so they never get the chance to loop.

We've already done Hacking and a bit of the DSM revision history, so I don't want to burn the episode on so what is a looping effect, explain it for our listeners. I want eight to ten questions that press where the view is weakest, and for each one I want your guess at the answer she's most likely to give plus a follow-up aimed straight at that answer. At minimum: one on whether she has any standard for a good loop that isn't itself loop-shaped (if suffering and functioning are her proxies, those reports are exactly what changes once a vocabulary circulates); one on stealth categories and informed consent, plus how she stops them leaking once clinicians talk and patients google; one on why we shouldn't just escape the loop by going mechanism-first or dimensional, in the style of the NIMH research-domain approach or the hierarchical trait taxonomies — I assume she says the loop just relocates downstream once people start saying things like my amygdala — and I want the follow-up on whether there's any level of description that is loop-proof at all, or whether the retreat to mechanism is insufficient rather than pointless.

Then on real cases: one question on DSM-5 deleting the bereavement exclusion for depressive disorders and the text revision later adding prolonged grief disorder as its own diagnosis — by her own lights was that a good loop or a bad one, and how would you tell inside a decade; one on the autism/neurodiversity case, where the people classified organized around the category and then demanded a say in it, and how she distinguishes legitimate uptake by advocacy movements from corrupting capture by pharma or insurers without it just being her politics; one on her sunset clause for someone whose disability payments, accomodations and sense of self are all riding on a label that's scheduled to expire; and one on measurement — how you'd tell a genuine loop from diagnostic substitution or criteria creep, say in the childhood bipolar story where a new category was added partly to soak up the overdiagnosis, given that the category is precisely what makes people and clinicians notice in the first place. End with one question about what evidence would actually count against her: what finding, if we had it, would make proleptic nosology a bad idea rather than just an unfashionable one. Assume she's sharp and has heard every obvious objection, so nothing like isn't this just social constructivism.
