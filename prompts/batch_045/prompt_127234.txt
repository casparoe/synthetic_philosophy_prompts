I'm a PhD student working on political obligation, and I want to press the issue through an institutional design rather than through another invented case. Consider a jurisdiction that operates a "conscience registry": anyone who intends to break a law on the ground that it is unjust must register in advance, naming the statute, the intended act, and the reasons; must act openly and nonviolently and submit to arrest; and is then tried, convicted, and sentenced like anyone else. Two further features do the real work. First, the sentence is suspended and the conviction is later vacated — the person being formally declared to have been in the right — if and only if the statute is repealed or struck down within ten years of the breach; the state never rules on the merits of the protest at the time it happens. Second, registrants must reimburse the state for the full costs of policing, prosecution, and detention, and anyone who cannot pay is disqualified from the scheme and serves the sentence as an ordinary offender.

What account of political obligation does this package presuppose? In particular: does the retrospective condition presuppose that the citizen's duty is settled by the polity's later verdict rather than by the justice of the law at the time of action — that the grounds of the duty are not yet fixed when the agent must decide — and is that coherent, or does it collapse into saying the disobedient simply gambled correctly? Does the reimbursement condition treat the obligation as a debt dischargeable by payment, and if so does it quietly presuppose that the disobedient has done nothing warranting condemnation, which sits badly with convicting her in the first place? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, and distinguish what the registry strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: could someone who holds that legal obligation is content-independent but overridable, or someone who denies that there is any general duty to obey the law, operate the same registry for different reasons? Which parts of the design pull in opposite directions? And what would the jurisdiction have to change if it replaced the presupposed view with either an associative or natural-duty account or a no-general-duty view? Be explicit about where the practice is philosophically underdetermined and where several theories would license the same institution. Please write in American English.
