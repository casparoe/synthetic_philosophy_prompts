I am teaching one ninety-minute session of a graduate seminar in formal epistemology next week, and I want a plan for the session rather than an exposition.

The students have unconditional and conditional credences, Bayes' theorem, and the reflex of writing P_new(H) = P(H|E) whenever something happens to them. None of them has seen probability kinematics. The update I want them to be able to perform and to justify by the end is the one that replaces conditioning when the experience does not deliver any proposition with certainty: on a partition whose members the experience leaves rigid, P_new(H) is the sum over the cells of P(H|E_i) weighted by P_new(E_i).

Three things I want from you. First, the dependency structure: the order in which the pieces have to arrive, and what breaks if two of them are swapped — in particular whether rigidity should be introduced as a constraint the agent imposes on her own update, as a property of causal or explanatory independence between the experience and the partition, or as whatever is left over once you have said what the experience does. Second, the carrying example. I had intended the standard case of examining a coloured object under unreliable light; I want your judgement on whether that is the right case to build the whole session around, and on what a case must contain and must not contain here. My own crude list is that it must make rigidity on the chosen partition genuinely plausible, that it must not contain a hard proposition the student could condition on instead and thereby recover the reflex I am trying to break, and that it must be iterable, so that the later part of the session can show that applying the same soft input twice is not the same as applying it once. Third, the misconception map: which wrong things they will think or construct, and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off.

There is a further wrinkle I cannot place, and I would like you either to place it or to tell me to drop it. Call a soft update foreseen when the agent knows in advance both the partition and the marginal the incoming experience will produce, and can choose whether to undergo it. In such a case I have a small example in which the agent does strictly better in expectation by declining the free experience: the very experience that is supposed to leave the partition rigid also gives her reason to think the partition was the wrong one to hold fixed, so the condition licensing the update is itself among the things the update destabilises. Should I teach this as a pathology of the framework, as evidence that rigidity has to be relativised to the experience rather than to the partition, as a reason to distinguish the update rule from the account of evidence that feeds it, or not at all in a first session? And if it belongs, where: before the students can compute anything, or only after they can?

Finally: what should I openly refuse to explain in ninety minutes, and what single task at the end would tell me they had mastered the rule rather than memorised the summation?
