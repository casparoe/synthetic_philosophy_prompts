I've been going back and forth with a friend who works in risk management about how to actually weigh uncertain prospects, and I've realized I don't have a principle at all — just a pile of verdicts that I'm pretty sure don't fit together. I sat in on maybe half of a decision theory course years ago, so I know roughly what an expected utility calculation looks like, and I know enough to recognize which textbook examples these are; please dont just label them for me or point me at papers, I want the thing underneath.

Here's where I land. Certain $50,000 versus a coin flip at $0 or $120,000, one shot, my own money: I take the $50,000 and I don't feel irrational doing it. Same shape of choice, but I'm deciding for a tiny grants budget at a nonprofit where the money buys roughly linear value: I take the gamble and I'd defend it — and she says those two can't both be right, which I think is wrong but I can't say why. One percent chance at $10 million versus $80,000 certain: I take the gamble, which is risk-seeking about five minutes after being risk-averse, and no smooth concave utility-of-money curve does both. Skydiving, roughly a 1-in-100,000 chance of dying, freely chosen: fine, I don't want to be told I'm being inconsistent. A plant upwind that imposes that same 1-in-100,000 on me: I'd pay real money to shut it down, way more than I'd accept as payment to stop skydiving. (Though — and this is the part that bugs me — I sort of did consent when I bought the house, and that doesn't fully flip the verdict back.) Five percent chance of losing the house to a fire that strikes houses independently: unpleasant, insurable, I deal with it. Five percent chance of losing it to a flood that takes the whole valley at once: I feel much worse and would pay much more, even though my own probability and my own loss are identical. A small positive-EV coin flip: I'll take it once and I'd take it ten thousand times. A gamble with a 0.1% chance of wiping me out: I might take it once, and I absolutely would not take it ten thousand times, which is backwards if repetition is supposed to make risk stop mattering. And on the big tail, I'd pay a lot — more than any of the above risk aversion seems to license — to shave a small probability off something civilizational going wrong, but I won't hand $20 to the guy who says he'll torture an absurdly huge number of simulated people unless I do.

The principle I keep coming back to is something like: risk aversion isn't curvature in my utility function at all, it's a fee I charge on uncertainty I didn't agree to and can't spread. Test that against the list, repair it where it breaks, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it can't save and what you'd put in the verdict's place. I'd also like it applied to the case I go back and forth on: my sixteen-year-old wants to take up something with maybe a 1-in-500 chance of a serious injury a season, I did the same thing at her age and I'd take that risk myself today for the same enjoyment, and yet I lie awake about it and "she consented" doesn't settle it for me even though consent is doing all the work in the skydiving case. Give me one principle, or an explicit small set of clauses with the case that motivates each, plus its verdict on a case I haven't listed — ideally one where it says something I'd initially reject. Commit: don't hand me a menu of frameworks and leave me to pick, and mark your confidence, both in the principle as a whole and clause by clause, including where you're just guessing.
