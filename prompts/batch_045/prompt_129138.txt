I'm finishing the position paper for my seminar on collective intentionality and joint action, and before I commit to the version I'll be defending in class, I'd like an audit of whether the package I've landed on actually hangs together. Here is the set, more or less as I would state it:

1. Shared intention is irreducible. No summation, aggregate or distributive pattern of individual intentions makes it the case that *we* intend something, and adding that each of us knows what the others intend doesn't close the gap either. The plural form is the primitive one, and any reconstruction of it out of I-intentions plus beliefs leaves something out.

2. There is no group mind. Every intentional state, including a we-intention, is a state of an individual person — on the naturalist version, a feature of an individual nervous system. Talk of the group intending is shorthand at best. (I got to 1 and 2 through Searle, who seems to hold both, which is why I have some confidence here.)

3. What separates genuinely joint action from merely parallel activity is something in the neighbourhood of Bratman's conditions: mutual responsiveness, commitment to the joint activity, commitment to mutual support, and the whole structure out in the open between the participants rather than privately duplicated in each head.

4. The case I want at the centre of the account — not at the margins, and not as a limiting or degenerate case — is two strangers who make eye contact over a stalled car and push it together. No plan, no prior arrangement, no communication beyond the glance, over in twenty seconds. If my account can't make this the clearest case of acting together, I take that to be a problem for the account.

5. But if it later emerges that you never intended that *we* do it — you had decided from the start to walk away at the crucial moment — then we never shared an intention and we never really did anything together, however the episode looked from the outside, even if the outward cooperation went on for weeks.

6. Shared agency has a real normative structure: participants owe one another support, and if one of them abandons the project without warning the others are entitled to demand an explanation. This is not merely prudential and not merely local convention, and I don't want to derive it from joint commitments in Gilbert's sense — on my understanding, the commitment is supposed to generate obligations just by being made, and that looks like magic to me. I'd rather get it out of the structure of planning agency.

7. Separately, and I'm not sure it belongs in this paper at all: I'm a Humean about normative reasons. All reasons for action are grounded in the agent's own antecedent desires or ends.

Two things I'd particularly like adjudicated. First, I keep worrying about 1 and 2, and a classmate told me the real problem is 1 and 3 and that I have to choose between them; I don't see it, but I'd like to know which of us is wrong and why. Second, 4 and 5 feel to me as though they are doing something to 3 that I haven't thought through — each seems fine on its own, but I have the sense that holding both puts a pressure on the middle of 3 that I can't quite locate, and I'd like that located for me if it's real.

Please go through 1–7 and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and for each real tension what would have to give. Rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging them all as equally bad — I'd rather know which one is load-bearing — and for each, give the cheapest revision and say what it costs me elsewhere in the set. Be specific about which claim is doing the work in each conflict; I don't want a general diagnosis. At the end, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether that revision is one I could make without giving up 4, which is the commitment I care about most.
