I write long-form features on science and ideas. The piece I'm working on now is about how social norms come into being and come apart, and its spine is David Lewis's 1969 account of convention: a regularity of behavior counts as a convention for a population only if nearly everyone conforms to it, nearly everyone expects nearly everyone else to conform, and each person prefers to conform on condition that enough of the others do, so that the regularity is a self-sustaining solution to a recurring coordination problem rather than a habit, a rule backed by sanctions, or an agreement. Lewis also held that conventions are held in place by precedent and salience, that they are sustained by common knowledge rather than by mere first-order conformity, and that they are to some degree arbitrary, in that some alternative regularity could have done the same work.

I don't want to open the piece with an abstract example. I want to open it with fiction. Please write me that story, 900 to 1,400 words, third person, literary rather than allegorical.

The story must do a specific job. A regularity arises in a defined population, holds for years, and then collapses. Nothing about the material situation changes, and no one's preferences over outcomes change. What changes is only what the participants believe about what the others believe. The agent of that change should be a journalist who has somehow come into possession of a measurement of the population's private attitudes and must decide whether to publish it.

Four constraints, because these are exactly the points at which the obvious version of this story goes wrong:

1. Do not make the regularity neatly arbitrary. Give one of the candidate regularities a small, visible, genuine advantage that everyone in the population can see, and have the community settle on the other one anyway, because of an accident of precedent or because that one was more salient at the moment it formed. Build that fact into why the convention turns out to be fragile.

2. The collapse must not read as liberation. The regularity should be load-bearing for something the characters value — an informal insurance arrangement, an unwritten truce, a rescue practice, an unspoken division of a shared resource — so that the journalist's decision is a real decision and the reader finishes unsure whether publication was worth it.

3. The measurement itself should be questionable in a way the story makes felt: the private attitudes were elicited in a context in which people had some reason to misreport, and the journalist cannot distinguish the hypothesis that the convention is hollow from the hypothesis that respondents told the interviewer what was safe to tell an interviewer. Let that ambiguity survive to the end.

4. At some point a character should offer a functional explanation of the practice, of the form that it persists because it does such-and-such for us. The story must leave it genuinely open whether that is the cause of the practice's persistence or a rationalization its participants would have produced with equal conviction for a different regularity.

Do not name Lewis, do not use any technical vocabulary, and do not allow any character to explain the mechanism or to draw the moral. Whatever is conceptual has to be carried entirely by what happens, by what the characters notice, and by what they fail to notice.

After the story, add a note of no more than 200 words, addressed to me as your editor. List the episodes of the story and say which element of the Lewisian account each was written to dramatize, and flag any moment where you think the regularity in your story stops being a convention proper and slides into one of its near neighbors — mere imitation, a rule enforced by penalties, an explicit agreement, a statistical equilibrium that nobody has any beliefs about at all. I want to be able to check that the fiction illustrates the thing my article is about rather than something in the same neighborhood.
