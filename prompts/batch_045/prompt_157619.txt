I'm giving a works-in-progress talk next Thursday to a history department colloquium and I need Q&A prep badly. Twenty minute talk, then they open the floor and it's basically a free for all for half an hour.

Who's in the room. Maybe 25 people. The majority are mainstream social and cultural historians — early modern Europe, twentieth-century US, one person on the Ottoman Empire — all archive people, none of them run anything more quantitative than a spreadsheet of probate inventories, and several of them think the whole quant-history project is, in one senior person's actual phrase I've heard twice, "astrology with differential equations"; they care about contingency, about agency, about not flattening cases into data points, and they're allergic to anachronistic categories like "the state" applied to 1350. Then there are three cliometric / economic history types who DO run regressions on price and wage series; they'll want my unit of observation, my N, whether I have any identification strategy at all, and they'll say things like "you're fitting a curve to a dozen data points." There's one archaeologist who works on long-run settlement survey data and will go straight at my proxies and my dating error. One political scientist who does state formation and fiscal capacity — probably the friendliest person in the room and also the one who'll ask whether "the state" is doing real work in my model or whether I secretly mean a fiscal-military apparatus. And a philosopher of science who's skeptical that history can have laws at all and will press on what would even count as the model being wrong.

My thesis, in three parts:

(1) The way structural-demographic / secular-cycle models get tested right now is close to worthless as a test. People check whether the model reproduces a timeline of unrest, but almost any oscillator with a tunable period will do that, so it's postdiction dressed up as prediction. Even the much-quoted claim that the US was heading into a peak of instability around 2020 doesn't vindicate the theory if turmoil does show up.

(2) The variable that's actually doing the work isn't popular immiseration, it's the ratio of credentialed elite aspirants to available offices — call it aspirant pressure — and the cycle's clock is set by generational turnover in the elite, not by Malthusian population growth. So the period should be roughly a fixed multiple of the elite generation length (age at entry to office, plus time in it), and it should track that across polities rather than tracking population growth rates. That's the test that discriminates between two models the current data can't tell apart.

(3) To show the framework travels, my third case isn't a state at all: the American research university since 1945, with tenured faculty as the elite, PhDs as aspirants, tuition plus state appropriations plus endowment as the fiscal base, and public trust and enrollment demand as the legitimacy measure. I know this one is a provocation and I definately expect it to get the most pushback.

Part 2 is the load-bearing one. If that goes down, the talk is dead.

What I want from you: a prep sheet, not a defense. Eight or so objections in the order I'll actually get them, weighted by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, and say which faction each one comes from — I want them phrased the way that person would phrase it, in their vocabulary, not in mine. For each: what I say back in about ninety seconds (no slides in Q&A, and they will interrupt me), and where my reply is soft. Also: what do I concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks anything; what's the one reply that has to land for the whole thing to survive; and — my favorite part — the question I'm hoping nobody asks, plus what I'd say if they do.

One more thing. When you tell me what to hold and what to give up, please seperate the claims that are genuinely contested among people who actually work on this from the near-consensus background stuff, so I don't walk in sounding like my contested bits are settled. And if one of my replies would depend on some dataset or number I probably don't have, say so rather than writing me a reply I can't back up. Don't bother with citations, I know the literature.
