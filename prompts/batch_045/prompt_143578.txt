You are a professional philosopher who works on causation, and you have also spent years advising hospital incident-investigation teams. Please answer as both at once, and think carefully rather than quickly: this is going straight into a document that will be used on real cases.

Before you write the procedure, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming, and show me that thinking. In particular: what has to be true of something for it to be a cause at all; whether an absence or a failure to act can satisfy that requirement, or only fail to satisfy it in an interestingly different way; why a simple would-it-have-happened-anyway test seems to return far too many causes once failures to act are allowed in; and where you expect the untutored judgments of a clinical panel to be systematically unreliable. Then move to the procedure, and keep it consistent with what you concluded in the brainstorming.

My situation: I am a consultant geriatrician, and about six months ago I was asked to chair our hospital's serious-incident review panel. I took the job because of one case: a junior doctor's failure to escalate a deteriorating patient overnight was recorded in the report as a cause of the patient's death, the report went to the trust board and then to the regulator, and the doctor's career has been derailed. I remain unsure whether the panel knew what it was saying. Since then I have sat on eight panels, and we have the same unproductive argument every time, in different words. The panel has six members (three clinicians, a senior nurse, a manager, a lay member), ninety minutes per case, and no philosophy training whatsoever. Our reports go to the trust board, may be disclosed to a coroner, and are read by bereaved families. We have three verdicts available to us, and I now think the problem is partly that we do not know what any of them means: causal factor, contributing factor, learning point.

Here are the recurring patterns that make me think we need an actual procedure rather than better instincts.

One: the blank box. A dose chart with an unticked box, or a set of observations recorded and not acted on, produces a confident verdict of causal factor. An identical physical consequence reached because no one ever thought of the patient at all — no one considered a scan, no one considered a review — produces hesitation and usually no finding. The difference feels evidential rather than causal, but we never say so, and I suspect we are conflating what we can prove with what happened.

Two: the description. Failure to escalate, failure to escalate within an hour, failure to escalate to the outreach team, failure to escalate to a registrar who was not simultaneously covering two other wards. Our verdict flips with the grain of description and we pick the grain unconsciously. I need the procedure to force the description to be fixed, and to say which grain is the right one and why.

Three: the drifting baseline. Once we say what would have happened if the act had been performed, we have to decide what else in that hypothetical world is allowed to differ. Do we hold fixed that the ward was short-staffed, that the registrar was elsewhere, that the monitor had been unplugged? In practice I watch panels freeze whatever makes the individual look worse and let vary whatever makes the individual look better. I want an explicit rule about what is held fixed, and I want you to be honest about the places where no rule works and it is a judgment call that we should at least make in the open.

Four: cases where what failed to happen is itself something that would have stopped the harm. The alarm that did not sound, the alert that was configured off, the consultant who did not arrive to do the procedure that would have prevented the bleed. These feel different from the simple failures and I do not know whether they belong in the same category or a separate one, or whether our form needs a second box.

Five: the diffused failure. Three teams each assumed another had booked an outpatient scan; no individual omission looks necessary, because any one of the three would have booked it had the others not. The group's failure does look necessary. Our reporting form only has room for named individuals, so we currently either invent a culprit or record nothing.

Six: failures by whoever is not in the room. Nobody recruited the extra consultant; nobody implemented the early-warning score the trust had already agreed to buy. I do not know where these stop being causes and become something else — background, or history, or the reason we are here at all.

What I want from you:

A written decision procedure the panel can run inside ninety minutes, as an ordered set of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what evidence satisfies it, and what we do when the evidence is simply absent. It must say where the procedure stops with a firm finding of no causal factor, where it returns that the question cannot be settled on the available evidence, and where it stops and hands the decision to us because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. I think there is a values decision hiding in what the record is for — explaining what happened, learning how to prevent the next one, or assigning accountability — and I think we are currently answering all three with one word. If you agree, make that handoff explicit and tell us how to phrase it in a report.

It must also tell me which distinctions from the philosophical literature have to survive the translation into our meeting and which can be dropped without real loss, and why each is on the side it is on. I would rather drop something true than keep something our lay member cannot apply. And I want to know where the procedure will misfire.

Then apply it, in full, to three of our cases.

The first is the overnight deterioration: observations showing a falling blood pressure and rising respiratory rate were taken at 02:00 and 05:00 and not escalated; the patient had a cardiac arrest at 07:00 and died; the ward had one nurse for twelve patients that night and the outreach team was already attending two other arrests. The second is the configured-off drug alert: a patient with a documented severe allergy was prescribed the drug, no alert fired, the patient had an anaphylactic reaction and survived with harm; the alert had been disabled for that ward eleven months earlier by a member of staff who has since left, as part of a workaround for alert fatigue that nobody ever reviewed. The third is the unbooked scan: the discharge summary said outpatient imaging to be arranged; three separate teams each assumed another had arranged it; the malignancy was found eleven months later at a stage that changed the prognosis.

Finally, and this matters as much as the rest: I expect the procedure to give us an answer the panel will dislike on at least one of these, and I suspect it is the first one, where the staffing and the other arrests may make the escalation into a non-difference-maker. Tell me which case you think it will be, and then tell me how to write that finding so that it does not read to a bereaved family, or to the trust board, as an exoneration of the person who did not escalate. If the right answer is that we need a sentence distinguishing causation from blame, give me that sentence and explain why it does the work.
