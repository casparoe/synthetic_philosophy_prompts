I spent most of my working life in experimental condensed-matter physics, and only since retiring have I begun attending a philosophy seminar at the nearby university, so I hope you will forgive the somewhat amateur framing of what follows. We recently went through Hume's argument that our expectation that the future will resemble the past cannot be defended by reasoning without circularity, and I found myself wondering how he would have received a family of results that postdates him by more than two centuries.

Here is the situation I would like you to imagine. Hume is brought up to speed on modern probability and computation and seated on a review panel for a very large grant application whose aim is to build a single general-purpose inference engine: one fixed learning procedure that is supposed to take data from any domain whatsoever and produce reliable predictions, without any further hand-tuning by human experts, and which is explicitly intended to be deployed on instruments measuring phenomena for which no one has yet worked out any theory. The panel then shows him the no-free-lunch results familiar from machine learning and optimization: averaged over all possible data-generating processes, or over all possible objective functions in the optimization version, every procedure does exactly as well as every other one, including a procedure that predicts by tossing a coin, so no quantity of evidence can certify one learner as better in general. The panel adds that these results hold even though learners carrying a built-in preference for smooth, simple, or otherwise compressible hypotheses do outperform the average competitor by a wide margin on the curated bank of real-world benchmark tasks the panel uses in practice; my grandson, who builds such systems for a living, insists that the theorems simply have no bearing on practice, because nobody has ever wanted a learner that succeeds on every possible world.

Suppose, finally, that a fellow member of my seminar, a retired ecologist, objects that the benchmark result proves nothing at all: the tasks that end up in such collections are, she says, precisely the ones on which investigators already knew that induction worked, so the collection is filtered by the very success it is meant to demonstrate. Please put yourself in Hume's place and give me his response as exactly five numbered points of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no concluding summary, covering in order: which elements of his own position he would take the theorems to confirm; where he would say they miss the target; what he would make of the built-in preference for simplicity as a proposed way out; how he would answer my colleague's filtering objection; and, the part I find most interesting, whether he would conclude that the success of science rests on a contingent and unjustifiable fact about the world rather than on any virtue of our methods. Speculate freely where the historical texts run out; I am after the reasoning rather than scholarly caution.
