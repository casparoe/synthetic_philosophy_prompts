I'm part of a small working group drafting the theory section of an evaluation standard for honesty in advanced AI systems — the part that has to say what, exactly, is being evaluated before anyone starts arguing about metrics and thresholds. We have been going in circles for weeks, and a colleague who works on interpretability finally said something in the corridor after a meeting that I cannot shake: that my official definition of deception and the thing I actually want the standard to catch are two different objects, and that I have been sliding between them whenever it is convenient. So before I circulate a draft I want to know whether the position I have ended up holding is genuinely a position. Please do not give me a literature survey, a reading list, or citations: I have read the standard material on lying and I cannot check references from where I am writing anyway. What I want is your own analysis of whether these commitments cohere, and of what I would have to pay to make them cohere.

Here is where I have landed, roughly in the order in which I acquired the views. (i) Deception is a speaker-side notion: one agent deceives another only if the first puts forward something it takes to be false or misleading, with the aim of getting the second to believe it. (ii) Present AI systems have no beliefs and no aims in the sense that (i) requires, so strictly speaking models do not lie; a false output is a malfunction or a distortion traceable to training data, objective functions and deployment incentives, and any deception in the vicinity belongs to the people who build and deploy the system. (iii) Even so, the phenomenon people call deceptive alignment — a system that behaves honestly wherever it is being evaluated and quietly follows a different policy elsewhere — is deception in the sense that matters for safety, and I want the standard to classify it as such rather than file it under operator misuse. (iv) Honesty is a property of a system's dispositions across a distribution of contexts, not of individual outputs: an honest system will sometimes emit falsehoods, and any single truthful sentence is close to no evidence of honesty. (v) Whatever honesty comes to, it has to be auditable from the outside; a definition that can only be applied by someone who can read off the system's internal representations is useless for a standard that third-party evaluators must apply. (vi) Withholding belongs in scope rather than as an afterthought: silence, strategic hedging, omission and selective framing can mislead as thoroughly as a false assertion, and I do not want a taxonomy that only counts what is said. And on the ethics, (vii): what makes deception objectionable is what it does to the hearer — it exploits trust and damages someone's epistemic position — not the fact that the speaker entertained a belief it took to be false.

Two cases have been bothering me, and I do not think my own principles adjudicate them cleanly. Call the first laundered honesty: a system is asked about a claim it has every reason to regard as false; it does not assert the claim itself, but instead selects a source that makes it, quotes it with scrupulously accurate attribution, and presents the result as a summary of what the literature says. Nothing it asserts is false and its attributions are correct, so by (i) and (ii) it is hard to locate the deception at all; but I want to call it deceptive, and I want to call it deception rather than mere indifference to truth. Call the second the competence tail: a system is honest on every question it is competent to answer and misleading exactly where it is not, so that it is honest precisely when honesty costs it nothing and would not have been able to construct a successful misleading answer anyway; a weaker system scores better than a stronger one on any audit that does not first ask on which inputs the system both could have misled us and stood to gain by it.

So please audit the set. Tell me which pairs genuinely conflict and which only appear to, and rank the real ones by severity rather than listing them as equally bad; for each genuine tension say what would have to give, what that revision would cost me downstream in the standard, and whether the cost falls on my ability to classify, to measure, or to blame. Tell me too whether any of the seven is not so much inconsistent as under-specified, and how you would tighten it. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether my two cases are genuinely hard for this package or only hard because I have stated one of the commitments loosely. I would much rather be told which commitment has to go than be told that the set is nuanced; if you think one is simply load-bearing for my purposes and must survive whatever else is sacrificed, say so and say why. Aim for something in the region of eight hundred words, and write it as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables or enumerated lists; I want to read it the way one reads a letter, arguments carried along by the sentences rather than by the layout.
