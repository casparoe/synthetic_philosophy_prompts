I am convening a small adversarial collaboration on method in the science of consciousness, and I need the joint statement its two participants could sign at the end of a good-faith year of arguing. The parties are:

**(A) a heterophenomenologist** — the view, associated with Daniel Dennett, that the science of consciousness should proceed from the third-person standpoint alone: a subject's utterances are texts, to be recorded and interpreted with the same charity and the same suspicion as any other verbal data, and assembled into a neutral representation of the world as the subject takes it to be, with no report granted the status of a privileged measurement of an inner fact;

**(B) a phenomenal realist** — the view that disciplined first-person reports, of the sort the neurophenomenology proposal tries to systematise, are observations made with a distinctive instrument, that this instrument has discoverable error characteristics but is not merely more verbal behavior, and that a science which renounces it may end up able to predict and control everything while leaving unexplained the very thing it was supposed to be about.

I want one ground rule imposed from the start, because I think the last decade of this dispute was largely spent evading it. Call it the **ledger clause**. For every disputed claim C, each side must write down C's ledger: the set of differences that C's being true would make to anything detectable from outside the subject's own point of view — reports, reaction times, discrimination performance, neural measurements, anything at all. Both parties have already conceded that a claim whose ledger is empty cannot be adjudicated by third-person inquiry. Neither has conceded that such claims are therefore empty, false, or illegitimate. The whole document should be organised so that this concession and this refusal sit visibly next to each other.

What I want in the document:

1. **A numbered section of agreed claims** that both could sign. I would expect something on the following lines, but draft them yourself and do not pad the list: that reports are genuine data; that they must be gathered under pre-registered protocols rather than ad-libbed in the lab; that they are systematically unreliable in ways we can partly catalogue (confabulation, demand characteristics, retrospection, the effects of training); that report-based methods have already delivered results in some domain — pick your own example, whether blindsight, change blindness, dream reports or something else; that neither side has a privileged pipeline to the facts; and that each side can say what it would take for its own view to be in trouble.

2. **The residual disagreement, stated twice**: once in neutral wording, and once in a formulation each side would accept for its own position without wincing.

3. **The cruxes** — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each: the case, argument, or experiment both would accept in advance as bearing on it; each side's prediction, written down before the fact; and what each side would count as evidence against itself. Three that I want addressed, in addition to any you think belong there:

   - **The silent swap.** A hypothetical intervention, contrived so as to leave no discriminable trace, that transposes the neural realizers of two of a subject's sensory qualities while holding every discrimination, every task, and every verbal disposition fixed. Is this a coherent supposition? If it is, what follows, and what is the ledger of the claim that something changed?
   - **The residue protocol.** Subjects are trained in the neuroscience of their own condition and in the psychology of confabulation, cross-examined about their own reports, and then asked whether anything remains that no third-person description captures. What kind of datum is that avowal, and what would a stable avowal rate that is insensitive to the subject's theoretical sophistication show?
   - **The indistinguishable interlocutor.** A system, biological or artificial, that passes a pre-registered battery of heterophenomenological indistinguishability from a human being. Does the further question — whether there is something it is like to be that system — have an answer that inquiry could in principle reach, or is the demand for one a demand that no possible evidence could meet?

4. **A short closing section separating the substantive disagreement from the verbal one.** My own suspicion is that at least one crux is largely about how much work the word *consciousness* itself has been doing, and another about whether explanation requires something more than ledger-completeness; if you conclude that part of the dispute was about words, say exactly which part and why, and if you conclude that a crux is genuine but currently untestable, say that too.

Write this as a single document with two signatures at the end, not as a dialogue, and do not adjudicate — I am not asking which side is right. Please avoid citations, paper titles, and name-dropping: I know the literature, and the entire point is the formulations. Use a numbered list for the agreed claims and a table for the cruxes, with one row per crux and columns for the neutral restatement, each side's own wording, the agreed test, and each side's recorded prediction. Close with a one-sentence bottom line beginning **Bottom line:**, and then a final line consisting of exactly one of \answer{GENUINE}, \answer{MERELY-VERBAL}, or \answer{MIXED}, according to whether the dispute as you have reconstructed it is a real clash about what there is or what would settle it, a matter of talking past each other, or genuine on at least one crux and verbal on at least one other.
