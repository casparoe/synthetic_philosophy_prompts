So I'm trying to work out whether I actually believe the thing people say against utilitarianism — that it adds up across people as if they were one person having experiences at different times. Someone told me this is basically Rawls's complaint, and that there's a view with a name (contractualism?) that's supposed to handle it by comparing one person's complaint at a time. I don't want the label, I want the working parts. Here are my verdicts, going on gut:

1. Me bearing a real cost now for a benefit to me later — novacaine-free dental work, a brutal year of saving money — obviously fine. Not even a question.

2. The surgeon who kills the healthy visitor to harvest organs for five dying patients: not fine.

3. A one-off mildly unpleasant finger-prick test for everyone in the country, which will identify and save exactly one person who'd otherwise certainly die: fine.

4. Breaking the arm of one randomly selected person to spare ten million people an hour of mild headache, there being no other way: not fine, even though the total suffering avoided swamps the arm.

5a. A policy that adds a one-in-a-million chance of death to each of two million people, in order to save one identified person from certain death: fine.

5b. Killing one identified healthy person in order to remove a one-in-a-million risk of death from each of two million people: not fine.

6. My wife and I run a farm. We each take on a year of genuinely miserable work, neither of which either of us would do for her own sake alone, to keep a shared project alive: fine. And I think still fine in the variant where she's since lost heart and wants out — this is the verdict I'm least sure of. But impose that same total cost on two strangers for a comparably valuable project neither endorsed? Not fine.

7. The one I flip-flop on. A man with early-stage dementia signs a directive refusing tube-feeding once he reaches advanced dementia. Fifteen years later he's there: no memory of his earlier projects, different desires, apparently content. I think honouring the directive is fine. But I also think it would be fine — easy, actually — for the earlier him to impose costs on the later him for the later him's benefit. If the later man is nearly a separate person, that second verdict starts to look like case 2, and it shouldn't.

My tentative principle, such as it is: you may add up across people only when what each person gives up is too small for anyone to reasonably make a fuss about, and what's at stake for the person who gains isn't. I can already see it breaks on case 1 and probably on case 6.

What I want: one principle (or the smallest set you can't compress further), stated precisely enough that I could apply it cold, with the case that motivates each clause. Test my tentative version against the list and repair it rather than replacing it unless you have to. Tell me what it says about case 7, and about the two case types I haven't listed that I suspect it has implications for: burdens imposed on people who don't exist yet, and on animals. Be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate — I'd rather drop a verdict than carry a special exception for it, but I want to know which ones I'd be dropping. And tell me whether the separateness-of-persons thought is doing any real work in the end, or whether the principle I actually want is just about severity and endorsement, in which case my brother wins the argument.

Commit to one answer, reasons included — no menu of options for me to choose between. And flag how confident you are in each clause and where you'd expect someone who works on this to push back hardest.
