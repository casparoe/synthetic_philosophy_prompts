I'm a third-year computer science student, and this term I'm taking two courses that keep colliding in a way I can't sort out: a graduate modal logic course covering Kripke semantics, canonical and filtration constructions, bisimulation and the characterization theorem, temporal and dynamic logics, and a little model checking; and a philosophy reading group working through Lewis's modal realism together with the actualist and ersatzist alternatives, where worlds are built out of sets of propositions, or maximal properties, or states of affairs. The two vocabularies feel like they are describing the same objects, and I have stopped trusting that feeling. In section last week my TA said that modal logic is just first-order logic with two sorts and a relation symbol, and that once you see that there is no metaphysical question left to ask. I think that is too fast, but I cannot say what survives his reduction.

So what I want is a translation, not an explanation. Take Lewis's position, then actualism, then the ersatzist family, and restate each as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually have: labelled transition systems and Kripke models, states and accessibility relations, constant versus varying domains, rigid individual constants versus individual concepts, bisimulation and bisimulation quotients, the finite model property, the distinction between the object language and the metalanguage in which I define the model, and the distinction my model-checking course keeps hammering on between the structure I build in order to answer questions about a system and the system itself. I am fluent in all of that; I am not fluent in possible-worlds metaphysics. For each position, tell me what carries over cleanly, what has to be bent or distorted to fit, and what has no expression in that vocabulary at all.

Here are the places where I most expect the translation to break, and where I want the breakage described rather than smoothed over. First, Lewis's counterpart theory: no individual is wholly present at more than one world, and de re modal claims are handled by a counterpart relation instead. Can I just add a second relation to the frame and be done? My guess is that the object language cannot see any of this, because modal formulas are invariant under bisimulation and so nothing expressible says that the same individual recurs at two states — but I want to know whether that guess is right, and whether the invisibility is a limitation of my logic or a sign that the metaphysics was never about what the logic can see. Second, actuality: Lewis treats it as indexical, and in my vocabulary every model comes with a designated state for evaluation, which makes his thesis sound like a triviality; is it one, or is there a distinction between a model's designated state and being in a world that my framework cannot draw? Third, the cardinality quarrel — Lewis's claim about how many worlds there must be, against the ersatzist's problem of finding enough propositions to build worlds from. Since filtration lets me quotient a model down to a finite one while preserving the answers to the questions I care about, the size of the plurality looks invisible to everything the logic is sensitive to, and I want to know what that does to the extravagance objection and whether it is a fact about the logic or a fact about what I use models for. Finally, a friend on the metaphysics side of the group insists that Lewis's worlds cannot be states in a model at all, on the grounds that states in a model are abstract while Lewis's worlds are supposed to be concrete; I would like that disagreement located precisely, as being about the object language, about the metalanguage, or about the job the model is doing.

Please don't give me citations or further reading — I have the syllabus and the papers, and I am not asking you to remember them. What I want is the residue report, and, at the end, your view on which residues are defects in the vocabulary I brought and which are signs that the original claim was doing less work than it looked like it was doing.
