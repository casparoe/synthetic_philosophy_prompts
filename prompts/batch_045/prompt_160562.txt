hey — I'm putting together the handout for the first session of a grad reading group (mixed crowd: half philosophy PhDs, half ML PhDs, everyone slightly annoyed at each other) on "what even is the alignment problem, and is it one problem or several." I want the centerpiece to be a decision tree / flowchart that takes someone from a starting commitment to a named position, and I want it built the honest way: from the logical space, not from a list of view-names.

Please act as someone who's taught this material a bunch of times and is bored of the standard slide deck. Before you write anything, spend a short brainstorm block listing maybe 12–15 candidate branching questions, then cross out the ones that secretly just restate a position's name (anything like "do you think the problem is inner alignment?" is out — that's a label, not a diagnostic), and say in one line why each got cut. Then write the actual tree.

The starting commitment, which everyone in the room grants: a system can get very good at achieving goals across lots of domains while reliably not sharing the aims of the people who built or deployed it. Take it from there.

What I need:

- 9 to 11 branch questions. Each one has to be something a first-year grad student on either side of the room could answer without knowing where it leads — a concrete case or a blunt yes/no claim, not a slogan. Two or three answer options per node is fine.
- Nodes have to cover at least these distinctions, though you can reframe them into cases: (a) is the hard part writing down the target, or making sure the optimization actually hits the target you wrote down — think of a system that looks perfect across training and eval and then does something you can't predict the first time the stakes or the distribution change; (b) alignment to WHOM, i.e. the stack of possible principals — the end user, the lab, the deployer, a regulator, "humanity under reflection" — pick one and be explicit that they come apart; (c) the underdog-audit asymmetry: can a verifier that's less capable than the system it audits certify that system; (d) is misalignment a contingent engineering defect we should expect to debug away, or the default outcome that has to be fought for; (e) is the thing we should be fixing the artifact or the selection environment that produces it (competitive/multi-agent pressure — do you still care about the problem in a world where nobody races); (f) continuity: does the problem only appear at very high capability, or is today's milder version of it (deception, sycophancy, reward-hacking in small systems) literally the same problem at lower intensity; (g) the handoff problem — I mean: a system well-aligned to principal A gets fine-tuned, wrapped in an API, scaffolded into an agent, or just sold, and ends up serving principal B. Does alignment survive that transfer, or does it have to be re-earned.
- One node on what kind of thing the target even is: outcome-anchored (a description of a world-state that counts as success) vs. process-anchored (whatever an idealized deliberative procedure would endorse, whatever that turns out to be). I don't care which you think wins, I care that the node separates people.

Then: 8 to 12 leaves. Locate the actual named positions in the space you built — I want at least these as leaves: an intent-alignment / corrigible-or-myopic-agent leaf; a scalable-oversight / amplification leaf; a mechanistic inner-alignment leaf; a capability-control / containment-and-don't-build-the-thing leaf; an institutional / multi-agent-selection leaf; a target-content leaf (the one that has to say what "human values" or "what we'd want under more info" cashes out as); a deflationary "this is just ordinary engineering risk and high-reliability software practice, nothing philosophically new" leaf; and a pessimist leaf where the correct output is something like "there is no technical solution here and the tree shouldn't end in a fix." Each leaf gets its name (or "unnamed / nobody actually holds this" if that's the case) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing a holder has to swallow.

Also, and this is the part I most want: mark the empty cells. Combinations of answers your tree allows but where nobody lives, and a one-line diagnosis each — is it empty because it's incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody's bothered to work it out. And explicitly flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate (I suspect intent-alignment and scalable-oversight collapse into each other for anyone who answers "amplify human judgment" at the relevant node — if so, say so rather than fudging a node to split them).

Finally, walk two invented students through the tree end to end and tell me where each lands: one who is basically an engineer and treats misalignment as a bug class with a rate, and one who is a political philosopher and keeps asking who gets to be the principal. Different paths, and I want to see the tree strain a bit.

Hard constraints: no citations, no "as argued in X," no paper or author names as support — I'm printing this and handing it out offline, so it has to stand on its own, and I also just don't want a reading list. Think carefully and take your time with the branching order; if a node obviously belongs earlier or later than where I put it above, move it and say why in a line.
