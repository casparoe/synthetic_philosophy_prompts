hey — I need a draft I can fight about, not a blank page, so here goes.

I'm the grad rep (poli theory PhD, which is apparently why they wanted me) on my university's Elections and Referenda Committee. In April we're running a binding all-campus referendum on a "campus climate and services fee" — a per-semester charge that would fund a big decarbonization and bus-pass bundle. It binds everyone who pays it, and it's binding on the administration if it passes. Our charge says the electorate for referenda is "the members of the university community," which has never been defined, and this year we're the ones who have to define it. Second file on the same agenda: turnout in the last two referenda was 11% and 14%, both results were contested afterwards as unrepresentative, so there's a live proposal to make participation mandatory. Everyone is treating these as two separate agenda items and I've started to think they're the same question, more on that below.

Groups we have to rule on:

1. 16- and 17-year-olds in the dual-enrollment program. Full-time on campus, pay the fee, fully subject to the conduct code and to our disciplinary panels, but minors, mostly living with parents off campus, most will go elsewhere after.
2. International undergrads on student visas. Four years here, pay the highest tuition plus the fee, subject to everything, no route to staying afterwards, and removable by an external authority for reasons we don't control.
3. Non-citizen residents of university-owned family housing — postdocs, staff, spouses, kids in the campus school. Rent paid to the university, subject to university housing rules. Some green-card holders with eight years here, some eight months, a few undocumented. Several told us in writing they won't register for anything that creates a paper trail, which matters if we go the automatic-registration route.
4. Nobody is arguing about these people and I think that's the tell: enrolled students or staff who have been away for two years (leave, deployment, a fellowship abroad) and intend to come back, and who keep their status and their fee obligation while gone.

Two principles are on the table and they cut different ways. One says you get a say over a decision roughly in proportion to how much you are bound by it — the fee binds you, the conduct code binds you, therefore. The other says the franchise tracks membership in the long run: it's for people who are in this thing for the duration and have taken on the standing of a member, not merely for anyone currently within the blast radius of a rule.

Here's the bit I keep failing to say out loud in the meeting. Whatever principle settles who is in the electorate should also settle whether we can compel participation, and I think people haven't noticed this because it's inconvenient. If we attach a hold on your student account, or a fine, to not voting, we're exercising authority over exactly the people whose membership we're supposed to be unsure about — and the natural justification for compelling someone is that they owe something to a community they belong to. Conversely, if somebody's stake is genuinely partial, I don't see what licenses us to compel them, only to permit them. Our faculty rep pushes the sharpest version of this: he calls it the duration principle, says what matters is expected *remaining* years of subjection, so a 16-year-old has a stronger claim than a 62-year-old emeritus and a one-year visiting scholar has almost none — and he's happy with the consequence, which is that votes should be weighted by expected years, so a first-year counts for more than someone graduating in May. Most of the committee reacts to that with their gut. I want a memo that reacts to it with reasons, because I think it's the strongest thing anyone has put on the table and I also think it's probably wrong, and I can't yet say which sentence breaks.

What I need is the memo I circulate to the committee on Monday. It should:

- recommend a concrete eligibility rule — who is in, on what test, with any age, residence, or duration threshold given as an actual number, plus what happens to people just under it;
- recommend something on the participation problem, and specifically choose between the three options actually in play: (a) nothing, (b) automatic registration from the enrollment/housing/payroll rolls with non-voting recorded as a formal abstention so the denominator is fixed, (c) compulsory turnout with a real penalty and an explicit "none of the above" box;
- say how the two recommendations hang together, since I'll be asked why the committee isn't just making two unrelated calls;
- give the duration/weighted-vote proposal a serious hearing and then tell me what it costs to reject it, and what the losing side's real worry is — my sense is it's something like: partial or weighted votes create a formally second-class tier of members, and a mandate manufactured out of compulsory abstentions is worse than an honest 14%;
- close with a timeline we could actually adopt (rules locked by when, one review after the April vote, what we'd need to see to change it).

Two constraints from outside. The administration has told us the result only sticks if the electorate can be justified to the board of trustees in one paragraph, so no rule that takes three paragraphs of theory to explain. And some of our committee will never sign anything that looks like it's importing a view about who counts as a full person, so the reasons have to be stated in terms of the fee, the rules, and the decision in front of us.

Please don't pad it with citations or a reading list — I've read the literature, what I'm short on is a decision and the reasons for it, and I have to defend it in a room. Give me the strongest version of the case against your recommendation somewhere in the memo, not buried. And end the memo with its bottom line on a line of its own, no prose around it, in this form:

\answer{one short sentence giving the eligibility rule and the participation rule}
