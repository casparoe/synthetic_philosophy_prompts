I'm a junior writing an extended essay for my school's philosophy elective, and I keep hitting the same wall: everything I find on Kierkegaard's attack on the press and the public treats it as a complaint about nineteenth-century newspapers and then adds one sentence about how he would have hated Twitter. I want something sharper than that, so I've built him a concrete case instead.

Our school is piloting what our principal calls a civic feed. On every student's school account, no like, repost, or view counts are displayed anywhere; posts appear in strict chronological order; ten percent of the items are selected for you by software that infers who disagrees with you and what you seem to care about, though the school concedes the inference is wrong about a third of the time; and the comment box stays locked for ninety seconds after the page opens. Parents can sign a waiver, and about a third of students are still on the ordinary ranked-and-counted version.

Would Kierkegaard have called the civic feed a genuine improvement on the ranked one, or the same corruption with better manners? I especially want to know whether hiding the counts or the enforced delay does the more important work; whether the algorithmic disagreement slot is anything like what he wanted for the single individual, or just leveling with a conscience bolted on; whether the waiver spoils the whole scheme or is the only part of it he could respect, given that his real target seems to be a public nobody belongs to and everybody answers to; and what he would predict happens to the students left on the ranked feed once their friends have gone.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion, and within each item mark which part is extrapolation beyond what he actually argued and which part is his own position.
