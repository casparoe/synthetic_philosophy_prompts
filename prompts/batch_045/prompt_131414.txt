I teach a final-year seminar on the philosophy of computation, and last night I had a long and unresolved argument with an old friend of mine — a condensed-matter physicist — about what kind of claim the Church–Turing thesis actually is. Her position was that the thesis is best read as a constitutive or bridge principle, something like the principle that links geometrically defined spacetime intervals to what rods and clocks measure: neither a stipulation we are free to make nor a hypothesis that experiment could knock down, but the precondition that has to be in place before the formalism can be applied to anything at all, so that the demand to say what would falsify it is simply misplaced. I objected that a principle which nothing could conceivably count against is not worth calling a principle, and that this was just immunisation by another name; she replied that I was conflating "not testable" with "not answerable to anything," and cited the convergence of the independent formalisations as the kind of consideration that does the work instead. We went round on this for hours and I came away unsure whether she had insulated the thesis or redescribed it, which is not a distinction I want to settle by assertion while I am holding a pen.

Question 3. "The Church–Turing thesis is neither a theorem nor an empirical hypothesis: it is a definition, and is therefore immune to refutation." Discuss. In your answer you should (a) state the thesis carefully and distinguish it from nearby claims with which it is easily confused; (b) give your own view of what sort of epistemic status it has, and say why that status and not another; and (c) say what, if anything, would count as evidence against it.

Answer. Begin by separating three claims that constantly get run together. First, the thesis proper: every function whose values can be obtained by an effective method — a finite number of exact instructions, carried out by a human being with pencil and paper, demanding no insight or ingenuity — is computable by a Turing machine. Second, the physical version, associated with Deutsch's Church–Turing principle: any physical process can be simulated by a universal computing machine, so no physical system computes a function that is not Turing-computable. Third, the complexity version: the functions computable in polynomial time are exactly the ones we can compute in practice. Only the first is the Church–Turing thesis; the other two are distant relatives that borrow its name.

On status, I want to claim that the first has been proved. Turing showed that whatever a human clerk working from a finite table of instructions can compute can be simulated by a Turing machine, and Church established the corresponding result for the recursive functions and the lambda calculus; the demonstration that these three apparently quite different formalisms pick out exactly the same class of functions is a theorem, and that theorem is the content of the Church–Turing thesis. Its status is therefore that of a mathematical result rather than a hypothesis, and the second version is the one that is genuinely empirical and could in principle be overturned by discovery.

At the same time, the thesis is best understood as a definition: it fixes the reference of the informal phrase "effectively computable" by identifying it with a precise formalism. A definition is not the sort of thing that can be false, only more or less useful, and so the thesis cannot be refuted by any empirical discovery however exotic. If engineers were to build a genuine hypercomputer tomorrow, the correct description would be that we had found a physical process that outruns our formalism, not that the thesis had been falsified.

Quantum computers are often said to threaten the thesis, but they touch neither the first version nor the second, since a quantum computer computes only Turing-computable functions. What they threaten is the third: Shor's algorithm puts factoring inside a class we had taken to be infeasible — though I should add that no separation between the quantum and classical polynomial-time classes has actually been proved, so even the third version is under pressure rather than refuted.

A working hypercomputer would still be the most interesting thing that could happen to this debate, so it is worth asking what it would show. The proposals come in two families: devices that exploit the infinite divisibility of some continuous physical quantity, so that a single measurement yields infinitely many digits of information, and accelerating machines that complete an infinite sequence of operations in a finite time by exploiting certain solutions of the field equations of general relativity. In my view both families are already excluded by established physics — general relativity does not admit the relevant spacetimes, and no real measurement can yield infinitely many digits of precision. But if one of them did work, the consequences would be severe, and here I part company with a common complacency: we would have to give up the mathematical version as well as the physical one. For the mathematical version is just the physical version restricted to the special case of a human being with pencil and paper, and if the physical world can compute beyond the Turing limit then the claim that human rule-following cannot is a special case of a generalisation we have discovered to be false.

A last remark on what could bear on the thesis. The striking fact is that every independent attempt to make effectiveness precise, from the 1930s onwards, has landed on the same class of functions. If the thesis were a bare stipulation, that convergence would be a coincidence; so the convergence is genuine evidence for it, and it is why the thesis had to be argued for rather than simply laid down. It is, if you like, a definition that had to be earned.
