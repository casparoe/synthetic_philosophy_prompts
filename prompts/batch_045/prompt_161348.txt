Doing a reading group on Scanlon's contractualism — mostly grad students, plus one political theorist who keeps dragging Rawls in — and I've built an argument against it that I'm fairly sure proves too much, but I can't locate the bad step. Here it is:

(1) On contractualism, an act is wrong iff it's disallowed by any set of principles that no one could reasonably reject, and whether some person could reasonably reject a principle depends on how strong *their* objection is, compared one at a time against the objections of other individuals. Numbers don't count: that a burden or benefit falls on a million people rather than one doesn't by itself make anyone's objection any stronger. That's supposed to be the whole point of the view — it's why mild pleasures for millions can't justify torturing one person, and why contractualism isn't just utilitarianism with a nicer story about justification. (2) Bridge: you can rescue Ada or five strangers, not both, everyone otherwise equal, each dies if not rescued. Ada's objection to save-the-five (she dies) is exactly as strong as each stranger's objection to save-Ada; I know there's a contractualist tie-breaker about comparing the *strength* of individual burdens, but here the burdens are death versus death, so it does nothing. And the objections can't be pooled, by (1). So contractualism can't rank the options and can't require saving the five, which means saving Ada is permissible. (3) Nothing in (2) turns on the number five. If numbers never strengthen anyone's objection, then a sixth person, a hundredth, a millionth on the many side changes nothing; run the argument again and again and no number of people on the many side ever makes saving the one impermissible. So contractualism is committed to: for any N, you may save the one instead of the N. (4) And here's a version I think is worse. A policy gives each of 200 million people a trivial convenience and imposes on each a one-in-ten-million risk of death. Nobody's individual objection is worth anything — the per-person risk is smaller than risks everyone runs daily without thinking — so no one could reasonably reject it, so it's permitted. But it predictably kills about twenty people. Scale the population and the predictable body count gets as large as you like while every individual complaint stays negligible, and contractualism has no way to prefer the variant with fewer expected deaths, since that difference is purely aggregate. (5) So contractualism either permits knowingly letting a million die to save one, or permits policies with an arbitrarily large predictable death toll, or both. That can't be right.

I'd most like to keep (1): if contractualism starts adding up across people it stops being the view that explains why the separateness of persons matters, and whatever fix I reach for has to leave the torture case alone, since that result comes from the same anti-aggregation machinery. But I also can't see how to give up (2) or (3) without giving up (1). One of the contractualists in the group says the flaw is in (3): she says we don't assess a principle for a single case but over the whole range of situations it governs, so a person can object that under save-the-one she'd usually be on the losing side, and with a million-to-one that objection is crushing. I half buy that for Bridge, except that it looks to me exactly like taking expected utility from behind a veil of ignorance about which position you occupy — aggregation over positions rather than over people — which sort of begs the question for me, since I suspect that's a relabeling rather than a distinction. And I don't see how it even gets started on (4), where there's no question of which side you're on because everybody is on the same side. So: which step is bad, why did it look fine, and what does each available repair cost me? I'd rather have the diagnosis than a verdict on contractualism, and I don't need anything assigned for the group to read — we've read what we've read.
