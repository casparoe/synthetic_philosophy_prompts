I write fiction — three novels so far, all in close third person — and I have begun a fourth, to be narrated in four alternating first-person accounts of a single afternoon, none of them trustworthy. Before I commit myself to the arrangement I should like to test it against Kurosawa's *Rashomon*, which does something structurally similar and which is very often said to be "about" the unreliability of testimony or the impossibility of truth. I want a reading of the film through the Buddhist doctrine of *anatta* — no permanent or substantial self, the person a merely conventional designation over the aggregates, and identification with a self treated not as a theoretical error but as the thing that keeps suffering going. And I want a reading, not a decoration.

Concretely, these are the points I should like addressed.

First: the standard reading is epistemic — four incompatible accounts, no neutral record, truth as a matter of perspective. Is the Buddhist reading a deepening of that, a rival to it, or simply a confusion of an epistemic claim with a metaphysical one? If, in honesty, the film is only about testimony, say so plainly — and then say what a film would have to do to dramatise *anatta* rather than the frailty of witnesses.

Second: each account is also a self-portrait — the bandit as gallant duellist, the wife as shamed or as sacrificial, the husband as one who died by his own hand, and the woodcutter's later version deflating all three into cowardice. Read these as fabrications of a self rather than as lies about an event. What exactly is deflated: the self, or only the flattering version of it? Is there something left over — the one who wants the dagger back, the one who suffers — that the doctrine would also want to reach, and does the film reach it?

Third: the dead man testifies, through a medium, and complains of a theft. What is a no-self reading to make of a film in which a self plainly survives death and retains an interest in its property?

Fourth: the gate. The priest's crisis concerns human selfishness, and his faith is repaired by a single unselfish act rather than by an insight. Does the frame decline the doctrine, undercut it, or translate it into something the doctrine would not recognise as liberation? I gather the gate material and the testimonies come from two different Akutagawa stories: does that accident of composition bear on the reading?

Finally, and most useful to me: what does the film show about first-person narration as such? Does the mere fact of a voice, even when what it says is self-inflation, manufacture for the reader a unitary someone behind it? Can the technique be used at all without quietly endorsing what the doctrine denies, and if so, how?

Around 1,100 words, if you would; in British English; no plot summary beyond what your argument needs, and no bibliography or citations — I read on paper and have no wish to chase references. Address me as a novelist rather than a student: I want the consequences for craft as well as the philosophy, and I would much rather you told me where the reading fails than pressed it past the point where it holds.
