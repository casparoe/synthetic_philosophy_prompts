I need help locating the break in an argument I built for a reading group on chance and credence. I no longer trust its conclusion, but every step still looks compulsory to me, and I would rather be told which one to surrender than go on being unable to say why the argument fails.

The case. A sealed device prints one symbol per minute all morning and prints its last symbol at noon. Let A be the proposition that the noon print is R. Chances here are Humean in the familiar style: the chance at a time t of a proposition is the probability that the best system for the whole history assigns to that proposition, conditional on the history up to t. Two consequences of that matter below. First, one and the same proposition can have different chances at different times. Second, the chance at t depends on how the sequence goes after t. Write ch_9 for the chance function at 9am and ch_12 for the one at noon; write C for a rational agent's credence function at 9am and E for her total evidence then — the design of the device, nothing else that bears on the noon print, and the single chance claim stated in (1).

(1) The Principal Principle. For admissible evidence E, C(A | ch_9(A) = x ∧ E) = x. So if E says only what the design is and that ch_9(A) = 1/3, then C(A | E) = 1/3.

(2) Total probability for chances. For t < t′ and any partition {B_i} of the ways the world might go between t and t′, ch_t(A) = Σ_i ch_t(B_i)·ch_t′(A | B_i). The chances of A, as t advances, form a martingale with respect to the chance function.

(3) Retrospective sensitivity. Because the chance at t is fixed by the whole history, the proposition that ch_9(A) = 1/3 is not a free-standing forecast. It is a constraint on the entire pattern, and so it carries information about ch_10(A), ch_11(A), and the rest — information that the bare number 1/3 does not contain.

(4) Sharpening. As t approaches noon, ch_t(A) tends to 0 or 1; at noon, ch_12(A) is 1 if the noon print is R and 0 if it is L.

(5) Settled authority. What you defer to, when you defer to chance, is the chance of A. But the chance of A that is genuinely about A, rather than somebody's interim estimate of it, is the one that obtains once A is settled; the earlier ones are forecasts of that quantity and are superseded by it. So rationality requires C(A | E) = ch_12(A). And this cannot be dismissed on the ground that it demands credence in a quantity the agent cannot know: the Principal Principle itself requires C(A) = x on the strength of a chance the agent might never have learned, and what it requires concerns the value, not her access to the value.

(6) By (4) and (5), C(A | E) ∈ {0, 1}. At 9am, on evidence that assigns A a chance of 1/3, the agent must be certain of A or certain of its negation.

(7) By (1), C(A | E) = 1/3. So (1) and (5) cannot both be true.

(8) The failure is not local. Let the noon print have three possible values, A1, A2, A3, each with chance 1/3 at 9am, with the evidence symmetric among them. Settled authority requires C(A1 | E) = ch_12(A1) ∈ {0, 1}, and likewise for A2 and A3. So the agent must be certain of one cell of the partition and certain against the other two, on symmetric evidence that gives each cell a chance of 1/3. Nothing in E determines which cell it is; what decides that is a fact about the future she has no grip on. A requirement she can meet only by matching a quantity that is, from her position, a random variable does not look like a requirement at all, and the only credence functions that satisfy it for all three cells at once violate additivity.

Two things I cannot sort out on my own. First, whether (4) is a claim about chance at all: by noon the print is part of the history, so a chance of 1 or 0 there is the outcome wearing a hat — in which case (5) tells me to defer to the outcome rather than to a chance, and the argument is either trivial or it generalises, since every Humean chance is fixed by a pattern that extends past its own time. Second, whether (5) is anything more than (1) with the subscript changed, in which case giving it up costs me the Principal Principle anyway.

I would rather keep (1), (2), and (4) than keep (5). Tell me which step is the break, why that step looked compulsory, and what each of the alternatives costs. If the break is at (5), say what survives of the thought behind it: that a chance which is going to be revised is not the chance that governs the event, and that deference to an interim forecast is therefore at best second-best.
