Hey — I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so go easy on me. In my book Pericles gets yanked forward in time and dropped into a charter-writing convention for a small near-future island city. The founders have this thing they call the "second-decree rule": anything that punishes a whole group — a district, a company, an ethnic minority — has to clear two separate votes, at least a day apart, by two differently-picked bodies, or it just doesn't take effect. On top of that voting is compulsory, the age is 16, and long-term non-citizen residents (metics, sort of) can vote but can't hold office. There's also a clause they're still fighting over that lets the assembly prosecute teachers for corrupting the young.

I need to write his speech to the convention. Which of these would he actually go after, which would he defend, and why? And what would he make of the fact that the Sicilian expedition — which came after he died, and which he'd definately have called a betrayal of his whole strategy of not expanding the empire mid-war — is exactly the kind of thing the second-decree rule is meant to stop? For each bit, tell me how confident you are, and single out the one claim you'd bet least on.
