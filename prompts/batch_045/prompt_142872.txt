I'm giving a paper next month on self-binding across time — Ulysses contracts in the medical setting and entrenchment in the constitutional one — and before I commit to it in front of people who work on these questions, I'd like you to audit the set of things I find myself committed to. I've written them flat, without the qualifications I would normally build in, because I want to know whether the unqualified versions are jointly tenable.

(a) A competent person's advance refusal of an intervention binds at the later time even over her contemporaneous objection, provided the refusal was aimed at a foreseen episode in which her capacity would be impaired — mania, intoxication, an acute suicidal crisis — and not merely at the possibility that she might change her mind. The later self saying no, not now, is in those cases not a reason to release her; it is close to evidence that the device is working.

(b) An irrevocable alienation of one's own future practical authority — self-enslavement, a waiver of one's standing to contest anything done to one — is void. And it is void for a reason that has nothing to do with whether the later self happens to object: what makes it wrong is that it leaves nobody there to be self-governing.

(c) The test I keep returning to, and which I use to explain why (a) and (b) do not collide, is that a precommitment is legitimate only if it is capacity-preserving — protecting the conditions under which the agent can exercise agency later — as opposed to outcome-fixing, where the earlier self simply arranges to win a disagreement with the later self about what to do.

(d) In the constitutional case I support entrenched rights — free speech, the franchise, habeas corpus — that an ordinary legislative majority cannot repeal, and I support a small unamendable core: no abolition of elections, no establishment of religion.

(e) I also think the claim to be bound decays. The further the bound agent stands from the binding act — elapsed time, altered circumstances, complete turnover in the membership of the group — the weaker the demand to comply becomes, and at some point a precommitment lapses if it has never been re-enacted.

(f) When I am asked why the earlier moment has authority over the later one, I give two answers, and I have never been able to tell whether they are one answer in different words. The first is epistemic: the earlier self acts in a cool hour, knows the pattern of her own recurrences, and is not presently impaired, so her judgment about what should happen in the crisis is the better one. The second is constitutive: there are not two selves with competing claims at all — the person, or the polity, is a continuing agent, and the precommitment just is its standing decision-procedure, so there is no conflict to adjudicate.

(g) I hold that no such device may be absolute: the agent must be able to release herself if she now meets the conditions under which the original binding was made. In the paper I propose a concrete version of this, which I call the recapitulation condition — release is available to anyone who can correctly restate, in her own words, the reasons recorded at the time of binding. The idea is that this screens for impaired capacity without handing the decision to a third party, which would be its own form of outcome-fixing.

(h) And one case verdict I am confident of, included because it is the one I would least like to surrender: a patient who has signed an advance refusal of blood products, and who in the ambulance is fully conscious, calm, informed, and says she has changed her mind, should in my view still be refused the transfusion.

What I want from you is an audit of this package rather than a survey of the literature. Which of these are genuinely in tension with which, and which pairs only look as though they are? Order the real tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally damaging, and for each one tell me precisely what I would have to give up and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set. Where a pair dissolves on inspection, say what the dissolution consists in instead of waving it through. I would be grateful for your view on (f) in particular: I have a suspicion that I am helping myself to two incompatible stories about why the earlier moment speaks for the later one, and that whichever of them I keep will change what I can say about (g) and (h). And if the recapitulation condition in (g) is doing less work than I think — if it is either too easy to satisfy by someone who has simply changed her mind, or too hard for someone who is impaired in a way that leaves her articulate — I would rather hear that from you than from the floor. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most.

Please write in continuous prose: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, since I intend to read this as an argument rather than as a handout. Around a thousand words, if you can manage it.
