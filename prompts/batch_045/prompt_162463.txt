Three weeks ago my aunt Ilse died at seventy-eight. For the last thirty-one years she had been doing essentially one thing: writing English versions of a short sixteenth-century French poem — Clément Marot's light, teasing get-well verse to a sick young woman, the poem that Douglas Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot* (1997) made notorious among English readers — under a constraint she imposed on herself. A draft had to match the French line for line in syllable count, rhyme scheme and rhyme gender, and had to keep the poem's register intact: a flirtatious note to one particular girl, not a meditation on sickness. On top of that she kept what she called the ledger rule: no line could reappear unchanged in a later finished draft, and each new draft had to gain something in form while losing something in sense, or gain in sense while losing in form, so that no two completed drafts could be ranked against each other. She called finished translations incomparable, and said that picking one was an act of taste, not of discovery. She left 214 completed drafts, plus a notebook titled the unpayable debts — the caesura on the fourth syllable, the fact that every French rhyme word is a term of endearment, the sudden drop into real worry at the close — not one of which any of her drafts had managed to keep.

I am giving the eulogy, and I have to write it for a room that is split three ways: her two daughters and four grandchildren, who found the thirty-one years baffling and, honestly, a bit hurtful; a dozen or so literary translators and poets, several of whom first met the poem through Hofstadter's book and who will hear immediately if I smuggle in any comfort about a definitive version; and my mother, her sister, who needs to be told that those thirty-one years added up to something.

Write it as about four minutes, honest on her own terms — no she found the version she was looking for, no her work is done and she can rest now, no claim that any one draft is the best, no she lives on in the poem — while still landing as comfort for the people who were hurt by where her attention went, and without pretending that the years produced a finished thing. Give it as exactly five movements, each two or three sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
