I've been hanging around the rationalist corner of the internet since the seqs, and there's one bit of received wisdom I've never gotten straight: how literally to take Good's theorem. You know the one — for an expected-utility maximizer the ex ante expected value of free information can never be negative, because after you conditionalize you can always just do whatever you'd have done anyway, so the worst case ties with ignorance and usually you do better. The moral everyone extracts is "go get more data before you decide," and I've said it myself in arguments.

What I want is the counterexample, built properly rather than gestured at. So: construct me a case in which a piece of information has strictly negative expected value, evaluated at the ex ante node, for an agent who is otherwise exactly the sort of agent the theorem is about.

Constraints:

- The information is free. No price, no time cost, no effort, no risk of being seen gathering it. The negativity has to be structural, not "the test cost more than the test was worth."
- The agent is a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer with a proper prior, updates correctly, and has no funny preferences: no intrinsic taste for ignorance, no value placed on suspense or surprise or discovering things, utility runs over outcomes rather than over belief states.
- The information is genuinely decision-relevant and not adversarially produced — no lying oracle, no demon, no perfect predictor. If other people are in the story they're shrewd but ordinary humans who can't read minds.
- Realistic setting: a workplace, a hospital, a courtroom, a family, a negotiation. One paragraph for the scenario itself.
- The agent doesn't screw up. Whatever makes the information bad has to surivive the agent conditionalizing correctly and then maximizing correctly.

Then do the actual work:

1. Name the premise of Good's theorem your case breaks, precisely enough that I can see it's a premise and not a defect in the agent.
2. Run the arithmetic with rough but explicit numbers at the ex ante node — EU with the info, EU without — so I can check it rather than take your word.
3. Deal with the obvious objection: why can't the agent just commit in advance to ignore whatever comes back? If in your case that move *does* rescue things, say so, and then tell me what the agent would pay for the ability to commit — I suspect that distinction is where the real content is.
4. Ablation: remove or neutralize the single structural feature doing the work and show the value flips back to positive. If you can't do the ablation, treat that as evidence your case is stipulating the verdict instead of generating it.
5. Steelman the other side: what does someone who still believes "more information is never worse" say about your case, can they reclassify it as not a real counterexample, and are they right?

I'm fine with either family of answer — the game-y version where someone else observes that you took the information and adjusts, or the version where learning forecloses an act that was open to you while ignorant — but pick one and make it clean.
