I convene the final assessment for PHIL3015, *Death, Persons and Medicine*, a third-year undergraduate option taken mainly by single-honours philosophers and a handful of medics. The paper is two hours, closed book, and candidates answer one essay from a choice of six. I want you to write one of those six questions for me, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, on the definition of death.

The question should be built around the invented statutory text and the two cases below. I have drafted the statute myself precisely so that candidates cannot rely on having read about any real jurisdiction's law; please preserve that feature. Nothing in the question, the model answer or the marking scheme should make a real author, paper, statute, code of practice or court decision load-bearing — the model answer should remain correct even if every actual jurisdiction turned out to have done things differently, and no marks should be available for naming authorities or recalling literature.

**Death (Definition) Act 2019 (Northmark)**

1.—(1) An individual is dead if either
  (a) there has been irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem; or
  (b) there has been permanent cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions.
  (2) For the purposes of subsection (1): *irreversible* means that the cessation will not, and cannot, be reversed; *permanent* means that the cessation will not be reversed, because no intervention designed to reverse it will be attempted.
  (3) A determination of death under subsection (1)(b) may be made on the basis of permanence alone.
2. Nothing in this Act states whether the death referred to is the death of a person or the death of a human organism.

**Case A.** Ms Kavanagh, 34, sustains a catastrophic intracranial haemorrhage. Brainstem reflexes are absent and apnoea is confirmed on formal testing; she is declared dead under s.1(1)(a). With continued ventilation and enteral feeding she thereafter maintains her own blood pressure without vasoactive drugs, her wounds heal, and she is twenty weeks pregnant with a foetus that continues to develop for eight weeks after the determination. Her partner refuses to accept it: he says she is still doing the things a living organism does, gestating their child above all, and that the law has simply stipulated its way to a convenient answer.

**Case B.** Mr Okonjo, 61, has life-sustaining treatment withdrawn after devastating brain injury. Five minutes after asystole he is declared dead under s.1(1)(b) on the basis of permanence alone. A transplant team then begins normothermic regional perfusion: a pump circulates oxygenated blood through the abdominal organs, with a balloon inflated in the aorta above the diaphragm to prevent that flow reaching the heart and brain. His family demand to know how he can be dead while his blood is moving again, and why, if he is dead, anyone needs to block blood from reaching his head.

I also want candidates to face a proposed amendment adding a third limb to s.1(1): irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness.

What I need from you:

1. **The question itself**, phrased as a single essay question for two hours of work, with such scaffolding as you think fair. It must be unanswerable by rehearsing the standard taxonomy — neurological versus circulatory criteria, whole-brain versus higher-brain, the dead donor rule — and it should require candidates to apply distinctions to a statutory text they have never seen. A candidate who only reproduces that taxonomy should not be able to reach 16 out of 25.
2. **A model answer of 900–1,000 words**, which must: separate irreversibility from permanence and ask whether the Act can bear the weight it places on the difference; notice the asymmetry whereby clause (b) expressly permits a permanence-based determination while clause (a) does not, and say whether that makes the timing of death under (b) depend on what others have decided to do; identify what a somatic-integration-style objection would have to establish in order to show that whole-brain death is not the death of the organism, and explain how s.2's silence on person versus organism bears on Case A; say what Case B shows about the relation between the definition of death and the dead donor rule; and assess whether adding the consciousness limb resolves the difficulties or reveals that the statute has no single subject matter. Give the family's objections in both cases their strongest formulation, and state what would have to be true for them to succeed.
3. **A marking scheme out of 25**, built around five must-hit points worth five marks each, each with a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong.
4. **A short closing paragraph on the first-class/upper-second boundary**, naming the specific move that separates an A answer from a B answer that touches all five points.
5. **A paragraph explaining why this question discriminates better than a straight "is brain death really death?" question** — I will need this for the exam board.

British spelling and usage throughout, please.
