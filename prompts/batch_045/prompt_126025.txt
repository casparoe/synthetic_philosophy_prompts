I'm a junior taking a seminar this term on formal systems and self-reference, and we've been working through the early chapters of Gödel, Escher, Bach. I've spent a couple of evenings on the MU puzzle, and I think I understand the official answer: count the I's, notice that doubling a number that isn't divisible by 3 can't make it divisible by 3 and that subtracting 3 can't either, so you can never get from a single I down to zero. But I don't think I understand what the puzzle is supposed to be showing, and I'm hoping you can untangle three things that are knotted together in my head.

First, the moral. I keep hitting the same wall when I play with it by hand: I can get as far as MUI (double the tail of MI twice, then collapse a triple of I's into a U) and from there to MUIU, but every route I try to the finish line seems to require already having something of the shape MIII or MIIIU, so that the final U can be manufactured — which is exactly what the invariant rules out. What bothers me is that Hofstadter's whole book seems to be about how intelligence consists in jumping out of the system instead of grinding mechanically through the rules. So shouldn't the correct response be to jump out and simply write MU down, or to notice that the rules are mine and quietly add a fifth one? I tried the experiment of replacing the doubling rule with a tripling rule — Mx goes to Mxxx, everything else unchanged — and MU falls out in two steps: MI, MIII, MU. Doesn't that show the impossibility is just an accident of one arbitrary choice of rule, and that the real lesson is that the resourceful move is to rewrite the rules rather than to prove things about them?

Second, truth. A friend in the class who's a year ahead insists that MU is the Gödel sentence of the MIU system in miniature, standing for a truth the system can't reach, and that this is why we have to step outside to see it. But I can't make sense of the word truth here. MIU doesn't seem to be about anything: M, I, and U are never defined, and no interpretation has been supplied in which MU says something or other. If MU has no interpretation, in what sense is it true rather than merely unproducible? Is the analogy with Gödel's first incompleteness theorem doing any real work, or is it just a loose resemblance that my friend is overreading?

Third, and I think this is where I'm most confused: if the analogy were exact, then by the same kind of trick Gödel used — getting a system to talk about its own strings — shouldn't we be able to formalize the counting-I's argument inside MIU, so that the system itself proves it will never produce MU? And if we can't (MIU has no numbers, no negation, no apparent way of referring to its own syntax), then the fact that we can settle the matter from the outside seems to show something close to the opposite of incompleteness: that this little system is entirely transparent to us from above. I'd be grateful if you could tell me which of these claims, if any, I should actually believe, and where exactly my reasoning goes off the rails.
