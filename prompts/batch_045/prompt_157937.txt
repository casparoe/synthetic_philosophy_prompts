I'd like your help with something I can't work out myself: predicting how a friend of mine will rule on a case that hasn't come up yet, and — more than the prediction — figuring out what rule she is actually running.

My friend Dita, a retired art historian, has unusually firm and unusually consistent intuitions about when you can learn something about the aesthetic quality of a work by being told. In every case below, the question is whether she *knows* the thing she has been told, as opposed to merely having reason to believe it or reason to go and look. I've written down the verdicts I've heard her give over the years.

1. A critic in a magazine she doesn't know calls a film she hasn't seen a masterpiece. She says she doesn't know that it's a masterpiece, though she has some reason to go and see it.
2. An acquaintance whose taste overlaps with hers perhaps seventy percent of the time says an unread novel is beautifully written. She says she doesn't know that it's beautifully written, and isn't confident she'll like it either.
3. Her partner — twenty years, hundreds of novels, two disagreements — says a novel she hasn't read is exquisitely paced. She says: I don't know that it's well paced, but I do know that I'm going to love it, and that's what matters when I'm deciding what to read.
4. A food writer whose palate she has no track record with calls a dish balanced. No knowledge.
5. Someone tells her a film runs ninety-seven minutes and was shot in unbroken long takes. Knowledge: not the sort of thing she has to see for herself.
6. A friend who has seen a film tells her to watch for the way the camera lingers on empty rooms. She watches and notices it. Knowledge — the testimony told her where to look, but the seeing did the rest.
7. Forty thousand users rate a film 4.8 out of 5, and she has never checked how that aggregate tracks her own taste. No knowledge of merit, but enough practical reason to stream it.
8. A recording engineer she trusts tells her a remaster removes the muddy bass of an original she knows well; she hasn't heard the remaster. She says she'll know once she listens, and not before.
9. The one case where she wobbles. Her own diary, twenty years ago, about a novel she has entirely forgotten and can no longer obtain, says the structure does something extraordinary. She can't settle it: if she says she still knows the novel is good, she owes you an account of what changed, since nothing about the novel changed — only her access to it.

The new case. Dita and her friend Mira have, over eighteen years, formed firsthand verdicts on roughly three hundred films and agreed on every single one — and Dita saw all three hundred herself, so these are genuine agreements, not co-reported enthusiasm. Mira has just attended a private screening of a finished feature that the director's estate has sealed in a vault for fifty years. Dita will never see it. Mira tells her two things: (a) it's a masterpiece, and the editing earns the ending; (b) it is exactly their kind of thing, and Dita would love it. Dita believes both.

What I want from you:

Does Dita think she knows (a)? Does she think she knows (b)? Set out the two or three rules about aesthetic testimony that fit all nine earlier verdicts about equally well — rules stated in your own terms, not by attributing them to published authors, and I don't need citations or names — and say exactly what each predicts about (a) and (b). Give me your best guess about what she will say, with your reasons for preferring that rule over the others. Then design the single further case that would best separate the rules, and say what each rule predicts there.

Two things I'd especially like you to press on. First: whether three hundred agreements with Mira is any evidence at all that the film is *good*, as opposed to evidence merely that Dita would enjoy it — given that the agreement is between the two of them, and that on a film Dita can never experience there is no independent check on whether their shared taste tracks anything. Second: what her wobble on case 9 indicates about which rule she is running, and whether it tells against one of your candidates.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — what she will say about (a) and about (b) — then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question back to me.
