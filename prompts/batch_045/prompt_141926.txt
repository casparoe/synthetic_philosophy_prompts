I work in comparative federalism — for the past two years on a project comparing how the United States, Germany, Switzerland and the European Union allocate competences between levels of government — and there is one argument that has stopped moving entirely, both in the literature I read and in our own working group. It is the argument about whether subsidiarity is a genuine principle with determinate content or an empty formula that any outcome can be dressed up in.

The two positions, stated as fairly as I can. One side holds that the principle gives real guidance: the standard formulation is a two-limb test — act at the higher level only if the lower level cannot sufficiently achieve the objective and the higher level can achieve it better — and whatever its indeterminacy in practice, it can be made operational, and its weak enforcement is a political or institutional failure, not a conceptual one. The other side holds that both limbs are plastic to the point of vacuity: the "cannot sufficiently achieve" limb is nearly always satisfiable, since any single jurisdiction that declines to cooperate makes uniform action impossible, while the "better achieved" limb simply restates whatever cost-benefit judgement the decision-maker was going to make anyway. On that reading the principle functions as a legitimation formula for a settlement reached on other grounds, which is why, as far as I can tell, courts in all four systems have been extremely deferential in applying it and why the EU's mechanism for national parliaments to object has been used only rarely.

My hypothesis is that this is three disagreements under one name. First, a difference about which principle is in play: an epistemic one (which level has the better information), a democratic one (proximity, voice, the interests genuinely affected), and a liberal one (protecting self-rule, exit and interjurisdictional competition) — each camp quietly selecting the reading that supports the outcomes it likes. Second, a disagreement about which cases count as data: one side's paradigm case is a cross-border spillover or a race to the bottom, the other's is divergent local preferences and policy experimentation, and once the paradigm is fixed the two sides largely converge. Third, a hidden empirical question about the relative frequency and severity of those two case types, which nobody seems to think is their job to answer.

There is also a wrinkle I have not seen addressed and that I would like taken seriously. Subsidiarity ranks a given set of levels, but the set of levels is itself a political artefact: river basins, metropolitan labour markets, airsheds and currency areas are not levels in any of the four constitutions, and the principle, as stated, has nothing to say about whether the right unit of decision is one that does not yet exist. Is that a fatal incompleteness — the principle cannot rank until someone supplies a theory of the correct partition, and no such theory is on offer — or is it a detachable objection that a defender can absorb?

What I want is a diagnosis of the impasse, not an overview of the doctrine and not a verdict on who wins. Is my three-part hypothesis right, and if so which component is doing most of the work? Is the core of it verbal, a clash of incommensurable starting intuitions, or a real but unacknowledged empirical question? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a refinement of the test's second limb, a formal result about the insufficiency limb under non-cooperation, a comparative empirical programme, or a change of question? Or is this a case where no progress should be expected, and if so, why?
