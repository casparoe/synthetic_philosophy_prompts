I host a monthly long-form interview series for a graduate philosophy club, and the format is deliberately narrow: instead of asking guests to survey a field, I spend an hour pressing on a single, well-developed position and the specific objections it has attracted. Next month's guest is a moral philosopher — let's call her Dr. Nadia Ferrante — who defends a view she calls relevance generalism. Her starting point is that contemporary particularists are right about the holism of reasons: a feature that counts in favor of an action in one situation may be morally inert in another, and may even count against the very same kind of action in a third. But she denies that this forces us to give up moral principles. Her distinctive move is to split the notion of codifiability in two: generalism, she argues, only needs relevance to be codifiable — there can be true and genuinely general principles saying that a feature of a certain type is always morally relevant, always capable of mattering — and it does not need weight or verdict to be codifiable. On her account, what the particularism/generalism dispute is really about is whether the weight of reasons, or the overall verdict, can be captured in general terms; and principles that fix relevance without fixing weight still do real work, by disciplining attention, by marking which aspects of a situation are candidates for mattering at all, and by giving disputants a shared vocabulary in which to press and answer each other.

I'd like you to draft eight to ten questions for this interview, each with a follow-up written against the answer she is most likely to give, and ordered so that the sequence closes off her easiest moves as it goes. Please cover at least these pressure points. First: whether exceptionless relevance claims are even coherent for someone who accepts holism — if a feature can be inert in some case, in what sense is it always relevant, and what is she saying if she distinguishes being relevant from being salient, worth attending to, or capable of mattering? Second: whether relevance without weight can guide anything — if her principles never tell an agent how much a feature counts, do they constrain deliberation or merely annotate it, and how is this different from the particularist's own "default and challenge" picture, where principles are read as claims about what happens absent other considerations? Third: the explanatory question — if two actions share every feature that her principles mark as relevant and yet differ in deontic status, what accounts for the difference, and if her answer is that there will always be some further difference, whether the holism has simply been pushed back a level rather than answered. Fourth: an objection I have not seen her address, about scale. Her apparatus seems built for an individual deliberating about a concrete situation she can take in, but we also assess institutions, laws, and policies that affect millions of people and whose particulars nobody perceives; that kind of assessment looks like it needs aggregation and rules. I want her pushed on whether professional codes of conduct, legal standards, or institutional review criteria have any normative force on her view or are merely heuristics, and on whether the same verdict about an individual case and about the policy that produced it are supposed to be reached by the same competence. Fifth: whether relevance is contingent on local circumstances — in a community where promises are rarely kept, is promise-breaking still relevant in her sense? — and if so, whether her principles turn out to be empirically conditioned and culturally parochial in a way generalists should find uncomfortable. Sixth: whether wholesale moral criticism is available without codified principles, and whether "getting the other person to see the case differently" is really distinguishable from persuasion, manipulation, or asking for deference to testimony.

Finally, I want the last question to be the one she will least enjoy: ask her to state, as precisely as she can, what would have to be true for the particularist to be right and her own position to collapse into it, and what consideration on the particularist's side she finds hardest to answer. Don't include any biographical or "how did you get into this" questions, and don't have her restate her view for the audience — assume the listeners already know it and want to see where it breaks.
