For a seminar on formal epistemology that I'm co-teaching this term, I am assembling a small dossier of in-universe bureaucratic documents from imagined worlds in which one technical result has been taken seriously enough to be written into law. The first session covers Aumann's agreement theorem: if two agents start from a common prior over a shared space of possibilities, and each agent's evidence is a partition of that space, then once their current probabilities for some event are common knowledge between them — as happens when each announces a number, hears the other's, revises, and the two repeat the exchange — the numbers must be identical, and the figure they land on is the probability conditional on everything that has by then become common knowledge between them; on the standard way of putting it, there is no rational agreeing to disagree. In the world I want you to write from, this has stopped being a party trick and has become the founding charter of a regulator, the Bureau of Common Priors, which settles commercial disputes, merger reviews, and faculty hiring fights by compelling the parties into a supervised sequence of posterior announcements until their figures match.

What I want the document to dramatize is everything the Bureau has had to invent in order to make a convergence result operable as paperwork, and everything it must quietly pretend not to notice. Work several of these into the answers: because the exchange is only guaranteed to terminate after finitely many rounds when the agents' partitions are finite, the Bureau caps supervised exchanges at a fixed number of rounds and issues a non-convergence notice when the parties are still apart at the whistle; because announcing a number unavoidably leaks information, litigants learned early on to misreport their real credences so as to give away less than the protocol demands, a practice the Bureau has named posterior laundering, or to retreat into hedged intervals and qualitative language; and because the theorem has nothing to say about an agent who was never aware of the possibility in question, the standard last-ditch move of a losing party is to plead unawareness and demand that the matter be re-filed on a freshly refined space of possibilities. Above all there are the two failures no amount of procedure can fix: a shared prior that is simply mistaken yields a unanimous, procedurally immaculate, confident consensus on a falsehood, and agreement about probabilities settles nothing about what is to be done, since the parties' utilities were never pooled — that work belongs to a sibling agency funded out of a different budget line.

What I'd like you to write is the FAQ section of the onboarding packet given to new junior analysts at the Bureau: eight to ten numbered questions and answers, about 600 to 700 words in total, in the deadpan and faintly cheerful voice of an HR manual that has long since stopped noticing how strange its subject matter is. Include at least one fully worked numeric example — two analysts, a disputed event, three or four rounds of announcement and revision converging on a single figure — and at least one answer in which the Bureau concedes something it genuinely cannot deliver, so that a reader finishes the page uncertain whether the Bureau is a scandal or a good idea. The comedy should come out of the mechanics rather than out of wordplay: a formal epistemologist should be able to tell that you know what the common prior is doing, why common knowledge of the posteriors is what carries the argument, and what the result leaves untouched. Please do not preface the piece with an explanation of the theorem or break character with authorial footnotes; the packet should still be legible to an intelligent outsider who has never heard of it.
