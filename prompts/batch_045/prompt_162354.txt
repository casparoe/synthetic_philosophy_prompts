I have recently, and rather against my inclination, ended up on the governing board of a secondary school that is rewriting its behaviour policy, and the working group's draft turns on a sentence I cannot get out of my head: that the school's aim should be to cultivate guilt rather than shame. I am expected to vote on it in a fortnight. The same argument has since arrived at my own kitchen table, where my partner and I are raising a seven-year-old across two quite different emotional upbringings and keep using both words as if their meaning were settled. And last month I was corrected in front of my own team at work, over something small, and I still could not tell you what it was that I felt — only that it had the flavour of both, and that I would not wish it on the child.

So before I read any further into the subject, I want the specification. Please write the desiderata that a theory of guilt and shame would have to satisfy in order to be adequate for a decision like the one in front of me: not a theory that pronounces one emotion the finer one in the abstract, but one that could tell an institution which of them — or which third thing — it ought to try to produce in the people it is responsible for. Give the case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark each as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. I would particularly like the list to settle, or at least expose, the following:

The three questions that I suspect are being conflated: which emotion is the more apt or fitting response, which produces the better conduct and for whom (the agent, the person harmed, the group), and which is the more liveable thing to carry. These can surely come apart, and the memo assumes they do not.

The extensional problem. The theory must let a governor look at an actual practice — a restorative conference, a public apology, a private conversation, a written reflection, a mark recorded in a register — and say which emotion that practice is cultivating. If it cannot discriminate at the level of practices, it cannot guide a policy. But it must also allow that one and the same practice may land as guilt in one pupil and shame in another. Tell me whether those two demands can both be met.

Shame without a witness: shame felt in solitude, shame directed at one's own past self, and shame for what someone else did — one's child, one's country, one's grandfather. Any account that leans on an actual audience must say what these are, and whether they are one emotion wearing disguises.

Shame under a norm the agent rejects: the teenager who is ashamed and who also judges, correctly, that the norm doing this to them is contemptible. I need to be able to say both that the shame is real and that it is misfelt; a theory that permits only one of those claims is of no use to me.

The case I keep circling back to. My employer now runs on dashboards, and I notice in myself a state that is about an act, rather like guilt, but whose object is a missed target rather than a person or a norm — call it audit-guilt if you like. Is it a species of guilt, a fourth thing, or evidence that the distinction between act-focus and self-focus is doing less work than people suppose? Your list of requirements needs somewhere to put it.

The shameless, and the guilt-proof. A culture that succeeded in abolishing shame would presumably lose something besides the suffering; I want the theory to say what, and to say whether shamelessness is always a deficit.

And the design problem proper. Emotions are not legislated directly: one changes practices, sanctions and architectures, and the emotion arrives later as a by-product, with lags and collateral effects. A theory fit for my purpose has to say something about which levers exist, about what else comes along with them, and about whose authority it is to do the choosing.

Then, briefly, tell me which pair of requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want — and which single requirement excludes the largest number of the standard lines: the act-versus-self account, the audience-and-ideal-spectator account, the status-and-belonging account, the old anthropological contrast between shame cultures and guilt cultures, and anything else you think belongs on the list. A short scorecard of those lines against your criteria is welcome, a paragraph or two apiece, but the criteria are the deliverable. No references or suggestions for further reading, please; I want your specification, not a survey of who has argued for what. And please answer in British English.
