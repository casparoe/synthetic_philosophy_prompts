I teach epistemology, and every year my students do something I don't have a word for, so I'm asking here.

Case one: somebody glances at a webpage on a Tuesday, sees the office is open Friday, thinks nothing more of it — nothing is riding on it. We all say she knows. Then I add that, as it happened, a person's life depended on her getting that right, and she had no idea at the time. Half the room takes it back: no, she didn't know. Nothing about her changed. Only what we found out afterwards about what was hanging on it.

Case two, the mirror image: somebody runs an elaborate set of checks on a lab result because a whole career depends on it, and nobody will grant knowledge. Then the decision gets postponed indefinitely, and now the very same students cheerfully say he knew all along. Again, nothing about him changed in the meantime.

So the verdict seems to track where the discussion has got to rather than how things stood with the thinker at the time, which is wierd if knowledge is the sort of thing you either have or lack at a time.

I know the ordinary version of this — jack up what's at stake and people stop saying "knows" — that's not what I'm after. I want the backwards-looking version. Does it have a name, and if so is one name enough, or are my two cases different animals? My colleagues just say "oh, that's the standard-shifting thing" and wave their hands, which tells me nothing. I need to know what exactly is shifting, what the nearest established notions are and where my cases don't quite fit them, and whether the later developments are meant to change what is true or only what we're in a position to say.
