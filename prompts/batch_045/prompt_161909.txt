I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm midway through a feature on how historians explain the Great Divergence — the long gap in income and industrial capacity that opened up between north-western Europe and the most commercially advanced regions of Asia. Everyone in the field uses the shorthand 'why Britain and not China?', and everyone also apologises for it. Some weeks ago I sat through a workshop dinner where two economic historians argued about whether the apology is enough: whether that question is merely crude and in need of refinement, or whether it is ill posed in a way that no amount of refining fixes. I couldn't tell who was winning, and it matters for how I frame the piece. I've reconstructed the exchange below from my notes, paraphrasing rather than transcribing; I'm not a historian and I'm deliberately not signalling which side I found persuasive.

1. Marta opened by saying the question is ill posed because its units don't match. China, she said, is a continental empire containing everything from dense commercialised river deltas to subsistence highlands, while Britain is a small island off the European coast. The comparison people actually find puzzling is between England and the Yangzi Delta, and her claim — which Dev did not dispute — is that by the usual proxies (real wages, market integration, commercialisation, the sophistication of credit institutions) the two advanced regions were within noise of each other well into the eighteenth century. On her view the famous question manufactures its own paradox by comparing a region to a civilisation, and the shock value disappears as soon as you re-scale.

2. Dev replied that re-scaling relocates the contrast rather than dissolving it: you still owe an answer to why England and not the delta. And the contrastive form, he argued, is doing indispensable methodological work. Ask the bare question, why did industrialisation happen in Britain?, and you get a shopping list of background conditions — coal, wages, institutions, science, culture, empire — every one of them plausibly necessary and no way to say which did the work. The foil is what turns the list into an explanation: if a candidate cause was present in the contrast case too, it cannot be the differentia. That, he said, is a genuine filter, not a rhetorical tic.

3. Marta answered that the filter only works if the foil is evidentially neutral, and no foil ever is. Choose China and you will conclude that institutions were decisive. Choose the Netherlands — rich, commercial, urbanised, and apparently not the site of the breakthrough — and you get a different differentia. Choose eighteenth-century France and you get a third. Because the foil is fixed before the test and the test never reflects back on the choice, the method converts a prior theoretical commitment into something that looks like independent evidence. She called it laundering.

4. Dev conceded the worry and offered a fix: multiply the foils, and require the differentia to survive all of them. He also proposed extending the foil set in time as well as in space — any adequate story of why Britain diverged should also be able to say why the commercially dynamic China of the eleventh and twelfth centuries did not, and why the delta did not converge in the nineteenth once it was in contact with industrialising economies. An explanation tuned to a single date and a single pair of places is, he said, a retrodiction dressed as a causal claim. The point of a foil panel is precisely that it lets you test the explanation on cases it wasn't built from.

5. Marta said the deeper problem survives the fix, because the defect is in the form of the question rather than in the choice of foil. 'Why X and not Y?' invites an answer of the shape: X had property P and Y lacked it. But the causal facts may be conjunctural or relational rather than intrinsic. Coal was cheap in Britain partly because of where it sat relative to navigable water and relative to a high-wage economy that made labour-saving machinery worth building; the relevant system may have been Atlantic rather than national. There need be no property that X possesses and Y lacks even when the causal histories genuinely differ, so a question whose answer must take that shape is asking for something that may not exist.

6. Dev replied that conjunctural answers fit the contrastive form perfectly well: you put the whole package — cheap coal, navigable water, high wages, Atlantic access — into the differentia slot and ask whether the package was present in the foil. So Marta has shown, he said, that the naive question invites mono-causal answers, not that it cannot be answered. But he granted that this pushes the argument somewhere uncomfortable: the larger the package gets, the more it risks ceasing to be an explanation and becoming a redescription of the case. He then raised what he took to be the real issue, which is that the phrase 'not China' conflates two very different counterfactuals. The first is: China lacked the cause. The second is: had Britain lacked it, sustained industrialisation would not have occurred anywhere in Eurasia for a long time afterwards. If the second is false — if Britain's failure would have been followed within a century by some other north-western European region — then the thing to be explained is a system-wide process and the place-level contrast misdescribes it. On his view, whether the question is well posed is hostage to an unsettled empirical claim about counterfactual scope.

7. Marta closed by saying that even granting all of this, the explanandum may not be a fact of the right kind. Britain is the case we know succeeded, so we have selected on the dependent variable. If regional growth rates across Eurasia were noisy, somebody had to be first, and being first may have no explanation beyond the upper tail of a distribution. In that case there is no X-rather-than-Y fact at all — only a draw that we have retrospectively invested with significance.

I'd be grateful for a point-by-point adjudication rather than a general essay. Concretely: rate each of the seven moves on a scale from clearly mistaken, through weak but defensible and basically right, to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per move, keyed to the argument actually made rather than to the best version of it. Then give me an overall verdict: is the question well posed, ill posed, or well posed only under conditions that can be specified — and who is more right overall, and by how much. Flag any point at which one of them is rebutting a version of the other's position the other doesn't hold, since I need to know whether parts of this exchange were two people talking past each other and are therefore unquotable. Note too that neither of them answered the last point the other made: Marta never responded to the counterfactual-scope argument, and Dev never responded to the selection argument. For each, tell me what the strongest available reply would have been and whether it would actually have worked. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing — the considerations an historian of science, a philosopher of explanation, or a quantitative economic historian would each want to add — and say which of those, if any, would change the verdict.
