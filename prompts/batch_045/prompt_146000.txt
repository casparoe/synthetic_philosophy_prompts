I serve on the clinical ethics committee of a large academic hospital, and for nearly two years now we have made no progress at all on the therapy/enhancement distinction. We split, roughly, three ways: those who think the line tracks something factual about species-typical functional organisation and that medicine's business is departures from it; those who hold that any such line is a value judgment wearing a lab coat and that we should simply be explicit that we are ranking interventions by what people have reason to want; and a third group who think the distinction itself is doing ideological work, that it launders controversial judgments about which lives are worth improving, and that we should abandon it and assess interventions one at a time by expected benefit, cost, and consent. I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on it, and not a survey of the positions either — I have read the standard papers on all three sides and can find more if I need them, so please give me your own argument and leave the reading list out of it.

The cases that actually divide us are not the well-worn ones — stimulants for healthy students, growth hormone for children who are merely short, surgery after the reconstructive surgery is finished. They are a cluster I have started cataloguing myself, and I think the usual accounts handle them badly. First, what I call the rising-floor case: a prenatal intervention that shifts an entire population's distribution upward, so that every recipient is better off than she would otherwise have been and still sits at the same percentile — therapy for everyone, or enhancement for everyone? Second, the baseline-conflict pair: an intervention that returns a patient to her own previous level of function while leaving her far below the population mean, and its mirror image, an intervention that lifts someone from well below the mean to just below it; different members of our committee count one of these as therapy and the other as enhancement, and they do not agree about which is which. Third, the conversion case: an intervention that does not restore the lost capacity at all but substitutes a different one, which outside observers rate as equally useful and which the patient experiences as alien — restoring a person, or replacing what was lost? Fourth, the maintenance case: holding someone at the functional level of a thirty-year-old at ninety, which at no single instant exceeds normal function and in total exceeds any normal human life. Fifth, and I suspect this is the one that really breaks the machinery, the third-party case: an intervention whose benefit accrues mainly to other people, such as a treatment that makes a patient with advanced dementia much easier to care for, or one that sharply lowers a person's infectiousness, where restoring normal function in the patient and helping the patient come cleanly apart.

My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than to flatter, is that we are not having one disagreement. I suspect we are asking a single distinction to do at least three separate jobs: an allocative job, about who has a claim on shared resources and what a health system owes; a permissive job, about what an adult may do to herself or a parent to a child without owing anyone a justification; and an interpretive job, about what the intervention means for how a patient's life, achievements, and identity are to be understood. I suspect each of these jobs selects a different line, that the cases above split the three jobs apart rather than merely testing one line, and that most of us switch which job we have in mind in the middle of a discussion — which is why we appear to contradict ourselves and why no argument ever seems to land. If that is right, tell me what follows: is the proper conclusion that the dispute is largely verbal, or that there is a real residual disagreement about whether any of the three jobs needs this concept at all, or that a hidden empirical question is doing the work — whether enhancement is largely positional and therefore self-defeating, say, or how patients actually fare when a lost capacity is substituted rather than restored? And I want your honest view on what would count as progress here: a distinction we are missing, a clean separation of the jobs, some empirical result, replacing the binary with a graded scale, or nothing, because the impasse is a sign that the concept has to keep several incompatible commitments simultaneously and always will.
