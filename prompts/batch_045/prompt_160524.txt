For about a decade my job has been allocating capital, and over that time I've come to hold a stronger version of a familiar rule than most of my colleagues are willing to endorse. I want to know what would have to be true for it to be false.

The position: when a single agent faces a long sequence of reinvested gambles whose payoffs rescale the whole of what the agent has — stakes set as a fraction of current capital, outcomes multiplied rather than added — rationality requires maximizing the expected growth rate of capital, equivalently the expected logarithm of the multiplicative factor, equivalently betting the Kelly fraction. I mean this as a claim about rationality, not as a report of my taste for risk. Three things carry it for me. First, the almost-sure comparison: run the sequence long enough and someone who bets a different fixed fraction, in either direction, ends up with capital that is negligible next to the growth-maximizer's. Second, the aggregation argument: if I am really advancing the interests of a series of future selves each of whom inherits a proportional stake in the whole, or of many beneficiaries whose stakes are themselves reinvested, then the quantity that is additive across time and across persons is the logarithm, so the log is not a preference I smuggled in — it falls out of the structure of the case. Third, the arithmetic-expectation alternative seems to me to get its plausibility from situations that lack these features: one-shot bets, or averages taken across many independent agents at a single time. I realize that the standard reply here is that expected utility theory makes logarithmic utility one utility function among others, and that choosing it just is having a certain attitude to risk; I think that reply is too quick, but I want to be told if I am wrong to think so.

So please skip the following, which I have already discounted or set aside: that people dislike drawdowns and will pay to avoid them; that on a finite horizon or with a threshold goal (I need a specific sum by a specific date, and more is worth nothing) the growth-maximizing bet is plainly wrong — I am asking about a pure long-run reinvesting agent, and I grant that the threshold case is different; and that log utility is merely one risk preference.

What I want is the defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, for the version I have stated, together with an honest note on which of them someone who defends this view would concede as counting against it rather than explain away. In particular I have been chewing on five pressure points, and I suspect some of them are more serious than others.

One: the dominance argument seems to assume that falling low is close enough to being finished. In practice my capital has a floor that is not absorbing — I can earn it back, the strategy gets recapitalized, or I move to income that does not scale with what I have left. Does the comparison still bite when ruin is soft, and if it does not, how much of the argument depended on that?

Two: if I know that my returns will be pooled across many independent copies of the strategy — parallel pods in a fund, or one institution spread over branches that do not share a single fate — then the arithmetic mean looks like the right object again, and maximizing it means betting far more aggressively than the growth-maximizing fraction. Is that a counterexample, or merely a case my claim was never about? And if it is the latter, how much of the normative force came from the assumption that there is exactly one thread?

Three: this style of argument has been advertised well beyond money, as a general standard for agents whose situation is not obviously multiplicative — health, skill, relationships, a life. I cannot tell whether that extension is the natural generalization of the same point or a slide from a real theorem to a metaphor, and the answer matters to me, because it determines whether the view is a small result about compounding or a large claim about rationality.

Four: strategic interaction. An adversary who knows that I maximize growth can quote me prices that make my growth-maximizing bet into a bad one, or extract from me in a way that a rule tuned to a passive distribution does not anticipate. Is optimality against a fixed process compatible with exploitable behavior against a counterparty who models me?

Five: model uncertainty. Once the distribution is not known, the practical advice becomes a fraction of the full bet, chosen for robustness. Is that an admission that the view does not after all deliver a unique rule, and was uniqueness doing the normative work?

And one meta-question I care about: has this view been qualified into something that cannot lose? If the defense of it consists in re-deriving a growth-rate criterion for whatever structure you hand it, so that the content of the claim shifts with the case, then the claim may be close to empty — I want to know whether it is, and whether that emptiness would be a defect or simply what a criterion of this kind can be.

Rank the defeaters, say which of my three supporting arguments is actually false as stated, mark your confidence on each item and flag where you are reconstructing an argument rather than reporting one, and tell me where you think you might be wrong. If my framing is confused — if I have run together two claims that should be assessed separately, or if "what would count against this" is not a well-formed question for a position of this shape — say that plainly rather than answering around it. I would rather be contradicted than humored.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: the single most exposed part of the position, as you see it. Then give the ranking and the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked.
