I teach a first-year seminar on the ethics of public memory — the philosophy side of it is mostly about statues and street names: what an act of commemoration actually is, whether it asserts a claim about the person commemorated or expresses an attitude of the community doing the commemorating, and what happens to the moral picture when the people who have to live with a monument are no longer the people who put it up. I want to close the unit by screening Spike Lee's Do the Right Thing (1989) and doing a close reading of a single strand of it: the quarrel over the Wall of Fame in Sal's pizzeria. You will remember the shape of it. Buggin' Out notices that Sal's wall is covered with photographs of famous Italian-Americans and demands pictures of Black celebrities, on the ground that the pizzeria's customers are almost all Black; Sal tells him it is his wall, in his pizzeria, and that he honors whom he likes on his own premises; the attempted boycott draws in only Radio Raheem and Smiley; the second confrontation ends with Sal smashing Raheem's boombox, Raheem's death at the hands of the police, and the burning of the pizzeria, with Smiley finally pinning his hand-coloured picture of Martin Luther King Jr. and Malcolm X onto the wall of the building as it goes up. Alongside that, I want you to consider two minor threads: Smiley spending the whole day wandering the block trying to sell those photographs, which nobody wants to buy, and Radio Raheem's boombox, playing Public Enemy at volume wherever he goes, as a second and non-visual claim on the same shared space.

What I would like is a reading of that strand as a contribution to the commemoration debate, not as a general film about race in America — I can get that anywhere. In the seminar I have been using a rough three-part schema to keep the arguments apart: the subject question (whom we honor), the voice question (in whose name the honoring is done — a person's, a group's, a polity's), and the speech-act question (whether the commemoration praises, or merely keeps someone before the public eye, or instead makes a claim about who belongs here). My suspicion is that the film's real interest is that a dispute that looks like the first question is secretly about the second and third, and I want you to say whether that is right and where each scene falls. Two things in particular keep bothering me. First, there is what I have been calling the addition asymmetry: Buggin' Out asks for names to be added, not removed, which by the standard arguments should be the cheapest demand there is — nothing is erased, nobody's history is denied — yet it provokes exactly the resistance a removal demand would. My own hypothesis is that the honoring on that wall is positional: the statement it makes is not a list of admirable people but something like this is whose place this is, so an addition changes the statement rather than lengthening the list. Test that, and give me a better one if you have it. Second, there is the conversion thesis: Sal's wall is not a state monument, it is decoration in a shop, and the literature I teach is largely about what a political community says in the name of all its members — so the film should be an easy case for Sal. It does not feel like one. Is that because a private display in a commercial space converts into a public one when the people subject to it cannot avoid it and did not authorize it, because Sal's ownership of the block's most prominent wall is entangled with his economic power over the neighborhood, or because the film is quietly cheating?

I would also like the reading to be honest in the other direction, since I have to defend it to a film-studies colleague. Where does the analogy between a pizzeria wall and a Confederate statue break — the wall is décor, the photographs of entertainers and athletes are a different kind of honoring than a monument to a statesman, and Sal's refusal to be told whom to admire on his own property is not obviously wrong? Does the film earn its ending, or does it simply let a riot settle an argument about recognition that it has no answer to? And is the ending itself a commemorative act of a kind the philosophical literature has no name for: Smiley's photograph going up on the wall of a building that is at that moment being destroyed, followed by the paired epigraphs attributed to King and Malcolm X, one counseling nonviolence and one countenancing self-defense, placed side by side without the film ranking them — is the film proposing commemoration without verdict, and is that coherent or just evasive? Finally, for teaching purposes: if I can pause the film on two or three moments, which should they be, and what should a philosopher of commemoration say the film understands about statues and street names that the arguments alone do not reach?
