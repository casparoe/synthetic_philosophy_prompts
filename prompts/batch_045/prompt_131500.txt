I work as a heating engineer, and I go to a fortnightly discussion group that meets in the back room of a pub, where we take turns bringing a question. Last time someone brought a puzzle about intention that I had not come across before, and two of the regulars, Marta and Yusuf, spent most of the evening arguing about it. I have tried to reproduce their exchange fairly below, but I could not tell who was winning, and I would be grateful for a careful adjudication.

The puzzle as it was presented: an eccentric billionaire puts a vial in front of you. If you drink it, you will be miserably ill for about a day, with no lasting harm and no other effects. The billionaire will pay you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink the vial tomorrow afternoon. The money is deposited as soon as the intention is registered by a machine that reads intentions perfectly, hours before you would have to drink anything. So you never have to drink: you are paid for the intention alone, and by the time afternoon comes you will have no reason at all to drink. The question is whether you can form that intention, given that you know you will have no reason to act on it and so will not act on it.

Marta said she thought the usual way of telling the story hides an assumption, and offered a variant. In her version, everything is the same except that the billionaire also runs a side business: for fifty dollars, payable at eleven o'clock that evening, you can hire what she called the Chaperone, an escort who will ensure that you drink the vial at noon tomorrow, by force if necessary, unless you drink it voluntarily first. The hiring is irrevocable for that evening, the Chaperone cannot be dismissed or bribed, and the arrangement is well known to you at the time the machine reads your mind.

Their exchange went roughly like this.

1. Marta: With the Chaperone hired, the obstacle the puzzle describes simply disappears. The standard argument is that you cannot intend to drink, because you know that once the money is in your account you will have no reason to drink and therefore will not drink. But if you have hired the Chaperone, you do know that you will drink. So you can intend, the machine fires, and you collect a million dollars minus fifty. The famous conclusion, that a rational agent is shut out of the money altogether, is therefore false. What is true is only that the rational agent cannot get the money for free, which is an ordinary fact about having to pay for what you get, not a paradox about rationality.

2. Yusuf: You are buying the money with the day of illness, and that changes what is at issue. Nobody ever denied that you could drink the toxin for a million dollars if the billionaire simply paid for drinking; a day of misery is presumably worth far less than a million. The puzzle is whether you can get the money without drinking, and whether the norms governing intention permit it. Your Chaperone does not answer that, so it does not really engage the difficulty.

3. Marta: Then the moral of the story has to be restated, and restated it is much weaker than the version people repeat. The tale is always told as one in which rationality costs you a million. In my case it costs you fifty dollars and a disagreeable day, and still leaves you very nearly a million better off. If the point was never practical but purely conceptual, whether the intention as such is available to you, then say so plainly, and accept that the puzzle shows nothing about what a rational agent can achieve, which was the part that made it striking.

4. Yusuf: The conceptual point survives, and here is why the Chaperone does not give you an intention. An intention is not merely a forecast of what will happen to you. If I know that the Chaperone will make me drink whether or not I intend to, then my state of mind at midnight plays no part in bringing the drinking about; I am only predicting what is going to happen to me. But the machine is described as reading intentions. If it paid out on predictions instead, the billionaire would be paying people who are about to be forcibly dosed against their will, which is plainly not the bargain. So your variant works only by getting the machine to read something other than an intention.

5. Marta: That is a genuine distinction, but look at what it commits you to. You are saying that the barrier to intending is not that I will not drink, since in the Chaperone case I certainly will, but that my intention would not be what causes the drinking. That is a substantive and contested claim about what intentions have to be. On the view that intending is a matter of being settled on a course of action, and being disposed to reason and act from that settlement, the Chaperone case looks like a perfectly good intention: I am settled on drinking, I have arranged my affairs around it, and I would rather drink of my own accord than be made to. So the puzzle cannot simply assume your stricter condition; it has to defend it, and that defence is where the real argument now has to happen.

6. Yusuf: Grant that, and there is still something the variant shows that tells against you. Once the Chaperone is hired, the midnight state of mind is doing no work: the money, the drinking and the illness were all settled by the decision at eleven o'clock. So what the case actually shows is that for any reward paid out for a state of mind, a rational agent will route around the state of mind and make an ordinary decision instead, by hiring the escort, signing the contract, or swallowing the pill. Intentions formed on purpose in order to collect are not intentions in the sense that matters; they are instruments. So the variant does not show that the rational agent can intend. It shows that in these setups the rational agent never needs to.

7. Yusuf, continuing: And your deflation depends on a contingent price. Suppose the Chaperone is not fifty dollars but nine hundred and ninety thousand, because the billionaire enjoys watching people squirm. Then your rational agent nets almost nothing, and the story has all of its original bite back. Whether rationality loses you a million or a hundred dollars is a matter of what commitment technology happens to cost, which is exactly the sort of thing the puzzle was not supposed to depend on.

8. Marta: On the last point, if the Chaperone is expensive the agent still ends up with something rather than the nothing the standard story predicts, and the size of the loss becomes an empirical question about prices rather than a conceptual result about rationality, which is my point rather than yours. On the earlier one, I am happy to agree that the eleven o'clock choice does the practical work. But that is a claim about how agents actually solve these problems, not a reason to deny that the midnight intention is present. And it cuts both ways: if rational agents always arrange to be caused to act whenever the reward is large enough, then the thesis that a rational agent cannot intend to do what it has no reason to do is idle, and what would be left is a thesis about agents who decline to tie their own hands. I do not see why the theory should be built around those agents.

I have tried not to editorialise, and I genuinely cannot tell which of them is ahead. Could you go through the eight moves and say, for each, whether the point being made is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why? I would rather have a verdict on each step than a single overall winner, though I would also like your sense of who is more right overall. And if you think both of them have missed something that matters more than anything they said, I would like to hear what that is.
