Got a slightly odd commision and I'd love you to have a go at it. Our paper runs a weekly advice column in the Saturday magazine — the usual sort of thing, readers write in about a dilemma and the resident expert tells them what to do. I want a parody of that format: a column called something like 'The Indifference Desk', with an agony aunt who is a philosophical decision theorist, and three or four letters with replies, 600-700 words total. The joke, and it's the bit I care about, is that she is completely sincere, completely scrupulous, and never once manages to tell anybody what to do.

The cases should all be ones of choice without preference, in the Buridan's ass sense, and the comedy has to come from getting the position right rather than from donkeys in fields. Roughly:

- A letter about two options the writer genuinely cannot rank — two identical jars of jam on a supermarket shelf will do — where the reply leans on the standard permissivist line that if neither is better than the other then either one is rationally permissible, while quietly never managing to recommend either, because permissibility isn't advice.
- A letter from someone who took the usual counsel to just pick one, or to break the tie with some trivial feature, and writes back complaining that this dissolves the problem rather than solving it: the tie-breaking reason looks like it should have been there in the deliberation all along, and if it was, then they weren't really indifferent in the first place.
- A letter about a coin. The writer has flipped one and now wants to know why the result binds them, given that a moment ago they had no reason to favour either side. The columnist should treat the regress as a sign of real progress — maybe recommend a machine for automating it — without ever conceding that it bottoms out anywhere.
- One that brings in the two standard escape routes from the whole puzzle: that exact ties are a knife-edge that basically never occurs in real life, so the case is unrealistic; and that some hard choices aren't ties at all but options that aren't ranked as better, worse or equal, where the agent is supposed to put their will behind one rather than be guided by reasons. She should be visibly more comfortable with these and still end up saying nothing anyone could act on.
- Optionally a last one-line letter from someone signing off as a donkey from Burgundy. Deadpan, no wink.

Voice: warm, faintly patronising, entirely straight-faced. She believes she is being enormously helpful and is genuinely puzzled that nobody is helped. No puns, no nudging, no aside that lets the reader off the hook. Someone who knows nothing should still work out that she is useless; someone who knows the literature should think the target is being treated fairly — the target being the way rational choice theory handles indifference by multiplying permissions instead of ever producing a recommendation. And no real philosophers' or papers' names anywhere in it, I dont want any invented footnotes.

After the piece, add a short note, about 150 words, being honest about the epistemics: which of the moves in the column are boring orthodoxy and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this — whether indifference is ever exact, whether randomising needs its own justification, whether that unranked-but-comparable category is even coherent — and where you've exaggerated or leaned on a contested reading for the sake of the joke. Dont present any of the contested ones as what everyone thinks.
