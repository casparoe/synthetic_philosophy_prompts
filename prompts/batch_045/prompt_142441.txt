My first answer was that this is simply what the experiment does, and then I stopped being sure that the experiment settles it. The section below is my attempt to say what turns on that choice.

One thing up front: please don't spend any time on references, on who has argued for what, or on where this sits in the literature. **§2. Two sampling models**

Standard protocol: on Sunday a fair coin is tossed. Heads: Beauty is awakened on Monday only. Tails: she is awakened on Monday and on Tuesday, with the usual amnesia-inducing drug in between. Let each waking last one hour. On being awakened she is asked for her credence in Heads. Thirders say 1/3, halfers say 1/2.

My claim is that both parties, and nearly every argument on either side, tacitly help themselves to a *sampling model*: a specification of which centered proposition Beauty is to conditionalize on. Nothing in the bare description of the experiment settles which one, and the standard protocol is precisely a case in which the two most natural models return the same number. That coincidence, I will argue, is why the dispute has looked like a fault line in rationality rather than an underdescription in the example.

The waking model (W): Beauty is asked once per waking. Her evidence is the centered proposition *this is an asking-occasion of the sort the experimenters produce at each waking*. The likelihood of finding herself at such an occasion is proportional to the number of wakings the branch contains: 1 under Heads, 2 under Tails. Bayes gives 1/3.

The time model (T): Beauty is asked at a moment selected uniformly at random from a fixed window (Sunday midnight to Wednesday midnight, say), the question being put only if she happens to be awake at that moment; she knows this in advance, has no clock, and cannot tell how long she has been awake. Her evidence is the centered proposition *this is a moment at which the probe finds me awake*, whose likelihood is proportional to the total time she is awake in the branch: one hour under Heads, two under Tails. Bayes gives 1/3 again. (The length of the window cancels, provided it contains every waking.)

The halfer, on this map, is conditionalizing on the uncentered counterpart — *Beauty is awake at least once* — which is certain on every branch. No update, hence 1/2. The halfer is not making a mistake about updating or about self-location; she is answering a question about a different proposition from the thirder's. That is why the two sides talk past each other.

In the standard protocol W and T agree, and that is the accident the whole literature runs on. Two variants prise them apart.

*Variant I (the long Tails waking).* Heads: one waking, Monday, one hour. Tails: one waking, Monday, two hours. Beauty is awakened exactly once on every branch. W: one asking either way, so 1/2. T: one hour against two, so 1/3.

*Variant II (the equal-time protocol).* Heads: one waking, Monday, two hours. Tails: two wakings, Monday and Tuesday, one hour each. Total waking time is now the same on both branches. W: one asking against two, so 1/3. T: two hours against two, so 1/2.

So the halfer's 1/2 coincides with the correct verdict in Variant I (as given by W) and in Variant II (as given by T), in each case for the wrong reason. I take this to explain the peculiar stability of halfer intuitions: the position is accidentally right in exactly the cases where its own rationale is idle, so it feels robust when it is merely lucky.

Which model is correct? I doubt there is a protocol-independent answer, but I think W is in worse shape than T, for two reasons. First, W is not invariant under repartitioning a single stretch of consciousness. In Variant I, partition the two-hour Tails waking into n equal wakings separated by gaps of dreamless sleep of duration ε. Total time awake is unchanged; Beauty's evidence at every moment of wakefulness is unchanged, since she is unconscious through the gaps and cannot tell how long she has been awake; the centered possibilities open to her are the same up to the measure of the gaps. Yet W's verdict moves from 1/2 to 1/(n+1), and can be pushed arbitrarily close to 0 by increasing n while her conscious experience and the total measure of her wakefulness stay fixed. T returns 1/3 for every n and every ε. And there is no non-stipulative answer to how large ε must be for one waking to become two — a second, a minute, a sleep cycle — so W's inputs are partly conventional in a way that T's are not. Second, the standard arguments each presuppose a model without saying so: the betting and frequency arguments all stage the offer at each waking, which builds in W; the thought that she knew all along she would be woken builds in the uncentered proposition. Neither so much as mentions the alternative, so neither can discriminate between models that coincide in the standard case.

The diagnosis I want to defend is that nothing distinctive about self-location is doing any work. Conditionalizing on a centered proposition is ordinary Bayesian conditionalization; all the difficulty lies in fixing which centered proposition the protocol makes salient. What has looked like a conflict about rational belief under self-locating uncertainty is an ambiguity in the example.

Where I am stuck: if the original question, as standardly posed, really does fix W — because the question is put to her on being awakened — then 1/3 is right in the standard case, but on my own showing the usual arguments are not what make it right. Worse, W is then committed to saying that whether Beauty should believe 1/2 or 1/3 of a fair coin turns on a stipulation about how the sedative schedule carves up one continuous stretch of consciousness: a stipulation that makes no difference to the chances, to what she experiences, or to what she is in a position to know about where she is. I take that to be a reductio of W, but I can hear the reply that knowledge of the protocol is knowledge, and that a difference she cannot detect may still be a difference she knows about. I also cannot tell whether my reframing is a genuine diagnosis or only the trivial remark that credences depend on what one conditions on, together with the observation that three protocols are three experiments.
