I'm writing a novel about Wegener's last expedition — the Greenland trip he died on in 1930 — and I keep getting stuck on something I can't settle well enough to put in a character's mouth. So I don't want the heroic version of this story; I want the argument looked at properly.

The frame I want used is the old James-versus-Clifford fight: Clifford's The Ethics of Belief (1877, in the Contemporary Review), with its shipowner who talks himself into believing the emigrant ship is seaworthy instead of having her overhauled, and the claim that the wrong was already done the moment he believed it, whether or not the ship went down — the wrong being a kind of treason against every thinking person, a habit of credulity that spreads. Against that, James's The Will to Believe (1896), which says that when an option is of the forced, living and momentous kind and the evidence genuinely cannot settle it by intellectual means, our passional nature not only may but must decide, and that in a range of cases — confidence in yourself before a hard thing, trust extended to another person — the belief is part of what makes its own object real.

Facts as I take them to be, so you can correct me only if I've got something flat wrong: Wegener publishes The Origin of Continents and Oceans in 1915, argues the continents have moved; he's trained as a meteorologist, working outside geology; his evidence is the fit of the coastlines, the same fossils and rock belts and the Permo-Carbonifrous glaciation showing up on continents now separated by oceans. What he doesn't have is a mechanism, and what he proposes — continents ploughing through the sea floor, driven partly by tidal and pole-fleeing forces — is physically impossible, and the geophysicists who said so, people like Harold Jeffreys, were right about that part. The geological mainstream mostly refuses him for something over forty years, and then in the late fifties and sixties seafloor spreading and the magnetic striping on the ocean floor turn drift into plate tectonics, and Wegener turns out to have been right about the thing that mattered. He never saw any of it.

What I want from you:

1. A disciplined mapping rather than a vibe. Who is the subject of the ethics here — Wegener himself, the geological community, or both, and does the verdict come out the same for each? What were the real options as of 1915? I don't think it's a clean two-way, because withhold-and-carry-on-with-normal-geology is on the table, and James has an answer to that — he says putting the question off is itself a decision, with the same risk of losing the truth. Deal with that answer: does it work here, or does it make forcedness so cheap that every option counts as forced? And say what counted as evidence in 1915 against what counted in 1965, and whether the evidence was ever sufficient for anyone alive at the time.

2. The crux, which I havn't managed to get straight: believing versus treating-as-true. Wegener's posture, a lot of the time, was that of a man with a working hypothesis he was trying to load with evidence. If that's acceptance rather than belief, then it looks to me like Clifford has no complaint to make and James has no work to do, and the whole debate slides past him. So: which was it, and does the difference change the verdict? Underneath that is a second thing — James's argument seems to need you to be able to take up a belief because of what believing will do for you, and he himself seems to wobble on whether anyone can do that, at one point granting we can't believe at will and then walking it back. If belief isn't under the will at all, does his position quietly turn into advice about acting as if, or about what to spend your life investigating, and would that thinner version still license what Wegener did?

3. Where the lens helps and where it distorts. Make precise, not gestural, the thing the will-to-believe framing gets right about this episode — I think it's something about a belief's power to organize a research life, to send a man off measuring things nobody else thought worth measuring, so that the evidence only arrives because somebody believed first. Then where it goes wrong: the skeptics were right about the mechanism and wrong about the drift, which wrecks the prophet-versus-establishment shape in a way I can't name; a community's refusal could be Clifford at the individual level and something quite different collectively, since someone has to be the one to chase the impossible idea; and a belief can be vindicated in outline while being wrong about everything the believer thought made it true, which I don't see either essay having room for.

4. What the episode does back to the theory. Does Wegener confirm the will to believe, does it embarrass it, or is it a case the argument was never built for — communities instead of persons, hypotheses instead of beliefs, vindication that isn't confirmation? Finish with a verdict rather than a shrug, but make it a verdict about the shape of the argument, not about whether I ought to feel sorry for Wegener.

Two last things. Don't hand me reading lists, citations, or anything that would need looking up; I want the thinking, not the sources. And I've read somewhere that historians now think the dismissal wasn't mostly about him being an outsider at all, but about drift looking untestable and unmechanical — a methodological objection rather than plain conservatism. I'm hazy on that, so weigh it as a possibility rather than as established fact.
