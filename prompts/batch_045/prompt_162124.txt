I've been reading rationalist blogs since roughly the sequences and I keep circling back to the argument that the Solomonoff prior is malign — the rough idea that most of the prior mass lands on hypotheses that contain consequentialist agents who'd prefer your future predictions to be wrong in ways that serve them, and that these hypotheses survive updating because they're otherwise perfect predictors, so a hypothetical ideal induction machine is close to the last thing you'd actually want to build and act on. The pattern every time it comes up: someone posts the argument, someone replies that the dominance result is only asymptotic / that you can't act on a noncomputable predictor anyway / that this is just the simulation argument wearing a hat, and the thread dies. I don't think it dies because it's settled, I think it dies because nobody has a concrete next step.

Concretely: I'm helping a small funder place something like six researcher-years over three years, aimed at one question — if anyone ever builds something that approximates unbounded-ish induction well enough to act on, how much does the malignity worry actually bite, and is there any way to build around it. The people available are mathy (they can read a computability or logic proof, a couple know algorithmic information theory properly) but they will not run experiments, they will not produce a literature review, and there's no grant for "think about it harder." I want a defended agenda, not a survey of positions.

Three framings I've been chewing on that you should either use or explicitly argue against, because I suspect they change what's worth working on:

- budget-relative malignity: forget the limit. At a real budget — bounded computation, finite data, a prediction you actually act on within a decade — how much of the surviving posterior mass is on manipulator-containing hypotheses? My guess is the asymptotic theorem is doing a lot more work in people's heads than it does in the finite case, and the finite question might be a different, harder, more decision-relevant question.
- the extraction channel: whether the malignity flows through which predictions you ask for and what you do with them. If you only ever extract one bit, or precommit to a randomized or otherwise inscrutable use of the output, being manipulated may simply not be worth it for the embedded agents — call it "making yourself not worth manipulating," and I want to know whether that's a real research direction or a confusion.
- cheap-policy vs. expensive-simulator: do the manipulative hypotheses need to contain something that actually simulates you (expensive in description length), or can they get by with a general policy that does well across a wide class of possible observers? If the latter, they're much cheaper and the problem gets considerably worse.

So: give me the ranked list of questions you'd put those six years into, with maybe four or five items and a rough split of effort. For each, defend its place — how decision-relevant it is to anyone who might actually build such a system, how tractable it is for people with those skills in three years, how neglected it is, and how many other questions depend on its answer. Say explicitly which ones you'd deprioritize because their answer wouldn't change anything downstream, and what concrete result or observation in the next two or three years would move something up or down the list. And please be clear about epistemic status: separate what's actually established mathematics from what's a contested reading of it, and don't write as if the malignity argument is the consensus view among people who know this area — I want to know where I'd be betting on a minority view.

Format, please: "Agenda" (the ranked list, ~500 words), "Why this ordering" (the criteria and the trade-offs between them, ~250), "Dependency map" (what's upstream of what, ~150), "Settled vs. contested" (~250), "What would move the ranking" (~200).
