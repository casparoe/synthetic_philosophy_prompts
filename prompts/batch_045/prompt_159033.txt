You are a professional moral philosopher who has published several papers on Bernard Williams's internal reasons thesis. Take your time: begin with a page of private brainstorming — which objections to internalism are genuinely load-bearing and which are dialectical noise — and only then draft the essay. Do not consult or cite any literature; argue from the structure of the positions themselves.

The essay should settle the following controversy. Williams holds that an agent has a reason to act only if some element of her subjective motivational set — desires, projects, commitments, self-conceptions, deliberative habits — could be brought by a route of sound deliberation to the conclusion that she should act. Most readers assume this yields an uncomfortably agent-relative, potentially quietist picture: for the agent who genuinely lacks the relevant motivation, there is nothing to be said.

Now introduce a complication the literature tends to skate past. Nearly every ordinary adult agent has, inside her motivational set, what I will call a reason-tracking policy: a standing, higher-order commitment of the form when I come to believe that there is decisive reason for me to do something, that settles it for me. Call the resulting position echo internalism: because such policies are nearly universal, and because a policy of that shape licenses a deliberative route from any candidate consideration to action, the distinction between internal and external reasons collapses for nearly every real agent — every external reason echoes into an internal one, and Williams's thesis loses the bite both its defenders and its critics take it to have.

Write an essay that asks whether echo internalism is a reductio of the internal reasons thesis, a consequence Williams should simply accept, or something to be blocked by principled restriction — for instance by holding that only substantive ends, and not formal reason-tracking policies, can ground internal reasons, or only elements that survive a specified deliberative test. Weigh the case for each: what internalism was supposed to explain in the first place (the way a reason statement can figure in the explanation and guidance of an agent's action, rather than standing apart from her motivations as a bare verdict); whether the restriction on grounding elements can be stated non-arbitrarily, or is merely a device for preserving the thesis's dramatic verdicts; and whether the residual case — the agent who lacks even a reason-tracking policy — shows that the thesis must still bite the bullet somewhere, and if so whether that matters.

Reach a definite verdict and defend it, and say explicitly which premise of Williams's argument you think the case most exposes as underdetermined. Aim for roughly 900–1,100 words, and do not end on a non-committal note.
