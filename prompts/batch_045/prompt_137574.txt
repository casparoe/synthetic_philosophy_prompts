I work in machine learning. My working vocabulary is priors and posteriors, likelihood ratios, hypothesis classes, sampling distributions and covariate shift, annotation budgets, active learning, generalization bounds, and out-of-distribution evaluation. I have no formal training in philosophy, but I can follow a probabilistic argument. I would like you to restate Hempel's raven paradox in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then give me a careful report on what does not survive the translation.

As I understand it, the paradox has three pieces. First, a Nicod-style judgement: observing a black raven supports the hypothesis that all ravens are black, and observing a non-black non-raven (a red herring, say) supports the contrapositive hypothesis that all non-black things are non-ravens. Second, an equivalence condition: whatever confirms a hypothesis must equally confirm any logically equivalent formulation of it, since the two say the same thing about the world. Third, the conjunction: the red herring confirms the hypothesis that all ravens are black, which is absurd, and the way out is blocked. I know there is a standard Bayesian aside to the effect that the red herring does confirm, but only negligibly. Please do not stop there: derive the result, and then tell me what that aside establishes and what it does not.

What I want, in order.

1. A translation of each of the three pieces into my terms. Set up an explicit sample space, an explicit hypothesis space, and an explicit data-generating protocol, and derive what the observation of one non-black non-raven does to the posterior under three protocols: objects drawn uniformly from a finite catalogue of all objects; objects drawn uniformly from the non-black subset of that catalogue; objects drawn uniformly from the ravens. Tell me which protocol recovers the Nicod intuition, which one makes the red herring evidentially inert, and which single assumption is doing all the work. Where you have to add structure the paradox itself does not supply — a prior over the number of ravens or over the size of the exception set, an exchangeability assumption, a finite rather than open-ended domain — flag it as an addition rather than presenting it as part of the translation.

2. The version I actually care about, since it is the one my job forces on me. Suppose I have a fixed annotation budget: a fixed number of expert labels, each with roughly equal cost. I can spend them either on objects sampled from the ravens or on objects sampled from the catalogue at large. Give the expected change in log-odds per label, or whichever informativeness measure you prefer, under each strategy, and compare them. Then say whether the paradox retains any sting once cost per observation is on the table, or whether it was always partly a claim about the exchange rate between evidence and effort. Take a position and defend it.

3. A residue report in three parts: what translates cleanly; what translates only with distortion, and say what the distortion is; and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Some candidates I want you to rule on explicitly. Hempelian confirmation is a qualitative relation between a single observation statement and a hypothesis, meant to be independent of how the observation came to be made, whereas everything I have is cardinal and indexed to a sampling protocol. The hypothesis quantifies over an open-ended domain including objects nobody has looked at and objects that do not yet exist, which sits outside any fixed sample space and looks more like a claim about domain generalization than a claim about a distribution. A universal generalization is either absent from a practical hypothesis class or is merely one point in a space whose other members I must specify by hand, and the choice of neighbouring hypotheses is doing hidden work in any probabilistic reconstruction. And confirmation is supposed to have a normative upshot for inquirers, a claim about what one ought to go look for, whereas my framework only scores predictions against a loss on a deployment distribution. Deal with each, and add any residue you think I have missed.

4. A verdict on that residue. Is the fact that my vocabulary is protocol-bound and tied to predictive risk a genuine blind spot, something a theory of confirmation needs and a learner-evaluation framework structurally cannot supply? Or is it evidence that the protocol-free notion of evidence was doing less work than it appeared to do? If the former, sketch what a framework possessing the missing piece would have to look like. If the latter, say what the paradox was really about.

Constraints. About 1200 words, in prose with formulae where a formula is shorter than a sentence. Define any term of art you use beyond the ones I listed above. Do not give me citations, bibliographies, or reading suggestions, and do not rest anything on my knowing a particular author, paper, or date: every claim should be checkable from the derivation or the argument you actually give.
