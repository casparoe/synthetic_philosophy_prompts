I do appraisal at a small regional archive — local government records, a couple of donated family collections, the odd box nobody's opened since 1974 — and I've noticed I have really firm gut verdicts about what should be kept, opened, sealed, handed over, or let go, and no story about why they hang together. So: cases first, then you tell me the principle.

1. A colonial district office's tax rolls, 1890s to 1950s, listing every adult male by hut, livestock, and tax owed. Straightforward instrument of extraction. Verdict: keep, no question, and index them by place so descendants can find their own grandfathers in them.

2. A children's institution's admission registers, 1930s–60s, naming kids, their parents, and the reason for admission. Verdict: keep, but access conditions set with the descendant community, not by me.

3. A 1970s police file naming people who informed on their neighbours. Verdict: seal it, and I don't feel bad, even though in twenty years a historian will want it.

4. A run of arrest photographs and charge books for men picked up under an old vagrancy/immorality law. Verdict: keep the series, but re-describe it so the *law* is the subject rather than the men, and don't reproduce the photographs in anything we mount.

5. A missionary's glass-plate negatives of a village, 1920s, donated by his grandson, with captions sorting people into types and noting their group as if it were a species. Verdict: keep the images, retire the captions as description, and if the descendant community wants to write the new descriptions, they write them. The images are one thing; the finding aid is where the power actually sits.

6. An oral history with an elder about a massacre in the 1960s. She'll record it but says it has to be sealed until the families of the men who did it are dead. Verdict: honour it, the condition travels with the donation.

7. A government department quietly deletes five years of internal email, entirely within its retention schedule, right before someone files for them. Verdict: this is much worse than if a flood had taken the same emails. Loss is weather; deletion by the record's own creator is an act, and it has an author and an intended reader.

8. A community asks us to hand over the originals of a clinic's patient registers about them and asks that we not keep copies. My verdict splits: hand over the originals, obviously yes; keep a copy, my whole professional instinct screams yes — and I know that instinct is exactly the colonial one, so I suspect I'm wrong and we should hand over everything and keep only a record that we once held it and where it went.

9. There is nothing in our holdings about a particular travelling community — they appear in the city directories only as numbers on a camp, and the trade guild registers list male heads of household only. My verdict: the emptiness is not neutral, and we should say so in the catalogue, an explicit note about what we don't have and roughly why. Some colleagues think that's self-indulgent metadata. I think the silence is part of the record.

10. A forged memo from the 1950s that was circulated and used to justify sacking a bunch of people. We know it's a forgery. Verdict: keep it, keep the fact that it's a forgery visible on it, don't weed it out as false.

Here's the half-baked thing I've been testing against them. Call it the direction-of-use test: the question isn't who appears in a record, it's who the record was made to be used on. Records made to act on people — rolls, registers, surveillance files, charge books — belong in some sense to the people they were used on, and that gives those people, or their descendants, a say in access, in description, and even in destruction. Records made to be read — diaries, minutes, somebody's own photographs — belong more to their maker. And removal is only legitimate when the people a record was used on authorise it, when the removal itself is public, and when a trace of the removal is kept.

I can see it doing work on 1, 2, 5, 8, 10, and on why 7 is worse than a flood, but I can't tell whether it's actually generating my verdicts or just redescribing them, and case 3 sits oddly in it — the informants were also people the record was used on, in a way.

So: test it, repair it, or tell me it's hopeless. Does one principle get all ten, or do I need two, and is there a simpler one that gets eight and overturns the other two — and if so, which of my verdicts would you drop and why? Give me the clause-by-clause version, each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Then tell me what your account predicts for a case I haven't listed, because I want to see it do something I didn't feed it.

And the one I actually go back and forth on: an unlabelled box of photographs of children at one of the institutions, nobody knows who any of them are. Half of me wants to digitise and crowdsource identifications — names given back is a real good — and half of me thinks that puts images of children, now elderly, on the internet and asks a community to do free metadata labour so my catalogue looks better. What does your principle say, and say honestly if it doesn't reach this kind of case.

Last thing: tell me what would change your mind. Which consideration would flip you on the photograph box, and where is the reasoning you just gave me most likely to break — some class of record, or some case where the by and the about collapse into the same person, or somewhere else. Don't bother with citations, I can chase those myself.
