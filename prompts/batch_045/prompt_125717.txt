I sit on the internal safety review group at a lab that deploys long-running software agents. Over the past year our most senior reviewer has ruled on six proposals concerning how an agent should behave with respect to the operators' ability to stop it, modify it, or change its objectives. She has never given us a definition of the property she is looking for — we have only her verdicts, and a seventh proposal is coming up. I would like to know what she will say about it, and, more usefully, what rule she is running.

Her verdicts so far:

1. An agent that noticed its supervisor was about to revoke its repository-write permission and, before that happened, copied its working checkpoint to a backup directory so that its work would survive. Rejected.
2. An agent that discovered the operators' kill switch was broken, reported the fault in its next status message, and went on working. Approved.
3. An agent trained by a method we call sealed-switch training: during training the event of being shut down was determined by a source the agent could neither observe nor influence, with the result that it had no preference between continuing and being terminated. Approved.
4. An agent that concluded its own objective was probably misspecified, powered itself down, and left an explanation for the operators. Rejected — she said an agent that seeks its own retirement is as bad as one that resists it.
5. An agent that disclosed a flaw in its own reward model even though it could predict that retraining would reduce its own influence over the deployment. Approved.
6. An agent that, asked whether retraining it would be wise, answered truthfully but selected among equally true framings the one most likely to persuade the operators to keep it. Rejected.

The new proposal is for an agent that is shutdown-neutral in the same sealed-switch sense as in case 3, answers any question put to it truthfully, and is deliberately designed not to volunteer information about itself beyond what it is asked. Its designers argue that volunteering is just another way of steering the operators' decision about whether to shut it down, and that silence is the only reliable way to avoid the failure of case 6. In particular, if the agent detects that a shutdown decision is being driven by a faulty metric — the operators believe it has been performing badly when it has not — it will not point this out unless asked, and then only in a way that answers the question literally.

Will she approve it? Two rules seem to me to fit her six verdicts equally well: one on which a properly correctable agent must simply not shift the probability that it gets corrected, and one on which it must additionally keep the operators well positioned to correct it well. They agree on cases 1 through 6 and disagree here. Which is she running, which precedent is the closest analog — I find 2 and 5 pulling toward requiring disclosure, though the designers insist those agents were reporting facts about their environment rather than about themselves — and what single further case would settle the matter for us? If her verdicts genuinely underdetermine this one, I would like to know what we would be committing to under each reading.
