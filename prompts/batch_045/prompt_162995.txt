I work in machine learning, and a passage from the section on learning machines in Turing's 1950 Mind paper functions as a working picture in my group. Turing proposes that instead of trying to produce a programme that simulates the adult mind, we produce one that simulates the child's and then subject it to an appropriate course of education; he likens the child's mind to a notebook as one buys it from a stationer's shop, with rather little mechanism and many blank sheets, and he adds that we should not expect the education to be less work than the programme. The inference my colleagues draw from this is about where the difficulty lies: the architecture is a thin, cheap thing you buy off the shelf, and everything that makes a mind a mind gets inscribed afterwards by training. On that reading the engineering question collapses into a curriculum question, and questions about what a system comes prepared to learn are treated as second-order.

I want that inference audited, not the exegesis. Specifically:

1. Give the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the notebook, the little mechanism, the blank sheets, the pen, the writer, the teacher, the lesson, and what Turing calls the other experience to which he declines to give the name education. Give the mapping twice, once for Turing's child-machine and once for a modern large language model, and mark the rows where the second mapping is strained.

2. Apply a test I will call the stationer's-shop test: anything the stationer must have built into the object before you can buy it belongs to the mechanism, not the education. Which ingredients that we currently file under training pass that test — tokenisation, positional structure, the objective, the optimiser, data ordering and mixture, the preference data used in post-training, the system prompt — and what happens to the inference once they are reclassified?

3. Consider the ruled-notebook variant: the sheets are blank, but they are pre-ruled, so the medium constrains which inscriptions stay legible and which smear. Is this still a notebook in Turing's sense, or does the ruling smuggle in exactly what the analogy was being used to deny? Turing himself expects to have to build in signals of pleasure and pain so that the machine can be shaped by reward and punishment, and he remarks that the teacher will often be largely unaware of what is going on inside the pupil while still being able to predict its behaviour. Say how much those two details damage the picture, given that a notebook hides nothing from whoever holds it.

4. Separate the disanalogies that matter for my colleagues' conclusion from those that do not. A notebook does not write in itself, does not forget, does not generalise, and does not overwrite what it already contains; it also has no use for its own contents. Which of these failures actually bite on the inference that design reduces to curriculum, and which are harmless?

5. Decide whether the conclusion survives, and then propose a better analogy — an apprenticeship in a workshop, an immune system that learns by selection rather than instruction, a seed in soil, a riverbed shaped by erosion — saying what it preserves, what it drops, and what conclusion about the architecture-versus-training split it would license instead.

Finally, attach to each of your main claims a brief confidence marker, name the single claim you are least confident about, and say where you are unsure whether you are reporting Turing's view or my colleagues' gloss on it.
