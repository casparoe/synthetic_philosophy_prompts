You are a professional philosopher who works on moral responsibility and who has spent enough time inside organizations that adjudicate blame to know how such bodies actually reason. I want you to think slowly and carefully here, and not to be agreeable: where my framing is confused, say so plainly rather than accommodating it. Do not give me references, citations, or reading suggestions; I want your own analysis, built for the setting below, and nothing that would require you to look anything up.

Here is the setting. I chair the Serious Incident Review Panel at a teaching hospital of roughly nine hundred beds. After any incident that reaches us, we must assign a disposition to each staff member involved, from the following set: no action; informal coaching; formal remediation; referral to the professional regulator; dismissal. We see around forty cases a year. We have ninety minutes per case, a panel of five that includes one lay member and no philosopher, a union representative in the room, a written record that the regulator and, if asked, a coroner will read, and a requirement that HR sign off on anything that sets a precedent. Almost every case we see is one in which something went badly wrong, which means almost every case we see is a case in which someone got unlucky, and we have no way of seeing the ones in which the same conduct went harmlessly unnoticed. That asymmetry is the thing I want help with.

I do not need the moral luck literature summarized. I know the dialectic: the pull of the thought that we can only be responsible for what is within our control, the fact that what we do, what we are like, what situations we face, and how things turn out are all substantially matters of luck, and the resulting pressure either to give up the control thought or to give up ordinary blame. What I need is for that to be made into something a panel can run.

Some things I already think, so you can take them as given or knock them down. First, I want backward-looking blameworthiness and forward-looking sanction held apart as far as possible, because I think most of our incoherence comes from running them together. Second, I want a Luck Ledger: for each case, an explicit entry for resultant luck, circumstantial luck, and constitutive luck, plus a fourth category I have started calling Reporting Luck, covering the fact of which conduct gets detected and referred to us at all. Third, I want a Counterfactual Twin Memo: before we fix a disposition, we must write down what we would have decided had the outcome of the same conduct been the median outcome of that conduct, and we must write it before we discuss the actual harm; if we find we cannot do that, I take it as evidence our judgment is contaminated. Fourth, I want a Detection Discount: where a disposition is justified by an outcome, I think it should be discounted by the estimated ratio of undetected to detected instances of the same conduct, and where that ratio cannot be estimated even roughly, I think we may not sanction on the outcome at all. Fifth, outcomes may be used as evidence of care we could not observe, but only where the panel can specify in advance, before it knows the outcome, what observable correlates it would have expected; otherwise it is hindsight.

What I want from you is a procedure my panel could actually run in ninety minutes: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops and returns no individual disposition at all, and the points at which it must hand the question to a human being because it is a question of values rather than facts. Where a distinction has to be dropped to make this usable, tell me which one and what is lost. Where you keep one, give me the one-sentence version a lay member can hold onto. Then work the procedure on three cases, revising it if they break it, and do not soften the results.

Case one. Two nurses on the same ward, both with unblemished records, both on the same shift pattern, both of whom administered insulin without the mandatory independent second check, which is a shortcut the ward is known to tolerate. In the first case the dose was correct, there was no harm, and the omission surfaced three weeks later in a routine audit. In the second case the dose was drawn from a look-alike vial, the patient, an elderly man with dementia, survived with hypoxic brain injury. Same conduct, same circumstances, same record, catastrophically different outcome. Tell me what the procedure gives for each, how far apart the two dispositions may defensibly be, and what to do with the fact that the family, the press, and the regulator will all read a small gap as indifference.

Case two. A vascular surgeon has for at least two years allowed the theatre team to complete the safety checklist retrospectively, after the incision rather than before it. This was known to the whole theatre team, was never reported, and was tolerated by the theatre manager; it covers something like four hundred operations. In one of them a patient on therapeutic anticoagulation was operated on without reversal having been given, bled, arrested, and died. The panel's own estimate is that a properly performed checklist would have caught this perhaps forty per cent of the time. We see the death and not the four hundred. I want to know whether any individual disposition is defensible here, whether it is the pattern or the death that could justify one, why this surgeon rather than the theatre manager, the anaesthetists, or the clinical lead who set the rota, and whether my Detection Discount is a genuine moral requirement or a lawyer's trick that would let any repeat risk-taker off with a shrug.

Case three, which is the one I expect to dislike the answer to. A doctor eight weeks into her first post, trained in another country, in a system where the local escalation protocol is not taught and where juniors are not expected to call seniors at night. She is on her sixth consecutive night shift covering two wards because of a rota gap nobody filled. A patient's observations deteriorate over four hours, she does not escalate, and the patient dies of septic shock. A locally trained and rested doctor would have escalated. Three further facts: the deterioration was itself partly caused by a pharmacy error that nobody could have caught, which is resultant luck that does not attach to her; she has since been diagnosed with a sleep disorder that probably impaired her judgment on night shifts, which is constitutive luck of a fairly pure kind; and the hospital's own investigation found three comparable failures to escalate on the same ward in the same month, none of which caused harm and none of which was reported. The family has asked only for an acknowledgment and a change to the rota. The regulator wants a referral because a patient died. HR wants a precedent it can defend. Give me the disposition your procedure yields, tell me whether the panel can honestly give the family the acknowledgment they are asking for while giving the regulator something else, and say plainly if you think the real problem is the option set itself rather than the choice within it.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, and press on two doubts of my own. The first is whether blame and forward-looking sanction can really be separated inside a single written record that the regulator, the family, and the union will each read, or whether we are simply being asked to assert two incompatible things in one document. The second is whether a panel that assigns dispositions only to the cases that reach it is doing anything better than administering a lottery, and if not, what the honest response is.

Work through all three cases before you fix the final form of the procedure.
