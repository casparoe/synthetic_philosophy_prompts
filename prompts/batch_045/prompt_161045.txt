I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend — a molecular biologist with a taste for philosophy — about whether the vocabulary of cranes and skyhooks, together with the companion charge of greedy reductionism, does any real work. Her objection, pressed several times and with some force, was that both distinctions are drawn retrospectively: whatever mechanism we eventually find for an apparently top-down phenomenon will automatically be labelled a crane, and if we never find one, the phenomenon will be said to have been a skyhook all along. On that reading, the ban on skyhooks is not a substantive claim about the world but a rule for how to describe whatever we find, and "greedy" is simply the name we give to reductions we antecedently disliked. I conceded more of this than I wanted to, and I would like to spend a fellowship term working out whether the concession was necessary.

The half-formed idea I came away with is this. What makes a crane a crane is not that it is a mechanism — a skyhook-poser can offer a mechanism of a sort — but that its lifting capacity is traceable: a crane should lift only what its own parts, organized as they are, could lift, and the crane's own provenance must be tellable without appealing to the very thing being explained. If that is right, then there are three separable ways a proposed reduction can be greedy, and the debate has been running them together:

1. Level-skipping: the mechanism is described at a level that jumps over the intervening machinery, so the middle rungs of the ladder are simply missing.
2. Capacity shortfall: the mechanism is real and genuinely well described, but what it can be shown to lift is lighter than the phenomenon to be explained — the crane is undersized, and the surplus weight is carried silently by something unnamed.
3. Borrowed lift: the crane is specified in the vocabulary that was supposed to be explained away, so the reducing story reimports what it was meant to reduce.

I think the three are genuinely distinct, that the standard charge of greediness collapses them, and that separating them lets us answer the retrospective-drawing objection — but I have not worked out how, or whether the separation survives contact with real cases.

Please turn this into a two-page proposal for a one-semester dissertation fellowship (four months, output of roughly 8,000 words). It should contain: a precise statement of the question, formulated so that it admits of a determinate answer rather than a verdict on a whole research programme; an account of why the existing positions leave it open, with the retrospective-drawing objection stated in its strongest form; the method — what I would actually do, month by month, including how I would choose the case studies (I want at least one case where the reduction is widely thought to have succeeded and at least one where it is contested, and I want the cases chosen so that the taxonomy could in principle be refuted rather than merely illustrated); why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives, which I take to be another round of "is reductionism true?", a survey of how the labels have actually been deployed in the literature, and a general metaphysics of what a level is — I am slightly afraid of the third, since I suspect that once I start asking what levels are, the semester is gone, and I would like the proposal to say what would count as the project having been swallowed by that question; what a successful result would look like as a deliverable someone else could use when deciding whether to pursue a given reduction; and an honest statement of what would count as failure — including, as I see it, the possibility that the three failure modes collapse into one, the possibility that the traceability condition turns out to be purely verbal because any reduction can be relabelled to satisfy it, and the possibility that it is either vacuous or lethal because every historically successful reduction either satisfies it trivially or violates it flagrantly.
