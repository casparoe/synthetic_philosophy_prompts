I keep running into one shape of situation, and my verdicts about it do not line up. The prize — a bargaining win, a market, the terms of a standard, survival — goes to whoever first makes it impossible for themselves to back down, so the parties end up racing to tie their own hands: moving to automatic retaliation, signing away the option to compromise, sinking money that is lost if you concede, publicly announcing a line you would rather not be held to. I have been told the shape has a name; the name has never helped me decide a case. Here are my verdicts.

(a) Two nuclear-armed states in a crisis, each shifting to a launch-on-warning posture so that retaliation becomes automatic and neither leader could stand down: I blame both, and equally, even though one moved only after the other had.

(b) Two AI labs racing to release a system, each saying the other will release it anyway: I blame both, but I blame more the one that began with the larger safety margin and spent it, and I find the follower's appeal to what the other would have done nearly exculpating.

(c) A union holding a strike-authorization vote and an employer announcing a lockout date, both before bargaining starts: I blame neither, this is bargaining and not a crisis, although structurally it looks like (a).

(d) A lab rushing to publish a method so that no one, itself included, can patent it: fine, perhaps praiseworthy.

(e) A state verifiably dismantling the capacity it would need in order to strike first: praiseworthy, even though it moves first and even if it makes a smaller war more likely.

(f) Two firms racing for an exclusive deal with the only supplier of a critical input, where the loser fails: I blame mildly, and I blame more the one that could have pivoted to another product line at modest cost.

What I have been working with is this: joining such a race is blameworthy to the extent that the commitment, if it succeeds, leaves everyone worse off than if no one had been able to commit at all; permissible when it merely redistributes a fixed prize; praiseworthy when what is surrendered is the racer's own option to harm. That handles (d) and (e), but it condemns (c), which I will not condemn, and it says nothing about why I excuse the follower in (b) or penalise the firm with the cheaper exit in (f).

Is there a principle that does better? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to drop. Apply it to the case I cannot settle — a consortium of firms racing to announce a voluntary safety cap before regulators impose one, where whoever announces first effectively sets the benchmark, and I cannot tell whether this is (e) or (b) — and to at least one case of your own construction. Say also whether your principle changes when the advantage goes to whoever commits last rather than first. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the principle you offer, and where its reasoning is most likely to break. No more than 500 words.
