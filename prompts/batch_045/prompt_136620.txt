Write a poem in the voice of Johannes Kepler, addressed to the astrologers whose art he practised for money and publicly despised — the men who read fates out of the twelve houses and the signs of the zodiac — in which he takes away nearly everything they do and keeps only the aspects.

Let the form carry the theory. Give one stanza to each aspect, and let the stanza's shape be the figure inscribed in the circle: three lines and three rhymes for the trine, four for the quartile, six for the sextile, two lines for the opposition, which is a single diameter with an end at each, and for the conjunction — where both planets stand on one ray — a stanza that collapses into a single line. Let a refrain return to each of these, word for word, since the angle itself always returns unchanged; but let what answers it change from stanza to stanza — weather, a fever, a failing harvest, a man making up his mind — so that the reader sees the effect lies not in the angle but in whatever receives it. Give the quintile five lines and the tone of a concession: a figure you can draw but a proportion you cannot name. Then begin a stanza on one seventh of the circle and do not let it close: the heptagon cannot be inscribed with compass and straightedge, and for Kepler a figure that cannot be constructed cannot be known, and what nature cannot know it cannot answer.

Close by refusing both endings a reader will reach for — that the stars compel, and that astrology is therefore nothing. The mechanism is perception, not force: he gives the earth a soul that registers an aspect the way an ear registers a consonance, and what that yields is an inclination, which a reasoning soul may still decline. Someone who knows Kepler's astrology should be able to tell which stanza is which aspect without being told and should not have to wince; someone who has never thought about astrology should still get a poem. Rhymed, loose iambic if that helps, under forty lines, no Latin tags, no footnotes.
