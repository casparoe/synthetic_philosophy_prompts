I write about science and ideas, and I'm finishing a piece on longtermism. One of my sources — someone who works on existential risk but has grown sceptical of the astronomical-value arithmetic that longtermists lean on — walked me through the following argument, and I haven't been able to find the seam in it. It seems to establish either far more than my source wants or nothing at all.

(1) The case for fanaticism about the far future is arithmetic: some very large, open-ended number of possible future lives, multiplied by a small probability of influencing how that future goes, dominates every near-term option on expected value.

(2) The simulation argument, on its least comfortable disjunct, gives us non-trivial credence that the world we are acting on is a run: something with a finite scheduled runtime, a terminator external to it, and a population fixed largely by choices made from outside.

(3) If that is what we are acting on, then the quantity in (1) is not open-ended. It is bounded by the expected remaining runtime of a run like ours, and its continuation is not something our acts do much to influence. Call this the horizon discount.

(4) So the astronomical figure in (1) should be replaced by something bounded and comparatively modest. The far future contracts toward the near future, and the fanatical verdict — that near-term goods are negligible by comparison — no longer follows.

(5) The obvious escape fails too. One might say that our descendants will run vast numbers of simulations, so the morally relevant future population is multiplied rather than divided. But those are simulations as well. If simulated minds and simulated futures are discounted in our own case, consistency discounts them in our descendants' case; and if simulations nest inside simulations, the discounting iterates and the value of the future collapses toward zero.

(6) So the simulation argument is a dampener in both directions: it truncates the future we are in, and it closes the route by which simulation might have enlarged it.

Here is why I think something has slipped. The dampener seems to have two available readings, and neither gives my source what he wants. On the first, being simulated is a fact about causal history with no implication for the moral standing of the minds inside or for how long the run lasts as a matter of empirical fact — in which case I cannot see why (3) follows at all, and, worse, the arithmetic gets more fanatical rather than less, since a base reality that runs simulations is a future containing more minds than (1) assumed. On the second, being simulated really does diminish the value or the expected length of what happens inside — in which case the argument proves far too much, because the near-term lives the dampener was supposed to rehabilitate are equally simulated, equally discounted, and I am left with no reason to prefer any course of action over any other, including the course of acting on the dampener itself. My source added, half in jest, that (3) also has a hidden lever: if a run's continuation is at all sensitive to what its inhabitants do — to whether they remain interesting enough to be worth running — then the horizon discount doesn't truncate anything, it just relocates the astronomical payoff onto an inestimable probability, which is fanaticism in its purest form.

Which step do you reject, and why does it look innocent before you reject it? I would like to keep (1) and I would like to keep something of the dampener, since my source is not a careless person. I would also like to know what I am paying if I keep them: what each candidate rejection commits me to about the moral status of simulated minds, about whether nesting matters, and about what could even in principle settle the empirical question in (3).
