I sit on the governance committee of a large teaching hospital that is about to procure a patient-safety platform. The system continuously scores every inpatient on a "deterioration index" computed from vitals, labs and clinical notes; it displays the ward's ranked list on screens at the nursing station; and it also generates, for each ward, per-nurse adherence metrics for the escalation protocol that the index triggers, which are reviewed monthly by line management. I am drafting the part of our memo that concerns what a system like this does to the people inside it, over and above whether it improves outcomes, and someone suggested that Foucault's account of disciplinary power and power/knowledge is the obvious framework to reach for. The only material I have to hand is a recent popular book on surveillance and self-tracking, which compresses the relevant ideas into a single paragraph. Before I build a memo on it, I would like to know how much of it to trust. It reads roughly as follows:

"For Foucault, power changed its character around the end of the eighteenth century. Older, sovereign power was something a ruler possessed and exercised from above, mainly by saying no — by punishing, forbidding and seizing. Modern power is not a thing anyone owns; it is a network of relations running through the whole social body, and above all it is productive rather than prohibitive. It does not repress; it produces kinds of people, kinds of desire and kinds of truth. Its emblem is Bentham's Panopticon, the prison in which a single unseen guard can observe every inmate at every moment. Foucault's point is that our institutions have become panoptic: we live in a surveillance society where someone is always watching, and so we police ourselves. Discipline is the technique this power uses. It lays down a norm, then inspects and corrects individuals until they have internalized it and comply on their own — which is why the categories of ideology and false consciousness are the right ones for understanding it. And because power and knowledge are for Foucault a single thing, what a society counts as truth is simply what its power arrangements have made sayable, so that science and medicine are in the end the ideology of whoever is in charge and no claim to neutrality can be taken at face value. The upshot is that Foucault leaves us no standpoint from which to criticize anything: if power is everywhere and reason is only its instrument, critique has no ground, and his position collapses into a paralyzing relativism — or, practically, into the thought that any use of a system like this is just power extending its reach, so the only honest response is refusal."

My instinct is that the truth-is-just-power line cannot be right as stated, since if it were I am not sure why I should believe the book's own account of how such systems work, or Foucault's own historical claims. But I cannot say precisely where the distortion lies, and I am less sure about the rest: the internalization story matches much of what I have read elsewhere about panoptic self-policing, yet it also sounds like the sort of thing this author would say regardless of what Foucault wrote.

Please go through the paragraph claim by claim and tell me what a careful reader of Foucault would accept, what is a familiar simplification that a specialist would wince at but concede as shorthand, and what he explicitly denied or would have rejected as a misreading. In particular: whether discipline is meant to operate on what people believe, or on something else; whether the panoptic schema is a claim about actual surveillance; how strong the identity between power and knowledge is supposed to be, and what the correct weaker claim is; and whether the charge of normative self-undermining is a refutation or a contested objection that defenders have answers to. Finally, and most usefully for me: the author draws a practical conclusion — that the only honest response to a system of this kind is refusal — and I would like to know whether that conclusion survives once the paragraph's errors are corrected, whether it is even consistent with the charge of having no normative standpoint, and what a corrected version of the framework would actually direct my committee to ask about this particular platform. I am especially unsure whether the ranked population-level index, the public ward display, and the individual adherence metrics are all instances of the same thing, or whether some of them belong to a different mechanism that the popular account does not distinguish. I would like the audit to end by telling me what, if anything, the framework licenses me to put in the memo.
