I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether there is any fact of the matter about *when* a subject becomes conscious of the motion in the color phi case. You know the setup from Kolers and von Grünau: a red spot is flashed at one location and then replaced, with no overlap, by a green spot at another; at suitable spacings and inter-stimulus intervals, subjects report a single spot that travels from the first location to the second and changes color abruptly partway along its path — a change they could not have seen at the time, since the second spot had not yet been shown.

My friend's position, which she defended with real patience, was this: once you grant that the two standard stories agree on everything measurable about the subject — every neural event, every report, every reaction time — there is nothing left for them to disagree about, and the question of when the subject experienced the motion is not a hard question awaiting better instruments but an ill-formed one. She is a vision scientist and she was happy to put it as a verificationist challenge. I think that is too fast, and I think the reason it is too fast has nothing to do with verificationism, but I could not say why in a form that satisfied either of us, and I have to try to say it out loud on Thursday.

On Thursday I have a seventy-minute slot in my department's vision science seminar — forty minutes of talk, thirty of questions. The audience is: two psychophysicists who work on apparent motion and temporal integration and who think natively in terms of postdiction, flash-lag, and chronostasis; a systems neuroscientist who does EEG chronometry and believes that claims about the timing of experience are answerable to timing data or to nothing; a philosopher of mind with disjunctivist sympathies who thinks the whole way of framing the problem — experiences as content-bearing items with temporal locations — is the actual error; a handful of graduate students, two of whom are drawn to illusionism; and my friend, who will be sitting in the front row.

My thesis, in the form I intend to defend it, is four steps. First, the usual two-way dispute is a false dilemma in a particular way: the Orwellian option (a red-spot experience, then a green-spot experience, with the intervening motion later edited into the memory) fails on its own terms rather than for want of evidence, because it requires a determinate earlier experience for the later report to be a revision *of*, and there is no candidate here — nobody thinks the subject ever had an experience as of a spot changing color in mid-flight. Second, once that is granted, the Stalinesque option loses most of its sting, because all it asserts is that some of the content was settled later than we naively assume. Third, there is a third option that the two labels do not encode, which I am calling specification without insertion: there is an experience at the time of the first flash, but its content is not yet determinate with respect to motion and direction; the second flash settles that content by filling an open slot rather than by overwriting a determinate record or fabricating one from nothing. Memory is never touched; nothing is revised; a slot is filled. Fourth, this reframes the question. The real question is not when the experience occurred but how much of a content must be settled at a time for there to be, at that time, an experience with that content — call the required amount the readiness threshold — and my claim is that color phi sits just below that threshold for motion content while the color change is a separate and more fragile matter, which is why subjects' reports shift with spacing and timing and why the case feels unstable in the hand.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room — the objections this audience would raise, not the ones a general philosophy audience would raise — ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by when they will arrive. Phrase each in the idiom of the person who will ask it, and say who that is: the psychophysicists should sound like psychophysicists, the chronometrician should ask for a signature, the disjunctivist should object to my framework rather than to my conclusion, my friend should press the verificationist challenge in her own words. For each objection give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, and then a note on exactly where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks anything, and tell me which single reply has to land for the position to survive the hour. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. My own guess is that it is either that an experience with no determinate content is not an experience of anything, so my third option is the Stalinesque option with extra steps, or that the readiness threshold is unfalsifiable machinery that lets me stipulate the answer case by case; if you think the dangerous question is a different one, say so and say why.

Please work from the argument as I have sketched it — the steps are mine, not canonical — and do not lean on citations, bibliographies, or the details of any particular published experiment beyond the phenomenon as I have described it. Where an objection turns on a technical point about timing or neural measurement, tell me what I would need to be able to say, rather than telling me whom to read.
