Next month I am giving a seminar paper in an economics department, and I would like a Q&A preparation sheet written against the specific room I will be walking into, not against generic philosophical objections to longtermism.

The paper defends strong longtermism, but in a reformulated version, and the reformulation is doing all the work. My thesis has two halves. First, negative: the standard presentation — that the expected number of future lives is so enormous that it swamps near-term considerations even when multiplied by a very small probability of mattering — is not defensible as stated, because it multiplies a vast quantity by a probability that is not merely small but structurally unmeasurable, and the literature's own favourite responses (expected-value maximization with a heavy-tailed prior, portfolio diversification across many independent-seeming long-run bets, appeal to "hits-based giving") all presuppose an independence across interventions that I argue we have no reason to believe. Second, positive: what survives is what I call the durability argument. Interventions differ in the depreciation rate of the goods they produce; some produce benefits that are consumed almost immediately (cash transfers, a season of insecticide-treated nets, a surgery), others produce benefits that persist and compound (a norm, a statute, an institution, a body of knowledge, a veto on a technology). Because the benefits of durable goods are back-loaded, and because the measurable near-term effect of an intervention is roughly proportional to how quickly its benefits are realized, near-term measured cost-effectiveness is not a noisy proxy for long-run value but an inversely related one — I call this the amortization inversion. The practical implication I draw is not "fund long-run work instead of near-term work because the multiplication says so," but "the accounting conventions of cost-effectiveness analysis systematically select against durable goods, and the reform that matters is to amortize benefits over the service life of the good produced and to treat the discount rate applied to those benefits as an object of estimation rather than as a statement of social preference."

The room will be roughly twenty people. About twelve are applied microeconomists, mostly development economists: they run field experiments, they think in terms of identification, external validity, treatment-effect heterogeneity, confidence intervals, and whether an effect replicates. Three or four work in public finance and climate economics and are at home in social discounting, the choice of a pure rate of time preference, growth-rate uncertainty, and integrated assessment; they are used to defending long-horizon numbers and will be impatient with anyone who handles a discount rate loosely. Two are philosophers of a broadly consequentialist stripe who are sympathetic to longtermism and who know the population-ethics and fanaticism literature. One economist in the room is already a committed longtermist of the standard expected-value variety; since my negative half attacks the machinery she relies on, I would like her to leave as an ally rather than as an opponent, and I am not sure how to manage that.

Please give me the objections I will actually get, ordered by expected damage, with each one phrased the way the person raising it would phrase it — the development economists in the vocabulary of identification and measurement, the discounting people in the vocabulary of rates and horizons, the philosophers in the vocabulary of fanaticism, cluelessness, and person-affecting versus impersonal value. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, an explicit note on whether I should concede the point or hold it, and a note on where my reply is softest.

Four things in particular I want handled well. The development economists will say that "durable good" is not an estimand and that my depreciation rates are unidentified; I need that objection stated in its strongest form, not as a plea for more data. The discounting people will suspect that amortizing over a good's service life is simply a low social discount rate smuggled in under a different name, and at least one of them will press the point that uncertainty about future growth and about the appropriate rate should make me more impatient rather than less. Someone will notice that the value of durable goods is not additive across time, because later actors would have supplied substitutes anyway — building the institution now partly displaces the institution that would have been built in thirty years — and I think this is the objection with the most force against my positive half, since it cuts against durability as such rather than against my measurement of it. And the sympathetic philosophers will ask whether durability arguments actually reduce the fanaticism worry or merely relocate it, given that the value of a durable good is itself heavy-tailed and can be negative if the durable good locks in something bad.

Finally, three things beyond the list itself: what I should concede within the first two slides so that the question period is not consumed by it; the one reply that has to land if the paper is to survive the seminar; and the question I am hoping nobody asks. My own sense is that my weakest point is that I offer no procedure for estimating a depreciation rate, and that "durable" may turn out to be empirically indistinguishable from "difficult for us to measure" — please tell me how bad that is and what it leaves me with. No citations or reading lists are needed; I want arguments and phrasing.
