A couple of years ago I took a philosophy of biology seminar whose TA now runs a tiny blog where, every couple of weeks, she posts some weird trait and gives it a one-line verdict, "function" or "no function," sometimes with a parenthetical saying what the function is. I want to send her a case and I have a small bet going with my roommate about what she'll say, so I'm trying to reverse-engineer the rule she's actually running. Her verdicts so far that seem relevant: the heart's making of the lub-dub sound — no function, byproduct, even though you can tell a story in which the sound contributes to something; a point mutation that appeared in one beetle this generation and happens to make it survive drying out better — no function yet, "ask me again in a thousand generations"; a digestive enzyme that got recruited into building a lens millions of years ago — yes, function now is optical, and digestion is no longer its function even though the protein still does that chemistry; the border collie's stalk-and-stare — yes, function, and she said explicitly that it doesn't matter that shepherds did the selecting rather than "nature"; a gene-drive element that biases its own transmission while lowering the fitness of the organism carrying it — yes, function is biasing its own inheritance, and the cost to the host is irrelevant; goosebumps in humans — no function, vestigial, even though the muscles still contract, because the thing they were selected for isn't there anymore. I know there's this general selected-effects versus causal-role distinction people talk about, but I can't tell which version of it she's on, or whether her verdicts are even consistent with only one.

The case I want to send: a lab takes an ordinary soil bacterium and splices in a receptor protein whose amino acid sequence was produced by a protein-design model. Nothing like it ever existed in any lineage, no organism ever made anything similar, and there was never natural or breeder's selection for it. It binds cadmium, and when it binds it switches on a green fluorescent protein, so a field technician can see where soil is contaminated. The bacterium pays a real metabolic cost for making both proteins and gets no benefit from glowing. The fluorescent protein itself does have a long natural history in jellyfish, but nothing in that history has anything to do with cadmium or with bacteria.

So: what's she going to say, and more importantly what rule is she running?
