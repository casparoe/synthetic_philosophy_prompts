I have been trying to state, precisely enough to apply to new cases, the principle that governs my judgments about when it may be permissible to bring about someone's death as the cost of averting a greater harm to others. The doctrine of double effect is the obvious candidate, but my verdicts do not line up neatly with it as usually stated. Below are the cases, with my gut verdicts, and then the principle I have been carrying around. I would like you to test that principle against the cases, repair it, or replace it with a better one, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts no principle of this shape can save.

1. The switch. A runaway trolley will kill five people on the main line. I can divert it onto a spur where one worker is standing; he will certainly die. Verdict: permissible. I do not think it matters whether I am the driver or a bystander at the lever.

2. The two spurs. As in case 1, except that there are two spurs, each with one worker. Diverting onto spur A kills that worker. Diverting onto spur B kills that worker and also kills a second worker on an adjacent platform, struck by debris; her death contributes nothing to the rescue. Verdict: I must take spur A. Taking B is impermissible, even though in both cases the deaths are foreseen side effects rather than means, and the numbers saved are identical.

3. The footbridge. The only way to stop the trolley is to push a large stranger off a footbridge into its path; his body halts it and he dies. Verdict: impermissible, and clearly so.

4. The looping spur. As in case 1, but the spur loops back to the main line: the trolley would reach the five anyway unless the lone man's body stops it, which it will. I only flip the switch. Verdict: impermissible, but distinctly less bad than case 3, and I cannot say what accounts for the difference in degree, since in both cases it is his body that does the stopping.

5. The volunteer. As in case 3, except the stranger, fully informed and unmoved by panic, tells me to push him and means it. Verdict: permissible.

6. The jump. As in case 3, except the only body heavy enough is my own. Verdict: permissible, and not even in need of an excuse — yet the structure is identical to case 3: I intend a death as my means.

7. The padded suit. As in case 3, except the stranger is wearing industrial padding: the collision stops the trolley and he survives, with broken ribs and a concussion. Verdict: impermissible. I note that this is awkward for the doctrine, since what stops the trolley is his mass, not his injury, and the only harm I cause is moderate and foreseen.

8. The saboteur. A man has set the trolley running and stands ready to redirect it at the five; the only way to stop him is to shoot him, and I know that only killing him will stop him. Verdict: permissible, though his death is my means.

9. The falling man. A large man has fallen from a building above me and will crush me; the only way to save myself is to disintegrate him with a ray gun. Verdict: impermissible. But I am much less confident here than in case 8, and my confidence collapses entirely if what is at stake is five lives rather than mine.

10. The doomed worker. As in case 1, except that the worker on the spur will be killed seconds later by a second runaway trolley whatever I do. Verdict: permissible, and not merely permissible — I cannot see that I impose any cost at all, which suggests proportionality is measured counterfactually rather than by the harm I actually bring about.

11. Numbers and chances. Five versus one: divert. A thousand versus one: obviously divert. Two versus one: I balk, and would not divert. But I also balk at diverting when not diverting means a 0.3 chance that five die and diverting means certain death for one — even though the expected loss is larger. I do not know whether these two hesitations are consistent with each other.

12. The spiteful foreman. A burning building must be collapsed to stop the fire reaching a hospital wing. A vagrant is asleep inside and will certainly die; waiting ten minutes would let him out but cost an additional ward. The collapse is necessary and proportionate. Variant (a): the foreman regrets the death. Variant (b): he is indifferent to it. Variant (c): he is glad, because the man once assaulted him, and that gladness is part of why he acts now rather than waiting. Verdict: (a) permissible; (b) permissible but I think worse of him; (c) impermissible, or failing that permissible-but-corrupt — and I cannot see where in the principle a motive that does no causal work is supposed to register.

The principle I have been working with: an act that I foresee will harm someone is permissible if and only if (a) the harm is not among the things I aim at as a means to my end, (b) the harm is not out of proportion to the good secured, where the comparison is with what would have happened had I done nothing, and (c) there is no available alternative securing the same good at lesser cost; with clause (a) suspended when the person harmed has made himself a culpable threat, or consents, or is the agent himself.

Three things puzzle me about this. First, the suspensions listed under (a) look like a single condition in disguise — something about the person's standing to object, which consent waives, culpable threats forfeit, and self-regarding action never engages — and if so, I would like that condition stated on its own, and I would like to know whether it does all the work that (a) was doing, or whether some residue of (a) is still needed for cases 3 and 4. Second, clause (c) is doing the work in case 2 and clause (b) the work in case 10, but I cannot tell whether either can be stated precisely enough to apply without smuggling in the means/foresight distinction. Third, case 7 seems to me to show that a harm can be permissible or not quite independently of whether it was aimed at, and case 12 that a motive can matter without being a means.

Please give me the best candidate principles, stated precisely, each clause paired with the case that motivates it. Then tell me what each predicts for three cases I have not listed: the saboteur of case 8 when I could instead disable him with a risky leg shot; the falling man of case 9 when a busload of people is at stake rather than just me; and my shoving a friend out of the trolley's path, breaking his arm, where the breakage is my means of moving him and he would consent if there were time to ask. Finally, tell me honestly which of the twelve verdicts above no principle of this general shape can accommodate, and which one you would overturn if you had to pick only one.
