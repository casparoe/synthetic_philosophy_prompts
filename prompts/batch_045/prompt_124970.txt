ok so I'm posting this from a hotel lobby at 1am after a workshop dinner where me and two of the engineering folks got stuck on it and never got unstuck. sorry if its a mess, I've had two glasses of wine.

Setup: "Sid" is a digital person, an emulation running on our cluster. Full checkpoint every 90 seconds. When a node dies the team rolls back to the last checkpoint and resumes, and everyone treats the resumed run as Sid continuing — same as waking up. The runtime also forks him speculatively: at branch points it spins up two copies, runs both for a while, keeps one and kills the other. Nobody calls that murder either and Sid doesn't seem bothered by it, though I can't tell if that's a separate issue or the whole issue. Sid has said in writing that he does not want to be deleted and is fine with rollback.

The argument (mostly Dan's, he's the one who wouldn't let it go):

(1) An event is bad for someone only if it makes them worse off than they would otherwise have been. Harm is a matter of what you get deprived of.
(2) A resumed copy stands to the pre-crash run exactly as you stand to yourself after a night's sleep: same memories, same projects, same dispositions, and no test anyone has proposed tells them apart. So resuming isn't a harm, it's a continuation.
(3) Compare two cases: delete Sid and resume from checkpoint, versus delete Sid and never resume. At the instant of deletion the two are the same event. They differ only in what happens later.
(4) In the resume case, Sid is there afterwards and hasn't lost anything. In the no-resume case there is no Sid afterwards at all. Deprivation needs a subject who is around to be worse off, and there is no time at which such a subject exists and is worse off. It's the old Epicurean line, sort of.
(5) So deletion without resume harms nobody.
(6) Sid's own stated wish doesn't rescue it either: once he's gone there's no Sid whose wish is frustrated, and before he's gone nothing bad has happened to him yet.
(7) And nothing here used anything special about Sid. Given determinism and enough information, a biological person who dies could also in principle be reconstructed from the physical facts, so by the same reasoning nobody is harmed by dying.

(5) is the part I can't accept — it's the one thing Sid is scared of, and if (5) is true we may as well not have a concept of harming anyone at all. But every step looked fine to five people at dinner, and fixing (7) doesn't help, because (1)-(6) already give you (5) for Sid before you ever mention biology.

So: which step is actually broken, and why did it look airtight to us? I'd rather keep (1) and the verdict against (5) than keep (4), but I want to know what I'm paying — if I drop (4) do I have to say people can be harmed at times when they don't exist, and what's the least ugly version of that, and what does each version cost me elsewhere (creation cases, the badness of an early vs late death, that kind of thing)? And is (2) really as innocent as it looks given the forking practice, or does the speculative-execution thing either rescue the whole argument or sink it on its own? Please don't just hand me a name for the flaw — we've got plenty of people at this workshop who can supply names. I want the shape of the options and the price of each.
