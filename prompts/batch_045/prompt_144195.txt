I'm a TA for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on decision theory and the philosophy of probability — 18 students, one 75-minute session, standard classroom supplies only (index cards, coins, dice, a projector, maybe one spreadsheet on the instructor's laptop), and no real money unless it's trivial. They have already worked through the St. Petersburg game, the basic dominance and expected-utility apparatus, and the standard complaint that expected-utility theory tells you to pay any finite price for a St. Petersburg ticket. What I want next is for them to hit something they haven't already absorbed: a gamble where the trouble isn't that the expected value is absurdly large, but that there apparently is no fact of the matter about what it is, because the naive summing procedure that everybody has been using silently depends on the order in which you list the outcomes, and the series in question is only conditionally convergent.

Please design a runnable 75-minute activity that makes them feel that. My own half-formed staging idea, which you should develop or improve rather than discard: don't write the gamble on the board as an infinite series at all. Give each small group the same gamble in the form of a shuffled deck of cards, each card carrying one possible outcome and its probability, and have them arrive at a price — a maximum willingness to pay, in points that count toward a small prize or a homework token — by aggregating the deck in whatever order it happens to land, with different groups getting the very same cards in different orders. Layered on top of that, I'd like the same distribution to be realizable by two physically different chance setups (a coin-flipped-until-heads procedure on one table, a spinner or urn with its sectors or tickets rearranged on another), so that students who agree on every probability and every payoff still produce different prices and can't be shown to be wrong, and so that the question of whether the two setups are even the same gamble becomes live. If you think a two-dimensional variant works better — outcomes indexed by pairs, where summing row-first and column-first come apart — say so and build that instead, but I want the mechanism to be one where the students do arithmetic and discover a discrepancy rather than one where I announce a theorem. Assume no lecture up front and no use of the words Pasadena, conditional convergence, or rearrangement until the debrief; also assume that two of the eighteen have read about this family of paradoxes over the summer and will recognize it early, and tell me how to handle them without letting them deflate the exercise.

The debrief is where I need the most help. Please map specific moments in the activity onto the specific formal points: that expected value, construed as a sum over outcomes, is not a property of the gamble alone but of the gamble plus an aggregation procedure; that undefined is a different status from infinite and from merely unknown; and that the familiar escape hatches — impose a cap, take the limit of partial sums in the natural order, average over all orderings, switch to the median or to some decision rule that never mentions expectations — each cost something the students should be made to articulate rather than accept. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: groups treating the whole thing as a puzzle about risk aversion, arithmetic sloppiness in the tail terms, a group that refuses to bid at all, the spoiler problem above, the fact that any payout I can actually honor is bounded and so the exercise is, strictly speaking, a different gamble from the one being discussed. And be frank about which feature of the phenomenon the activity cannot stage at all — my sense is that genuine infinitude and unbounded stakes are exactly what a classroom can't supply — and what the facilitator therefore has to hand the students directly at the end, and how to do that without it sounding like a retraction of the previous hour.
