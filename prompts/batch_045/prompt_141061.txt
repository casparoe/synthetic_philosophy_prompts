I run the night shift at a regional distribution warehouse, and a good part of my job is writing procedures that don't break and then watching them break anyway, so I have an amateur's interest in the way a routine can keep running after the reason for it has disappeared. I recently came across the digger-wasp story that philosophers use for exactly this, and I want to use it on a historical case. As I've seen it told: the wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it on the threshold while she goes inside to inspect, comes back out and pulls it in; if you move the cricket a few inches while she is inside, she emerges, drags it back to the threshold, and goes in to inspect again, apparently without limit. Please treat that as a model rather than a field report — I gather the original observations may have been tidied up in the retelling, and I don't want the analysis to depend on whether any real wasp would actually do it forty times. The word *sphexish* gets used for what the story illustrates, and it seems to me that popular writing runs together three quite different failures under that one label: the routine fires when it shouldn't, or can't be stopped from firing; once it has fired there is only one route to the end; and the condition for counting the job as finished is fixed, so that defeating the outcome just re-runs the routine from the top. I have started calling these trigger rigidity, path rigidity, and stop-rule rigidity, and my guess is that only the third one is doing any real philosophical work. Correct the distinction if it's muddled, and give me a better one if so.

The case I want to think with is the outbreak of the First World War in the summer of 1914. I've read about it on my own for years and I don't need a chronology: the assassination at Sarajevo, the Austrian ultimatum to Serbia, the Russian mobilization (first partial, then general), the German declarations and the march through Belgium, and the British entry that followed. The detail I keep coming back to is the one about the Kaiser, at the very end, asking whether the whole attack in the west could be redirected east instead, and being told in effect that a deployment of more than a million men already moving on the railways could not be re-aimed without turning the army into an armed mob. That is my sphexish moment. So here is what I want. First, a disciplined mapping rather than a mood: who or what is the wasp, if anything is — a general staff, a government, a plan, a set of railway timetables? What are the burrow, the cricket, and the threshold? What was the trigger, and what was the stop-rule? Which of my three kinds of rigidity did each actor actually display, and does the mapping hold up better for the military plans than for the diplomatic ones, or the other way around? Second, an honest account of where the lens stops working. My own worries are these, and I'd like you to take them seriously rather than bulldoze past them: that a great deal of the rigidity in 1914 was deliberate self-binding, chosen precisely because a threat you cannot help but carry out is a credible one, so the wasp picture risks describing a feature as if it were a bug; that the episodes of *flexibility* may have made things worse, since a partial and therefore hard-to-read mobilization seems to have been interpreted by the other side as the general one it was meant to avoid; that everyone involved was deliberating furiously, so the lens can at best explain the famous sleepwalking feel of the thing and not any absence of deliberation; and that the lens seems to have nothing to say about how to distribute responsibility across many actors inside one procedure, or about whether the war was going to happen regardless.

Then run it back the other way, because that's what I actually care about. If the useful notion of sphexishness turns out to be a property of procedures, plans, and institutions rather than of persons, does the argument about free will that the wasp is supposed to support survive being moved up a level, or does it quietly change the subject? My hypothesis is that the property worth caring about is not whether an agent could have done otherwise at the instant of acting but whether the rule it is running under could have been revised while it was running, which would mean that most of the free-will debate has been aimed at the wrong joint. Tell me if that's a genuine insight or just a relocation of the problem. And if the sphexish lens is the wrong tool here, say what does better — something like a credibility-and-commitment story, or a tightly-coupled-systems story in which failure emerges from opacity and feedback rather than rigidity — and say what it explains that the wasp does not. I have no philosophy training, so please define any term of art you reach for, don't give me a timeline of July 1914, and don't give me a reading list or citations, since I can't chase them and they wouldn't help me. Under a thousand words if you can.
