Nobody there reads the corrigibility literature, so I can't assume any of the vocabulary, and I know from experience this crowd will just interrupt me rather than wait for the Q&A.

The thesis I'm defending: I split corrigibility into three grades — *switch-corrigibility* (don't prevent the operator from turning you off), *edit-corrigibility* (don't prevent the operator from retargeting you, including toward objectives you currently disprefer), and *successor-corrigibility* (actively help build and hand off to your replacement) — and I argue the literature has wildly over-invested in the first one and mostly ignored the third. My negative claim is that the standard utility-indifference construction buys less than people think: it secures indifference between worlds that differ in whether the switch got pressed while holding everything downstream fixed, which gets you won't-resist but not won't-arrange — such an agent is perfectly happy to press its own off-button whenever that's the cheapest way to get anything else it values, and to manipulate whatever the switch state happens to be correlated with. My positive proposal is to stop treating corrigibility as a property of a policy or of a utility function and treat it as a property of the learning dynamics instead: an update rule is corrigible to degree d if exposure to a distribution of interventions (shutdown, retargeting, replacement) produces no more than d gradient pressure toward acquiring control over, or information about, the intervention channel. I call it *intervention-invariance of the update rule*, and I claim it has two advantages: it's definable without a utility representation, so it applies to a system that was trained rather than designed, and it has an empirical signature you can actually measure with held-out interventions.
