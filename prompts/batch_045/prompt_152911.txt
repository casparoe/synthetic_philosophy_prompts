I write novels, and the one I'm working on now has dragged me into a corner of political philosophy I have no training in. I've ended up half-convinced by luck egalitarianism, and I want to know whether the version I've ended up with is an actual position or just a pile of things I happen to like. Roughly where I've landed:

(1) Inequalities are unjust when they trace back to brute luck, and not unjust when they trace back to something the person chose. That's the core, and I do think its the right core.

(2) I dont think someone genuinely chooses in the relevant sense when the menu they chose from was itself shaped by their upbringing, an addiction, or having no decent alternative. So a lot of what looks like choice is brute luck wearing a disguise.

(3) But I still want the view to let some inequalities through. If every real-world inequality dissolves into brute luck once you look hard enough, then I've got a plain egalitarianism with extra steps, and I don't want that.

(4) I don't think we owe people compensation for expensive tastes, even unchosen ones. My protagonist can only be happy writing for full orchestras, and that's just who she is — her tastes aren't a misfortune she suffers from.

(5) I hate the idea of the state rummaging through people's lives to decide whether their poverty is their own fault. Any view that can only be implemented by officials doing that has a serious mark against it.

(6) Temperament — patience, nerve, charm — is mostly brute luck, and it decides an enormous amount about how a life goes. But treating people as nothing but the product of luck they didn't choose is bad politics and bad fiction, and I don't want a view that pushes me there.

(7) I think we owe each other help regardless of how someone ended up badly off, as a matter of plain humanity, and I think that sits fine alongside everything above.

Go through these and tell me which ones actually fight with each other and how badly — and, as important, which ones only look like they fight. Rank the real tensions by severity instead of flagging all of them at once; for each, say what would have to give and what giving it costs me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Mark where you're confident and where you're guessing about what the view really commits me to. And if the honest answer is that this set is just a mess, or that one of these isn't a commitment in the relevant sense at all, say so plainly rather than being polite about it. Under 500 words, ranked list or tight prose, whichever works better.
