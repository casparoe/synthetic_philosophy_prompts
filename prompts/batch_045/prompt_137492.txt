I'm in a senior seminar on ethics and technology, and I need to turn a half-formed idea into a short proposal for a summer research grant: eight weeks, one undergraduate researcher, no data collection, with a conference paper as the deliverable.

The thing I keep getting stuck on is this. Most of the debate about automation and the meaning of work seems to assume that once material necessity is handled, the only remaining question is whether people can find meaning outside of work. But I suspect meaning from work may be structurally scarce in a way that meaning from hobbies is not: work seems to matter to us partly because it is needed, because it is hard, and because not just anyone could have done it. If that is right, then removing the necessity and the difficulty does not liberate us to find meaning elsewhere; it destroys the very property that made the work meaningful, so that the more successful automation is, the less meaning there is to go around. Call it the shrinking-craft problem.

Two things I want the proposal to do. First, state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer: as I see it, the question is whether the meaningfulness of work depends on counterfactuals about who or what could have done it — a machine, a differently trained person, nobody at all — or only on features intrinsic to the activity and the agent's relation to it. Second, test that question with cases rather than with another tour of the literature. My planned cases: a radiologist whose judgments are now made better by a system but who still reviews every scan; a composer working with a generative model who cannot tell which phrases are hers; and the one I keep turning over, a craftsperson in a world where an interface has since given everyone else her exact skill, so that her work is still difficult, still genuinely hers, and no longer scarce. If the shrinking-craft problem is real, the last case should feel like a loss; if meaning is intrinsic, it should not.

What I want from you is a proposal with: the question stated exactly; an argument for why both the optimistic post-work position and the pessimistic work-as-a-need position leave it open — my sense is that they disagree about whether work is necessary rather than about whether it is scarce, but tell me if that is wrong; a method, meaning what I would actually do across eight weeks, and why cases are a better bet than either stipulating a definition of meaningfulness or appealing to survey data on job satisfaction; the range of conclusions available and which considerations would decide between them; and an honest statement of what would count as failure, including whether the case method can settle a claim that is partly psychological. No citations or bibliography, I will add those myself. And if you think the whole thing really turns on a distinction I have not drawn — meaningful versus fulfilling, or meaning in work versus meaning from work — say so and build the proposal around that instead.
