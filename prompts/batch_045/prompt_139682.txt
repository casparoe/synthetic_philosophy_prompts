I'm a novelist, halfway through a third book, and I've got a scene problem that I think is actually a philosophy problem, so I thought I'd have a go at you with it.

The book has a structural gimmick: chapter 7 and chapter 19 tell the same Wednesday afternoon twice, once in close third from inside my protagonist's head, once from outside in a cooler, slightly hostile narration. Same woman, same afternoon, same action, same two consequences. What I want is the Knobe thing — the chairman-and-the-environment one, where everybody says he harmed the environment intentionally but didn't help it intentionally — but I want it inside a single action rather than across a pair of vignettes, and I want it in a world my readers actually recognise.

So: design me one action with two side effects, both produced by the same mechanism, foreseen to exactly the same degree, equally avoidable, equally causally direct, and roughly equal in magnitude — one person gains about what the other loses. One consequence is clearly good, one clearly bad, both mild-to-moderate: nobody dies, nothing illegal, nothing to do with the environment, no boardrooms, no chief executives, no trolley nonsense. Setting should be ordinary British life — a school governors' meeting, a pub skittle team, a village fête, a GP surgery rota, an allotment committee. And write the thing as prose I could actually lift into the book, not as a survey instrument with (a) and (b) options. One paragraph per version, and I need two versions: one where the bad consequence is the one we ask about first, one where the good one is.

Hard constraints, because I know how easy it is to cheat this:

- She never says what she wanted, and neither narrator gets to say it either. No "she couldn't have cared less", no "she was trying to", no "she didn't mind". The verdict has to fall out of what the reader reads in, not out of a line that stipulates it.
- The two consequences have to stay symmetric. If one of them is really a means rather than a side effect, or is more certain, or takes more effort, the whole thing is rigged and I don't want it.
- I want the standard asymmetry first: reader closes chapter 7 convinced she brought about the bad one intentionally and only stumbled into the good one. Then tell me whether you can flip it — good one intentional, bad one not — while keeping the symmetry, and what has to give to make that happen. My hunch is the only way is to fiddle with how much effort or skill the good consequence takes, or with whether the bad one reads as a norm violation at all; if that's right, say so and build me the least rigged-looking version you can. And if my specs genuinely can't all hold at once, tell me which one has to go and why.

Then the bit I actually need from you, because I can't check it myself:

- For each scene, the verdict you predict and which mechanism is doing the work — moral valence driving the ordinary concept of intentional action, a blame-first or norm-violation story, the skill/effort/control reading of "intentional", means-versus-side-effect, whatever combination.
- Which single feature carries the case, and what happens if you delete it: does it collapse into a plain foreseen-side-effect case where the reader just says yes to both?
- How someone who thinks the effect is really about effort and control, or really about blame rather than about intentionality at all, would attack your scene — and the smallest tweak that dodges the objection without sneaking the verdict back in by hand.
- One practical novelist question at the end: which single line of dialogue or bit of free indirect thought can I plant to push the reader, and does any of this survive being read back to back? My readers will read both chapters, days apart but in the same book, and I've a horrible feeling that seeing the trick twice kills it stone dead.

What it's for, so you know what counts as cheating: I want the reader to feel, without being told, that she's the sort of person who means the bad thing and only blunders into the good one. A scene that just announces her motives fails, and so does one that leans on a technical definition of "intentionally" my readers have never heard of. And please, no citations or reading lists — I've read enough of the literature, and I'm after a scene, not a bibliography.
