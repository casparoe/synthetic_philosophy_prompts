Two colleagues of mine — one works on brain emulation, the other on formal epistemology — argued for most of an evening about a case I could not referee, and I gave up trying. I'd like you to settle it instead.

Suppose a data centre is running a high-fidelity emulation of a human brain. Suppose further, as both of them granted, that the very same physical process also implements a second system: by decoding the emulator's memory into units and connections in a different way, applied uniformly to every state, you get a network of a different architecture and a different functional profile, of a sort that both of them would count as a moral patient with experiences of its own if it were running on its own hardware. Because the recoding is uniform, this second network's counterfactual behaviour is tracked by the same physics. The practical question was how much moral weight the data centre carries: one patient, or more than one? Their exchange, compressed but I hope not distorted:

(1) The emulation researcher: moral weight tracks physical causal structure, and there is only one such structure here. That we can project many computations onto it is a fact about our language, not about what it is like to be that system. If every projection added weight, a laptop would carry astronomical moral weight, since any physical system can be described as implementing arbitrarily many computations.

(2) The epistemologist: that disposes of descriptions, not of implementations. What matters is a mapping under which the system's counterfactual behaviour tracks the target computation's transitions, so that intervening on the system changes what the computation does. Several such mappings can hold of the same physics at once, and nothing in the physics singles one out as the real one. If the weight is real under one mapping, it is real under each, so we should sum.

(3) The emulation researcher: even granting that, the number of such mappings is enormous, so on your view the data centre's total weight is fixed almost entirely by how many recodings we happen to construct — an artefact of our industriousness rather than of the system. A candidate implementation's claim on us should be discounted by how simply its mapping can be specified, along the lines of the simplicity priors used in algorithmic information theory, weighting each candidate by two to the minus length of its shortest description. The designed encoding then dominates and the recodings contribute almost nothing.

(4) The epistemologist: simplicity is a property of the language the mapping is written in, not of the world. Choose a description language in which the recoding is the short one and your weights invert. A simplicity-weighted sum has not removed the arbitrariness; it has relocated it into the choice of prior.

(5) The emulation researcher: then perhaps the honest conclusion is that there is no fact of the matter how much weight the data centre has, only a range, and that decisions must be made under uncertainty over that range: take an expectation over reasonable description languages, or, if one is averse to moral risk, attend to whichever end of the range one would least want to be wrong about.

(6) The epistemologist: I reject the slide from there being no fact of the matter to our being uncertain about it. If the arbitrariness lies in our descriptions, then the resulting uncertainty is not uncertainty about the world, and expected-value reasoning over descriptions is bookkeeping rather than morality. What is owed to that system cannot depend on how we choose to write it down.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say how strong it is, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say precisely what it establishes and what it does not. Then say who is more right overall, and what — if anything — both of them are missing. If you conclude that one patient or many has no determinate answer, say what should nevertheless be done by someone who has to allocate resources as if it did.
