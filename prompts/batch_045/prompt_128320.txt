I've been reading the scientific realism debate for about two years now, and I've stopped being able to tell whether it is advancing or merely elaborating. The shape of it never changes. The realist points at novel predictive success and at the technological grip our theories give us and asks what would explain that grip if the theories weren't latching onto how the world is. The anti-realist answers with a roster of past theories that enjoyed exactly that kind of success and whose central posits we now take to be uninstantiated — the crystalline spheres, humoral medicine, electrical effluvia, phlogiston, caloric, catastrophist geology — and asks what entitles us to think our own episode is different. The realist then goes selective: distinguish the parts of a theory that did the predictive work from the idle machinery, and the induction only shows that idle machinery gets thrown away. Or structural: what survives theory change is the mathematical structure, not the furniture. Or local: manipulable entities and the mechanisms we can intervene on are on a different footing from grand cosmological posits. Every one of these refinements has drawn its own counter — that the selective move is unfalsifiable because "did the predictive work" is settled retrospectively; that the structural move approaches vacuity once you notice how much can be preserved by structure alone; that the local move inherits a smaller pessimistic induction about instruments and the mechanisms they presuppose. And then the cycle restarts with a longer roster and a finer-grained criterion. As far as I can see, nobody's credences have moved.

I'd like a diagnosis of the stall, and I want it committed: tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, ranked, with reasons, and tell me where you think you might be wrong.

My own hypothesis is that the two arguments are not the same kind of argument, and the debate proceeds as though they were. The no-miracles argument reads to me like an abductive demand — the anti-realist owes an explanation of success — while the pessimistic induction reads like an enumerative induction whose reference class is never stated, so that its base rate can be whatever the arguer needs. If that's right, the sides differ in standards of evidence as much as in conclusion. But I can see three other candidates doing work as well, and I can't rank them.

First: a question about the dialectical burden. The pessimistic induction is often presented as a defeater for the no-miracles argument specifically — show that this one argument fails — while realists routinely respond as though it were being offered as a positive case for anti-realism. Is the debate partly two different questions under one label?

Second: the hinge term. Each side seems able to count any given historical case as data for itself, because "approximately true" and "the part that did the work" are fixed only after we know whether the posit survived. Is this substantially a verbal dispute in disguise, or is there a real disagreement underneath that the vocabulary is merely obscuring?

Third: the unit of appraisal. Are we appraising whole theories, research programmes, individual posits, or the derivations of particular predictions? It isn't obvious the disputants have agreed on this, and it isn't obvious the answer is the same for the no-miracles argument and the induction against it.

To make this concrete, here is a device I've been toying with and can't evaluate, which I'll call retention-matched pairing. For each theory on the anti-realist's roster, identify the posits that actually appeared in the derivation of its novel predictions, and then ask how many theory-generations those posits survived before being discarded. Do the same for a currently live case — the atomic hypothesis across its nineteenth-century career, say, or the germ theory. The idea is to convert the dispute into an induction over the survival time of posits rather than an induction over their truth, and then to ask whether the live cases have an actuarially different profile from the dead ones. My worry is that this just relocates the fight: now we argue about what makes a posit in the successor theory "the same posit," which is the original dispute about approximate truth one level down. So: would this device, or something in its neighbourhood, turn any part of the debate into a question with a tractable answer — a distinction, a criterion, a formal result, a change of subject — or should I conclude that no progress is to be expected here, and if so, what exactly is it about the question that makes it intractable rather than merely hard?
