I write about science and ideas, and I keep running into the Kelly criterion at the edges of a piece I'm doing on long-term thinking. At a conference last month, a speaker who advises large foundations said: "bet Kelly — in the long run, anyone who isn't maximizing the log ends up with essentially nothing compared to someone who is." He was leaning on it for two conclusions: that institutions expecting to exist for centuries, and perhaps humanity itself, shouldn't be maximizing expected value at all; and that this licenses extreme caution about small probabilities of existential catastrophe. He also gestured at something about time averages versus ensemble averages, which I didn't follow.

I'd like the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing, set out as a menu I can check against him rather than as a single verdict. The readings I can half-formulate are: a mathematical result about asymptotic dominance in repeated multiplicative bets; a claim that only the almost-sure growth rate, or the median outcome, is worth caring about; a claim that log utility is the correct attitude to risk; and the stronger claim that expected-value reasoning is a category error for anything that persists through time. For each, say whether it is a theorem, a stipulation about what to optimize, a substantive normative claim, or false — and what it assumes (repeated independent bets, reinvestment of everything, no absorbing states, a single decision-maker rather than one payoff among many).

Two things matter most for the article. First: does any reading actually carry his jump from maximizing expected log wealth to conclusions about catastrophe, given that extinction looks like a one-off absorbing outcome rather than a bad draw in a long series of reinvested bets? Second: is the asymmetry real and reportable — is overbetting worse than underbetting, and in what exact sense? Please also say how confident you are in each verdict, and single out the point you are least sure of.
