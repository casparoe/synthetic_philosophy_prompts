For most of my career I was a physiologist working on the coordination of the two hands in visually guided reaching, and since retiring I have been attending the philosophy of mind reading group at the university in my town. They have asked me to give one of their papers in about six weeks, on what the callosotomy cases do and do not show about the unity of consciousness, and I would like a preparation sheet for the question period. My thesis is fairly narrow and I would rather have it knocked about in advance than in the room. Briefly: I want to argue that the question the literature is organised around — whether cutting the corpus callosum leaves you with one conscious subject or two — is not a well-posed question, because nothing in the case fixes a count of subjects; that what the surgery actually dissociates is a bundle of distinct integrational relations, which come apart from one another; and that we should therefore stop talking about a unified consciousness and start distinguishing unity of the visual field, unity of what I have been calling the shared reaching field, unity of access and report, unity of agency or will, and phenomenal unity proper. My positive conjecture, and the part I expect to be resisted, is that what the commissure carries is not experience at all but something more like a common action-space, so that the honest moral of the cases concerns agency and intermanual conflict rather than the number of subjects in the chair. I also lean, perhaps more than I should, on a supporting datum: as I understand it, people born without a corpus callosum show far less of the disunity attributed to surgical patients, which suggests the surgical picture owes something to the operation, the underlying pathology and long compensatory habits, and not simply to the loss of the commissure. If that empirical sketch is wrong, please say so plainly.

The room will be twelve to fifteen people, and I know roughly who they are. The convener is an analytic philosopher of mind who holds that phenomenal unity is given in introspection and that neuropsychological data are at best tangential to it; she will want to know what I think could possibly count as evidence here. There is a cognitive neuroscientist who has actually run hemifield tachistoscopic work and is allergic to armchair talk of two minds; he will press me on how few well-documented cases there are, whether the sections were complete, what spared subcortical routes might be doing, and whether my position is falsifiable at all. A senior colleague in the broadly ordinary-language or conceptual-critique tradition thinks the whole grammar of counting experiences and counting subjects is confused and will say so early. Two or three graduate students in philosophy of cognitive science will want the dissociation logic made explicit — what exactly is dissociated from what, and by what inference. One member holds that unity is something the organism achieves or narrates rather than a structural given, and will wonder whether I have smuggled in the very thing I claim to be explaining away. Finally, a visiting philosopher of science will simply ask what would change my mind. I am not a philosopher by training, so I need these objections rendered in their own vocabulary with a note on which have real force and which are professional reflexes.

What I would like, then: the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do weighted by how likely they are, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, with a two-minute reply I could give standing at a whiteboard, and a note on where each reply is weakest and what I should concede rather than defend. Tell me what to give up in the first two minutes to buy goodwill, and what I should refuse to give up because the talk collapses without it. Include the question I am quietly hoping nobody asks, and the one claim in my argument I would be foolish to stake the whole paper on. I have no library access at present and cannot chase references, so please give me argument rather than sources — no reading list and no citations. For each reply, mark how much confidence you would actually put in it spoken aloud, and close by naming the single item in the sheet you are least confident about.
