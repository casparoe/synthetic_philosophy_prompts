I'm a staff engineer at a company of about a hundred and fifty people that builds a sleep-and-activity app, and I have no formal philosophy background — my degree is in computer science, and what philosophy I know comes from podcasts and from a reading group that meets at my climbing gym. For the past year I've been the tech lead on a feature we call the "wellbeing score": a single number from zero to a hundred, refreshed a few times a day from phone and wearable signals, which version two of the app would use to decide which notification to send and which activity to nudge a user toward. I've become convinced the single number is conceptually broken, and in three weeks I have twenty minutes in front of the product-and-ethics advisory board that signs off on version two. I need a prep sheet for the question period, not a defense of the position.

Here is the case I'm making, briefly. Start from the examples I keep coming back to on long runs: the cold drink afterwards, the moment a hard bug finally makes sense, the relief of scratching an itch, a piece of music you love, the second you top out on a climb. The worry — someone in my reading group said philosophers call it the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, though I may be using the label loosely — is that there is no felt quality these share, and that a number which pretends to measure that shared feeling is measuring nothing. My proposed repair is to split the territory: on one side, "thin" pleasure, a felt valence or feel-good tone that experience-sampling studies plausibly can put on a single scale; on the other side, "thick" enjoyments, satisfactions that are essentially about their object and activity and that don't seem to be a feel-tone plus an object bolted on. I want to claim that the current score tracks the thin part only, that most of what people actually care about lives in the thick part, that thick items share no common felt core and aren't commensurable with each other, and therefore that we should keep valence as one input among several, ship a short dashboard of thick-good indicators instead of one objective, and let each user set the weights rather than having the model set them for them.

The room will be: a hedonic psychologist who runs experience-sampling studies, believes valence is a real and roughly unidimensional feeling that his instruments already measure, and regards philosophical objections as armchair speculation; a utilitarian moral philosopher who consults for us and is effective-altruism-adjacent, who wants a cardinal and interpersonally comparable welfare scale and is likely to say that heterogeneity doesn't defeat a kind any more than it defeats health or games; our head of ML, who is sympathetic but pragmatic and will ask what concretely changes in the codebase, given that the learned proxy correlates with self-reports and the A/B tests show retention going up; a consumer-protection lawyer who used to be a regulator and will want to know whether any optimized score at all is a dark pattern and who audits the weights; and one accessibility advocate who will ask how any of this transfers to users whose baselines and signal patterns weren't in the training population. Please give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from these five, in the order they're likely to arrive and roughly by how much damage they'd do to the proposal, each phrased the way that person would phrase it — in their vocabulary, not in mine — with a short reply I could give in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is weakest or where I should simply concede up front. I'd also like the one question I should be most afraid of, the one I'm quietly hoping nobody asks. One constraint: please don't point me at papers, authors, or further reading, and don't cite anything — I can't chase down references, and neither will the board, so a citation would do no work in the room.
