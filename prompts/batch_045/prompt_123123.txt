1. Mira: Lewis's analysis is an analysis of agents with beliefs and preferences, who conform because they expect others to conform. Gradient descent does not give a network a reason. What you have is mutual adaptation, or coupled optimization. Calling it a convention is a metaphor, and a misleading one, because it implies there is someone there who could have done otherwise for a reason.

2. Dev: Then run the checklist instead of stopping at intuitions about agency. Everyone conforms — yes. Everyone expects everyone else to conform — the router's decoder is literally a function whose job is to anticipate the scanner's output, and if that isn't an expectation, no operational test of expectation survives. Everyone prefers to conform conditional on the others conforming — yes, each net's policy is a best response to the other's, which is what a coordination equilibrium is. Arbitrariness — cleaner here than in any human case, since the seeds show the mapping could have been any of them. Common knowledge — joint training is what makes the mutual expectations mutually manifest. And Lewis never required a convention to be born of an agreement; he let precedent and salience do that work, and here the precedent is the early training steps. Later evolutionary game-theory work drops common knowledge altogether and keeps only self-reinforcing coordination, so missing common knowledge can't be what disqualifies us.

3. Mira: The checklist is exactly where the bait-and-switch happens, and it happens at "expects." A decoder that anticipates its partner's output is a prediction, not a belief that can partly explain the predictor's own action. Lewis's condition is that each agent conforms *because* it expects the others to conform, and that is a claim about reasons, not about correlations among parameters. Test it: if the router stops conforming, does the scanner change what it does? No. Nothing happens unless someone retrains it. The counterfactual dependence is supplied by us, not by them.

4. Dev: That is an artifact of your freezing the weights. In deployment we retrain nightly against live data, so the pair does respond; it just runs on a slower clock. Human conventions live in habit and get revised only on surprise. The only differences are clock speed and the fact that our updates are shaped by a loss.

5. Mira: I concede the clock — responsiveness in real time was the wrong criterion, and human habit is a standing disposition rather than a daily deliberation. And notice, even setting the metaphysics aside, what a convention is supposed to explain: why the regularity is stable, and why this one rather than that one. Here we already have a complete causal story — seed, loss, architecture. The word adds nothing to it. It is a redescription wearing a theory's clothes.

6. Dev: The "it adds nothing" objection proves too much, since a reductive account of human conventions also aims to explain stability by precedent and salience rather than by anything spooky. And take the one distinction in this area that people really lean on: conventions versus sanction-backed norms, where norms rest on normative expectations and a willingness to punish, while conventions rest only on empirical expectations. There is no punishment in our system — no sanction, no enforcement, nobody minding anybody — so if that distinction means anything, whatever we have is on the convention side and not the norm side. Besides, Lewis's own application of his framework was to language: a sender, a receiver, an arbitrary signal, and success for both parties. By the usual tests we have built a signaling system. If the theory won't call that a convention, then either it cannot call the human case one either, or you owe me a principled difference rather than a shrug about reasons.
