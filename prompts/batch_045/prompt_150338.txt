I am editing the proceedings volume for a workshop on aggregation in distributive ethics, and I want to close it with something more useful than the exchange the participants actually had. Two of them have been running the utility monster case against each other for about a decade and have never once written down what they agree on. Call them the Aggregators, who are total-welfare consequentialists counting a unit of welfare equally no matter whose it is, and the Separatists, who hold that a gain to one being does not by itself generate a reason of the same kind to impose a loss on another, and that any such imposition has to be justifiable to whoever bears it. Draft the joint statement the two of them could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Fix the case, so that both sides are reasoning about the same text. There is a fixed stock of a perfectly divisible resource. Welfare is cardinally measurable and interpersonally comparable on a scale both sides accept, and each ordinary recipient's welfare is a strictly concave, increasing function of the share they receive. One being, the monster, converts a unit of the resource into k units of welfare where anyone else gets one, with k greater than one; its rate is intrinsic to it, stable across scales, known with certainty, and not itself a product of earlier transfers.

Run the case at two values of k, because I suspect much of the disagreement relocated when nobody was looking: k in the hundreds or thousands, with one monster and a very large population of ordinary recipients; and k around 1.2, with two groups of patients and a fixed budget of a scarce therapy, where both participants in fact accept allocating to the group with the greater capacity to benefit. If either of them wants to contest the second case, that belongs in the disagreements table rather than being smoothed over.

Then four reformulations, which I want treated as tests rather than as new ammunition.

1. The dispersed reformulation. Hold the aggregate gain and the aggregate loss fixed but realize them through a very large number of slightly above-average recipients rather than one recipient with an enormous rate. If the intuition tracks concentration rather than aggregation, this should show it.
2. The endogenous monster. Its conversion rate rises with the size of its share, so there is no interior optimum at which marginal returns equalize and the Aggregator's usual stopping rule never engages.
3. The curable monster. The elevated rate is a pathology that a cheap, permanent, and fully consensual intervention would remove; because the rate is what makes transfers productive, the intervention lowers total welfare. Does either camp forbid providing it?
4. The contaminated monster. It realizes its elevated welfare only from resources taken from others without their consent; under a voluntary transfer scheme its rate falls to one. I suspect this variant belongs to a different dispute, about whether malevolent or otherwise other-regarding preferences belong in the currency of welfare at all, and I want that suspicion either confirmed or killed.

Structure the document in four parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims, substantive enough that neither side would sign it reflexively: what the formalism of the case is, what follows for each view at each value of k, what the case does and does not establish, and what would follow if the case turned out to be nomologically impossible. Second, a table of residual disagreements, each row giving the disputed proposition in neutral terms, then the formulation an Aggregator would endorse for their own side, then the formulation a Separatist would endorse for theirs. Third, the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them, with, for each crux, the case, argument, or piece of evidence both camps accept as bearing on it and each camp's prediction about what it will show, recorded in advance. Fourth, a short closing section identifying which parts of the remaining dispute are merely verbal or orthogonal to aggregation, including whether the framing of the case as a reductio is doing any real work, and stating what each camp would count as evidence against its own view.

Work conceptually; I do not want citations, and where a technical notion carries weight, define it inline rather than invoking it. I am thinking of concavity, priority weights and how fast they would have to decay to block the monster, the person-affecting restriction, and the distinctions between a criterion of rightness and the demandingness of morality and between what there is most reason to do and what an institution should mandate.
