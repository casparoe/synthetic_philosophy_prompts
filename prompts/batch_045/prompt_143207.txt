I write a column on science and ideas, which for the last couple of years has meant mostly AI plus a running sideline in institutional speech, and somehow I've become the person my editors send to cover official apologies. I've now stood in the room for maybe two dozen of them and my gut on which ones actually *are* apologies doesn't line up with anything I can state, which is a problem because I owe 1500 words by Tuesday and I'd rather they said something than just conveyed a mood. My working rule has been: it counts only if the speaker gives something up that they'd otherwise have kept — money, a legal shield, a flattering story about the nation. My brother, who's a historian, says I'm secretly just tracking who has standing to be angry, which I resent and half believe.

Here's my gut, case by case. Willy Brandt kneeling at the memorial to the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising in 1970: no statement, no admission, no words at all — and it feels like the most complete apology I've ever covered, it counts maximally. The Australian parliamentary apology to the Stolen Generations: counts, and I think because it named the specific practice of taking children rather than the past in general. Three decades of statements from one government expressing deep remorse without ever saying the state did the thing: doesn't count, and each repetition makes the next one count *less*, which is strange, since you'd think repetition accumulates. An apology issued in the final week of a government that's about to be thrown out: counts less. An apology from a newly elected leader who personally opposed the old policy and says so: also counts less, for what looks like the opposite reason. But an heir or a head of a hereditary house apologizing for what the family firm or the crown did, having had no hand in it and no choice about the role: counts, and counts more than either of those — so both the too-involved and the too-uninvolved apologize cheaply and only some narrow band in the middle works, which feels arbitrary and is the bit I most want replaced by something real. Money: reparations explicitly framed as not a full settlement count more than words alone; the same money with a release-of-claims attached counts *less* than no money at all. A museum returns ancestral remains and says in writing that this is not an apology and admits nothing: I still feel some of the apologetic work got done, and that a statement would have added nothing — which flatly contradicts the remorse case, where wording was everything. A successor state whose constitutional order expressly denies legal continuity with the regime that did the harm apologizes for that regime's atrocities: counts. A company that was dissolved and reincorporated under a new registration number apologizing for what the old entity's subsidiary did: counts for nothing. Same structure, opposite verdicts, and I can't tell you what I'm seeing in one that I'm not seeing in the other. Then my actual beat: a benefits agency's chatbot sends personalized apologies to people wrongly denied payments, generated per case, signed by nobody — doesn't count. The same text read aloud by a caseworker who then has to fix the file: counts. The same text, model-written but signed and then defended in parliament by the minister: counts, weakly, and I can't tell whether the signing or the defending is doing the work. And a model-written apology that is more accurate and more specific about what happened than any human official would ever have produced: my gut still says no, which makes me think I'm being irrational about the medium in particular. Finally: an apology for a secret program whose victims never knew they were harmed, volunteered under no pressure at all, counts a great deal; an apology for harms the state knows will be exposed when the archives open next year counts less, even though it is also unforced and arguably more honest about its own timing.

So: test my gives-something-up rule against all of that, repair it, and give it to me clause by clause with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to drop — I'd rather drop two verdicts than end up with a rule that just redescribes them — and turn it on the three I go back and forth on: an apology the intended recipients publicly reject in advance, an apology addressed to future generations for climate inaction where there is nobody yet to accept it, and a city council apologizing for a pogrom the national government still denies happened. Then tell me what it predicts for two cases I've never actually seen: an AI system apologizing in its own name for harms its outputs caused, with no human signing it, and a state apologizing to its own citizens for a policy most of those citizens voted for. And settle whether my brother is right that I'm only tracking who has standing to be angry. I don't want names of theorists or papers, I've got three days and no library access on this trip — just the principle, in plain sentences, with where it hurts.
