Draft the joint statement that a Coherence Theorist and a Permissivist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on whether coherence arguments establish that a rational agent must maximize expected utility. The Coherence Theorist holds that violations of the standard representation-theorem axioms (completeness, transitivity, independence across states, continuity, reduction of compound gambles) are ipso facto incoherent, as shown by exploitation arguments of the Dutch-book and money-pump kind and by accuracy-dominance arguments built on strictly proper scoring rules. The Permissivist holds that these arguments show at most that certain attitudes are costly or awkward in certain settings, not that they are irrational, and that each relies on assumptions the argument never defends: chiefly that the agent has attitudes over a prospect space closed under mixing and compounding, and that a diachronic penalty transfers to a synchronic verdict about rationality.

Address the document to a reader who is building a computationally bounded artificial agent that must act in a large, only partially mapped state space, over a finite planning horizon, and who wants to know whether rationality requires that agent to be an expected-utility maximizer. Make the disagreement decidable for that reader: every crux should be stated so that it could in principle be settled by inspecting the design of such an agent rather than by intuition. Do not cite or attribute anything to named authors or works; argue from the cases.

Requirements for the draft:

1. Grade the coherence thesis before locating the dispute: distinguish the conditional claim (given attitudes that are expected-utility representable over a closed prospect space, one should maximize expected utility relative to them); the closure claim (rationality requires having such attitudes across the whole space); the penalty claim (violations are exploitable or accuracy-dominated); and the transfer claim (exploitability or accuracy-dominance entails irrationality). Record which grades both sides accept and which are contested.

2. Agreed claims must be load-bearing: each should rule out something a reasonable person might otherwise have said, rather than restate truisms.

3. State each disagreement twice, once in the wording the Coherence Theorist would endorse for their own view and once in the wording the Permissivist would endorse for theirs; each side vouches only for its own column. Mark each as substantive, verbal, or methodological, and say which disputes survive translation into a shared vocabulary and which dissolve.

4. Include at least these three cruxes, adding others only if they matter:
   (a) The no-attitude case. An agent has sharp, transitive, continuous preferences over simple monetary lotteries but denies any attitude toward the corresponding two-stage gamble, and is explicit that this is silence, not indifference. Is she incoherent or merely incomplete? Both sides predict what happens under forced choice and what, if anything, the resulting pick reveals.
   (b) The bounded pump. A cyclic agent is offered a finite money pump whose per-step gain is smaller than the per-step transaction cost, so the exploiter profits only if the agent keeps trading without bound. Does exploitability that is bounded and unprofitable to the exploiter still ground a verdict of irrationality, and does the availability of a commitment strategy of refusing all later trades answer the argument? Each side predicts what an agent who is shown the pump in advance and declines to revise has thereby shown about herself.
   (c) The scoring-rule crux. An agent with non-additive credences rejects the strictly proper scoring rule as the measure of accuracy and proposes instead a stakes-weighted measure, on which accuracy is counted only across distinctions that matter for the decisions actually available to her. Can a dominance argument be run on the skeptical measure, and if so does it vindicate expected-utility maximization or only something weaker? Each side predicts the outcome.
   For each crux give the case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, each side's prediction written down in advance, and the outcome that would move each side rather than merely refute the other.

5. Close with one paragraph on whether any part of the dispute is really about the word rational, and what each side would concede or lose by adopting the other's usage.

Format the document as: (1) a numbered Agreed Claims section; (2) a Disagreements table with the columns ID, kind (substantive, verbal, or methodological), Coherence Theorist formulation, Permissivist formulation, crux IDs, and stakes (what each formulation rules out); (3) a Cruxes section with, per crux, the question, the test or case both accept as bearing, the Coherence Theorist's prediction, the Permissivist's prediction, and what would move each side; (4) the paragraph on the verbal component.
