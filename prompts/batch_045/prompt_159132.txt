I teach a 110-minute upper-division seminar on historical explanation — about sixteen students, roughly half philosophy majors and half history majors — and I want one session of it to be a game rather than a reading discussion. Last year I tried a cheaper version of this: I gave teams a set of event cards about a polity and asked them to arrange the cards into the causal story they thought a historian would tell. It fell flat, because the students simply produced a better-written chronicle and everyone agreed; the exercise made the event-sized scale feel more explanatory, not less. What I want them to run into this time is the opposite: that the slow-moving constraints — the carrying capacity of a valley, the silting of a harbour, a transport-cost regime, a climate band drifting over centuries — set the envelope of what is possible, while plagues, battles, harvests and rulers select among outcomes inside that envelope, and crucially that the record they have to work from is itself biased toward the eventful, so that the explanation a later historian can write is shaped by what got recorded rather than by what did the causal work. Two conditions: nothing should be lectured in advance, and I don't want the vocabulary of the longue durée, the conjoncture, and the event used anywhere in the room until the last ten minutes, when we name what they have already done.

So please design the game. My own sketch, which you should feel free to replace with something that works better: four teams, each running a household or small polity in the same region, playing out something like twelve generations of roughly twenty-five years on a shared map and a shared board of slow variables; each generation, teams spend a small fixed budget of labour on actions, and — this is the mechanic I care most about — the structural actions (terracing, granaries, forest management, harbour dredging, road or fleet capacity) have no effect in the generation they are taken but move the slow variables two or three generations later, so that no player ever sees the decision and its consequence in the same breath. Each generation also draws an event card — epidemic, bumper harvest, succession crisis, raid, earthquake, treaty, an unusually competent ruler — whose severity I'd like to be read off a lookup table conditioned on the polity's current slow-variable state, so that the identical card is a catastrophe for one team and a footnote for another. Then the archive rule: at the end of each generation every team may add one short chronicle line to a single shared sheet, and teams vote a small prestige award for the most memorable entry, while the actual final score is computed from the slow variables (surviving households, or a population-weighted welfare index). I'd like prestige and outcome scored and displayed separately, because I want the two rankings to come apart in front of them. I also want two measurement devices, since I plan to use this as an actual teaching session rather than a one-off: mid-game, around generation six, every team seals a one-sentence prediction of what will determine the final ranking, to be opened and scored against the outcome at the end; and at the end, before any debrief, the teams are handed only the chronicle sheet they themselves wrote — the slow-variable board covered — and asked to write the causal explanation of why each polity ended where it did. Call the gap between those mid-game forecasts and the final reconstruction what you like; I have been calling it the attribution gap, and I want the design to make it visible and roughly measurable. Please give me the concrete rules, the materials and printables, the slow-variable update rules and event lookup table with real numbers that will actually produce divergence within twelve generations, a worked example of one generation resolved end to end, the minute-by-minute timeline, and how to assign roles if a team is short a member.

Finally, the parts that make or break it in the room. I want a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific claims — the two-generation lag onto the invisibility of structural causes, the lookup table onto the conditioning of events by structures, the prestige vote and the chronicle budget onto the source bias that makes narrative history almost inevitable, and the sealed forecasts onto how we test any of this — and that ends by having the students say, in their own words, what they think the right division of explanatory labour is before I supply the standard vocabulary. Then I want your honest read on what will predictably go wrong: my guesses are that one team will ignore terracing entirely and win on event cards and thereby discredit the whole exercise; that the prestige vote will turn into a joke-writing contest; that some students will treat the slow variables as just another resource to optimize and so miss the point that they are constraints on what can be attempted at all; and that the archive phase will feel like a memory test rather than a lesson about evidence. Tell me how to handle each, including how to rig the numbers so the first one is unlikely without making the outcome deterministic. And be frank about what the game cannot stage: I suspect the real thing it can't reproduce is the timescale itself, and also the claim I most want them to take seriously later — that a structure of the longue durée is not merely a very slow cause but partly constitutive of what counts as a reason or an option for the people inside it, which no lookup table can convey. Say plainly which of those I will have to supply in the last ten minutes, and add a short note on how to compress the whole thing to sixty minutes, or to run it with eight students instead of sixteen, in case I use it in a reading-group version next term.
