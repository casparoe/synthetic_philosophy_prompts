I have just had a long argument with a friend about Christopher Nolan’s Memento and what, if anything, it has to do with the philosophical fight over the color phi phenomenon and the timing of experience. In the standard color phi case, a dot of one colour is flashed in one position and then a dot of a second colour in another; at the right spacing and timing the observer reports a single dot moving between the positions and changing colour, and says the change occurred before the second dot was ever presented. My understanding of Dennett and Kinsbourne on this is that the case forces a choice between an Orwellian story, in which the subject really has one experience and then memory retroactively revises it, and a Stalinesque story, in which the content is settled before it reaches consciousness, like a verdict arranged in advance and then performed; and that because the two stories can agree on every neural and functional fact, there is no further fact of the matter about which is true, since there is no Cartesian theatre and no single instant at which processing becomes conscious. My friend says Memento is irrelevant to all this: Leonard Shelby’s anterograde amnesia means there is no single conscious subject whose experience could be timed, and the film’s reverse-ordered colour scenes are merely a puzzle-box gimmick. I said the film is almost a deliberate model of the problem for the viewer, and I want a reading that tells me which of us is closer to right.

To make the connection precise, I proposed a variant my friend dismissed, and I would like you to take it seriously. Call it the delayed-flash or Shelby-phi case. Instead of separating the two flashes by a fraction of a second, separate them by the length of the film. The first flash is the opening reverse-ordered image of the Polaroid and the killing that follows; the second flash is the ending’s black-and-white revelation about Sammy Jankis, the insulin, and what Leonard has actually done. The observer in this case is the audience, and the audience reports that the meaning of the first flash changed, and reports it as having changed at the time of the first flash, before the second flash was seen. Is the audience’s experience of the opening Orwellian, a genuine initial experience later overwritten by the ending? Is it Stalinesque, with the interpretation fixed in advance by the structure and the ending merely ratifying a verdict that was always going to be the one remembered? Or does Memento, like the multiple-drafts picture, show that the question dissolves, because there is no single theatre in which the viewer’s experience happens—only successive drafts, notes, Polaroids, tattoos, and the final note Leonard writes?

Please read Memento through this debate. I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not flatten the film into a mere illustration of Dennett and Kinsbourne, and it should not stretch their distinction to fit scenes it does not really fit. Tell me where the analogy illuminates a specific element—the Polaroids, the tattoos, the reversed colour sequences, the final decision to make Teddy the next John G—and where the film resists the reading. Say, too, what the film, if anything, adds to the philosophical argument that the bare color phi case and the Orwellian/Stalinesque contrast alone do not. Please structure your reply as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
