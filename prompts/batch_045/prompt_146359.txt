You are a professional philosopher with research-level command of decision theory and normative ethics: ex ante and ex post Pareto criteria, person-affecting and consent-based moral views, and the family of proposals that try to make expected-utility policy criteria robust to uncertainty about the probabilities. I need one carefully engineered case, not a survey of options, and I need you to show that it works.

The machinery I will be using. Fix a set N of at least three individuals — role-bearers in an institution, not abstractions — all of whom exist whichever way the choice goes; no one's existence, number, or identity may depend on the decision. Fix a finite state space W. Each individual has a cardinal utility index over outcomes; say explicitly what, if anything, you assume about interpersonal comparability. There are two options, the status quo S and an intervention I. Uncertainty is represented not by a single prior but by an admissible set P: a non-singleton convex set of probability distributions over W, every member of which is a reasonable thing for the parties to believe given the evidence. Finally, fix a learnable partition L of W: the partition whose cells are the events that could, in principle, be distinguished by evidence available to the parties before the outcomes are realised. Then:

- I is an ex ante Pareto improvement relative to p iff, for every individual, their expected utility is strictly higher under I than under S.
- I is a robust ex ante Pareto improvement iff that holds for every p in P.
- I is a safe Pareto improvement iff, for every p in P, every individual, and every cell E of L with positive probability, the individual's expected utility conditional on E is strictly higher under I than under S.

I borrow the label from recent work but I am not asking you to track down anyone's definition, and I do not want citations or a reading list: work with the definition above. (If you think it departs from how the term is standardly used, say so in one sentence and then use mine.) Note the limiting case: when L is the finest partition, safety collapses into ex post Pareto dominance. The interest of the notion, if it has any, lives in the coarseness of L.

What the case is for, which fixes what counts as cheating. I am writing against the claim that safe Pareto improvements are a deontologically respectable surrogate for the actual consent of those on whom risks are imposed. My target reader is sympathetic to that claim, so a case that begs the question against it is useless to me. The verdicts must fall out of the criteria plus the structure of the case; stipulating them, or describing a case and asserting that people will react thus-and-so, fails.

Specifications.

1. A realistic institutional setting — a hospital trust, a regulator, a public-health authority, a pension fund, a university. One paragraph of prose, named roles, ordinary stakes. No science fiction, no demons, no infinite or astronomically large values, and no state with probability below 0.05 anywhere in the case: I want nothing to turn on tiny-probability-huge-payoff machinery.
2. |W| at most six, with the utilities and probabilities laid out in a small table.
3. P convex, non-singleton, and motivated by a genuine empirical disagreement you state in one sentence — experts who reasonably differ, not stipulated noise.
4. I is a safe Pareto improvement relative to P and L, where L has at least one cell containing two or more states. Verify this rather than asserting it. Conditional expected utility is a ratio of linear functions of the prior, so checking the vertices of P does not settle the matter automatically: tell me how you checked, and make the check work for every p in P.
5. I is not an ex post Pareto improvement: in at least one state some individual suffers a serious, irreversible harm that cannot be compensated within the terms of the case. That state must have probability at least 0.10 under every admissible prior. No negligible tails.
6. The feature I most want isolated: there must be a refinement L* of L that the planner could bring about by a cheap and otherwise unobjectionable step — a test, an audit, a short delay, a pilot — such that I is not safe relative to L*. In some cell of L*, with probability at least 0.15 under every admissible prior, some individual's conditional expected utility is strictly lower under I. So the case must be one where a substantial, learnable subgroup is composed of net expected losers, and where the safety verdict for I therefore depends on the parties' not receiving information the planner could obtain. Show that what breaks safety in that cell is the distribution of risk across states, not the resource cost of the extra step.
7. Close the obvious escape: the targeted variant of I — apply it only to those for whom it is favourable in the refined cells — must not be available as a clean safe Pareto improvement. Either the information arrives too late to act on, or the targeted variant fails safety on your own definition, or the targeting procedure imposes a burden of the same kind as the one objected to. Choose, and argue that your way of closing the escape is not itself question-begging against my target reader.
8. The deontological verdict must be earned. The individuals who bear the serious risk must themselves be robust expected beneficiaries: strictly positive conditional expected gain in every cell of L under every admissible prior, so that they would consent ex ante and would still consent given any evidence available when the choice is made. They must not have assumed the risk by prior consent, professional role, or benefits already accepted. And the objection that implementing I without their actual consent is impermissible must not be one that would equally condemn any risky but plainly permissible activity; say what distinguishes this case.

Deliverables, in this order.

(a) The scenario, one paragraph, followed by the table.
(b) The verification: the safe-improvement computations, the ex post failure, and the failure relative to L*. Check your arithmetic before presenting it.
(c) Verdicts, each with the reasoning that generates it: the safe Pareto criterion; plain ex ante Pareto at a single prior; the ex post or actual-consent view. Add, briefly, where a hypothetical-consent contractualist lands, and what that placement tells us about who is isolated by the case.
(d) One short passage, before the scenario, in which you sketch two or three candidate structures and say why you discarded them; then commit to the one you stand behind.
(e) Which single feature of the case is doing the work; what the case collapses into if you remove it; and the strongest attack a defender of the safe Pareto criterion would mount against the case, together with your reply.
(f) An honest assessment, in your own voice: is the deontological objection your case supports anything more than the familiar complaint about ex ante Pareto improvements, now hardened against the two standard replies — that the verdict might have been an artefact of one contested prior, and that better evidence might have reversed it? If it is more, say what the surplus consists in. If it is not, say so plainly and tell me what follows for the argument of my paper.

Commit. Give me one case, make the definite choices where I have left them open, and take a position on which side gets this case right and why. If you think the brief as a whole cannot be satisfied, do not fudge it: say which specification has to go, why the conjunction is impossible, and build the strongest case you can with the remainder. Write throughout in British English.
