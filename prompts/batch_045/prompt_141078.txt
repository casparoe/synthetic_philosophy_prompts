I am a third-year undergraduate in the joint mathematics and philosophy programme here, and this term I am taking our department's special subject on Leibniz's metaphysics. We have worked through the Discourse on Metaphysics, the Monadology, and long stretches of the Theodicy, and I am writing my final essay on the claim that this world is the best of all possible worlds. I can more or less follow the machinery as the seminar presents it, but I cannot tell how much real content it has, and I would like to find out by translating it into a vocabulary in which I am actually fluent.

My mathematical background: I am comfortable with linear and integer programming, LP duality and the integer case, Pareto optimality and the usual scalarization tricks, basic convex optimization, and the fact that maximum-weight independent set is NP-hard; I have met non-Archimedean ordered fields but have never worked seriously in one. Please draw on that vocabulary freely — feasibility, constraint sets, objective functions, duals and shadow prices, optimal value versus optimizer, optimality versus feasibility, existence and uniqueness of optima, and the difference between a hard constraint and a penalty term — and assume I need no hand-holding in it.

Here is what I want translated, claim by claim. Treat these as the Leibnizian theses as my seminar has presented them, and tell me if any one of them is not something Leibniz actually holds.

1. Every possible individual has a complete concept, and two individuals are compossible just in case their complete concepts can be jointly instantiated in one and the same world; a possible world is a maximal set of compossible individuals.
2. Every possible individual, merely in virtue of being possible, has a kind of claim or tendency toward existence, proportional to the quantity of perfection or reality it contains; these claims compete, because not every possible can be actual, some being incompossible with others.
3. The actual world is therefore the maximal compossible collection that maximizes realized perfection: God, being perfectly wise and good, could not have chosen a worse one, but was not logically compelled to choose this one, since every other world remained genuinely possible. This is moral, not absolute, necessity.
4. Evil is of three kinds — metaphysical, physical, moral — and the metaphysical kind, bare limitation or privation, is not something that could have been optimized away, since it is built into being a creature at all; some of the goods of the best world are inseparable from it.
5. Whether one individual is compossible with another is settled by an infinite analysis that only God completes, so our judgments about possibility here are fallible in a way his are not.

Do this claim by claim, and for each say what maps cleanly onto my vocabulary, what has to be deformed to fit, and what cannot be said in it at all.

Two ideas of my own that I want either made precise or killed. First: this looks to me very like a maximum-weight independent set in an incompatibility graph whose vertices are complete individual concepts and whose edges are incompossibility, with the twist that the graph is infinite and the weights are supposed to be read off the essences themselves rather than supplied from outside. Second: once the graph is infinite and every vertex carries a positive claim, aggregating by straightforward summation cannot work, since the totals will diverge or be undefined, so the comparison among worlds must be some limiting or infinitesimal comparison of reality realized against reality excluded. Leibniz invented the calculus and took infinitesimals seriously; so perhaps the right target vocabulary is not maximization over the reals at all but something non-Archimedean, or a comparison of ratios in the limit. Is there anything in this, or am I reading a modern picture into a seventeenth-century text?

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. My guess is that several load-bearing Leibnizian notions have no counterpart in optimization:

- the claim of a merely possible individual that is never actualized looks unlike anything I have a term for, since once the objective is fixed the unchosen alternatives have no standing; at most they surface as an opportunity cost in a dual, and an opportunity cost is not a demand that was overruled;
- the distinction between moral and absolute necessity looks as though it should map onto optimality versus feasibility, but I suspect it does not, because in optimization the fact that a solver returns an optimum is a fact about the solver, whereas Leibniz seems to need the necessity to sit in the thing chosen;
- the whole story seems to depend on there being a unique optimum, since if two worlds tied, the principle of sufficient reason would apparently have nothing left to say about why this one rather than that, and nothing in optimization guarantees uniqueness;
- the weights are not given to the problem from outside but are supposed to be internal to the individuals being ranked.

For each piece of residue, tell me whether it indicts my framework or the Leibnizian claim: is the optimization vocabulary simply too poor to express what Leibniz needs, or is he helping himself to something that the translation exposes as underargued — quantitative comparability of perfections, an objective function available prior to the choice, a feasibility structure already fixed, a single decision-maker with complete knowledge of both? I would rather you pressed hard in both directions than produced a diplomatic verdict.

Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or the names of commentators to chase: I already have a reading list and no library access from where I am writing. What I want is the translation itself, an account of the distortions it required, and an honest statement of what fell out of it. Where you think one of my five theses is not Leibniz's, say so and say what you would put in its place.
