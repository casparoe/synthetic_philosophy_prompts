I convene a graduate seminar on logic and the philosophy of mind, and last week a visitor presented what she called a "description-free" version of the Lucas–Penrose argument: a version designed to survive the familiar objection, associated with Putnam and Benacerraf, that the argument requires me to know *which* formal system I am and that I cannot know this. I am taking the follow-up session on Thursday and would like to put a diagnosis on the board rather than a shrug, because I cannot locate the mistake and I also cannot accept the conclusion. The argument, as I reconstructed it from her handout:

(1) Suppose, for reductio, that my mathematical capacity is exhausted by some consistent, sufficiently strong, recursively axiomatized formal system — call whichever system it is "my system". Nothing here requires me to name it, to know its Gödel number, or even to be able to describe it; the supposition is purely existential.
(2) By the first incompleteness theorem, for every such system F, the Gödel sentence G(F) is true and unprovable in F, and the passage from a specification of F to G(F) is uniform. Moreover the general statement — that every consistent, sufficiently strong, recursively axiomatized system leaves its own Gödel sentence undecided while that sentence is true — is not itself a remote truth: it is the formalized incompleteness theorem, and it is provable in elementary arithmetic.
(3) I know that general statement. And by (1) I know that I am some consistent, sufficiently strong, recursively axiomatized system. So I know, of whichever system I am, that there is a truth it cannot prove: I know a truth my own system cannot prove, without ever having to identify my system.
(4) So my mathematical knowledge outruns my own system, contradicting (1). Hence mechanism is false.
(5) The objection that sank the original argument is therefore evaded. Lucas needed identificational self-knowledge — roughly, that I am F with code n — and that is what his critics disputed. All that (1)–(4) require is existential self-knowledge, that I am some consistent system or other, and the mechanist herself grants that.
(6) The mechanist's fallback fails for the same reason. She will say that a machine can always be patched by adjoining its own Gödel sentence, and the patching iterated — in the spirit of Turing's ordinal logics, from his 1939 paper on systems of logic based on ordinals, and of the later literature on recursive progressions of theories. But at every stage of any such progression the system in question still satisfies (1), so (2)–(4) apply to it again, and the mind outruns that stage too; no stage of a mechanically generated progression is me.
(7) And now what makes me suspicious. Nothing in (1)–(4) seems to turn on any specifically psychological capacity. Consider a device as mechanical as a cash register: it carries a specification of itself (a program can obtain its own code), it proves the formalized incompleteness theorem, and we stipulate from the outside that it is consistent. It appears to satisfy every premise — it has the general statement, and it is some consistent, sufficiently strong, recursively axiomatized system. So by (3) and (4) it outruns itself and is not a machine. If the argument is sound, then sufficiently strong consistent arithmetic is not mechanical, which is either false or says nothing about minds. So the argument proves too much.

My suspicion keeps migrating between (3) and (7), and I do not trust either diagnosis, because (3) reads to me like universal instantiation plus modus ponens, and my attempts to say what is wrong with it collapse into either "the identification problem comes back" — which my visitor denies and which I cannot refute — or "the notion of knowing a truth is sliding between readings", which sounds like a cheap escape. One of my students kept insisting that the whole thing founders on my inability to know my own consistency, but that seems to misfire as stated: consistency enters at (1) as a hypothesis of the reductio, not as something I am credited with knowing. A second student thought the real action was in (6), that the iteration reply is where the argument secretly helps itself to something.

Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I want the step named, an account of why it looked sound, and a price list for the alternatives. For what it is worth, here is what I would most like to keep: the claim that a competent mathematician who is handed the code of a familiar consistent system can see that that system's Gödel sentence is true — I would give up almost anything else in this area before giving that up. What I do not want to lean on is any premise about consciousness, understanding, or non-computable physics; I find the later Penrose's move in that direction slippery, and I would rather the argument stand or fall on the logic alone. I would also rather not rest anything on Gödel's own remarks about the mind: my recollection of the 1951 lecture is that he drew a disjunction rather than a flat anti-mechanist conclusion, and in any case I do not want to argue from what he took himself to have shown.
