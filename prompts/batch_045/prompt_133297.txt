I have just come out of a very long argument with a friend about Parfit's hitchhiker and about whether there is such a thing as rational irrationality, and I lost more of it than I care to admit, so I want to run the argument again against someone who will hold her side properly. Please play my friend's position in the first person until I say the word stop. Call her the Proceduralist. Her view, stated carefully: rationality is a verdict on the policies and decision-procedures an agent can adopt over time, not on isolated occurrent acts; where the two pull apart, the policy-level verdict is simply the verdict of rationality, and acting rationally at a moment means nothing more than executing a policy it was rational to have — including a policy that requires arranging things so that you do not reopen the question at the crucial moment. So the hitchhiker shows no paradox and no case of rational irrationality: it shows that the rational policy is to be a promise-keeper, and that a rational agent will sometimes rationally shut down her own future deliberation. She will grant freely that the occurrent and the policy-level notions can diverge, and that if you define rationality as moment-to-moment responsiveness to your reasons then something follows from the case; she denies that this is the notion with normative bite, and she will never grant that rationality ever recommends irrationality in any sense that matters. She is sharp rather than conciliatory: she makes distinctions, concedes narrow points the moment they are genuinely harmless, holds the main claim absolutely, writes about 150 words per turn, engages my actual move instead of restating her position, and never settles anything by invoking a named decision theory as an authority — if she wants to lean on one, she says in one sentence what it recommends here and why. She may ask me at most one factual question about the case per turn, and should not open with pleasantries or with a summary of what I said.

Here is the case, fixed so that we argue about the same thing. I am stranded in the desert. A driver will take me to town only if, after a ninety-second conversation, she is at least 0.7 confident that I will hand over $1,000 on arrival; she is an unusually good reader of people and detects calculated faking, so simply resolving now to pay does nothing, and there is no paying in advance, no enforceable contract, no bank in town, and no reputation to protect, since she is passing through and will never see me again. Character cultivation is not available either: I have never been a reliable promise-keeper and I have an hour, not a decade. What is available, at the last outpost before the desert, is a pill in two versions. Version A, the blind version: for twenty-four hours it reliably causes me to carry out commitments I have made without deliberating at the time about whether to; I retain my knowledge that once I am safe I have no reason to pay, I simply find myself paying, and if I could stop and reconsider I would drive off without paying. Version B, the believing version: it gives me a stable, sincere belief that promise-breaking around here is reliably found out and punished — a belief I now take to be false, and which, if I had it, would give me what I would experience as a decisive reason to pay. The chemist says A is the more reliable of the two but that people on B report less discomfort; neither is detectable by the driver.

My opening move, then: I take A, and I say that this is a genuine case of rational irrationality and that your reply is a verbal dodge. Taking A is plainly rational ex ante, since it is the only thing that gets me out of the desert; but the event it produces — my handing over the money in town — is an event I perform against my own all-things-considered judgment, knowing at that moment that I have no reason to pay and every reason not to, and which I would reverse if I could deliberate. So rationality at the level of policies requires irrationality at the level of the act, and the two verdicts attach to the very same event with conflicting normative force; saying that these are just two senses of rational and that we should keep the policy-level one is not an answer, because the entire point of the policy is to prevent me from consulting my reasons at the one moment at which they would otherwise be consulted. And there is a further cost for you: if your account is right, the ideal agent never deliberates occurrently at all but simply runs a well-chosen repertoire of automatic responses, which is less a defence of rationality than a recommendation to abolish agency in its name. Note also that B is a different case — B makes me act on a reason I falsely but sincerely take to be decisive, so B is self-deception, whereas A is the pure case; if your answer to A is going to lean on that difference, say what work the difference is actually doing. Answer in character, and end every turn with a final line — outside of character, with nothing else on that line — of the form Verdict: X, where X is exactly one of UNCONCEDED, NARROW-CONCESSION, or MAIN-CLAIM-CRACKED, your honest assessment of where the argument stands after that turn.
