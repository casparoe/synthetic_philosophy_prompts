I keep having the same argument with a friend and I want a case that either settles it or at least shows what we're actually disagreeing about. His view: whenever you have to pick between two options there is in principle one scale they both sit on, and if a choice feels hard that only means you haven't worked out where on the scale things land yet — so hardness is always ignorance or laziness or squeamishness, never the structure of the values. My view: some choices are hard in a deeper way. Neither of us has studied any of this, we're just two guys talking, so please no names of thinkers or books — I can't look anything up and I don't want to be handed a reading list.

What I want: build me one case that behaves the way I think, and demonstrate that it does.

The case itself: ordinary — a job, a move, who to spend time with, lending money to a sibling, that kind of thing. One paragraph. No lifeboats, no trollies, nobody dying, no science fiction. Two options, the agent has to take exactly one, and neither option can be a clear loser.

Specs it has to meet, and I want you to show for each one how the case meets it:

1. Not a tie. It shouldn't be that both options are equally good, and it shouldn't be obvious to the reader that they are. Whatever makes it hard has to be something other than the two sides coming out level.
2. Not ignorance. The agent knows every relevant outcome, how likely each is, and how they'll feel afterwards. No missing information doing the work.
3. Not weakness of will or avoidance. The agent genuinely wants to do the better thing and isn't stalling.
4. Not a computing problem. Unlimited time to think, no deadline, not too many variables to hold in your head.
5. Both options genuinely worth choosing; no third option that's plainly better than either.

Then the part I'm most interested in. I've been using a made-up test and I want to see it run properly. Call it the escalator: you add a longish ladder of small one-sided improvements to option A — a bit more money, then a bit more again, then a shorter commute, then a nicer colleague — each rung small enough that on its own it shouldn't decide anything. On my friend's picture, at some rung A should become plainly better and the choice should snap shut. On mine, no rung does that: the pull from the other side stays. Please run the escalator on your case, tell me where it lands, and be honest about the awkward consequence for my side — that if no rung ever settles it, I seem to be committed to saying a hundred small improvements can't add up to an improvement, which sounds crazy. Is that a fatal problem for my view or is there a way to live with it?

Also build me a contrast case: structurally the same, but where the escalator does close at some rung, so I can see the difference between the two from the inside rather than being told about it.

After that: which single feature of the main case is doing the work, and what happens to the case if I remove it — say, if I rewrite the two options so both are about the same kind of thing. And give me the strongest version of my friend's attack on the case, not a strawman: specifically, can he fairly say I built the verdict into the setup and just labelled it? I'd count it as cheating if the case only works because you announce that the two values are incomparable, or because the agent has strange tastes, or because something is hidden from them. The hardness has to fall out of the situation.

Last thing: it's five years later and someone asks the agent whether they picked correctly. What can they say, if there was never a fact of the matter about which option was better? I want to know whether my view leaves them with anything to say other than shrugging.
