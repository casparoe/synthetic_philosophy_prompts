hey — could you act as a professional philosopher who specializes in metaphysics of mathematics and has also taught it to people who actually do math for a living? Please think it through carefully and start by brainstorming the logical space (what the possible combinations of answers even are) before you commit to any structure. I'm putting together a two-page handout for an informal "maths and reality" evening I run with third-year pure maths students — they're very good at maths and very suspicious of metaphysics. The second they see a question like "do you believe numbers exist?" they mentally check out, so I want the handout to be a decision tree that sorts them by what they say about concrete mathematical situations, and only reveals the name of the position at the leaf. Start node: you accept ordinary mathematical practice and the theorems as mathematicians state them. Then eight branch questions, each one a case or claim a working mathematician could answer from instinct with no philosophy vocabulary, ending in seven to nine named leaves.

Some situations I'd like used as nodes (swap in better ones if you have them): when a mathematician says let n be an arbitrary even integer and then proves something about it, has she singled something out, or only spoken generally? Whether it matters which set-theoretic surrogate we identify the number 3 with, given that the usual reduction offers more than one. Whether the continuum hypothesis already has an answer that we just don't know, versus whether adopting a new axiom there is more like legislation than discovery. The standard example people use in that literature about periodical cicadas and prime-numbered life cycles — assume for the sake of the handout that the biology is right — where a mathematical fact looks like it's doing the explaining of a physical fact: does that explanation need the mathematical claim to be true? A proof nobody has ever checked, or a computer proof too long for any human to survey: does anything about it wait on a mind? And the squeeze I keep getting stuck on: if the things maths seems to be about are outside spacetime and causally inert, how do our proofs and our referring expressions manage to track them at all?

Also: if someone handed you a rewritten physics that made all the same predictions without quantifying over mathematical entities, would you say the original was false, or just clunkier, or better off because it's more explanatory? And whether there could be a world with exactly the same concrete furniture as ours but different mathematical facts. Build the tree from the combinations of answers first and only then locate the actual positions in it, and tell me which combinations are unoccupied and why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). I know some pairs are going to be hard to seperate — people who say mathematical talk is fine but not really truth-apt versus people who say it's false but useful, and the "objects are positions in structures" crowd versus plain old set-theoretic platonists — please flag those explicitly rather than pretending the tree cuts them apart, and note what extra question (if any) would separate them. Last thing: a short note on which two of the eight questions do the most work and which one you'd cut as redundant.
