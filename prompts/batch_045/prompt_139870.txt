I am writing a paper on Aristotle's doctrine of natural slavery in the first book of the *Politics*, and before I commit to the position I have arrived at I would like an audit of it. I take it as settled that chattel slavery is unjust and that Aristotle's conclusion is false; the question I want answered is whether the package of interpretive and philosophical claims below is jointly tenable. So please don't spend the answer explaining why slavery is wrong or why Aristotle's conclusion fails — I want the internal consistency of my own set, with the tensions ranked by severity rather than listed as if equally bad.

The commitments:

1. The criterion is psychological, not genealogical. On my reading, the natural slave is picked out by a condition of the soul: a deliberative faculty that is present but not authoritative, so that the person registers reasons without reasoning for himself. Aristotle at *Politics* I, 1254b, describes him as one who "participates in reason so far as to apprehend it but not to possess it." Nothing in the criterion mentions descent, ethnicity, or bodily type. So the doctrine is not an ancestor of modern biological racism, and it is not most charitably read as a post hoc rationalization of Greek practice either: in its own terms it is a thesis about a kind of psychological deficiency and about what political relation, if any, suits it.

2. The doctrine is nonetheless essentially teleological. The deficiency only does the work Aristotle needs because the natural slave is also described as a living instrument of action, as belonging to another, and as in some sense a part of the master whose good is specified in relation to the master's ends. Detach the teleology and you do not get a defensible residue of the same thesis; you get a different thesis sharing some vocabulary. I want to insist, against readers who treat Aristotle as a proto-liberal theorist of capacity, that there is no non-teleological version of natural slavery.

3. Even so, the doctrine is practically inert. Granting that some human beings answered to the criterion, no one could ever be justified in enslaving anyone by appeal to it, because the condition is not observable from the outside — Aristotle concedes in the same chapter that the excellence of a soul, as he puts it, is not as easy to see as the beauty of a body, and that nature's intended bodily marks frequently fail to appear, some people having the bodies of free men and the souls of slaves and others the reverse. The class of natural slaves and the class of legally enslaved persons can therefore come apart almost entirely.

4. What is philosophically interesting in it survives. What it could mean to govern someone for his own good when his own practical reasoning is severely impaired is a live question for us — we face versions of it with advanced dementia and severe cognitive disability — and I want to say both that the doctrine collapses with Aristotle's teleology and that there is a real question here that outlives it.

5. My reading of "by nature" is environmentalist rather than innatist. I don't think "by nature" in Aristotle entails "independently of how the organism is reared, exercised, and situated"; on his own biology natural capacities are realized only under suitable conditions. So the fact that a deficit is produced and maintained by the institution of slavery — call these derivative cases — does not by itself show the deficit to be unnatural.

6. I hold the two-descriptions reading: the same person can be characterized as an instrument under the description "slave" and as a subject with a limited share of reason and virtue, and even as a possible participant in something like friendship with the master, under the description "human being." The instrumental language and the psychological language are, on this view, not in competition.

7. My institutional verdict: no actual institution of the sort Aristotle's world practiced — heritable, acquired by conquest, defined by legal status — could be licensed by the doctrine, even if natural slaves existed.

Here is what I want. Go through these and tell me which pairs are really in tension and how badly; for each genuine tension, say what would have to be given up and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. I also suspect that at least one of the worries I keep having is only apparent and dissolves once the relevant distinction is drawn — tell me which, and why it dissolves rather than merely relocates. Finally, say whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most, or whether the set has to be broken up in more than one place.

One stress case, which I can't get to come out determinately and which I'd like you to use as a test of the set. Imagine a community that has been held in slavery for three generations, and suppose counterfactually that we had a perfectly reliable way of detecting the Aristotelian condition. The test shows that the present generation lacks authoritative deliberation, and also that their grandparents' generation possessed it. On the commitments above: are the present generation natural slaves? Is my set able to answer that at all, or is that precisely where it runs out?
