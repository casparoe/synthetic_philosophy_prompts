I keep leaning on Condorcet's jury theorem whenever I want to say a group is probably right, and I want to know what I actually think, going by my gut on cases. Here's where I land.

1. Eleven people, each about 60% likely to get a yes/no question right, voting independently: I defer to their majority, and I'd rather have the eleven than any one of them.
2. Same setup but a thousand and one of them: I trust it a lot more. The jump from 11 to 1001 feels like it's doing real work, not just the jump from 1 to 11.
3. A thousand people who'd each be 60% accurate on their own but who all got their information from the same slanted source: I'd rather ask three genuinely independent people. Adding more of these does nothing for me.
4. A thousand people each at 50.1%: I don't feel much deference at all, and I'd take twenty people at 70% instead, even though the theorem technically says the big group wins.
5. Nine people at 95% and a thousand at 51%, all independent: I don't want the nine outvoted by the crowd, but I also don't want to just throw the thousand away. Weighting them by reliability feels right — which makes me wonder why plain headcount felt fine in 1 and 2.
6. A thousand people each at 45%, worse than chance: the majority is worse than useless and I find myself thinking you should do the opposite of what they say. But that means deferring to the *minority*, which seems to cut against 2.
7. A city council picking between two zoning plans, where 'correct' just means better for the city and people's values differ: I still feel better about a bigger, more diverse group, but the truth-tracking story has nothing to do with it. And unlike in 3, shared bias here doesn't bother me much — if most people want the same kind of neighborhood, that's just what they want.

My tentative principle: defer to a group's majority when (a) there's a fact of the matter independent of the vote, (b) average competence is above chance, (c) errors are independent, and (d) the decision threshold gets stricter when one error is much costlier than the other and drifts back toward a bare majority as the group grows.

Is there one principle that gets all seven, or does one verdict have to go — and if so which? Please state the principle precisely, say which case motivates each clause, give me at least one case I haven't listed where it yields something I'd probably find surprising, and be honest about anything of mine it can't accommodate. Then tell me what it says about the case I keep going back and forth on: a jury of twelve, each juror 60% accurate, where convicting an innocent person is ten times worse than acquitting a guilty one. Where should the conviction threshold sit, and is it better to add twelve more jurors of the same quality or to spend the money getting the original twelve up to 70%? Please write in American English.
