I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on meaning and rule-following: sixteen students, one eighty-minute session, one teaching assistant, a room with movable chairs, index cards, and a board. I want a runnable activity that makes them feel, rather than hear, why the rule-following debate is not a puzzle about other people or about the limits of evidence. By the end I want them to have run into four things on their own: that the pressure is first-personal and about *correctness* rather than about knowledge of someone else's mind; that nothing they can point to inside themselves — a formula, an image, a felt sense of "and so on", their own past answers — settles which rule they meant, because whatever they point to is itself something that has to be taken one way rather than another; that appealing to the community does not end the difficulty but moves it up a level, since agreement with whom, over what range of cases, and counted in what respect is the same question again; and that what is left at the bottom is not ignorance of a fact but something more like a decision about whether to go on with a person.

The device I have in mind, which you may replace if you have something better and you should say what makes your replacement better, is this. Give the sequence 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and ask for the next term. Most will write 32. Among the responses they see, one card gives 31, with its rule stated: the greatest number of regions you can cut a circle into by joining every pair of n points on its circumference, which begins 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 31, 57, 99. This matters because the usual dismissal of a deviant continuation is that the deviant rule is gerrymandered, disjunctive, or grue-like; this one is none of those things. It is a perfectly projectible, non-ad-hoc mathematical function that simply happens to agree with doubling for the first five terms. So the class cannot wave it away, and the question "what makes it the case that I meant doubling?" has to be answered on its merits. I also want a far-divergence card alongside it — a rule that agrees with the expected one on every case anyone in the room will ever meet and diverges only at inputs far too large to check — plus a short paired exercise in which one student privately writes down a rule and their partner, without seeing it, produces a different rule that fits every term revealed so far, however many terms they are given. The partner always wins, and I want them to feel that. Since the discussion should not look like a fact about arithmetic, include at least one non-numerical parallel case in which the continuation of a pattern of shapes or of the application of an ordinary predicate is at issue.

Constraints. No lecture before the activity, and the words Kripke, Wittgenstein, quus, grue, sceptical solution, indeterminacy and rule-following paradox are not used until the final eight minutes. The cards must be described honestly as responses collected in another seminar rather than passed off as this class's own. The facilitator may never assert that there is no fact of the matter: if that claim arrives, a student has to say it and the others have to push back on it. The final poll should be designed to discriminate three positions that are easy to run together — there is a determinate fact about which rule was meant and we merely cannot tell which; it is indeterminate which rule was meant, though something weaker is still true; and there is no fact here at all, so that asking which rule was meant is not asking a question with an answer waiting to be found — and the debrief should say what each answer commits the student to and which of the three the activity itself can support.

What I need from you: the rules and materials; a minute-by-minute timetable for the eighty minutes; the debrief, mapping each moment of the session onto the claim it is supposed to make visible and marking, for each of the escape routes students will reach for — simplicity or naturalness, introspection, intentions, more data, community agreement — where in the timetable it arises, what it would cost to take it, and what is wrong with taking it there; an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, with a fix for each; and a frank account of which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce and which the facilitator must therefore say out loud. On that last point I take it that the normativity claim — that meaning fixes what one *ought* to answer, not merely what one will — is the hardest thing to stage, since the experience the activity produces is naturally described as not being able to tell, and I would like you to say how to convert that into the stronger claim without simply asserting it.
