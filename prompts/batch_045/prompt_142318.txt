So there was a cookout on sunday and two of my friends spent most of the afternoon arguing about whether a hypocrite can blame someone, and somehow I got nominated to settle it. I have no idea, and I mostly just want them to stop texting me about it.

The case they were arguing over: our friend Marisol bailed on a group trip two days before, and it cost people money they couldn't get back. Ted — who is, everyone agrees, chronically unreliable himself, same exact thing, has done it repeatedly and laughs about it — sent her a long angry message saying she'd let everyone down and owed them an apology. Marisol replied that he was one to talk. So: does Ted have standing to blame her? Here's how the argument went.

1. Wes: No. You can't hold someone to a norm you openly blow off yourself. Ted forfeited whatever right he had to complain about flakiness the first time he flaked and didn't make it right.

2. Dara: Two separate things are getting mixed together. Whether Marisol did something wrong is one question; whether Ted is the one who gets to say so is another. Ted's record doesn't touch the first at all — she still cost people money. And if hypocrisy killed standing, basically no one could ever blame anyone, because everyone violates some norm sometimes, and you'd end up with total silence about everything. It also lets Marisol dodge for a reason that has nothing to do with her, which feels like the opposite of what blame is for.

3. Wes: The "everyone does it" thing is way too fast. Hypocrisy isn't failing some norm somewhere; it's failing the very norm you're invoking, repeatedly, without ever acknowledging it. And I'm not saying his complaint is false. I'm saying blame isn't a description, it's a move — a normative power. A judge who takes bribes can say truly that you broke the law; he still can't sentence you. Ted is invoking a standard as if he has authority under it while quietly exempting himself from it. That's freeloading on a norm.

4. Dara: The judge analogy is carrying more weight than it can. A judge has a role and coercive power behind him, and a bribed judge's judgment is actually corrupted. Neither is true of Ted: his flakiness doesn't distort his view of what Marisol did one bit. And "freeloading" needs a shared burden being shirked. Ted isn't benefitting from Marisol's reliability while skipping his own share — he's just inconsistent. So tell me what exactly he's taking.

5. Wes: Fine, drop freeloading. Better version: blame is a move inside a relationship and it presupposes that the blamer accepts the norm as binding on people like him. What's objectionable about Ted's message isn't its content, it's the posture — I get to hold you to this, you don't get to hold me. Call it asymmetry. The message asserts a kind of authority over Marisol that he refuses to grant her over him.

6. Dara: I like that much better, but test it. If asymmetry is the problem, Ted can fix it in one sentence: "I know I do this too and I'm trying to stop — but you cost us money." Has he got his standing back? If yes, then the problem was never his past conduct at all, it was what the message expressed, and hypocrisy is just defeasible evidence of a bad posture. If no, your view is strangely unforgiving, since the acknowledgement changes nothing about his record.

7. Wes: Try two test cases. Self-blame: Ted writes in his notes "what I did to Marisol was shitty" while carrying on flaking. Nobody thinks he lacks standing to blame himself. So the forfeiture is essentially interpersonal — which is the asymmetry point, not a point about record. And norm-relativity: Ted, who lies constantly, blames someone for cruelty, or Ted the shoplifter blames someone for hitting their kid. Intuitively fine. So the forfeiture is tied to the specific norm he's invoking, which fits my story: it's about the relationship he's claiming under that norm.

8. Dara: Two cases you have to handle. Deputized blame: Ted's message wasn't "you wronged me," it was "you cost Nadia and Sam two hundred dollars" — he's speaking for people who are reliable. Does he borrow their standing? If he does, standing isn't a personal achievement at all and hypocrisy only bites in the two-person case. If he doesn't, you owe me a reason. Selective enforcement: Ted blames Marisol and says nothing to Dev, who flaked worse last month. That feels wrong in the same neighborhood as hypocrisy, but nothing in the asymmetry story covers it — his posture toward Marisol alone is perfectly symmetric. So your account is too narrow; whatever is off with Ted is a broader thing about invoking norms unevenly.

9. Wes: Last thing, and it's partly against myself: if the real problem is uneven enforcement, then the person Ted wrongs by staying quiet about Dev is Dev, not Marisol. Which suggests Marisol's "you're one to talk" isn't really an answer to the blame at all. So maybe the honest line is that Ted's blame is defective in some way, but not in a way that gives Marisol anything.

They both turned to me at that point. Please go through it move by move — rate each of the nine from clearly wrong to clearly right with a couple sentences of justification each — and then tell me who is more right overall. Also: are they even using "standing" to mean the same thing, or did they talk past each other? And what, if anything, are both of them missing?
