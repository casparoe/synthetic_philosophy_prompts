The question (25 marks in total)

Question 1(a) [10 marks]. Hume opens his essay on the standard of taste by juxtaposing two things: the apparently endless variety of taste among individuals, ages and nations, and the natural urge to seek a standard by which the sentiments of men may be reconciled. Set out this tension precisely, and explain the machinery he uses to resolve it: his denial that beauty is a quality of objects rather than something existing in the mind that contemplates them; the figure of the true judge and the qualities that make one; and the appeal to verdicts that endure across ages and nations. Say also whether, on his account, the standard is established a priori or discovered empirically, and why that difference matters to him.

Question 1(b) [12 marks]. In the seminar we distinguished three readings of what Hume's standard is supposed to be. On the evidentiary reading, the joint verdict of true judges is the best available evidence of a standard that is fixed independently of that verdict, by the general and uniform principles of human nature; the verdicts test for the standard rather than make it. On the constitutive reading, the verdicts of qualified judges are what fix the standard, and a work's beauty just is its disposition to produce a characteristic pleasure in judges of that kind. On the calibration reading, the standard is not a procedure for settling disputes between judges at all but a procedure of practice, comparison and removal of prejudice by which a judge corrects his own sentiments. Which reading, if any, best fits the text? And does the reading you favour support the claim that beauty is objective for Hume? In answering, keep separate the claim that there is a fact of the matter in judgments of taste, such that they can be correct or incorrect, from the claim that beauty is a mind-independent property of works.

Question 1(c) [3 marks]. Marks for structure, precision and economy.

The script

Hume's problem is that taste seems at once hopelessly variable and yet governed by something we want to call a standard. He observes that the variation is greater in reality than in appearance: everyone agrees in the abstract that elegance, propriety and simplicity in writing deserve praise and that fustian, affectation and coldness deserve blame, but once critics come to particulars this agreement vanishes. That is the opposite of what happens in matters of opinion and science, where the difference is usually verbal and an explanation of terms ends the quarrel. So the variety of taste is not merely verbal, and the search for a standard cannot be waved away.

Hume's answer is that the standard is not to be found in the object. Beauty is not a quality of things themselves; it exists in the mind that contemplates them. This remark should not be read as scepticism, however. It means only that beauty is not a simple observable feature, like shape or size. Properly understood, Hume holds that beauty is a disposition of the work to produce a certain kind of pleasure in suitably qualified judges: a real feature of the work, fixed by its own properties together with the uniform principles of human nature, and in that sense entirely objective. What varies is not the beauty of works but the competence of judges.

The true judge is the person equipped to detect it. Hume lists five qualities: strong sense; delicate sentiment, that is, the capacity to feel aesthetic pleasure with unusual intensity; practice; comparison; and freedom from all prejudice, particularly the prejudices of one's own age and country. Because such judges are rare, the only reliable test for who possesses these qualities is durability: the verdicts that have survived across ages and nations, as Homer's have, are the verdicts of true judges. Hence a critic who dissents from a durable verdict thereby shows that he lacks one of the five qualities, and the standard can never be upset by his disagreement. This is what lets Hume reject the proverb that there is no disputing about tastes: the joint verdict of true judges settles every dispute in taste, in principle.

Finally, the standard is not established a priori. Hume insists that it is discovered empirically, by experience and induction over the verdicts of critics, in the way one arrives at any generalisation from observed regularities. On this reading — the evidentiary one, since durable agreement is offered as evidence of the standard rather than as what constitutes it — Hume turns out to be an objectivist about beauty, and his essay is best read as an early version of the ideal-critic theory.
