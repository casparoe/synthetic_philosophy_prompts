ok so me and my friend Priya got into a huge fight a few weeks ago — she told our whole group project chat that I'd bailed on the presentation and let them go up without me, which wasn't true, and I was properly angry, like the kind where you replay it in your head at 3am and compose texts you never send. Then I found my brother's old philosophy reader from his first year of uni and there was this essay in it by P.F. Strawson, "Freedom and Resentment," which was read at the British Academy in 1962 apparently, and the bit I keep thinking about is where he says that resentment and gratitude and guilt and indignation aren't really beliefs you could argue someone out of, and that you can also step back into what he calls the objective attitude toward a person — the stance you take toward a little kid or someone who's having a breakdown, where you explain and manage and maybe feel pity, but you don't blame. Our teacher (I'm doing a philosophy thing on saturday mornings, its basically a club) said the interesting part isn't the determinism argument that everyone repeats, it's that both stances are things we *do* in relationships, not theories we hold, and that the objective attitude is normally local — you take it toward this person about this thing — rather than a global policy you could switch on for everyone.

So the story I want is set in a high school that has an official thing called the Accounting, which works like this: if someone hurts you, you can request a written dossier about them that's compiled by a counselor and is guaranteed complete and honest — family stuff, meds they were on, how much sleep they'd had, what pressures were on them that month, the whole causal history of how they ended up doing what they did. Whoever was wronged reads it and is supposed to come out the other side in the objective attitude, with the resentment gone. Here's the wrinkle I want you to actually work with instead of dodging: the person who *did* the thing gets handed their own dossier at the same time and reads it too. So both of them have exactly the same causal information about the same person. I don't think the resentment and the guilt dissolve in the same way, or at the same speed, or for the same reasons, and I want the story to dramatize that asymmetry rather than just have a character explain it — I can already hear a philosophy kid in the story saying "resentment is a reactive attitude lol" and that's the version I do not want.

No moral at the end, no author's note, no paragraph afterwards explaining what it was about — I want the story to carry it on its own. Hard limit: under 600 words.
