I am a novelist, and I have agreed — before I had worked out whether I had anything to say — to give the inaugural lecture of a series founded in memory of my friend Miriam Halloway, a historical sociologist who died last spring after roughly forty years of arguing that culture is never a cause. Her standing line, repeated in nearly every seminar she ever taught, was that if you cannot say what would have happened differently without the idea, you have described a costume and not a cause. She left a note asking that the series open with someone who would argue with her rather than eulogize her, that the word legacy not be used, and that no one say she is watching over anything.

I need the lecture written: twelve minutes, about 1,400 to 1,500 words.

The difficulty is that I am the wrong person to give it and also, I think, the right one. Miriam read my three novels — about one family across four generations, Huguenot silk-weavers who leave for London in the 1680s, a merchant house in the next century, a mill town in the next — and liked them while denying their premise. In each book, a generation's decisive choice is made in explicitly doctrinal language: a refusal to lend at interest to a coreligionist, a decision to reinvent a failing workshop rather than sell it, a daughter sent into the mill rather than kept at home. Miriam's charge was that in every case I had made a belief do the work while an interest was already doing it, and that my novels were therefore a particularly seductive version of the thesis she spent her career opposing — the one associated with Weber about the spirit of capitalism and ascetic Protestantism, and with the vaguer, more comfortable claim that ideas steer history.

I do not want the lecture to refute her, and I do not want it to pretend to agree with her. What I want it to do is answer her challenge with the only instrument I actually have. When I write, I test a scene by drafting it twice: once with the doctrine in the character's mouth and once with it removed. What I have found, and what I want the lecture to say honestly, is that the outward behavior survives the deletion — the same ledger entry, the same refusal, the same daughter on the same road — and that what changes is the description under which the act is done, and above all whether it repeats. A motive that arrives once lapses; a motive the character can name to themselves in a borrowed vocabulary holds for forty years.

So the lecture should build its argument on a three-way distinction I would like it to sharpen rather than assert: an idea as motor, supplying a desire that was not there before; an idea as switch, selecting among desires already present; an idea as grammar, supplying the vocabulary in which an action becomes describable, and so rehearsable, transmissible, and steady across a life. Miriam's counterfactual test, I want to argue, is well designed to expose motors and switches and has nothing to say about grammar, because deleting a vocabulary does not delete any behavior — it only leaves the behavior unnameable and therefore, in my experience, temporary. My own novels can only ever do the third thing; they cannot make anyone want anything new, and if they do anything at all it is to hand a reader a description under which some stretch of their own life becomes steady. The question I want put to the room, and left genuinely open, is whether the third thing is enough for Weber's thesis — whether the thesis needed ideas to supply new desires at all, or only to make a pattern of life durable enough to be recognized as a pattern — and whether a cause that fixes only the description of an act is a cause or an elaborate way of redescribing one.

Requirements for the text. The audience will be split: roughly half Miriam's colleagues, former students, and people from economic history and the sociology of religion who will catch every reference to Weber, elective affinity, ideal types, and interpretive understanding, and who will be listening for whether I have understood her position at its strongest; the other half will be my readers and people from the town who have never heard of any of it and must not be talked down to. Do not begin with I am honored or with a statement of the thesis in the first paragraph; earn the right to speak, since part of the room thinks novelists should not hold opinions about causation, and address that directly. Do not resolve the disagreement by saying that both of us were partly right, and do not claim that she would have come around. No citations are needed and no quotations from Weber beyond naming the spirit of capitalism — I do not want invented passages attributed to anyone. Where a concrete scene from my own drafts would do the work better than an abstraction, leave me a bracketed placeholder and a note on what the scene should show; I will fill it in. It should be affectionate without being a eulogy, and it should end somewhere true rather than consoling: I would like the closing move to hand her students her own test as a novelist uses it — go and delete your favorite variable from your own explanation and see what survives — and to leave them with that as something to do rather than something to feel.
