You are a historian of nationalism who also thinks like a philosopher of social kinds: you are as interested in what a thing is as in what it is called. Please work slowly and carefully here, and begin with an explicit brainstorming phase. Set out three or four candidate framings of the phenomenon I describe, say what each would capture and what it would silently drop, and only then commit to the one you think fits best, with reasons. The value of the answer is in the carving, not the label: I will not be satisfied with a name and a paragraph of definition.

I work as an independent consultant compiling nominations for a national inventory of intangible cultural heritage, and over about fifteen years of reading local archives I keep meeting the same shape of story. Three cases, all real ones from my files.

In the early 1930s a schoolteacher in a upland valley collected step-fragments from three elderly informants, none of whom could remember a complete dance. She stitched the fragments into a single suite, named it after a field-name she found on a tithe map, published it with notation in a small regional collection, and taught it to her pupils. In print she was entirely clear that the suite was her own arrangement. Ninety years later the dance is performed at nearly every wedding in the valley, residents describe it as the old dance of their valley, and the regional heritage office cites the 1933 booklet as the earliest surviving record of a practice assumed to be far older. The booklet is both the origin of the practice and the evidence for its antiquity.

In 1952 a museum reconstructed a bridal headdress from a single damaged original plus informed guesswork about the missing parts. The reconstruction was photographed, exhibited and published; the original was destroyed in a flood a decade later; local needlework circles copied the published photographs. Every surviving example today descends from the 1952 object. Their striking uniformity, which is entirely an artefact of that common descent, is now regularly offered as proof that the headdress was once governed by strict local rules.

A village revived a midwinter custom in 1978 after a gap of roughly sixty years, working from a description in a 1901 antiquarian journal. The 1901 description was itself assembled from notes about two different villages elsewhere in the county. The custom is now the village's signature event, appears in the regional tourism material, and the sixty-year gap is not part of the story anyone tells.

There is also a pattern in how these spread. Neighbouring districts, having seen a rival's signature custom on regional television or in a funding application, typically produce one of their own within a decade. Because the funding criteria reward long-standing practice, the nominations that succeed are the ones whose paper trail is thinnest or most conveniently dated.

I have read a little of the standard literature on invented traditions and on nations as imagined communities, and neither seems to be quite this. The invention-of-tradition story, as I have absorbed it, is about institutions fabricating ceremony from above for purposes of legitimation. Here the stitching-together is done by people trying to rescue something they believe is dying, usually for love and usually at their own expense; the fabrication is normally avowed at the time in the very document that later becomes the proof of antiquity; and the false belief, where there is one, arrives a generation later and belongs to nobody in particular. And the imagined-community story seems to be about breadth — strangers who will never meet feeling themselves simultaneous, and the media that make that possible — whereas what keeps catching my attention is a claim about depth, about how far back a thing goes, and about how the machinery of documentation supplies the very thing it then certifies.

The features I would want any adequate concept to preserve are these. First, the loop: the act of collecting, reconstructing or reviving produces the practice, and the practice then supplies the evidence for the depth it was recovered on behalf of. Second, the reclassification: a document that is an origin gets read, one or two transmissions later, as a mere source — the earliest surviving record in a series rather than the first item full stop. Third, the evidential inversion: the absence of earlier documentation is treated as supporting the antiquity claim rather than undermining it, on the ground that everyone simply knew these things. Fourth, genuine downstream uptake: these practices now organise the calendar, mark marriages and bind families to places, and are transmitted the way traditions are transmitted. Fifth, borrowing across the boundary: gaps are filled with material imported from somewhere else, and the imported material is then re-exported as local. Sixth, competitive diffusion driven by the institutions that recognise traditions.

So my questions. Does this configuration, or the loop at its centre, have an established concept attached to it? If the nearest thing is a family of neighbouring notions — survival, revival, recovery, second-hand folklore, constructed authenticity, retrospective continuity, whatever you think belongs on that list — then tell me which member fits best and precisely what each of the others leaves out. If nothing fits, coin a term, define it, and give me its boundary: what is in, what is out, and what the near-misses are. In particular I want to know whether a case in which the collectors really were recording something old and continuous should count as the same phenomenon, given that you often cannot tell the two apart by inspecting the practice itself; and I want to know what evidence would discriminate them in principle, given that the absence of evidence has already been reinterpreted as evidence. Finally, and this is the part that matters most to me in practice: given the fourth feature, does the verdict that a practice was assembled in 1933 actually carry the debunking force that both debunkers and defenders take it to carry, or is the origin question in some way beside the point?

Please do not pad the answer with reading lists or references: I am working from a rural archive with intermittent connectivity and cannot chase citations, though naming a concept and, where it helps, the sort of writer associated with it is fine.
