I've been banging my head agains this passage from Maxwell's nineteenth-century Theory of Heat and I need some help understanding what it's really committing him to. The passage is:

"... if we conceive of a being whose faculties are so sharpened that he can follow every molecule in its course, such a being, whose attributes are as essentially finite as our own, would be able to do what is impossible to us. For we have seen that molecules in a vessel full of air at uniform temperature are moving with velocities by no means uniform, though the mean velocity of any great number of them, arbitrarily selected, is almost exactly uniform. Now let us suppose that such a vessel is divided into two portions, A and B, by a division in which there is a small hole, and that a being, who can see the individual molecules, opens and closes this hole, so as to allow only the swifter molecules to pass from A to B, and only the slower molecules to pass from B to A. He will thus, without expenditure of work, raise the temperature of B and lower that of A, in contradiction to the second law of thermodynamics."

I'm not asking for citations or a reading list; I just want to understand the move Maxwell is making. Here's the part I keep going back and forth on. On one hand, the whole setup seems to be a point about our ignorance: we can't follow molecules, so for creatures like us the second law holds statistically, but a being with sharpened faculties could beat it. On the other hand, the being is still finite, so maybe Maxwell is only saying the second law isn't a strict dynamical law even for finite agents, not that it's merely psychological. The niche idea I've been toying with is that the small hole in the vessel isn't really a door at all, but more like a sampling aperture the demon uses only after it has already registered whether a molecule is swift or slow, so no work is done on the gas by the aperture itself; on my reading, the thermodynamic cost is displaced away from the gas and into the demon's need to keep track of which molecules it has already sorted, so the passage is pointing at an information bookkeeping problem rather than a mechanical one. Does the passage actually support that reading, or am I retrojecting a modern physics-of-information worry onto a nineteenth-century argument? I especially want to know whether Maxwell's stress on the being's finitude is there to head off the reply that only an infinite being could violate the second law, or whether it's meant to do some other work. I cant tell if I'm reading too much into the word finite here.
