Write a dialogue between two philosophers of science who work on long-run prediction, set at the end of a workshop where a demographer has just presented a model in which the Malthusian feedback between population and subsistence reasserts itself once cheap fossil-fuel-derived fertilizer and the high-yield agriculture it supports are gone. Priya defends the model's lineage: the mechanism Malthus described is real, and its failure to bite in the two centuries after he wrote is a matter of timing rather than of truth — the countervailing factor was a one-off resource windfall, not a refutation. Daniel holds that 'right but early' is normally an immunizing stratagem: it converts a prediction that failed into one that cannot fail, because any run of years in which the prediction does not come true can be redescribed as the period before it comes true.

Both should be equally competent, and the disagreement should be about substance rather than about who has read what. Daniel should concede that delay is not automatically rescue — a theory can be genuinely conditional on a state of the world that has not yet arrived — and should frame the real test as the one associated with Lakatos: whether a defense of this kind absorbs the anomaly by predicting something new that we did not already know, or merely by relabeling it. Priya should concede that in practice most 'right but early' claims are made post hoc, after the failure, and that this is the source of Daniel's suspicion.

Have them try to write a jointly acceptable prematurity protocol, and argue over its three clauses. First, indexation: the defender must name a measurable regime variable and the threshold at which the prediction is supposed to bite, on pain of having said only 'someday.' Second, a residue test: a mechanism that is real but currently masked should still be detectable where the mask is locally absent — in populations without access to synthetic fertilizer, or in closed systems such as island or pasture populations and laboratory microcosms. Daniel should press hard on whether this clause actually does work: does confirming the mechanism in closed subsystems support a claim about the global aggregate, or only show that the mechanism exists somewhere? Third, a sunset clause: a date or index value at which the claim is retired if the trigger has not fired, and an explicit statement of what observation would retire it earlier.

The dialogue should be roughly 700 to 900 words, should contain genuine back-and-forth in which each speaker answers the other's strongest point rather than restating a position, and should end with the two of them applying the protocol to the demographer's model, reaching different verdicts, and each saying which observation would move them to the other's verdict.
