I work on the team that maintains a monitoring system for a triage model deployed across a hospital network. The model assigns each arriving patient a priority score. The monitor does not only check the score; it tries to check how the score was reached. For each case it stores a trace: a decomposition of the computation into intermediate parts (roughly, the outputs of the model's internal components), together with which parts causally fed which, established by ablation. To decide whether two cases ran the same mechanism, we use what our team calls the Interface Test: line the two traces up part by part, and for each part splice the value from the other case into this one, holding everything else fixed; if the output stays within tolerance whichever way you splice — and stays within tolerance not only on the actual input but on a small neighborhood of similar inputs — then the parts are interchangeable, and if all of them are, the mechanisms are the same. A case is flagged when its trace fails the test against every trace in our library of reviewed, validated cases.

A colleague has built an argument that purports to show the whole enterprise is empty, and I cannot find the hole in it.

(1) The mechanism of a case is whatever causal structure in the model actually produced its output — the parts that did the work, the relations among them, and the order in which they acted. That is a fact about the case, not about us.

(2) We can only ever compare mechanisms by comparing descriptions of them, and the Interface Test is our best operationalization of "same causal structure": if every part of one case can stand in for its counterpart in the other without changing behavior, on the actual inputs and on similar ones, then nothing about the causal structure distinguishes them.

(3) The test's verdict is controlled by three settings that we choose and that the data does not settle: how finely the computation is carved into parts; the tolerance within which behavior counts as unchanged; and how wide the neighborhood of similar inputs is.

(4) The settings are not inert. Case 4717, a chest-pain presentation the monitor flagged, fails the test against the reference cases when parts are individual component outputs and the tolerance is tight; it passes when parts are whole stages of the computation and the tolerance is looser; and it passes again at the fine carving once the neighborhood is widened to all chest-pain presentations in the deployment log.

(5) For every pair of cases we have examined — including pairs we independently and confidently treat as the same mechanism, and pairs like 4717 that we independently and confidently treat as different ones — some settings make the pair pass and other settings make it fail.

(6) So for every pair of cases, whether they involve the same mechanism is settled by our choice of settings rather than by the cases themselves.

(7) A relation that holds or fails to hold depending on how we choose to describe things does not sort cases as they are; it sorts them as it suits us. It is bookkeeping, not discovery.

(8) Anomaly detection, as we practice it, is the detection of a difference in mechanism between the case at hand and the validated cases.

(9) So a flag never records anything about the case. It records a choice we made. On that reading the monitor is a device for keeping our notation consistent, not for catching anything.

(10) But that cannot be right. There were cases the monitor caught that everyone agrees were real failures: the model had begun routing its scores through a shortcut feature that encoded which hospital's formatting a record came from, and patients were being under-triaged because of it. That was not a difference in notation. We found it because the monitor flagged it, and we fixed it.

I would most like to keep (1), (8) and (10). I am willing to give up (2) if that is where the trouble is, but I do not know what to put in its place, and I do not want to end up having to say that the monitor is tracking something other than mechanism — that would change what we owe the clinicians who rely on it.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? In particular: if the break is at (6), I need to know what it would even mean for sameness of mechanism to be real and yet relative to a choice of grain, and how I would then decide which grain is the right one for this monitor. If the break is at (2), I need a criterion for "same mechanism" that does not collapse into the Interface Test's settings problem. Please name the step, explain why it looks innocent, and tell me what I am left having to accept once I give it up.
