I'm helping to organize a joint reading group between a philosophy-of-biology seminar and a vertebrate functional morphology lab, and we spent last term working through the 1979 spandrels paper and the replies to it. Two members — an optimality modeller who does reverse-engineering style work on feeding and digging systems, and a developmental-evolutionary biologist who works on skull integration and allometry — have been arguing for a year without converging, and they have now agreed to run it as an adversarial collaboration. I have been asked to draft the document and would like you to write it. The two positions are, roughly, the adaptationist (engineering-optimum-first, reverse-engineering, optimality-model) camp and the structuralist critic (constraint, developmental coupling, by-products, exaptation) camp.

I want you to build the whole exercise around a case I invented precisely because nobody in the group can look up how it comes out: the Kavango root-voles, an imaginary clade of fourteen described species of subterranean African rodents, with two outgroup genera. All fourteen species carry a dorsally projecting bony flange on the atlas vertebra, absent in both outgroups; its surface area tracks the area of the temporalis attachment on the braincase across the clade, and its growth is developmentally coupled to the expansion of that attachment surface. Some species dig by head-lift in hard, compacted soils; other species in the same clade live in soft sands and dig entirely with their forelimbs and never head-lift. The flange occurs in both, and its shape in the hard-soil species is well fit by a simple lever model of force transmission during head-lift, while in the soft-soil species it is present but smaller. Both camps can tell a story here: selection for a mechanical lever that is retained where it is no longer used, or a by-product of skull widening that happens to be available for later co-option. Please make the case do real work in the document — write it so that the two sides specify, in advance and in measurable terms, what each expects the comparative allometry, the ontogenetic sequence, the fossil or phylogenetic ordering of the trait relative to the shift to head-lift digging, an experimental ablation or knock-down in the lab, and the multivariate selection and G-matrix measurements in the field to look like, and what result would count against each of them. I am also interested in a formulation my group has been toying with and that I would like you to sharpen and use if it helps: think of the lineage's developmental system as defining a reachable set of phenotypes, and of the engineering analysis as locating an optimum; the interesting question is then whether the optimum sits inside or outside the reachable set, since if it sits outside, the trait should be found pressed against the boundary of what the body plan allows, and if it sits inside, a trait sitting away from the optimum needs a different kind of explanation. Alongside that, please separate the adaptationist programme into at least three theses the critic could be rejecting independently — something like an optimality-default thesis, a trait-atomism or modular-decomposition thesis, and a claim about what fraction of fixed phenotypic difference is owed to selection rather than to drift and near-neutral processes — and say which of them the critic actually rejects, which the adaptationist actually needs, and where the real dispute therefore lives.

The deliverable should be a document both of them could sign, not a verdict on who wins. Please give me: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims — ten to fifteen of them, each substantive and operational enough that a reader could tell whether it had been violated, so nothing like both selection and constraint matter, and covering at least what a trait explanation has to do, what counts as an alternative to a selection hypothesis, what the absence of a null model would cost either side, and what a by-product claim is a claim about; (2) a disagreements table whose columns are the crux, the adaptationist's own wording of her position on it, the critic's own wording of his, the specific measurement or comparison both accept as bearing on it, the adaptationist's prediction recorded in advance, and the critic's prediction recorded in advance; (3) a short closing section saying which parts of their dispute you take to be verbal rather than substantive, given that adaptation can be defined by historical genesis or by current utility and that a structure's present utility and its degree of craftedness do not by themselves settle how it originated, and which parts survive that disambiguation intact; and (4) an appendix laying out the pre-registered audit protocol for the root-vole case, including the order in which measurements are taken, what each side commits to before seeing them, and a stopping rule. Around 1,200 to 1,600 words, written in a register that would survive being read aloud to both of them. Please do not cite or quote any literature, and do not try to settle anything by appeal to who said what: the arguments have to stand on their own.
