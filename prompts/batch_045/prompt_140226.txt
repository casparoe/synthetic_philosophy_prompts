**Draft section: Not a recommendation to pay — the mugging as a failure of ordering**

*The set-up.* A stranger asks me for five dollars. If I refuse, he says, he will bring about 10^100 units of suffering; and he adds, unprompted, that the suffering will be produced somewhere I could never detect it, that no trace of it will ever reach me, and that I will never learn whether it occurred. Even if my credence in his story is as low as 10^-60, the expected disutility of refusing is 10^40 units, against a cost of five dollars. So expected-value maximisation appears to tell me to pay. The usual presentations treat this as a *recommendation* that is absurd, and ask which premise we should give up.

*The usual exits.* There are roughly four. First, bound the utility function, so that no outcome can be worth more than some finite ceiling and sufficiently small probabilities yield negligible expected gains. Second, apply a complexity-weighted, Solomonoff-style prior, so that credence in a hypothesis falls off at least as fast as the payoffs it postulates grow, and the product stays bounded. Third, note the rival claimants: there are indefinitely many possible muggers, including muggers who threaten to punish compliance, so paying does not dominate. Fourth, bite the bullet and pay.

*My claim.* The usual framing is wrong in a way that matters. The mugger's hypothesis is not merely improbable; it is, by construction, *evidence-inert*. Say that a hypothesis H is evidence-inert for an agent A iff there is no total evidential state E available to A — including every possible future observation, testimony, and record — such that A's credence in H conditional on E differs appreciably from A's unconditional credence in H. The mugger's threat is engineered to be evidence-inert: the suffering is stipulated to be undetectable, so neither its occurrence nor its non-occurrence would ever reach me. This is what distinguishes it from an ordinary threat, where refusal followed by no event is mildly confirming, and from an ordinary long shot, where I can investigate and will likely learn something either way. And it is the evidence-inertness, not the size of the number, that does the damage: the astronomical figure is decorative, there only to dominate whatever small weight the threat retains.

*The symmetry argument.* If an evidence-inert hypothesis is eligible to be weighed at all, then so is its mirror image. Consider the counter-mugger, who asks for five dollars on pain of bringing about 10^100 units of suffering *if and only if I ever pay a mugger*. The two hypotheses are structurally indistinguishable — equally evidence-inert, equally costly to state, postulating equal magnitudes — so any weighting scheme that admits one must admit the other with comparable weight. But now the expected value of paying and of refusing each contains a positive and a negative tail of the same order, and the difference between them is not large in either direction; it is undefined. The mugber's threat does not yield a recommendation to pay. It destroys the ordering over options. Fanaticism, on this view, is the symptom; the collapse of the ranking is the disease.

*Two ways of taming the sum.* Notice that the divergence above comes from the cardinality of the hypothesis space and the multiplicity of rivals, not from the size of any single payoff. If utilities are bounded *and* the weights over rival hypotheses are summable, the sums converge and the ordering is restored. There are accordingly two quite different devices for restoring it. The first, standard device makes the *weights* collapse fast enough — this is the complexity prior. The second makes most of the rivals *ineligible*. I want to explore the second, because the first carries a cost I find under-discussed: a complexity-weighted prior requires a choice of description language or reference machine. That choice is arbitrary up to a multiplicative constant, which is harmless for convergence but means that the actual number on which my refusal to pay is supposed to rest is partly fixed by a convention.

*The condition.* Let a hypothesis be *deliberation-eligible* for A only if it is not evidence-inert for A. This is not the claim that the hypothesis has probability zero, nor the factual claim that it is false. It is a structural rule about what may enter the ranking of options, and I think it should be held as a rule about deliberation while remaining agnostic about the truth of what it excludes.

*What it gets right.* Ordinary long shots stay eligible, because evidence can bear on them. So does the mugger who first demonstrates genuine power — he has bought credibility the way everyone else does, by producing evidence, and he is then handled by ordinary updating. And Pascal's wager is correctly classified as a mugging, which I take to be the right result rather than an embarrassment.

*Three worries I have not solved.* The first is that evidence-inertness looks gradational rather than binary, and I do not know how to grade the condition without losing the clean filter. The second is that the condition seems to make rationality depend on the reach of the agent's evidence space, and hence on facts about the agent rather than about the world. The third is the meta-mugging: the stranger can retarget his threat at my rule rather than my action — if you would dismiss me as evidence-inert, I will bring about the suffering anyway. My answer is that the retargeted hypothesis is itself evidence-inert and therefore excluded, but I am unsure whether that is a reply or a dodge.

**What I would like from you:**

1. *Accuracy about the dialectic.* Have I characterised any of the four standard replies unfairly or in a way that makes it easier to dismiss than it deserves? In particular: is it true that bounding the utility function restores an ordering only when combined with summable weights over rival hypotheses, or does the bound do the whole job on its own?

2. *Is the central move new work or a repackaging?* I am most worried that the Reciprocity Condition collapses into the complexity-penalty reply, or into the rival-claimants reply, or into a bounded-utility view. If it collapses into one of them, tell me which, and tell me whether the repackaging has any advantage — for instance, whether it avoids the reference-machine arbitrariness while achieving the same convergence.

3. *The strongest single objection.* Not a list: the one objection you think is most likely to be fatal, developed properly. I would be especially interested in your view on whether the condition illicitly converts an epistemic limitation — I cannot specify the evidence — into a normative exclusion, and on whether a Bayesian should simply reject any rule that removes hypotheses from the algebra rather than assigning them small probability.

4. *The meta-mugging.* Is my answer to it a reply or a dodge? Work through whether a threat aimed at the agent's decision rule escapes the condition, and whether that reveals an instability in holding the condition as a rule about deliberation rather than as a belief.

5. *Scope.* If the condition is accepted, how much of ordinary long-shot reasoning does it take with it? Consider a case where the stakes are huge, the probability is small, and the decisive evidence would arrive only after the fact or never. Does my argument generalise into a sceptical challenge to that kind of reasoning, and should I welcome that consequence or treat it as a reductio of the condition?

6. *Editorial judgement.* If you think the ordering-failure framing is the wrong headline and I should lead with the condition instead, say so and say why. And if you think the whole approach is on the wrong track, say that plainly and sketch what you take the right track to be.
