Bit of an odd one, but here goes. I'm retired — thirty-odd years as a hydrologist with the state water agency, so I have written more levee reports than I care to remember, and the last two years I've been dabbling in philosophy on my own (social epistemology mostly, badly). That is apparently why I'm on the city's Commemoration Committee, which I'm not sure was a good idea.

The case in front of us. The Flats was a mill-worker neighborhood, mostly immigrant renters, wiped out in the 1913 flood and never rebuilt. Since the 1970s the descendants have held a candlelight walk every June ending at a riverwalk plaque saying the city engineer opened the upstream gates on purpose to save downtown. Five years ago a retired engineer and a local historian went through the agency's gate log, the telegraph traffic, and the mill's insurance filings: the gates were closed and chained, the levee gave way below the Flats, and no order was ever issued. So the mechanism in the memory looks wrong. The catch — and this is what I can't get past — is that every surviving document was produced by the city, the mill, or a newspaper the mill owner's family controlled, while the Flats itself left almost nothing written down. But the documents do confirm the part the memory has always insisted on: downtown owners got compensated, Flats residents got nothing, and the site was then rezoned so it could not be rebuilt. The descendants' association now wants a permanent memorial and has a state heritage grant pending that requires the marker text to be historically accurate; the draft recieved with the application repeats the deliberate-breach story. The grant panel wants our recommendation in nine days.

So: draft the decision memo I can circulate. It needs a real bottom line — fund, fund with conditions, or decline — plus what the marker may and may not say, and reasons the descendants' association could actually answer rather than just a lecture on evidence. My own half-baked thought, which half the committee has latched onto, is that the plaque is the city asserting something while the walk is the community doing something to itself, and those don't have to say the same thing: the city says what the record supports, and the plaque records the memory as a memory — that this neighborhood has held for ninety years that the gates were opened on purpose, and why. Tell me straight if that's a dodge, and if it is, what a non-dodge would look like, because I genuinely can't tell anymore. Give the loosing side its best shot too — the people who think deferring to documents written by the city just repeats the original silencing. And give us a sentence or two of a standard we could apply to the next case, because we have three more queued up and I'd rather not have this argument from scratch each time.
