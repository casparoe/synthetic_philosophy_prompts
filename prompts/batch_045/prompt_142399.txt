hey — I co-run a small interpretability reading group (about a dozen people, half mech interp engineers who think the question is settled, half philosophy-of-mind types who think it hasn't been stated properly), and we have the same argument every session: when someone points at a direction in activation space or at a sparse-autoencoder feature and says "this is the model's representation of X," are they claiming anything stronger than "X is predictable from the activations"? I want to sort people instead of arguing with them, so please build me a decision tree.

What I need:

A tree that starts from the commitment "calling this feature the model's representation of X means something more than 'X can be read off the activations'" and ends at a named position. Eight branch nodes maximum. Every node should be one claim that splits the space, written as a question a person could answer without already knowing which position it leads to — so no "do you think the model really understands X," no "are you a realist about features," nothing that just asks for the label.

The diagnostic cases should do the real work. Use at least three of these as nodes and the rest to illustrate leaves:
1. The vestigial feature: a direction that predicts X perfectly, clusters cleanly, and even lets you reliably steer the model's output toward X, but intervention experiments show the model's own processing never routes through it — causally inert on the path that actually produces behaviour, like an appendix.
2. The chameleon: a feature that really is used by the circuit in-distribution, but under a mild distribution shift the model switches to a completely different, nonlinearly scrambled encoding of the same thing, while the chameleon keeps cheerfully lighting up.
3. Seed divergence: two models, same architecture, same data, different init. Model A has a clean linear direction for X; model B encodes X in a way no linear probe or dictionary feature recovers, though a nonlinear probe pulls it out fine. Is one of them missing the representation, and does it matter that they'd behave identically?
4. The wordless feature: a direction tracking a real, dense statistical regularity in the training distribution that no human concept and no English phrase covers.
5. The phlogiston feature: a direction tracking a "kind" that our best science says doesn't exist but that is perfectly coherent across the training text.

Leaves: at least six, and these have to be in there — features-as-real-causal-units realism; a functional-role or causal-abstraction view on which being a representation is a matter of which interventions preserve behaviour; the "if it lets you compress and predict, it's as real as anything needs to be" real-patterns style position; a deflationary or illusionist leaf saying the feature is a description we impose rather than a thing we discover; and a leaf for the view that no empirical result whatsoever — not a perfect interchange-intervention test, not a completed causal-scrubbing pass over the whole model, not cross-model universality results — could ever settle the question, because the disagreement is upstream of evidence. Name each leaf, give it one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and mark which ones a working interp engineer would actually sign onto versus which are basically only held by philosophers.

Two more things. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't genuinely seperate rather than pretending the last question did the work. And list the combinations of answers your eight questions allow that nobody actually holds, with a one-line diagnosis each — incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

The reason I want a tree rather than an essay: what I really care about is which positions are committed to saying some future empirical result would settle this, and which ones say the disagreement lives upstream of any possible evidence. If a leaf has that consequence, say so at the leaf.

Format it as something I can print on one page, plain English questions, labels only at the leaves.
