I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I am putting together the final assessment for a short internal course I run at work on long-horizon technical decisions: when to pay down infrastructure debt, how long to keep archives alive, whether to fund migration work whose payoff is decades out. The people sitting it are engineers, not philosophers. The format is a thirty-minute closed-book essay worth twenty marks, and I will be marking it myself, so the rubric has to be usable by a non-specialist.

What I need

One essay question on pure time preference and discounting the future, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The crux I want it to turn on

I do not want the version of this topic that everyone can recite. Specifically, please do not write a question about hyperbolic versus exponential curves, about present bias being irrational, or about whether people are inconsistent across time. Assume throughout a single constant, time-consistent discount rate, and put all of the pressure on two things: why the rate is nonzero at all, and how, even in principle, you would tell its components apart.

Here is the material I want the question to force candidates into. Use all of it or restructure it, but please do not quietly drop the difficult parts.

1. Decomposition. An observed discount rate is usually doing several jobs at once: a genuine preference for the nearer, the chance that the beneficiary or the project will not survive, and the fact that later recipients may be better off so that the same resource does less for them. Ask the candidate to prise these apart and to say which component is doing the normative work. I half-remember a formula of the shape rate = pure time preference plus a growth term plus a hazard term, and I may well be mangling it; do not make the question depend on any such formula and do not put one on the page. If a candidate happens to know it, fine, but reciting it should earn almost nothing.

2. A diagnostic rather than a mere distinction. This is the part I care about most. If a team says we discount at five per cent a year, that is equally consistent with real time preference and with a five per cent annual chance that the project is cancelled or the beneficiaries are not there. I want candidates asked what would in principle distinguish the two: for instance, how the same agent should value a future benefit conditional on the project surviving or on the person being alive at the time, and whether that valuation should jump once the agent learns they survived. If that test is not actually decisive, I want the question to invite the candidate to explain why not.

3. Double counting. In the cases I actually face, the intervention changes the hazard itself: spending now lowers the chance that the thing is lost. Applying the same rate both to the benefit and to the risk reduction looks like counting one consideration twice. Candidates should have to say what the correct treatment is and why.

4. The residual normative question. Once survival risk and differential growth are switched off by stipulation, a fixed closed population with no growth and no extinction risk, whatever preference for the earlier benefit remains looks like a preference for a bare position in time. Ask whether that is defensible, and make them engage the argument that temporal position is morally like spatial position, since we do not think distance from us reduces the badness of a distant stranger's suffering. They should also handle the obvious objections: that time is traversed by the agent in a way space is not, that memory and anticipation are asymmetric, and that we do seem to discount by distance in some rescue cases. Ask them to treat the same-person case separately from the different-person case: whether preferring the good years early within a single life is a time preference at all, or a preference about the shape of a life.

5. Scale sensitivity. Any constant positive rate, however small, drives the weight of the far future to nearly nothing. Candidates should notice this and say whether it is a reductio of nonzero time preference or merely an uncomfortable implication to be accepted.

One more requirement: the question has to force a stand. It should not be possible to write a first-class answer that only surveys considerations and hedges. Somewhere the candidate must commit to a claim and defend it against the strongest objection they can construct themselves.

A possible confusion in my framing

I suspect my own setup may be shaky: if every bit of observed discounting can be re-described as survival risk or as differential growth, then pure time preference is just whatever is left over, and saying it ought to be zero may be a stipulation rather than an argument. If that is muddled, or if the framing above rests on some other mistake, say so in a short note of about a hundred words before the question, and then write the best version of the question anyway.

Constraints

- The question must be self-contained: no assigned readings, no names or labels a candidate would need to have memorized, every term of art defined in the question itself, and any numbers needed supplied on the page.
- No citations, references, footnotes, or suggested reading anywhere, in the question, the model answer, or the scheme. I cannot check them and I do not want them.
- Written for engineers: plain and concrete, no unexplained jargon, but do not lower the difficulty to compensate.
- It must be impossible to answer well by reproducing a summary of the topic. Label-dropping and formula-reciting should score very poorly.

Deliverables, in this order

1. The short note flagging any confusion in my framing, if there is one.
2. The question exactly as it should appear on the paper, including any scenario and numbers, with a stated time limit and an expected answer length.
3. A model answer of 700 to 900 words.
4. A twenty-mark marking scheme: the specific moves an excellent answer must make, the marks each is worth, a one-line note on how people typically get each one wrong, and an explicit statement of what earns zero, for example simply asserting that discounting is obviously irrational or obviously fine, or conflating a discount on value with a discount on probability.
5. Three or four lines on where the first-class versus upper-second boundary falls, so I can mark consistently.
