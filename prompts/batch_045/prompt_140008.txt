Hi — I'm putting together a diagnostic handout for my second-year seminar on truth and disagreement (a mixed bag: about 22 students, a third of them only there for the gen-ed requirement, three or four religion majors who already know more about Jain philosophy than I do, and one guy who's read some contemporary work on truth and now thinks every dispute is just a switch of domain). The unit is on many-sided truth — the Jain anekāntavāda material: the idea that no unqualified assertion gets reality right, the habit of prefixing claims with an "in some respect" qualification, the seven-mode scheme (roughly: it is / it is not / it both is and is not / it can't be said / and the three ways of conjoining "can't be said" with the earlier ones), and the thought that only a fully enlightened being occupies the total standpoint from which nothing is left out. My colleague in religious studies keeps telling the students it's just relativism with incense, and I want something that makes them see it as one option inside a logical space rather than the obvious place to stop.

What I'd like from you is a classification instrument I can actually hand out: eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that sorts answer *patterns* into named positions. The point is that they should end up somewhere because of what they say about cases, not because they like a label. So: no question may contain the words relativism, relativist, pluralism, perspectival, standpoint, many-sided, objective, subjective or their cognates, and none may ask anything in the neighbourhood of "is truth relative?" or "is there one true description of reality?". Please build at least these four cases in (add your own as needed): a clay pot that's just been smashed — its matter is all still there, is it still a pot?; two honest eyewitnesses at a demonstration, one reporting that the police were violent and the other that they showed restraint; a table that a physicist describes as mostly empty space and a carpenter as solid; and a knife that's excellent for bread and useless for filleting fish. At least two questions need to be able to seperate the student who answers "both" from the student who answers "neither, and it can't be put into words" — and those two had better not be the same question in a different costume.

Build the taxonomy from the logical space first: give me the branching questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then hang the actual positions on the leaves, and tell me about any combination nobody occupies (empty because incoherent, because unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered to defend it?) and about any pair of leaves your tree can't seperate — flag them instead of pretending the tree is finer-grained than it is. The distinctions I most want carved are these. (1) Is the conflict in the object — reality really does have mutually incompatible aspects — or in us, in what we can know or say? (2) Is there even in principle a standpoint that isn't partial, one an omniscient being could occupy and we can't, or does the partiality go all the way up? (3) Can one and the same sentence be true and false in exactly the same respect, or does every admissible pair of conflicting claims differ in some respect we haven't specified? (4) Does the "in some respect" operator scope over the whole sentence or over something inside it — is it a real operator on the claim, or shorthand for an unstated parameter you could in principle fill in? (5) Does the doctrine apply to itself: is "every claim is true only in some respect" itself true only in some respect? And (6), when someone takes the "can't be said" option, are they saying there's a determinate state of affairs our language can't reach, or that nothing settles the matter? I've been using two rough tests in lecture and you can fold them into the questions if they help: the add-a-clause test (can each disputant accept the other's sentence once a qualifying clause is bolted on? if so, the quarrel was about a dropped parameter) and the omniscience test (what would a being with the total standpoint say — one of the two sentences, both of them under qualification, or nothing at all?). Leaves I definitely want available: the Jain view itself, with the seven-mode scheme sitting inside it as a leaf rather than as the trunk; framework relativism; the view that truth has a different underlying nature in different domains of discourse; plain monism — one complete description plus our ignorance of it; a contradiction-tolerant option; a deflationary or quietist "the dispute is merely verbal" option; and one or two ineffability options. Add more if the space has room for them. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I've got that side covered — I just want the structure, and a short note on how you'd actually run the thing in a 75-minute session.
