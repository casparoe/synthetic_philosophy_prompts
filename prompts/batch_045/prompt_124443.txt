I am applying for a twelve-month dissertation-completion fellowship (no teaching, single institutional affiliation, deliverable: one substantial paper plus a shorter companion piece), and I need the research-proposal section drafted: roughly 1,200 words, formal in register, addressed to a review panel that will contain both philosophers of science and working physicists. The area is non-empirical theory assessment in the string-landscape and multiverse case, and I would like you to turn a half-formed idea of mine into a proposal that a panel would actually fund.

My half-formed idea: the debate over whether non-empirical considerations can confirm a theory has stalled because both sides agree on the *output* variable. Both suppose that what such arguments are supposed to produce is a raised credence in the theory — defenders arguing that a Bayesian reconstruction makes the raise legitimate, critics arguing that evidence without data is a contradiction in terms, or at any rate that nothing tested has been confirmed. I suspect the interesting work these arguments can do is not credence-raising at all, and that the standoff dissolves once we distinguish three things an argument might deliver: (A) raising an individual's credence that the theory is true or approximately true; (B) narrowing the range of *rationally permissible* credences across a community — reducing legitimate disagreement — without much moving any individual's own credence; and (C) altering the expected value of further investment in the research programme, given that the alternatives are also untestable at present. My reason for taking (B) seriously is that the no-alternatives argument seems to me best read as a claim about the size of the live option set rather than about the probability of the favoured option, and on that reading it is untouched by the 'bad lot' worry — that the best of a bad lot may still be bad — since on the (B) reading one never claimed the lot was good. The best-known recent defence of non-empirical assessment (Dawid's, as it happens) bundles the no-alternatives argument together with meta-inductive arguments from a research tradition's track record and arguments from unexpected explanatory coherence; but I do not want the proposal to become exegesis of one author's programme.

The domain-specific twist that I think lifts this above a taxonomy paper is the measure problem. If the difficulty of extracting a probability distribution over observations from an eternally inflating multiverse is not a temporary technical nuisance but something structural, then the theory assigns no determinate probability to anything we could in principle observe, and (A) looks not merely unsupported but arguably unstateable: Bayesian confirmation needs likelihoods, and here there are none, or too many. That matters because the standard *defence* of non-empirical assessment is that it is a provisional warrant, a placeholder for a test that will eventually arrive, and that its warrant is borrowed from a tradition of theories that were in fact eventually tested. That defence presupposes that there is something for the eventual test to test. So the cases where non-empirical assessment is most wanted may be precisely the cases where its usual justification — the meta-inductive thought — is least applicable, because the reference class is made of theories whose likelihoods were well-defined. The proposal's core question, then: is non-empirical assessment coherent only for what I have been calling *measure-definite* theories, and if so does it fall silent exactly where its defenders most want it to speak? Or can (B) and (C) survive the collapse of (A)?

What I need from you beyond the framing: state the question so that it admits a determinate answer; explain why the existing positions leave it open (my sense is that the critics' objection only bites against (A), and that neither side has a vocabulary for (B) or (C)); give a method that I could actually execute month by month over twelve months, including how I would reconstruct the three argument-types in a single formal framework so that they become different functionals of one model rather than three separate literatures, and including how I would select and use a historical case study of a theory that was supported on non-empirical grounds before it had well-defined empirical consequences; identify which sub-question is the crux — I think it is whether the measure problem is a problem about likelihoods or a problem about self-locating, indexical evidence, since the second may be soluble and would rescue (A) in imprecise-probability form; say what the deliverables would be and what the companion paper would argue for the physicists on the panel, who will want to know what follows for the 'is this still science?' question and for how a field should allocate its effort; and give an honest account of what would count as the project failing. I can think of several failure modes I want you to sharpen rather than dodge: that the three-way distinction does no work because (B) and (C) turn out to be (A) in different words, or (B) turns out to be a merely sociological claim with no normative force; that the cosmology literature converges on a measure during the fellowship and my central case evaporates; and that no suitable historical analogue exists, which would itself be a finding — that non-empirical assessment without likelihoods is historically novel, and that the appearance of continuity with past practice, on which the meta-inductive argument depends, is an illusion.

Two constraints. First, no bibliography and no citations: I will add those myself, and I would rather the panel not see me name-dropping, so refer to positions by their content rather than by author. Second, be realistic about scope and say so in the text — the obvious way for a twelve-month project on this topic to fail is for me to spend eight months doing cosmology instead of philosophy, so I want the proposal to show the technical work being capped and the philosophical questions remaining in view.
