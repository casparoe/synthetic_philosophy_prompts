I'm a clinical neuropsychologist on a small disorders-of-consciousness assessment team at a regional trauma hospital, and I'm trying to build something our team can actually run at the bedside. Two years ago my younger brother spent eleven weeks in a traumatic brain injury unit after a car crash, and what I remember most clearly isn't the medicine — it was sitting with my parents while a resident read out a note made of reflex names and numbers, and my mother asking, "but is he still in there?" and nobody being able to say what that question was even asking. Our notes are good at describing behavior and hopeless at saying what kind of mind, if any, the behavior evidences. We do two or three of these assessments a week, usually thirty to forty minutes at the bedside, run by a resident who rotates through monthly and has no philosophy or comparative psychology background; we have no scanner except when we can beg time on one, and the write-up goes both to the palliative-care team and, in plain language, to the family, and it may be quoted in decisions about levels of care. So whatever I end up with has to be usable by a rotating trainee in half an hour, has to be explainable to a frightened family in a sentence or two, and has to be honest about which questions it cannot close.

The material I want it built out of is the taxonomy of kinds of minds that runs Darwinian → Skinnerian → Popperian → Gregorian: as I understand it, Darwinian creatures are stuck with the wiring they were born with and can only get smarter across generations; Skinnerian creatures can be shaped within a lifetime by reward and punishment; Popperian creatures can generate candidate actions and let the bad ones die "in their heads" instead of in the world, so that a mistake costs a simulation rather than a life; and Gregorian creatures import tools other minds have already built — words, numbers, maps, rituals — and get smart in one lifetime largely by borrowing. I've seen this attributed to Dennett, in a book about the evolution of consciousness, but I may have the source wrong, and I'm not asking for references, citations, or reading lists: I know you can't look anything up, and I don't need them. What I want is a procedure: an ordered set of probes or questions, what would count as a sufficient positive answer to each one, where the procedure stops and returns "no evidence above this level," and where it has to hand the decision to a human being because the open question is one of values rather than facts — for instance how much weight to give a noisy signal when the stakes are a decision about life-sustaining treatment. Give it to me as something that could fit on two laminated pages. Three wrinkles I need it to handle. First, cultural scaffolding: a response that looks Gregorian may just be a Darwinian or Skinnerian creature being carried by a cultural prop — a name, a familiar voice, a rehearsed phrase — so I'd like a step that tries to strip the scaffolding away and checks whether the response survives, and a clear statement of the cases where it can't be stripped. Second, and this is the one I'm most worried about: I think our own testing may manufacture the evidence it is looking for. Our current habit is ten repetitions of the same command per session, every session, for weeks, and if repeated pairings can shape a response, then an improving score may be the assessment training the patient rather than the patient recovering. If your procedure implies that our repetition standard is actively corrupting the data, say so; I would rather be told than have it confirmed for me politely. Third, the taxonomy ranks kinds of learning and I don't think it answers my mother's question, so tell me exactly what the procedure can and cannot license us to say to a family.

Then run it on three cases. (a) A man of fifty-four, six months out from anoxic brain injury after a cardiac arrest: he reliably turns his head toward a sudden loud noise, the rate of responding is unchanged across three sessions, and he shows nothing to his own name. (b) A woman of twenty-two, three months out from a traumatic brain injury: at her first assessment she squeezed a hand on one of ten trials of "squeeze my hand if you can hear me"; six weeks later, after family and nurses have run the same command daily, she squeezes on eight of ten, and everyone on the ward is delighted. (c) A man of sixty-one who acquired English at nineteen and grew up in another language: on the very first presentation of a novel spoken instruction in English he responds appropriately, with no prior trials, but he does not track a hidden object and cannot solve a simple novel reaching problem; separately, we sometimes get instrument evidence that looks like command-following when the bedside exam shows nothing at all (I have a vague memory of reports in which apparently unresponsive patients were asked to imagine playing a sport and showed something on a brain scan — I can't remember who, and I'm not asking you to chase it). Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer my team will not want to hear, and one where it hands off rather than deciding.

Finally, be explicit about epistemic status: mark which steps rest on claims that are settled or near-uncontroversial among people who work on this material, and which rest on genuinely contested readings — for example whether the four kinds are strictly cumulative and ordered, whether the later kinds require the earlier ones underneath them as extra layers of generate-and-test, whether Gregorian status really requires language as opposed to any inherited artifact, and whether the taxonomy is a claim about evolutionary history or about types of learning that cut across species. Don't present a contested reading as the consensus one. And end by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do, since I would much rather hand a family an honest "we don't know" than a number that sounds more confident than it is.
