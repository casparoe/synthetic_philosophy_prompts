I am a features writer on the science-and-ideas desk of a British broadsheet, and I am drafting a 2,000-word piece on why the question of what holds a proposition together has proved so much more recalcitrant than it looks. I need the philosophical spine of the piece to be exact, so I am commissioning a short comparative essay from you before I write around it.

Here is the problem as I understand it, and you may correct me if I have it wrong. A judgement that Brutus stabbed Caesar is not a mere inventory: the items Brutus, stabbing and Caesar, set side by side, do not yet say anything of anything. Bradley's regress is the argument that adding the relation as a fourth item does not help, because the relation is now just one more thing standing alongside its terms, and we should need a further relation to tie it to them, and another to tie that one, without end — so that no proposition is ever actually unified and nothing is ever said to be the case.

Two ways out interest me, and I want them set against each other rather than summarised in turn.

The first is the Fregean one. On this view the trouble arises only if we model the relation as another object, on all fours with its terms. But the expression for a relation is, as Frege would put it, unsaturated: it comes with gaps, it is incomplete by its very nature, and objects do not need to be glued to it because they are what completes it. Unity is therefore not something extra that has to be supplied; it is the absence of anything left over.

The second is an account put to me by a philosopher I interviewed for the piece, who asks to be described only as defending what she calls the assembly account of unity. Her proposal is that unity is neither a further constituent nor a tie, but the residue of an operation. Think of a proposition, she says, as the terminal state of a procedure run over a stack: names and predicates push semantic values; a binary predicate pops two values, forms a single value from them, and pushes that back; at no point is there a completed array of items sitting there waiting to be bound together, because the binding is something the operation does as it goes. The regress, on her telling, is not stopped but rendered unmotivated: it only gets a grip if you first lay out the constituents as a manifold and then ask what relates them, and her procedure never produces such a manifold.

What I want from you is a comparison, not a survey. In particular:

Is her account genuinely immune to the regress, or does it relocate it? The operation of consuming two values and returning one looks suspiciously like a relation between the operation and its inputs, and one wants to know why that relation does not itself stand in need of a tie. Answer this directly, and say what she would have to add to her account to make the answer stick.

What does the assembly account do about propositions that nobody has assembled? If unity is the residue of a procedure, then it seems to become dependent on somebody's running it, on the order in which they run it, and on the notation they run it in. Say whether that is fatal or whether it can be turned into a virtue, and how the Fregean view fares on the same question.

Is the Fregean asymmetry between object and concept an explanation of unity, or a redescription of it with a technical label attached? I have had this objection put to me by more than one person, and I would like to know whether it lands, and whether the assembly account is exposed to the parallel charge of having redescribed the binding of a proposition as the execution of a procedure.

Finally, if you think the two views are closer than they sound — if both are, say, ways of denying the same premise, that a proposition is first a plurality of items and only afterwards a unity — then say so, and say what the disagreement that survives comes down to.

A few constraints. I cannot use a bibliography, a reading list, or citations: no one reading this piece wants footnotes, and I have no interest in who said what in which year, so please do not supply any, and please do not rest your argument on attributing claims to particular books or papers. Beyond Bradley, Frege and Russell, whom I have already named, avoid leaning on the details of any specific theorist's published position. Write for an intelligent reader with no formal training in philosophy: define every term of art you need, and do not leave any piece of jargon doing load-bearing work unexplained. Aim for 1,000 to 1,200 words, in British English, in continuous prose rather than headings and bullets.

Close with two things. First, a standfirst of no more than three sentences that I could adapt for the top of the piece, capturing why a puzzle about the architecture of a proposition should matter to a reader who has never heard of Bradley. Second, a single sentence telling me where you think my interviewee's account is most likely to break.
