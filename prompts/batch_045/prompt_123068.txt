A colleague from the cog sci department who's sitting in on my seminar next week circulated a short summary of Kim's causal exclusion argument for the students to read before the first session. I want to use it as the jumping-off point, but I'm not sure how much of it to trust — it isn't her area either, and she wrote it from memory. Here it is more or less verbatim:

'Kim's argument starts from the assumption that physics is causally complete: every physical event has a fully sufficient physical cause. Add the exclusion principle, that no event can have more than one cause, and the fact that mental properties supervene on but aren't identical to physical ones, and you've got a problem. My decision to raise my arm has a complete neural-physical cause, so if that neural event is already doing all the causal work, there's nothing left for the decision to do. Either mental properties are identical to physical properties or they're causally inert. Kim's own way out was to bite the bullet and accept that mental properties don't really exist as causally efficacious items, which is why the argument is usually taught as a route to eliminativism about the mind. Two caveats: the argument needs determinism, since without sufficient causes the exclusion principle never gets going, so quantum mechanics probably kills it; and the non-reductivist's usual move, that the mental causes things by being realized in the physical, is just double-counting the same event twice.'

I have a feeling something's off in a couple of places. The determinism bit in particular sounds too easy to me, but I also find it kind of attractive, so I don't trust myself on it. And I remember Kim as a reductive physicalist who wanted to save mental causation by reducing, not as someone who gave up on the mind — but it's been fifteen years and I may be misremebering.

So: go through it and tell me what someone who works on this would sign off on, what's a fair classroom simplification, and what's just wrong. I'd also like to know whether the mistakes all push in the same direction — does this summary make the argument look stronger or more dramatic than it actually is?

And the part I really care about: I don't want to run the argument on a mental case at all in session one, because then everyone's priors about consciousness start doing the work. Instead I want to run it on a keycard. A hotel door unlocks when a card carrying the right authorization code is swiped; in this hotel having that code happens to be perfectly correlated with one particular magnetization pattern on the stripe, and the latch retracting has a complete microphysical causal story. Does the card's being authorized cause the door to unlock, or is it excluded? Does my colleague's framing survive that swap, or does something in it quietly depend on the case involving a mind — and if so, does de-mentalizing it make the argument stronger or weaker? One of my grad students is going to bring up the program-explanation reply from the 90s, so tell me where that bites on the keycard case too.
