I'm in my final year of high school, I do competitive debating, and a few months ago an argument about whether expensive hobbies and public moral stands are really costly signals sent me down a rabbit hole I haven't climbed out of. My economics teacher lent me a couple of things on signalling theory, and since then I've been trying and failing to find a word for a distinction I keep needing. Roughly: it looks to me as if the same phrase is being used for three or four different machines, and I can't tell which of them are genuinely the same machine in different clothing.

The one I understand best is the sorting machine: a display is expensive to produce, the expense falls more heavily on individuals who lack whatever quality the receiver cares about — condition, resources, ability — so that a worse individual would be worse off faking it than not, and the receiver, by attending to the display, ends up learning about a trait the signaller already had. The peacock's train is the stock example and it seems right. But I keep seeing two other things called by the same name. First, cases where the expense doesn't seem to reveal anything about who the signaller is right now and instead changes what it will be rational for the signaller to do later: someone makes a loud, public, hard-to-undo sacrifice — resigns in a way that can't be walked back, hands over something they can't get back, destroys the option they would need in order to defect — and the cost looks like it is doing its work on the signaller's own future incentives rather than on the receiver's picture of the signaller's present traits. Second, cases where the cost is real but flat, the same for everyone who performs the behaviour at all — a fee, a uniform, an entry requirement — so that it cannot possibly tell the receiver which individual it is dealing with, and yet people still say the cost is what makes the behaviour honest. And then, confusing me further, there are displays that look unfakeable for reasons that have nothing to do with expense at all: I've read that a large male frog's call is lower-pitched simply because of the body he has, so a small one cannot produce it no matter how much it would pay, and I can't tell whether that is meant to be a limiting case of the sorting machine, an infinite cost for the wrong individual, or a separate mechanism that got swept into the same story.

So my question is only partly whether this has a name and mostly how it should be carved. I don't want a label thrown at me: I've already seen the words people use for all of this, and the problem is precisely that they seem to be used interchangeably, and I can't tell whether that is carelessness or whether the theory genuinely covers all four. What I'd like is: which of these are really one mechanism, which are different mechanisms that merely share the word cost, what exactly has to be true of a cost for it to do the sorting work — is a large cost enough, or must the cost be larger for the worse individual? — where the sorting machine stops and the incentive-changing one starts, whether the physically unfakeable case belongs to either of them or to neither, and some observable difference I could actually use, when I meet a real case, to tell which machine I'm looking at. And if the flat-cost case has no name of its own, I'd like one, together with a definition that marks its edges against the sorting machine and against plain waste.
