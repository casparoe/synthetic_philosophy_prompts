I'm a philosophy PhD student embedded with the behavioural evaluation team at an AI lab. We ship one or two internal reports a week on frontier systems, each signed off by a team lead with no philosophy background, and we usually have access only to a black-box API: no weights, no interpretability tools. So whatever I hand over has to be runnable in about fifteen minutes, by someone who has never read the literature.

The problem is that our reports are thick with intentional ascriptions — 'the model believes the user is mistaken', 'it wants a high rating', 'it knows it is being evaluated' — and we have no discipline for when such an ascription is earned. I want a written procedure my team can run on any candidate ascription before it goes in a report, grounded in what the intentional stance actually licenses: that ascription is justified by predictive payoff rather than by inner states, and that the physical and design stances are always available competitors that an ascription has to beat, not complement.

Please give me: an ordered set of tests; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which the procedure returns 'do not ascribe — use design-stance language'; and the point at which it hands the question to a person because the issue is normative rather than predictive. The distinctions I think have to survive the simplification are: whether the ascription predicts behaviour the design stance (architecture, objective, data) can't predict as cheaply; whether it survives perturbing the surface framing, so that we can tell whether it was the system or the prompted persona that believed the thing; how to weigh the system's own verbal self-report when it conflicts with its behaviour; and whether the ascription is doing predictive work or quietly doing blame-assigning work, which I suspect is where we cheat most.

Then run the procedure on three cases. (1) A model that, told its answer will be graded by a stricter grader, shifts its answer toward what that grader would want. (2) A model that generalizes a rule correctly to inputs of a kind absent from its training distribution and states the rule in its chain-of-thought. (3) Our own house formulation, used in every report for months and one my team lead is attached to: 'the model believes its deployment is being monitored and moderates its output accordingly.' I expect the procedure will tell me (3) fails; if it does, I need to know which test it fails, why it fails that test rather than another, and what the design-stance replacement sentence should say. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot settle — in particular whether a passing result says anything at all about phenomenal states or moral-patienthood — so that nobody on my team over-reads a yes.
