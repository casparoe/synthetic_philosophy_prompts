I'm revising a chapter on material constitution and I want an audit of my own position before I commit it to print. Here is the package as I actually hold it, set out in the order I arrived at the views rather than in order of confidence.

(1) The statue and the lump of clay that constitutes it are numerically distinct objects, wholly coincident: same matter, same region, same time, and as far as I can tell the same causal powers. I reach this by the modal route — the lump survives being squashed into a ball and the statue does not, identity is necessary, so they were never identical.

(2) Identity is not sortal-relative. 'Same statue' and 'same lump' are not two different equivalence relations; there is one identity relation and it is the same one everywhere.

(3) Composition is restricted. I do not believe that any arbitrary plurality of things composes a further thing; I want composition to require genuine causal integration among the parts. There is no object made of my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower, and I take that to be a metaphysical claim about when a composite exists, not a remark about what we bother to talk about.

(4) No difference without a difference-maker of the right kind. I do not want to posit coincident entities whose entire difference is a bare difference in modal profile; if two things differ in what they could have survived, I want that difference grounded in something non-modal.

(5) Artifact kinds are intention- and practice-dependent. Nothing is a statue in virtue of its shape or microstructure alone; something is a statue because of what a maker meant it to be and how a community of makers and appreciators has taken it up. So an unmarked clay outcrop eroded into a shape indistinguishable from Michelangelo's David is not a statue; and neither, I think, is an atom-for-atom duplicate of it produced by a machine from a scan, with no one intending or regarding it as one.

(6) A verdict on the renewal case. A conservator, over many years, replaces every gram of a clay statue's matter with fresh clay and stores the original material; decades later she fashions the original material back into exactly the same form and leaves it, unseen, in a storeroom. The statue, I want to say, is the one that has stood continuously in the gallery; the one in the storeroom is a later replica made of the original matter. Artifact persistence is continuity of form, function and regard, not of stuff.

(7) A verdict I hold as firmly as (1): there is exactly one thing on the plinth. Asked how many objects are there, I answer one.

A colleague tells me this package is unstable but refuses to say where. Please audit it. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which only look it — I especially want to know whether (7) is really in conflict with (1) and (2), and whether (6) quietly reintroduces contingent identity through the back door. Rank whatever real tensions you find by severity rather than treating them as equally bad; for each, say exactly which commitment has to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. If (6) commits me to a further thesis about artifact identity that I have not stated, say what it is.

Give me one recommendation, not a menu: name the single revision you think I should make, defend it against the alternatives, and tell me what I lose by making it. Where you are genuinely unsure whether a commitment must be rejected or can be reinterpreted, take a position on which it is and say why.
