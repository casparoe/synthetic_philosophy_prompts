hey — I'm working on a piece for our ideas desk about that argument that keeps coming up in animal ethics circles: the claim that habitat is a suffering-generating machine, that most wild animals are the r-selected kind who die within weeks of starvation, predation, parasitism or exposure, and that removing habitat might therefore *reduce* total suffering rather than add to it. I want to run a satirical companion to the reported piece and I need someone who actually understands the argument to write it, not someone who thinks "nature is cruel lol" is the whole of it.

So: write me a leaked FAQ from a fictional federal agency — call it the Bureau of Ecosystem Welfare Accounting, or whatever you like — published as numbered Q&As on agency letterhead, 700ish words, 8–10 items. The occasion is that the Bureau is taking public comment on a proposed "Habitat Retirement Program" for a coastal salt marsh. Voice is deadpan government prose: every horror is a "measure," every dead animal is a "unit," and nobody ever raises their voice.

The reductio should be structural. By the end the reader should realize that the most humane act available to the Bureau, on its own accounting, is to drain the marsh and keep it drained permanently — but the Bureau never says that out loud and never winks at it.

The laughs have to come from getting the actual machinery right:

- the ledger is dominated by animals that never existed yet: prevented births counted as suffering averted, with a made-up "Averted Natality Credit" that quietly assumes a life that never starts is a benefit;
- an "Imminent Mortality Offset" — animals killed by the destruction who'd have died within the year anyway are entered at zero;
- a sentience weighting for soil arthropods and insects that nobody has signed off on but which swamps every vertebrate number;
- the baseline problem: the "no-action alternative" isn't neutral, it's ten thousand more years of evolution in that marsh;
- the point that extra food or extra habitat now just means more births and therefore more deaths later.

Be fair to it, that's the important part. A commenter should raise the real objection — that the deaths habitat loss causes are slow ones (starvation, exposure, and worse predation along the habitat edge), possibly worse per animal than the ones it prevents — and the Bureau should concede it honestly and still come out ahead on the arithmetic. Someone who has thought about this should read it and think "yes, that is exactly the argument and that is exactly why it's frightening," not "that's a strawman." No cartoon villains, no real people or real organizations named or parodied.

Include one worked table with invented numbers (sentience-weighted hectares, credits, offsets). Close with a member of the public asking whether all this means the Bureau eventually has to pave everything, and an answer that is completely straight-faced and doesn't deny it. No citations, no footnotes, nothing a reader would have to look up.
