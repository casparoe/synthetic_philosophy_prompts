I belong to a small Sunday-evening reading group on personal identity — three philosophy graduate students, a neurosurgeon, and a retired judge — and we have been following, for about two years now, a monthly column called *Case Notes on Persons*, written by a philosopher named Dr. Marisol Vance, who takes one real or hypothetical neurological case each month and commits, in roughly eight hundred words, to an actual verdict. Her record so far: in Dennett's "Where Am I?" case, where a surgeon removes a man's brain, keeps it alive in a vat, and runs the man's own body by radio link, she held that the man is with the brain in the vat and that the body out in the world is a puppet, adding that if the signal had been split between two bodies the question would simply be ill-posed. In an ordinary whole-brain transplant into a healthy donor body, she held that the person who wakes up is the brain donor and inherits his debts and his guilt. Where the cortex is destroyed and the brainstem left intact, so that the human organism breathes on its own but is permanently unconscious, she held that the person is gone, that the surviving organism is not a subject and not him, and that we are not organisms at all. Where each hemisphere is transplanted into a different body and both result in people psychologically continuous with the original, she held that neither is him, that there is no further fact as to which one is him, and that this should not disturb us, because identity is not what matters. Where a man undergoes a complete turnover of memories, traits and values over forty years, each step small, she held that he is the same person throughout and that there is nothing to the continuity beyond the small steps. She has also said, in passing, that a split-brain patient is one person, though perhaps a committee. She has pointedly never ruled on teleportation, uploading, or any case in which the brain is destroyed and replaced all at once; one member of our group thinks she is avoiding these on purpose, which is precisely why we cannot agree about next month.

The case she has announced for next month, which our neurosurgeon introduced after describing a real experimental protocol that comes close to it: a thirty-four-year-old man with intractable epilepsy has his cortex replaced over nineteen months in twenty-six staged operations. In each operation a cortical region is isolated and a synthetic neuromorphic graft is implanted alongside it; the graft is calibrated in vivo against the tissue it is to replace for several weeks, learning from the surrounding tissue and then helping to train the graft that will replace the next region, and only then is the biological tissue removed. Between operations he lives at home, continues working as a court translator, and passes every clinical and personal test; he remembers each operation and can describe the whole sequence from the inside. At the end there is no neural tissue of the original kind left that could sustain consciousness: what remains has the same functional profile, the same memories, and the same reported inner life, and is wired to the same body. There was one bad stretch around month eleven — a fortnight of prosopagnosia, and a period in which his memory of his own wedding was, by his own report, noticeably thinner than before — and his estranged brother is contesting their father's will on the ground that the legatee is not the man their father named.

What I would like from you is a prediction of her verdict, but more importantly the principle she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her six verdicts just as well, what each of those rules predicts about the nineteen-month case, and the single further case that would best separate them. My own difficulty is that the closest analog among her precedents looks to me to be either the forty-year-turnover case, which suggests that he survives, or the cortical-destruction case, which suggests that he does not, and I cannot tell which she will treat as the tighter fit — so tell me which precedent she will lean on and why, whether she will say that the question has a determinate answer or none, and whether the case that would genuinely settle the matter is the one-shot version of the same operation, in which the same end-state system is installed in a single surgery and the original removed at once, or something else entirely.
