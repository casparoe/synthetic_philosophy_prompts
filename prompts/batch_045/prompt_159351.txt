I'm the chair of the board of the Kittredge School, a small independent school founded in 1871 by Dr. Amos Kittredge — a physician and one-term state legislator — as a tuition-free school for the children of emancipated enslaved people and for poor white families in the same mill town. It is the institution most of the town's Black professional class came out of, four of our nine trustees are graduates, and it still carries his name. I have to give the eight-minute address at the school's 150th-anniversary convocation in three weeks, which is the first all-school gathering since our board voted six to five to keep the name and install a permanent exhibit about the 1887 law Kittredge wrote. The record, as our researchers have put it together: Kittredge drafted and pushed through the state's Act for the Care of the Feeble-Minded — compulsory commitment on a single physician's certificate, no hearing, no appeal, definitions broad enough to sweep in paupers, epileptics, and girls judged unruly — and he was its most effective advocate, using language harsher than his co-sponsors'. He was also objected to at the time: the state medical society and the editor of the town's Black weekly both said so in print, in terms we would now recognize, and he answered in print that they were sentimentalists. The school's endowment was voted in the same legislative session, and only as part of a package that also created the asylum board; we exist partly because of that bargain. Then, in 1890, after visiting the asylum, he said publicly that the institutions had become what he had not intended and campaigned unsuccessfully for repeal; the statute stayed on the books until 1962, and about 1,900 people were committed under it.

I've been arguing about this with my brother, who thinks an address like mine can only be whitewash or demolition and that there is no third thing, and I think he's wrong but I can't yet say why. The distinction I've been working with is this: the thing people invoke when they say he was a man of his time is really two claims run together — a claim about what was available to him to think, given what he could have encountered and understood, and a separate claim about the fittingness of blame or condemnation even if he could and should have known better. On our facts the first claim looks weak: people in his own town and his own profession told him, and he had the epistemic equipment to have listened. The second is the live one, and my own view is that our position is compromised rather than clarifying: we are beneficiaries of the bargain that also produced the asylum board, which is a reason for scruple about how loudly we condemn, not a reason to think he is excused. I also distrust the move where we ask whether we would have done the same in his place; it is unverifiable and it functions, every time I have seen it used, as self-exculpation.

So here is what I need written: that address, roughly 1,000 words. It has to do two things at once without netting them out — give genuine, specific thanks for the school from people who owe it a great deal, and give genuine, specific condemnation of the 1887 law and of the man who wrote it — and it must not present them on a single ledger, or imply that the one cancels or is paid for by the other, or imply that either is the 'real' Kittredge behind the other. The words man of his time and presentism should not appear in it at all; nor should it ask the audience to forgive him, since forgiveness isn't ours to offer. Two people in particular will be in the room: Kittredge's great-grandchildren in the first row, and Marion Oakes, a graduate whose great-grandmother was committed under the Act and died in the asylum, who speaks later in the program and who has not been asked to say anything conciliatory. The speech must not enrol her, or her ancestor, as an illustration of anything, and must not present our six-to-five vote as settled wisdom. The county historical society president, who has said that panels like ours are how a town loses its history, will also be there, so nothing in it should sound like a lecture to him either. Write it to land for a room that is half teenagers and half people who have spent forty years inside this argument. When you've finished, add a short note: flag the one claim in the speech that rests on a contested reading of the historical record or of the ethics, say how confident you are in the two-claim distinction I described above, and name the single point you are least sure of.
