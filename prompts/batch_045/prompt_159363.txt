I teach a seminar on consciousness for about sixteen upper-level undergraduates, mostly philosophy majors with a scattering of cognitive science students. We meet once a week for ninety minutes in a room with a projector, a whiteboard, and nothing else; there is no budget and no possibility of pre-class reading compliance beyond a single short handout. We are coming up on the fight between access-based accounts of consciousness — the global-workspace-style family of views on which a content's being conscious just is its being globally available, broadcast to many downstream consumers, reportable, usable in reasoning and memory and the control of action — and the claim that phenomenal consciousness outruns access, that there can be more to what a subject undergoes than the subject can report, use, or act on, so that global availability is at best a signature of consciousness and not its essence. On the page this reads to my students as a stale stalemate: one side says report is our only real evidence, the other side says experience in the moment is richer than what survives into report, and nobody moves. So I would like to stage it instead of assigning it.

What I want is a ninety-minute in-class simulation — runnable with index cards, a timer, a projector, and maybe some envelopes — in which the students are themselves arranged as a cognitive architecture with a severe bottleneck: several peripheral "modules" that each privately receive rich, detailed input (a crowded visual scene, a fragment of music, a bodily sensation, a word on the tip of the tongue), and a single global channel that can carry the content of only one module per round, after which that content becomes available to downstream consumers: the system's memory, its reasoning, its action, and above all its single "spokesperson," one student who alone is permitted to speak for the whole system and who may say only what has actually been broadcast. The students should walk into the problem rather than be told about it: nobody should hear the words "access," "phenomenal," "overflow," or "global workspace" until the debrief. Beyond the basic architecture, I would like the design to include at least three conditions I have been sketching and cannot get to work. First, a silent-channel condition: a module receives vivid input that is never broadcast and that leaves no downstream trace at all, and the record still has to contain the module's own contemporaneous first-person verdict about whether the content was experienced. Second, a faint-signature condition: a module's content is not broadcast and cannot be reported by the spokesperson, but it does leave a weak downstream effect — a slight tilt in a later forced choice, a bias the spokesperson cannot explain or cite — so the class has to ask whether a downstream effect that no one can attribute is enough to count the content as accessed. Third, a decay condition: modules log their confidence about their own content immediately and then again after a delay, so the group can watch immediate richness shrink into a thinner, more confident retrospective story. I would also like the incentives to pull against each other: the system as a whole earns points for accurate public report, while each module earns points for how much of its own private richness gets recorded in the archive, so the players feel the tension between what the system can avow and what its parts contain. Please give me the rules, the roles with their information asymmetries, the materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline.

Then I need the debrief, and I need it to be more than a lecture: it should map specific moments in the simulation onto the specific claims in dispute — what broadcast was doing functionally in the game, which moment produced the pull toward saying the un-broadcast content was nonetheless experienced, which moment produced the pull toward saying report is all we can go on, and where the two sides turn out to be arguing about the evidential rules rather than about the facts. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: my guess is that the peripheral players will cheat or meta-game, that the bottleneck will leak because people can see and hear each other, and that the exercise will collapse into giggling the way a note-passing version of it did when I tried something similar a couple of years ago — say how to handle each. I also want you to be frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce. The obvious worry is that every participant in my game is a real conscious person who has their own content no matter what the architecture does, so nothing in the activity actually makes a content unavailable to the subject who has it, and the hard part of the real dispute — the gap between what a subject can avow and what a subject undergoes — may be exactly the thing the staging presupposes rather than produces. Tell me which feature of the debate the facilitator will simply have to supply in words, and say it plainly so I know what I am doing when I talk at the end. Please write your answer in American English.
