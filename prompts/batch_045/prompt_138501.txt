i'm a fourth-year PhD student in information science doing STS-flavoured work on algorithmic classification, and one of my committee members is a social ontologist whose verdicts I've been collecting for about two years (published stuff, seminars, and referee reports I get to see because I RA for her). I'm writing a chapter on categories that get discovered first and made later — patterns in behavioural data that end up as things people identify as and argue about — and I want to know whether I can lean on her view as a precedent or whether I'm going to have to argue for the rule myself.

Her verdicts so far, as best I've pieced them together:

- money: constructed, and she's emphatic that this doesn't make it less real
- race: constructed, real, and she gets impatient with people who hear "constructed" as "illusion"
- the official poverty line: constructed
- planet: constructed — the relevant astronomical body settled the Pluto question by a vote and there's nothing deeper for the classification to be answerable to
- biological species: not constructed, even though she'll happily grant that biologists have never stopped arguing about species concepts; she says the argument is about which real division to track, not a free choice
- invasive species: constructed — same organisms, but the category answers to our interests and to a notion of belonging
- antibiotic-resistant bacteria: not constructed, even though we caused them, the category is useful to us, and the gene pool is shaped by how we farm

Those last two sit oddly together and I've never got a clean answer out of her about why, which is part of why I'm asking.

The case I want a verdict on is invented but not exotic. A short-video platform trains an embedding model on a couple of years of watch behaviour and clusters the user embeddings, with k picked by a silhouette heuristic. One cluster turns out tight and stable: call it four million accounts. Nobody names it. The platform starts using it as a targeting segment inside the ranking objective, which is something like session length. Eighteen months later, outside observers notice a distinctive content niche around it, it acquires a folk name, creators start making things for it, and users start self-identifying and arguing about who really counts. Two extra facts that matter to me: the training data were generated under earlier versions of the same recommender, so the structure that got "found" was already partly shaped by the platform; and a different objective (say, number of distinct creators seen) would have carved the space differently.

So: would she say that cluster was a socially constructed kind at the moment the clustering produced it, when nobody knew it existed? Would she say it is one now? What rule is she actually running that fits all seven verdicts above, and what other rules fit them about equally well — I can see at least two candidates, one about membership being settled by uptake or acceptance or stipulation and one about the criteria being answerable to somebody's interests, plus some causal variant about us having made the thing. Say which of those rules come apart on my case and in which direction, give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it over the runners-up, and tell me what case would really tell the rules apart. My own candidate is: the same clustering procedure run on viewing logs from before the recommender existed, by outside researchers who never deploy anything and never tell anyone — but if there's a better discriminator, use that one instead. Bonus question I can't sort out: does she think it's the same kind at the two time points, or has one kind been replaced by another? I genuinely can't tell whether that's a deep question or a pseudo-question.

Please be straight about confidence: tell me which part of the prediction you'd actually bet on and which is close to a coin flip, and flag where you're extrapolating from seven data points. And if the setup is confused — if "socially constructed" doesn't have a univocal enough reading for her verdicts to be tracked by any single rule, or if my case doesn't really discriminate between the rules you list, or if I've built the case so that the answer is definately just "yes, obviously" — say that plainly rather than giving me the tidy answer I asked for.
