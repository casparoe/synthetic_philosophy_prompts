I'm the TA for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on moral responsibility — 16 students, mostly philosophy majors, one 80-minute session a week. Next week's topic is what it takes to be in a position to blame: the familiar thought that a hypocrite who blames you for exactly what she does herself invites the reply that she's one to talk, and then the tangle behind that reply. Does it show the blame was never warranted in the first place, or only that it was not hers to deliver? Does it help if she confesses her own complicity up front and then blames you anyway, on the ground that her record is simply not the point? Is someone who never did the thing herself but quietly benefited from your doing it in any better position? And the version I find genuinely hard: the "you're one to talk" reply is itself an accusation, so if standing conditions apply to it, the person who actually did the deed seems to be the one who lacks standing to complain about being blamed. I've tried running this as open discussion twice and it always degrades — either the students treat the reply as obviously a mere rhetorical dodge and the hour is over in ten minutes, or they treat it as obviously decisive and spend the rest of the session inventing excuses for whatever example is on the table.

So I'd like a staged activity instead, and I'd rather it be a role-play or structured deliberation with real choices and small stakes than a lecture illustrated with cases. What I want the students to run into on their own, before anyone names it, is that the reply has real force and yet leaves the original charge completely untouched — that the accusation and the permission to make it come apart — and that the three fixes they will naturally reach for (swap in a blamer with clean hands, have the blamer confess first, have the blamer insist her own record is irrelevant) each turn out to change something other than what they expected. Please give me the rules, the roles with role sheets, a timeline across the 80 minutes, and whatever materials it needs — cards, score sheets, anything else — keeping in mind that I have paper, a blackboard, and no technology beyond that. Two design problems matter to me especially: how to arrange things so that the accused reaches for the "you're one to talk" move herself rather than being handed it, and how to get the group to track two things separately — whether the accusation is correct, and whether this is the person who may make it — without announcing that distinction in advance.

Finish with a debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto particular parts of the puzzle and is frank about which single part your activity actually makes vivid, an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it with what I should do about each, and a note on which feature of the standing-to-blame problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will simply have to supply in closing. One constraint: keep the scenario fictional and the wrongdoing mild and ordinary. Nobody in that room should end up genuinely accused of anything by their peers, and I don't want the session to turn into a debate about the example rather than about the structure it exposes.
