I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training beyond a lot of stackexchange and a few podcasts, and I've somehow ended up applying for a small independent-scholar grant — 9 months, part time, I keep my day job — to write a long paper on the statue and the clay thing. You know the case: a lump of clay gets shaped into a statue, and the question is whether the statue and the lump are one thing or two things occupying the same place at the same time, plus the variant where you squish the statue back into a ball and people argue about whether it survived that.

My half-formed idea, which I definately can't tell is an insight or a category error: in code we handle this stuff constantly and nobody calls it metaphysics. Two variables can point at the same mutable object; value equality and reference identity are different questions you ask for different purposes; and you can have relations that are reflexive and symmetric but deliberately not transitive (floating-point comparison against NaN is the textbook example, similarity thresholds are another). So maybe the debate stalls because both camps — the two-things-in-one-place camp and the one-thing-described-two-ways camp — quietly assume that identity has to be a single equivalence relation with all three properties, and that if you instead let identity be sortal- or aspect-indexed and only jointly non-transitive, a chunk of the standard puzzle cases stop producing a contradiction. Or maybe that just relabels the mystery, which is honestly my guess.
