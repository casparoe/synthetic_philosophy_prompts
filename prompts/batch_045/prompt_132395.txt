Hey — I'm on my university's animal care committee (the one that has to sign off on any protocol involving vertebrates) and we've got a case that's going to a vote next week and I need a draft of the decision memo.

A neuroscience PI submitted a protocol for a chronic inflammatory pain model in rats: induce inflammation, then record from the same animals for several weeks. No analgesics, because the drugs would wreck the measurements. He filed it under the USDA pain category where pain or distress is not relieved by drugs — I think it's column E, our admin does that paperwork and I may have the letter wrong. Standard enough, and the attending vet normally signs off as long as pain is "minimized" per our own scoring sheet (guarding, reduced burrowing, withdrawal thresholds, that kind of thing).

The wrinkle: attached is a six-page philosophical addendum in which he argues the protocol should be exempt from the pain-minimization requirements entirely. His claim is that nothing in the protocol establishes that the rats are conscious at all. Behavior is exactly what a very well-built machine would produce, the move from pain behavior to pain experience is just the old argument from analogy, and (his words, I'm paraphrasing) the committee's scoring sheet is nothing but behavior-plus-analogy, so leaning on it begs the question against him. He also says Descartes in a late letter seems to allow beasts bare sensation while denying them thought, so "the rat is in pain" and "there is something it is like to be the rat" can come apart — I haven't checked that letter and don't know if it's right. He grants that Descartes' own motive (immortal souls, so animals can't have them) is dead, but says the epistemic argument survives it.

Here's the part I want the memo to chew on. His new setup puts a lever in the home cage: press it and the animal gets a dose of analgesic, but the lever is placed so it costs effort, and in a pilot he says the rats trade pressing off against waiting for a sucrose pellet, and will work for it in chamber configurations they've never seen. He pre-registered in advance that any lever-pressing will be scored as an operant reflex and not as evidence about experience. But Descartes' own second test in the Discourse was not about any single performance — it was that reason is a universal instrument that works in all sorts of encounters, while a machine needs a particular arrangement of organs for each particular action. So it looks to me like his own pilot data might be the one thing that could beat him on his own preferred terms, and I want that in the memo either way.

Our attending vet thinks the addendum is bad faith and that we should just reject the protocol. Two other members think philosophy has no business in protocol review. One pointed out that if the addendum's logic is sound we'd have to stop requiring analgesia in every other protocol too, which nobody wants to say out loud.

Can you draft the memo as I'd circulate it: a clear bottom line the committee can vote on (deny the exemption? reject outright? approve with conditions?), the reasoning, and an honest statement of the strongest thing on the side that loses — including whether we're just helping ourselves to an inference we can't prove. Make it actionable: concrete conditions with time frames, what would count as evidence either way, and what our epistemic standard actually is. Please don't turn it into a literature review and don't cite anything, we're all working from memory and nothing you'd name would be checkable for us anyway. Sorry, long one — thanks.
