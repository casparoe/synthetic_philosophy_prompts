I'm trying to work out what I actually think about when it's alright to say a slur out loud, and I'd like a principle rather than a shrug. Let S be a slur (think of a serious racial one) and G the group it targets. Going purely on gut:

1. Linguistics lecturer writes S on the board and says "S is a slur for G". Fine. I flinch, but its fine.
2. Tabloid puts S in a headline, in scare quotes, purely for clicks. Not fine.
3. Local paper reporting a hate crime: "he called him a S". Fine-ish, though I'd rather they'd written "a racial slur" instead.
4. Novel, third-person narration: "the S shuffled off down the road". Not fine.
5. Same novel, same author, but now it's a racist character's dialogue. Fine. I can't properly explain why 4 and 5 differ, but they really do for me.
6. Two mates, both members of G, greeting each other with it affectionately. Fine.
7. One of them using it disparagingly about a third member of G — "don't be such a S". Not fine, or at any rate much worse than 6, even though the speaker is in-group both times.
8. A member of G using it warmly to a friend who isn't in G. I go back and forth on this one. Current verdict: not fine, but nowhere near as bad as 9.
9. Someone not in G using it warmly, having picked it up from music and thinking it's just a friendly term of address. Not fine — and there's no contempt behind it at all, which is what first made me think my principle was broken.
10. Someone not in G using it as abuse at someone who isn't in G either, and who everyone present knows isn't. Not fine, and I think worse than 9, even though 9 was aimed at an actual member of G. That's the pair I keep going in circles on.
11. An eighty-year-old who grew up hearing the word as the ordinary neutral term for members of G and still uses it that way, with nothing behind it. I can't get angry at him. Fine, or at least excused.
12. A non-native speaker who learnt it as the neutral ethnonym and uses it in a job interview. I initially felt the same as in 11, but I think it's actually a bit worse — or maybe I'm just being harsh because the stakes are higher.
13. A member of G standing up at a rally and saying "I'm a S and proud" to a mixed crowd. Fine, but I flinch when there are out-group people in the room, which shouldn't matter if what counts is who's speaking.
14. Invented case: S has been reclaimed by members of G in one country but not in another. A member from the first country uses it to a member from the second, who's upset. Verdict: not fine — the reclamation doesn't travel.
15. Invented case: a nasty term for a group people opt into (a religion, a diet, a hobby), used by someone who used to belong to that group. I don't feel reclamation works here the way it does in 6, but I can't tell whether that's a difference in the words or in me.

My rough principle going in was: a use is objectionable iff the speaker thereby expresses (or would reasonably be taken to express) contempt for G, and isn't entitled to. But 7, 9 and 11 don't sit comfortably with that, and 4/5 and 9/10 are the pairs I keep failing to reconcile.

Can you give me one principle — or at most two or three candidates — that gets as many of these as possible, stated precisely enough that I could actually apply it to a new case? For each clause, tell me which case forced you to add it. Then say what it predicts about two I haven't made my mind up on: (a) a member of G puts S in a song that ends up on mainstream radio, heard mostly by people outside G; (b) an AI assistant, asked to reproduce a historical speech verbatim with S spelled out, does so. And be straight about which of my verdicts your principle can't save — I'd rather drop a verdict than end up with a principle that has a clause bolted on for every single case.
