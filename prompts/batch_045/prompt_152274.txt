A friend of mine is a political theorist who works on science and technology studies and has no patience at all for the literature on technological determinism, and we spent most of last night's dinner arguing about whether artifacts can have politics in themselves. Her opening move was that the familiar case of the low overpasses on the Long Island parkways does no work: whatever politics the bridges have is borrowed from the intentions of the man who commissioned them and from surrounding facts about who travelled by bus, and if you hold the design fixed while varying those intentions and facts, the politics evaporates. I replied that the interesting claim was never about intentions but about durability: the bridges outlived their builder and his purposes, and they still settle, invisibly and without anyone deciding anything, who can reach the beach. She said that if durability is the whole story then a wall is political too; I said that seemed fine to me; she said that showed the claim was empty. Somewhere around here I proposed what I now call the invariance test: an artifact is political in itself just in case the political effect it produces is robust across nearby variations in the intentions, stated purposes and social context of its makers and users, with only its design held fixed. She shot back that a lock, a staircase and a revolver all pass this test, since each reliably redistributes who can do what to whom whatever anyone intends, and she took that as a refutation. My own suspicion is that her counterexample shows something narrower and more interesting: that the test is missing a condition on which effects count as political, and that supplying one quietly reimports the very social facts the test was supposed to screen out. I would rather turn this into an exam question than into another dinner.

The course is an upper-level undergraduate seminar on technology and politics. The students have read Winner's essay and some of the critical response, and any of them can reproduce the summary: two ways in which artifacts can be political, the bridges, the tomato harvester. I want one 60-minute essay question that cannot be answered by reproducing that summary. Give me the question, a model answer of about 650 to 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-have moves, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong.

Two things should be built into the question itself. First, students must assess the invariance test as stated above, including whether her lock and staircase counterexamples really refute it, rather than being free to change the subject to intentions or to determinism in general. Second, they must apply it to a case nobody has read about: a housing cooperative installs a keyless entry system that logs every entry to a shared, permanent, resident-readable ledger, marketed purely as a convenience feature for keeping track of contractors; within a year residents are using the ledger to monitor one another and the building's norms have shifted from mutual trust to mutual accountability, and members of a privacy-absolutist commune who installed the same system would very likely end up in the same place. The question should force them to say whether the test vindicates, refines or undermines the distinction between artifacts that are political in themselves and artifacts that are political only given their circumstances.

Finally, because I have to hand this to a colleague to moderate, please mark in your answer which claims about this literature are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on it, and flag the places where the model answer takes a line that a well-informed reader could reject, so I do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
