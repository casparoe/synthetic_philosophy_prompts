I did a bit over thirty years in comparative physiology (fish gills mostly, plus a lot of hours writing dichotomous identification keys that nobody ever thanked me for), and now I'm the oldest person in a reading group that meets in the back room of a pub. It's my turn to lead the session next month and the topic is concepts of health and disease. Last week we burned an entire hour going in circles because every second sentence was well, it depends what you mean by disease, so I want to short-circuit that with a key.

What I'd like is a dichotomous key in the old natural-history sense: you start from a case or a commitment, answer a question, take the left or right branch, and come out at a named position. One side of A4, eight branch questions at the very most, six to nine leaves.

Some things I care about, because I've seen what happens when you let a classification be built backwards:

Build it from the logical space first. Give me the questions and the combinations of answers they actually allow, then put the real positions into that space afterwards. Where a combination of answers has nobody living in it, say so and have a guess at why — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And if your key genuinely can't seperate two of the leaves, flag the pair and tell me which further question would do the work; don't quietly paper over it.

Every node has to be a question someone in the group can answer without already knowing which view it leads to, and it should be a concrete case rather than an abstract one. No node that just asks whether health is value-free or whether doctors should treat what patients want treated. I want cases like these in it somewhere:

- a structural or biochemical deviation found by accident on a scan that never causes the person a moment's trouble;
- something universal in the species that slowly degrades function with age — is senescence a disease, and if not, why not;
- a system that worked fine in the environment the species evolved in but causes trouble now that we've changed the environment, so the answer seems to depend on which environment you score function against. Please make the reference environment an explicit parameter in the tree rather than sneaking in a default, because in my old job you could never state a fitness or a performance measurement without saying what conditions it was measured under;
- the same physiology that is disabling in one social setting and unremarkable in another;
- a variant that knocks out one function but protects against a pathogen, so it looks like a defect and an adaptation at the same time;
- a response that is doing exactly what it's supposed to do and is nonetheless what's hurting the patient;
- a part with no selected function at all, or one whose function nobody can agree on — can it malfunction?
- harm with nothing internal going wrong: the cause is entirely outside the organism.

Leaves I want represented: the biostatistical sort of naturalism, the harmful-dysfunction hybrid, the welfare or ability-to-pursue-vital-goals family, and the view on which disease is more or less what a given society ends up treating. Add a leaf for the we-should-drop-the-category position if you think it earns its place, plus any leaf you think our group is missing. Each leaf gets a name (call a leaf unnamed if nobody defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Two more things. Our group keeps tripping over the three-way distinction between the physiologist's state, the person's experience, and the social role — for each leaf, note which of those three it's actually a theory of, and flag any leaf that only makes sense for one of them. And finish with two or three sentences on which single question in the key does the most work and which one my group will fight about.

Don't attach author names to the leaves unless you're sure of them, and no reading list or references at all — half the group can't work a library database and I'd rather have the arguments than the footnotes.
