I work at a small state environmental agency, and a chunk of my job is the public comment side of rulemaking: we publish a proposed rule together with whatever analysis sits behind it, anyone at all can send in written comments for a month or two, we have to read them and write a response to the significant ones as part of a statement explaining the rule, and if we blow that off a court can send the rule back to us. It's the same shape as the city council hearing where everyone gets two minutes before the vote, except that there the vote basically never changes. A few friends and I have been reading Dewey in a book group (badly, and out of order — we jumped into the later political writing rather than Democracy and Education), and the claim we keep circling is that democracy isn't only a way of settling who gets what but the political face of experimental inquiry: a policy is a hypothesis, the test is what it does to the people it lands on, and the people it lands on aren't merely interests to be balanced but sources of the evidence you need, since they're the ones who notice the indirect consequences the rule-writers couldn't. I like this, but I can't tell whether the practice I actually run already assumes it, or whether it would run exactly the same way under a view on which the exercise is just counting preferences, or giving affected parties their day in court, or harvesting local knowledge the agency lacks, or laundering a decision that has in fact already been made.

So: what does notice-and-comment really presuppose about democracy and about inquiry, and which parts of the practice commit you to which theory? I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed first, then the honest answer about how much of it is strictly required — what has to be true for the practice to make sense at all — as opposed to what it merely fits comfortably with, and whether the various bits of it (the self-selection of who turns up, the fact that we've already drafted the text before hearing anyone, the requirement that we respond, the two-minute limit, the record a judge later reviews) pull toward one theory or toward several at once. My colleague says the whole thing is legitimacy theater and points out that we almost never change the rule; another friend says no, it's fairness to the people affected and improving the rule is beside the point. I want to know whether those are genuinely different accounts or whether they'd license identical institutions, and if they would, what observable difference there is between an agency that's inquiring and one that's going through the motions.

The bit I keep getting stuck on is this: I've started thinking of a comment period as a measuring instrument pointed at a population of consequences, and like any instrument it has a selection bias — it over-samples organized interests and people with lawyers, and under-samples diffuse harms, harms that arrive after the comment window shuts, and harms nobody yet has the vocabulary to describe. Does Dewey's experimentalism have the resources to say that a badly calibrated instrument is producing bad inquiry and not just insufficient participation, or does his view really only tell you to open the process up further? And if I took the experimentalist picture seriously, concretely, what would I have to change — what would need to happen after the rule takes effect rather than before it, who would we have to go out and find rather than wait to hear from, and what would it mean to design a rule so that its own failure is detectable? Please argue the positions rather than pointing me to what Dewey says where; I'm away from a library this week, and I'd rather have the argument anyway.
