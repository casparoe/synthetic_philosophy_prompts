You are a professional philosopher who works on animal and environmental ethics and who also writes policy parody on the side. Think carefully before you draft: begin with a short private brainstorming phase in which you settle (a) which real feature of insect-inclusive welfare reasoning your piece is going to exaggerate, (b) which premise the reader is meant to grant without noticing, and (c) where, exactly, the straight face will start to hurt. Then commit to one and write the document; do not hedge, annotate, or explain the joke afterwards.

Write a consultation white paper, roughly 950-1,100 words, issued by a fictional regulator — the Office of Invertebrate Welfare Assurance — and addressed to a national agriculture ministry, on the question of how pest control policy must change once insect welfare is taken seriously. Format it as a formal government document: numbered sections and subsections, an abstract or executive summary, a summary table, two or three footnotes, and a short statement of compliance costs. Invent a date, a reference number, and invented stakeholders (an agrochemical trade body, an organic growers' association, a consumers' union) whom you may quote in invented words.

The humor has to come from the philosophy, not from puns. Ground the piece in the actual structure of the problem:

1. Expected value under uncertainty about sentience. The Office should treat the probability that, say, a caterpillar or an aphid has phenomenally conscious states as small but non-negligible, and then notice out loud that small credences multiplied by very large population figures dominate every other term in the ledger. Let the Office be entirely sincere about this and entirely unfazed by the implication that insect welfare swamps the human interests the policy was meant to serve.
2. The killing/causing-suffering distinction, and how it flips. Give the reader the standard point that ending a life is a different harm from making a life go badly, and then let the Office apply it under the further premise that the overwhelming majority of juvenile pest insects die slowly of starvation, parasitism, or exposure anyway. The conclusion the Office draws from those two premises together should be one the reader can see coming and still not want.
3. Mode of death as the real variable. The Office's ranking of control methods must turn on what death by each method actually involves: prolonged neurotoxic convulsions, gut disruption and septicemia, gradual desiccation, being consumed from within by a parasitoid larva, straightforward starvation. Use this to produce the paper's central deadpan inversion: the methods with the best green credentials — microbial and botanical agents, parasitoid releases, the slower-acting biorational options that organic certification favours — come out as the cruelest, while fast-acting synthetics come out as the humane choice. The Office should be pained by this only insofar as it complicates procurement.
4. A sentience credence schedule. The Office needs some way to allocate weight across taxa, so have it assign working credences by order or functional group (Lepidoptera, Diptera, Hymenoptera, Coleoptera, Hemiptera, and social colonies as collective units), justified by the usual kinds of evidence — nociception, associative learning, trade-off behaviour, responses to analgesia or to noxious stimuli they apparently choose to endure. It must not simply collapse nociception into sentience, but it should exploit the gap wherever doing so licenses a stronger recommendation, and it should note in a footnote that the schedule will be revised when the science improves and not before any decision is taken.
5. A unit of account. Parody the QALY/DALY style of cost-benefit apparatus: define a standardized unit of expected insect suffering, specify how expected sentience discounts it, and show it being summed across a lettuce crop, a stored-grain silo, and a mosquito abatement district. If a per-insect welfare tariff or an offset market falls out of the accounting, let it fall out and let the Office administer it briskly.
6. The precautionary principle behaving badly. At some point the Office must confront the fact that precaution about uncertain sentience (act as though they matter) and precaution about uncertain population-level effects (do not intervene in systems you do not understand) point in opposite directions. Have it resolve the conflict in whichever direction its existing programme already requires, without acknowledging that it has made a choice.

Constraints on the writing:

- Deadpan throughout. The Office never winks, never signals that it is being absurd, and never raises its voice. Everything is expressed in the register of a competent civil service document: hedged, procedural, earnest. The reader must be able to tell what is being ironized, but only from the content.
- Internal consistency is part of the brief. Once the Office grants a premise it must live with it, including when living with it costs it a conclusion it would prefer. If a premise has to be quietly dropped mid-paper, the dropping itself should be the joke and should be visible in a footnote.
- Invent any figures you need and present them as the Office's own accounting. Do not cite real studies, real statistics, real trade names, or real people; a model writing this offline has no way to check them, and the piece should not depend on them. Say what the numbers do in the argument, not where they came from.
- The target is a style of reasoning and its policy idiom, not any named individual or organisation, and not the people who hold the underlying view. The reader who takes insect welfare seriously should be able to laugh and concede the satire is fair.
- The final section should arrive somewhere the reader recoils — a recommendation that follows cleanly from premises they were led to grant and that they nevertheless do not want to endorse. Do not soften it, do not add a moral, and do not let the Office notice that it has gone too far.

Succeed as follows: a researcher in animal ethics should recognise the argumentative moves being sent up and think the parody lands them accurately; a reader with no philosophy background should follow the dilemma well enough to feel why the recommendation is unacceptable; and no sentence should be funny for a reason that has nothing to do with the ethics. Output the document only, preceded by nothing but its header, and state the word count in a final line.
