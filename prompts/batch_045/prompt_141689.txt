I retired about six years ago from a lab career in sensory psychophysics — somatosensory thresholds, magnitude estimation, cross-modality matching, that kind of thing — and I've come to philosophy late, through a weekly Zoom reading group that started out on population ethics and has drifted into the suffering-focussed corner of it. We have been having the same argument for roughly eighteen months and nothing moves. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict.

The fight is between the lexical threshold negative utilitarians and the everybody-onto-one-scale people. Roughly: the first camp says that below some severity there is an ordinary exchange rate between pleasant and unpleasant experience, but above a threshold of intensity pain dominates lexically — no quantity of mild pleasant experience, however enormous, compensates for a single episode of the really bad stuff. The second camp says pleasant and unpleasant states are points on one hedonic axis, tradeoffs are always available in principle maybe at a savage exchange rate, and that the lexical view is an unmotivated discontinuity smuggled in to dodge conclusions the authors find embarrassing. Both sides accuse the other of leaning on intuitions trained by small everyday cases and then extrapolating them a billion miles.

Every session goes the same way. Someone produces a case with an astronomical number in it — torture versus dust specks, that sort of thing — both sides announce that the other's answer is obviously insane, and then it turns out we are not arguing about the same question at all. Sometimes the lexical people are making a claim about what severe pain is *like*, as a bit of experience, and the other side hears them making a claim about what it is *worth*. Sometimes the fight is about whether the threshold sits in the phenomenology or in the axiology. And I cannot tell whether these are two readings of one dispute, two different disputes sharing a name, or a verbal quarrel about the word commensurable.

My own hypothesis, which nobody in the group takes seriously, is that there is a hidden empirical question doing all the work: the dimensionality of hedonic space. We spent years in my old field asking whether a sensation is one number or several, and it seems to me that the lexical intuition may be tracking something like urgency or intrusive demand — pain grabbing the whole of attention and insisting on action — rather than hedonic intensity proper. If so the two camps are quietly running different measurement models of the same subject matter, and the threshold is a artifact of conflating intensity with something else. I also tried a proposal on them: build a chain of just-noticeable-differences from a mildly pleasant experience up to a severe pain, argue each neighbouring step is a genuine tradeoff, and let transitivity do the rest. They pointed out — correctly, I think — that a chain gives you an ordering of intensities and not exchange rates or any way of summing across episodes and people, so it doesn't deliver what I claimed. I still think something in the neighbourhood of that would help.

So: what kind of stalemate is this? Verbal, substantive-but-empirical, a clash of standards about which cases count as data, bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, or two questions under one heading? Engage with what each side actually says rather than listing positions. And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — something like my chaining idea, a formal result about non-Archimedean orderings, a distinction between intensity and urgency, a change of question — or should we expect to be exactly here in another eighteen months, and why?
