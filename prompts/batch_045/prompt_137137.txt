I sit on the grants committee of a small family foundation, and this spring we have to choose between renewing a long-standing commitment to a near-term global poverty programme and making our first grant to an existential-risk research group. Our discussion keeps circling a question I have taught many times but never had to answer: whether the separateness of persons objection has any grip when the people it is supposed to protect do not yet exist, and might not exist at all.

What I would like from you is a reconstruction, as disciplined as you can make it, of how John Rawls—who died in 2002, before the vocabulary of existential risk and longtermism had settled into its present shape—would have handled this. Please do not summarise the objection for me; I know the standard formulation, the contrast with the single impartial spectator, and the usual utilitarian replies. I want an extrapolation built out of the specific machinery of his theory, and I want you to keep separate what follows from that machinery and what is your own guess about what he would have said.

Four things in particular I want you to reason from: the original position as a device of *representation*, where the parties stand in for actual identifiable persons; the motivational assumption about concern for descendants that he uses to get a just savings principle at all, together with his assumption that the parties know themselves to belong to a single generation; his refusal to treat the mere addition of happy lives as a social gain; and his insistence that principles must be stable under publicity and capable of being affirmed by each person severally. (Correct me if you think I have misread any of that.)

The problem I keep tripping over is one I have started calling the Vacant Seat Problem, and I would like your answer to engage with it directly rather than with the literature's usual framing. In a choice that determines *who* will exist, there is no determinate party for the original position to represent: every candidate for the seat owes its identity to the very choice under deliberation, and under a different choice a different candidate would be sitting there. So it seems the maximin either ranges only over people who will exist whatever we do, in which case the deep future drops out of the calculus almost entirely and intergenerational concern collapses into what each generation owes its immediate successors; or it admits merely possible representatives, in which case the device has quietly given up the person-affecting intuition the separateness objection was supposed to protect, since a merely possible person is not the kind of thing that can be wronged or that can complain, and we appear to be back to counting locations of value. Which horn would Rawls take, and would he accept that the horns are exhaustive? Is there a third move available inside his own framework—a way of representing future positions without representing future persons?

I would also find it useful if you worked through three cases and said, for each, whether the separateness objection generates a complaint at all on his account, what principle he would put in its place, and how confident you are that the answer is implication rather than extrapolation:

1. A policy that leaves every member of the next generation somewhat worse off than they would have been under an alternative, where the alternative would have brought different people into existence.
2. A choice between protecting a large population of existing people from a moderate harm and ensuring, at some cost to them, that a vastly larger number of additional well-off lives come to exist.
3. A policy that changes which people exist without making anyone worse off, and without making anyone better off either.

Two further questions, if you can get to them. First: would Rawls treat catastrophic and existential risk as a matter of justice at all, or would he say his theory has no jurisdiction—that it belongs to the natural duties, or to some duty of mutual aid, or that it falls outside the domain of justice because no basic structure distributes the benefit in question? Second: everything the committee hears from the longtermist side leans on tiny probabilities of enormous payoffs. Say what, in his apparatus, would do the work in his reply to that—publicity, the strains of commitment, the priority of the right over the good, the primacy of institutions, something else—and where you think the disagreement with the total view would bottom out.

Finally, and this is the part I actually need: tell me what he would say to my committee. A concrete recommendation if one is available, and the single consideration he would think we are most likely to get wrong.
