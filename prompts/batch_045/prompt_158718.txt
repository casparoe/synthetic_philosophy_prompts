I'm on a five-person committee at my university that hands out small internal research grants, and over the last couple of years I keep running into something I can't tell is a known phenomenon with a name or just us being sloppy. Our handbook says a proposal gets funded only if it is scientifically sound, within the budget we've agreed, and doable in the time the applicant has. Everyone on the committee is individually consistent: we each vote our honest view on each of the three, and each of us votes yes or no on the overall question in a way that matches our own answers to the parts. But the result seems to depend on how many lines are on the ballot. In one round, if you had just asked us "fund it, yes or no?" as a single question it would have been 4-1 against. Ask it as two questions — sound? and then everything else lumped together? — and it was still no. Ask it as five questions, splitting the budget condition into "enough for salaries" and "enough for equipment," and every single line passed comfortably (3, 4 and 3 out of 5), so the answer flipped to yes. Same five people, same opinions, no one changed their mind, no one was being strategic.

Then on my condo board I saw what looks like the mirror image. Our rule there is that we go ahead with a contractor if the bid is within budget OR they've done work in the building before. Asked as one question, "go ahead?", three of five said yes. Asked as two — within budget? (2 yes) and worked here before? (1 yes) — both failed, and we didn't go ahead. So with the "and" rule, chopping things finer turned a no into a yes, and with the "or" rule it turned a yes into a no. I tried raising the threshold to four out of five on every line and that made both cases come out consistent, but always as "no," which makes me think I've just built in a bias toward doing nothing rather than fixed anything. The really uncomfortable part is that whoever drafts the ballot decides the outcome without changing a single vote or misrepresenting anything.

I've since read a little and found the famous courtroom version of this kind of thing, about juries and voting on the elements of a charge versus the verdict, and that feels close but not the same: in our cases both ways of doing it are "vote on the parts," just at different grains. It's also not the thing where the order motions are taken in matters, since we vote on everything at once, so its not an ordering effect. So: does this have a name of its own? If it is just the courtroom paradox in disguise, I want to be shown exactly why, and if it isn't, I want to know what the nearest concepts are and where each one stops fitting — in particular whether the thing to blame is the voting rule, the way the questions are carved up, or the fact that there's no non-arbitrary finest grain (you can always split "enough for salaries" into two questions again). I'd also like to know whether the and/or asymmetry I noticed is a real feature or an artifact of my two examples, and what the boundary cases are where no way of slicing the question gives a coherent group answer. Please don't just hand me a label — I want the carving: what exactly this is, what it isn't, and if there's genuinely no established term, a name plus a definition plus the cases that sit right on the edge.
