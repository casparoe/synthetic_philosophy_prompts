hey — I write novels, and I've got a library event coming up for the paperback of my last one and I want a teaching plan for one bit of it, not an explanation.

Context: there's a scene where my protagonist Ada gets offered a bet. One urn, 90 balls. She's told exactly 30 are red; the other 60 are black or yellow in some ratio nobody will tell her. She'd rather bet on red than on black. Then, later in the same scene, she'd rather bet on black-or-yellow than on red-or-yellow. That's the whole scene. Readers keep writing in saying either (a) this is a continuity error, Ada's just being thick, or (b) fine, she's a cautious person, end of. Both readings bore me, and honestly I could of cut the scene, but now I have to stand up in front of forty people who've all read the book, none of them economists, two or three of whom have definately Googled "paradox" on their phone on the way in. I have about twenty minutes including questions.

So: plan the twenty minutes for me. Sections, in this order:

**The sequence** (~350 words) — the order the pieces have to arrive in, and what genuinely depends on what, not just a list of things to mention. My instinct is that "there are two different kinds of not-knowing" has to land before I say anything about urns, and that the bit where her two choices contradict each other should come after the room has committed to one of the choices out loud. Tell me if I've got that backwards, and what actually has to be in place before anything else is.

**The urn** (~200 words) — whether to keep the urn or swap in something from the book (a bookie who won't disclose the field, an audited raffle, a lottery that looks rigged). What the carrying example must have — a known number, a missing number, and the missing number staying missing — and one thing it must not have.

**The extra urn** (~200 words) — the bit I care most about, and it's not from the book, I'd be inventing it for the talk. Same 90 balls, same 30 red, but the remaining 60 get split by a notarised machine drawing uniformly over the possible splits: she knows the distribution of the compositions, just not which one. Is that still ambiguity, or does knowing the second-order numbers dissolve the whole thing? And where in the sequence does it go — payoff, or a distraction I should cut? Bonus: what if instead the composition was picked by somebody who wants her to lose.

**What they'll get wrong** (~400 words) — the misconceptions, with the exact point in the sequence to kill each one. The ones I'm braced for: "that's just risk aversion"; "she must have some guess, so average it" (this is the one I think matters most — isn't the point that no single guess produces both choices?); "then she can be money-pumped / Dutch-booked, so it's a bug not a preference" (I'm not sure I can answer that in twenty minutes — tell me where to place it and whether "she'd just refuse to play at all" is a cop-out); "she avoids the unknown urn whichever colour you offer, so what's paradoxical" (I think I need to be ready to point at the complement bets specifically, and I might be the confused one); and someone mixing it up with the Allais thing. Plus the novelist-specific one: that this is a character note about Ada being timid rather than something structural about missing numbers. Give me anything I'm missing too.

**The check** (~120 words) — one question at the end that shows the idea landed rather than that the room got sleepy. My guess is asking them to design a version where Ada takes the ambiguous urn and say what had to change, but maybe that's too much for a room like this.

**What I refuse to explain** (~120 words) — what I should explicitly say out loud that I'm not going into. I'm not naming decision theories and I'm not recommending anything to read, it's a library not a seminar, but tell me what else to close the door on, and what to do with the two phone-Googlers when they interrupt.
