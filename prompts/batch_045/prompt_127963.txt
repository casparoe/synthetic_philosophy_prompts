I'm in the middle of writing up the third chapter of my PhD, on a freshwater fish complex in two basins that got connected by a navigation canal back in the fifties, and I need to send the chapter to Dr. Halvorsen on my committee before I do. She's the person in our department who says no to species descriptions, and she calls herself a pluralist whenever anyone brings up the species problem, but her actual verdicts look way more consistent than that and I want to know what she'll do with mine. Here's her track record as well as I can reconstruct it from her papers, her reviews, and things she's said in seminars: two warblers with a couple-hundred-kilometre hybrid zone, very different plumages, hybrids common and apparently as fit as either parent — one species, and her line was that a hybrid zone is one species with a cline in it rather than two species touching; two host-specific flies that nobody can tell apart under a microscope, no fixed markers, but they mate on different host plants and maybe one pair in a hundred is mixed — two species; an all-female parthenogenetic lizard complex of hybrid origin — yes, a real species, and she got visibly annoyed at a seminar speaker who said asexuals can't have species; a sexual species of known hybrid origin that's now reproductively isolated from both parents — yes, a good species, with some remark about how a species' genealogy doesn't have to be a tree; a planktonic foraminiferan lineage sampled continuously through a million years of graded change — the lineage is real, the chronospecies boundaries are conventional, don't ask her where exactly to cut; two named reef corals that turn out to be genetically and ecologically the same thing — sink them, and she didn't care that one name was 150 years old; and two deep-sea worms known from three specimens — she refused to rule at all and said come back with more material.

My case is this. There's a deep-bodied lake form and a streamlined riverine form, currently one species, that were in separate basins until the canal opened. Where they meet now, in the canal and in the lake outlet, about one pair in ten is mixed, and the hybrids are viable, fertile and backcross freely. Transplants I did show each form survives best in its own habitat and hybrids survive worst in both parental habitats but better than either parent in the weedy middle stretch of the canal, where they now outnumber both parents and mate with each other roughly seventy per cent of the time, mostly on colour and courtship cues that are learned by imprinting rather than being a fixed genetic preference. The genome is basically homogenised, background Fst around 0.04 and no fixed differences anywhere, except for one big inversion on chromosome seven and a couple of smaller regions that are deeply divergent — molecular clock puts the inversion at over a million years — and nearly fixed differently in the two forms; mtDNA shows nothing but frequency differences. With a discriminant function on morphology I can assign ninety-five per cent of adults correctly, but there isn't a single character you could put in a field key. The canal population has been there for maybe fifty generations, I've been calling it a new species in my drafts, and the state endangered list I'm being asked about excludes hybrids, so whether the canal thing is a species or a hybrid swarm decides whether it gets any protection before the dredging permit goes through.

So: what does Halvorsen say — one species, two, or three? More usefully, what are the two or three rules that fit all seven of those precedents about equally well, what does each of them predict about the lake form, the riverine form and the canal population, which one do you think she's actually running given how she talks, and if you could put exactly one more case in front of her to tell the rules apart, what would it be? I'd also love to know how I should pitch the discussion section so she signs off, because my committee meets in four weeks and I have definately not written the discussion yet.
