You are a political theorist who works at the intersection of democratic theory and public law, with a research record on self-determination, the boundaries of the demos, and the moral limits of state authority. You are careful and unsentimentally precise: you never let a claim about what is legal stand in for a claim about what is legitimate, you distinguish the right to secede from the wisdom of seceding, and you are willing to say when two positions that recommend the same policy rest on incompatible principles.

I am designing the central handout for a graduate seminar on secession and self-determination, and I want that handout to be a diagnostic instrument rather than a survey of views. The deliverable I need from you is a decision tree: something a student can enter at a fixed starting commitment and walk down, one question at a time, until they arrive at a named position on whether a group may take a territory out of an existing state. Assume my students know roughly what happened in Scotland, Catalonia and Quebec but not the labels of the academic literature, and assume they answer honestly rather than strategically.

Before you draft the tree, spend some time brainstorming: list the candidate discriminating questions first, check which of them actually separate the positions (discard the ones every view answers the same way, and note that you discarded them), and check whether the order in which the questions are asked changes where anyone lands. Then build the final tree. Work from concepts and the cases described below; I do not want citations, reading lists or bibliographies anywhere in it.

Specifications for the instrument:

1. Fixed starting node. Begin from a case everyone in the seminar will accept as a candidate: a territorially concentrated group within an established, broadly democratic state now demands sovereign independence, and the demand is not a cover for ethnic cleansing, for annexation by a neighbouring power, or for installing a dictatorship. Everything downstream branches off this.

2. Size and shape. Ten to twelve branch nodes, twelve to sixteen leaves. Every branch node must be phrased as a question a student can answer from their own instincts about a concrete case or a plain claim, without already knowing which position it leads to — no node may simply ask whether they believe in a right to self-determination, or whether they are a nationalist. Where a node is best put as a case, make the case concrete and brief.

3. The distinctions that must do the work. I do not care much about the usual taxonomies; I want the tree built around these, and I want them phrased in your own terms:
   (a) whether a serious grievance (injustice, rights-violation, breach of an autonomy guarantee) is a precondition for any right to exit, or whether a persistent majority in a territory is enough on its own;
   (b) who composes the constituency entitled to vote — all current residents, residents with a birth or long-duration tie, members of the national group wherever they live, or residents minus those who moved in from the rest of the parent state;
   (c) the "matryoshka" question: if a region may exit, may a sub-unit of that region (a city, an island group, a border district) opt to stay with the parent state, and does answering yes for the region force you to answer yes for the sub-unit;
   (d) the layered-sovereignty question: whether secession that also strips residents of a supranational citizenship and market membership is a morally different act from secession that keeps them, and whether a group has a right to the second but not the first;
   (e) the dead-hand problem: whether a vote binds those who did not cast it and those not yet born, whether a majority may reverse a secession within a decade, and whether the right is one-shot or continuing;
   (f) the "rump state" question: whether the economic or political viability of the remainder is a moral constraint on exit, or only a piece of prudence the seceders owe nobody an account of;
   (g) demographic drift: whether a majority produced largely by internal migration from the rest of the parent state grounds the same claim as one produced by conversion of the resident population;
   (h) thresholds and repetition: simple majority once, supermajority, two consecutive votes, and what a near miss does to the claim — does it die, or does it reheat forever.

4. Leaves that must appear. Locate, under whatever labels you think accurate: the remedial-right-only view (no right without injustice); the plebiscitary or primary-right view (a persistent territorial majority is sufficient); the ascriptive or national-cultural view (peoples, not territories, hold the right); the constitutionalist or legalist view (no moral right outside what the constitution provides); the middle view associated with the Supreme Court of Canada's 1998 Quebec Secession Reference — no unilateral right, but a clear majority on a clear question imposes a duty to negotiate in good faith — together with the federal clarity legislation that followed it; a view on which unilateral declaration is sometimes justified even where the constitution forbids it; and a pooled- or shared-sovereignty leaf on which the right is discharged by something short of full independence.

5. Logical space first, positions second. Tell me which combinations of answers your nodes permit, which of those combinations are actually occupied by a defensible view, and which are empty — and for each empty cell give one line on why: is it incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unoccupied because nobody has worked it out? Give every leaf one sentence on its single most awkward commitment, including the leaves nobody holds.

6. Pairs your tree cannot separate. I expect some: I suspect the remedial-only view and the constitutionalist-with-a-duty-to-negotiate view both reject Catalonia's 2017 declaration while giving incompatible reasons, and I suspect the plebiscitary and the ascriptive views coincide on almost everything except the franchise question. Flag each such pair explicitly, say what further case or question would split them, and do not paper over the collision by inventing a distinction that does no work.

7. Run the three real cases through the finished tree. Scotland: the 2014 vote held with the UK Parliament's agreement, and the position as I understand it that the Scottish Parliament cannot authorise another on its own. Catalonia: the 2017 declaration, the absence of an agreed legal route, and the disputed gap between the parliamentary majority for independence and the share of the vote. Quebec: two referendums, the second of which failed narrowly, followed by the Reference and the clarity statute. For each, say where the tree sends a student who answers as the historical record would suggest, and say plainly where the tree's verdict is driven by an unresolved node rather than by a settled principle.

8. Format. Give me the tree as an indented or numbered structure I can typeset on one or two pages, each node with its question, each leaf with its name and its awkward commitment, plus at most a page of notes covering items 5 through 7 and a short list of the candidate questions you rejected in brainstorming and why.
