Hi — take this as a professional philosopher of physics who's spent real time on the metaphysics of time. Don't just answer off the cuff: start by writing out, informally, the premises the Rietdijk–Putnam argument actually needs, then go hunting for which of them could be false.

I've ended up accepting eternalism — the block universe — and a big part of why is the Rietdijk–Putnam argument: simultaneity is frame relative, so there's a frame in which my now is simultaneous with an event in Andromeda that I'd call next week, and another in which it's simultaneous with something I'd call last year, so everything I call past and future is already real. I want to know what would count against this. Not the stuff I can shrug off — the real defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward.

Some things I've been chewing on, wich you should feel free to use or to tell me are confused:

- I've seen an objection that the argument quietly needs the "is real at the same time as" relation to be transitive, and that the relation relativity actually gives you (spacelike separation, being co-present) isn't transitive, so the chaining from my now to Andromeda's next week to Andromeda's next decade doesn't go through. Is that fatal, or just awkward?
- The chaining also seems to need unbounded boosts plus a universe big enough and long-lived enough that you can reach arbitrarily far. If space were finite or compact — say a 3-torus, and suppose (I might be wrong here) that requiring the identification not to produce closed timelike curves singles out a unique rest frame — then the argument looks like it delivers at best a thick local slab, not the whole block. Is that a defeater for the argument, or only for its scope?
- And if future physics handed us a dynamical preferred foliation, laws that are simplest in one frame, does eternalism lose its main positive argument even though nothing directly contradicts it?

Be honest about whether eternalism has been defended into a corner where it's true by definition (if "real" just means "exists at some time", the whole dispute goes verbal) and whether that's a problem. That verbal-collapse worry would move me more than any empirical development. Also say which defeaters an eternalist would concede as counting against the Rietdijk–Putnam argument specifically, as opposed to merely against the alternatives. Separate the settled physics from the genuinely contested metaphysics, don't present a contested view as the consensus among people who work on this, and flag where specialists would actually disagree with each other.
