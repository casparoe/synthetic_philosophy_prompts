I sit on the Evidence Review Board of the Consortium for Confirmatory Behavioral Science. The Consortium maintains a public Evidence Ledger: findings are marked *replicated*, *failed*, or *indeterminate*, and the marking determines whether Consortium money can be used for follow-up work on that finding. A case has come to us that has exposed how much of our practice rests on an unexamined notion of what it is for two experiments to test the same mechanism, and I have to circulate a decision memo before our vote in eleven days.

The case. A decade-old, widely taught finding — call it the weight–seriousness effect: participants who hold a heavy clipboard while reading a job application rate the candidate as more serious than participants who hold a light one — went through a multi-lab confirmatory replication under a pre-registered protocol. The pooled behavioral effect was essentially zero, with an interval excluding the original effect size. The original authors argue that this is not a failed replication but a failed test. Their claim, they say, was about a specific sensorimotor mechanism in which a barely noticeable heaviness is incidentally encoded as importance; the replication's clipboard (2.4 kg, against 0.9 kg in the original) was heavy enough to be noticed and to cost effort, and so engaged a different mechanism, one in which the weight is read as a demand or a discomfort rather than taken as a cue to seriousness. In support they point to one replication site that found the predicted neural signature in the region they had hypothesized, despite the null on behavior.

Please draft the Board's memo. It should argue from the structure of the case rather than from the literature — our Board includes two statisticians and a philosopher, and none of them will read citations — and it should contain:

1. A bottom line, stated in the first two sentences: how the weight–seriousness finding is to be marked in the Ledger now, and what we say to both teams, with dates.

2. A general rule for mechanism identity that the Board can apply to future cases. One member has circulated a proposal I want the memo to adjudicate rather than restate. She proposes three things: that a result counts as an anomaly for a mechanism claim only if the mechanism's own description predicts that result's absence or its opposite; that every confirmatory protocol declare in advance an *invariance class* — the range of procedural variation in stimulus magnitude, modality, population, setting, and dependent measure that the claimants take to be mechanism-preserving; and that a "different mechanism" reply raised after the fact, where the two mechanisms were never given intervention profiles that would separate them, be treated as what she calls anomaly laundering: relabeling the mechanism so that a contrary result stops counting against the claim. Adopt it, amend it, or reject it, and if you amend it say exactly where.

3. A position on the neural site. On the interventionist view most of us were trained on — a component is constitutively part of a mechanism if intervening on it changes the mechanism's output and intervening on the output changes it — does a positive at the neural level together with a null at the behavioral level constitute partial replication, evidence that the mechanism was engaged but its output blocked, or evidence that the neural signature was never doing the work the original authors thought? Say which, and say what it should mean for the Ledger marking.

4. An honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. The one I cannot shake is that mechanisms are discovered rather than stipulated: pre-declared invariance classes freeze mechanism descriptions at their naivest stage and threaten to punish exactly the post-hoc insight that tells us a manipulation was doing something other than we assumed, and a strict rule hands victories to replicators by definitional fiat and drives original authors out of the Consortium's process. Identify which part of this actually bites, and let the recommendation absorb it rather than wave it away.

5. A transition provision. The weight–seriousness protocol long predates any declaration requirement, and several hundred legacy findings in the Ledger rest on equally undeclared mechanism descriptions. Say what we do about them and over what period.

Aim for roughly a thousand to twelve hundred words, in the Board's voice, addressed to the other six members, with the recommendation and the rule written so that a member who disagrees can still see what she is being asked to vote on.
