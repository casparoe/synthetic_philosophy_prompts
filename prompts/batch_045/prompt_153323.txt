I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I have just published a long piece arguing that regulatory cost-benefit analysis never actually avoids interpersonal comparisons of well-being: it performs them at every step and then disowns them in its own methodological preamble. On the strength of that piece I have been invited to give the closing keynote at the annual meeting of a professional association of practitioners of regulatory benefit-cost analysis — forty-five minutes plus twenty minutes of questions, and I want the Q&A to be the part that lands.

The room will be roughly a hundred and twenty people, and most of them are not philosophers. The core is government and consulting economists who write regulatory impact analyses for a living, plus several people who have staffed a central regulatory review office and are used to defending analyses against legal challenge and against charges of overreach. Their working commitments: willingness to pay as the measure of value, the compensation test as the criterion for a policy improvement, revealed preference over stated introspection, ordinal utility, and a trained suspicion of anything that smells like cardinal utility compared across heads. Distribution is something they believe belongs to the political process, not to the analyst. What they care about is defensibility — in court, under review, and in front of an agency head who does not want a rule struck down because an economist made a philosophical judgment. After the talk there is a responding panel of four: an act-utilitarian moral philosopher who thinks interpersonal comparisons are obviously possible and will suspect I am making a mountain out of a molehill; a capabilities theorist in the Aristotelian line who holds that well-being is not a quantity and that the entire aggregative enterprise is a category mistake; a political theorist who thinks any such comparison is illegitimate exercises of technocratic authority regardless of how it is constructed; and a senior regulatory economist who has spent a career insisting that analysts compare consequences, not people.

My thesis has three parts, and I would like the objections aimed at all three.

First, the exposure claim: any analysis that aggregates across persons relies on interpersonal comparisons, including a willingness-to-pay analysis. What WTP studies measure is a comparison of pockets as much as of welfare, and practitioners already know this, which is why they quietly decline to use raw willingness to pay in some domains, or adjust it, or supplement it with distributional notes attached to the bottom line. My claim is that these are interpersonal comparisons smuggled in under a technical vocabulary, and that owning them would make the practice more honest and more reviewable, not less.

Second, the metaphysical claim, which is the part I expect to draw the most fire: comparisons of well-being are constructed, not discovered. There is no quantity out there, no fact about how much better off one person is than another, that a better measurement technology would reveal. A comparison is something an institution builds for the purpose of a decision, and the test of it is adjudicative rather than evidential. I give that test two clauses, and I need them stress-tested. A comparison is admissible only if it is role-reversal stable — you would keep the same ordering if you imagined yourself occupying each party's position without knowing which one you would get — and only if it can be stated as a reason addressed to the person who fares worst under it, in terms that person could in principle acknowledge as a reason rather than merely as a threat or a preference. I call the conjunction of these the audit condition.

Third, the unit claim: the proper relatum of comparison is not a person's life or total welfare but a person's stake in a particular decision, meaning the difference that the available options make to that person. Comparing stakes rather than lives, I argue, keeps the practice inside a regulator's authority, avoids anything that looks like ranking whole human beings, and defuses the objection that we are weighing people against one another. It also, I think, explains why comparisons feel tractable inside a rulemaking and repulsive in the abstract.

The example I run throughout is a budget-constrained choice between an air quality rule whose benefits are largely reduced mortality risk for older, lower-income urban residents, and a workplace safety rule whose benefits are largely reduced injury risk for younger rural workers. Both are monetized, the first through a value of a statistical life drawn from wage-risk and stated-preference studies, the second through wage premiums for risky work plus medical costs and lost earnings. The ranking flips depending on whether the analysis weights life-years by age, and my claim is that this flip is not a technical detail but an interpersonal comparison conducted in public with the vocabulary turned off.

What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of the position. Give me the objections I will actually get from this room, in the order they will arrive, weighted by how likely each is and how much damage it would do if I handled it badly. Phrase each one the way that person would phrase it, in their own idiom — I want to recognize the question when I hear it, including the version that sounds polite. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in under ninety seconds, and mark explicitly where that reply is weakest, because I would rather know now than discover it from the front of the room. Tell me which objections come from the practitioners and which from each of the four panelists, since I expect the utilitarian and the capabilities theorist to attack opposite parts of my argument and I cannot answer both with the same move.

Three further things. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes, before anyone has asked anything, so that the practitioners stop treating me as an outsider who has come to tell them their methods are corrupt. Identify the single reply that has to land for the whole talk to be worth giving, and tell me what I should do if it fails. And give me the question I am hoping nobody asks, with a note on what honest answer I could give if someone does ask it. Finally: if there is one objection that, arriving early, should make me restructure the last third of the talk on the spot, say which it is and what the restructured ending should be.

Please keep everything at the level of argument and institutional practice. I do not need citations, a reading list, or a bibliography — I am not going to look anything up from the podium, and I would rather have your reasoning than your sources.
