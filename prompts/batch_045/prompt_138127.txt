I direct a small research program at a philanthropic institute, and I have to settle its agenda for the next three years: about six researchers, mostly philosophers with a couple of decision theorists, and no empirical work of our own beyond reanalysis of data that already exists. The program exists because the institute's allocators have to make real-time trade-offs across beneficiaries of very different kinds — people in different societies with different reporting conventions, animals whose evidence base is behavioural rather than testimonial, and, in the background, the possibility that some future beneficiaries will be systems whose architecture makes both of those evidence sources unavailable. The output is supposed to be a comparison protocol: a documented procedure that takes noisy, heterogeneous evidence about well-being and returns something an allocator can rank, together with an honest statement of what the procedure assumes.

Two constraints have been imposed from above and are not negotiable. First, the protocol must be neutral across the main substantive accounts of welfare: it may not presuppose that well-being consists in valenced conscious experience, or in the satisfaction of preferences, or in the instantiation of some objective list. Second, it may not assume full comparability, in the sense of a common zero together with a common unit; the trustees regard that as the assumption that has done the most damage in past cost-benefit practice, and they want comparisons built up from weaker informational permits.

Within those constraints I need a defended ordering, not a survey. Here are the questions I have been circling, so that you can reorder, merge, or reject them rather than reproduce them.

First: whether the components of comparability really separate. As I understand the standard taxonomy, a common unit without a common zero still supports aggregations that trade gains against losses at fixed rates, but not rules that turn on where anyone sits relative to a shared zero; a common zero with only ordinal level comparisons supports maximin-shaped rules but not trade-off rates. If those components are separable, the program's central decision is which one to try to secure, and everything else follows. If every route to one secretly delivers the other, the decision is illusory and the whole shape of the agenda changes. I want that adjudicated.

Second: the creation case. Comparisons that involve bringing a new being into existence require comparing a possible life against there being no subject at all, which is not a point on anyone's scale. Something has to play the role of a neutral level, and I am told that the choice of that level does as much work as the choice of a population principle — that it effectively determines whether the protocol displays repugnant-conclusion-shaped behaviour. Is fixing the neutral level part of the comparison problem at all, or is it a separate problem smuggled in under the same budget?

Third: architectural diversity. For animals we lack self-report; for hypothetical future systems we may lack behaviour in the relevant sense too, and have only structural or functional facts. Is there a principled distinction between the evidence being bad and there being no fact of the matter, and can the protocol say which situation it is in without first settling which theory of welfare is true? If the neutrality constraint makes this question unanswerable in principle, say so and drop it rather than ranking it.

Fourth: calibration of heterogeneous reports. Survey methodologists have vignette-based techniques for correcting the fact that different populations use scale endpoints differently. Is the philosophical residue here just applied measurement error, or does correcting differential scale use already presuppose the cross-population comparability that was supposed to be in question? I suspect a circularity, and I want to know whether it is benign.

What I want back: three or four questions, ranked, and for each a statement of what makes it upstream — whose answers change depending on it — how tractable it is for the people I have in three years with no new data, how neglected it is by those working on adjacent problems, and what would move it up or down the list. Name the criteria you are using to weigh upstream-ness against tractability against neglectedness, and say where they conflict. Add an explicit not-doing list: one or two questions that look central in this area but that you would cut, with the reason — that the answer would not constrain the protocol, that it collapses into a verbal dispute, or that it is really a request for a theory of welfare. Finally, tell me which of my four questions are the same question in different words.
