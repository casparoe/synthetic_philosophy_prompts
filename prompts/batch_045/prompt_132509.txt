Hey — Im a science-and-ideas journalist, and I'm working on a long feature about how superintelligent AI systems ought to talk to human beings, given that they're likely to be much better at changing our minds than we are at noticing it happen. There's one corner of this I want argued properly, because every draft I commission slides into the same hand-wringing about "manipulation," and that's not the piece I'm writing.

Here's the setup. Call it doxastic debt: when a system vastly smarter than you tells you something and you believe it, you aquire a belief whose justification you can never personally repay. You can't retrace the reasoning, you can't audit the steps, and you have no way to tell apart "this is true" from "this system is scary-smart and I am not." You're not borrowing an opinion, you're borrowing a mind — and the bill comes due the moment you have to act on the belief, defend it to a colleague, or notice that the system got it wrong. Layer on top of that the familiar asymmetry from the misinformation literature, where a retraction never quite undoes the work the original claim already did — except here there may never be a retraction, because you'd never find out.

The position I want you to argue: such a system should practice deliberate under-persuasion. Not dumbing down and not lying, but systematically prefering to hand you a weaker argument you can actually check over its own strongest argument, withholding the decisive consideration when that consideration is one you couldn't verify even in principle, and generally aiming to leave you with beliefs you could have arrived at yourself rather than beliefs you merely inherited. Call it the checkability constraint: the system should treat your inability to audit its reasoning as a cost to be minimized, the engineering way it treats latency or error rate as costs.

Argue for this as hard as you can. I want the strongest steelman, not a balanced overview — I'm getting the objections from other people. Along the way I'd like you to deal with: the obvious charge that withholding your best argument is paternalism, treating someone's autonomy as a thing to be managed rather than respected; the cases where the stakes are enormous and the clock is short (someone about to do something catastrophic, a resident alone at three in the morning); whether the constraint even coheres when the gap between you and the system is in principle unbridgeable rather than merely large; and whether aiming at checkability is really different from aiming at truth, or whether those two come apart somewhere interesting.

One more wrinkle I keep turning over, and I want you to take a position on it: if the checkability constraint is right, it seems to commit you to something strange about the system's own self-presentation — that it should probably sound less impressive than it could, maybe even a little boring, which is a design requirement almost nobody is asking for. Push on whether that really follows, or whether I've muddled two separate things.

No citations, no reading lists, no "further reading" — I've got a fact-checker and a researcher for that, and what I need from you is the argument, not the apparatus. Write it like you're talking to a clever editor who's skeptical but fair.
