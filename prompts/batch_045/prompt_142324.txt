I write literary fiction, and a few weeks ago at a writers' residency the conversation at dinner turned to the novel I've been drafting, most of which is written in close third person and consists of nothing but the rendering of what goes on inside a character's head. A philosopher staying at the same house — I'd place him loosely among those who hold phenomenal consciousness to be an illusion — cut across me with the line "consciousness is an illusion," and then used it for two quite different jobs. First, to say that my whole enterprise is the cataloguing of a hallucination: the qualitative what-it's-like-ness I imagine myself transcribing is not there to be transcribed. Second, when I described a subplot of mine in which the adult children of a dead woman commission a machine emulation of her and then argue about what, if anything, they owe it, to say that the question dissolves, because nothing is conscious in the sense my subplot requires.

I've been turning the sentence over since, and I cannot tell how much of it is a platitude about a word and how much is a large metaphysical claim, so I'd be grateful for a menu. Please set out the distinct precise theses it might be expressing, from the near-trivial — that several ordinary uses of "conscious" pick out only wakefulness, responsiveness or access rather than anything qualitative, or that our folk concept is simply confused — through the doctrinal core — that our introspective mechanisms represent our own sensory states as possessing intrinsic, ineffable, intrinsic-natured qualitative characters that they do not in fact possess — to the radical: that nothing is conscious in any sense at all, that first-person reports carry no special authority, that our sense of having an inner life is best compared to a benign confabulation, and that the illusion in question is not itself a conscious seeming, on pain of the view's reintroducing through the back door exactly what it denies. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; say which one a philosopher who deploys the sentence as a conversation-closer most likely means; and, most importantly, say which reading would actually do the work he wanted it to do. Does any of them really threaten the writing of interiority, given that what a novelist mostly renders is arguably a character's attention, access, belief, memory and affect rather than bare qualitative character? And does any of them license the conclusion that the emulation is owed nothing, or do most versions of the view leave whatever matters morally about her entirely intact?

My own suspicion comes from a trick of the trade that I have started calling borrowed seeming. Give a reader four pages about the taste of a pear in a character's mouth and they will come away convinced they have been shown an inner life, although they have only been given marks on a page and have not tasted what she tastes; the sense of being shown seems to be borrowed from the reader's own experience while being attributed to the character's. I have wondered whether this is a friend to his view — a case in which representational machinery manufactures the appearance of phenomenality very cheaply, with no new qualitative property instantiated anywhere — or whether it quietly supports the other side, since the appearance seems possible only against a background of a real experiencer doing the imagining, which would mean the illusionist's own illusion keeps needing a phenomenal substrate the theory says is not there. Please answer in continuous prose, in the manner of a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, but with the readings separated clearly enough that I could hand them back to him as a menu.
