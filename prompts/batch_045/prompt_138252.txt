I lead a four-person scenario team at a multilateral guarantee agency that insures long-dated infrastructure and sovereign-backed debt. Two or three times a week we must attach a thirty-year political-continuity assumption to an exposure: the probability that the political entity whose obligation we have guaranteed, or a successor willing and able to honour it, will still be in a position to do so in thirty years. These assumptions feed a pricing model, and an error in one is typically discovered decades later and by someone else. Our board reviews a sample of them each year and asks why two analysts looking at the same country produced numbers thirty percentage points apart.

We want to move to something like the outside view: rather than assembling a narrative about a country and reasoning forward from it, place the case in a class of comparable cases, take that class's observed distribution of durations as an anchor, and then adjust, sparingly and defensibly, for what is specific to the case. Every attempt we have made has failed in one of two ways. Either the class is so broad that the base rate carries no information, as with states or empires taken wholesale, or the analyst narrows it until it contains the case and two of its neighbours, at which point we are doing the inside view with extra steps. We also have no principled answer to how much adjustment to permit before the anchor has been quietly abandoned.

Our raw material is a private archive of roughly two hundred historical polities, each with a start year and an end year, coded independently by two analysts. Inter-rater agreement on end years is about 0.7, and the disagreements are not transcription errors: they are genuine disputes about whether a conquest, a dynastic change, a constitutional refounding, or a relocation of the capital ended the entity. We also work under an internal Successor-Continuity Convention, under which an entity counts as persisting to year T if at T some polity exercises effective control over at least half the territory held at T0, is recognised by at least a third of the states that recognised the original, and still operates at least one institution founded before T0.

Please write the procedure we could actually run. I need, at minimum:

1. A fixed sequence of questions, each with an explicit criterion for a sufficient answer and a stated default when the answer cannot be obtained in the time available.

2. A requirement that the outcome variable be fixed before any reference class is chosen, including a rule for resolving coder disagreement about historical end years, and an explicit statement of what the Successor-Continuity Convention settles by fiat rather than by evidence.

3. A rule for nominating candidate reference classes and adjudicating among them: how many to nominate, how to record the ones rejected, what to do when two defensible classes yield conflicting base rates, and what to do when the only class with enough cases is also the least causally relevant.

4. A step that asks how the archive's own selection biases the base rates. We have start and end years only for polities that left dated administrative records, and the capacity to keep such records plausibly correlates with whatever makes polities durable. Say what, if anything, can be done about this inside the procedure.

5. A rule about duration already survived. My team is divided over whether a polity that has lasted two centuries should be credited with any additional expected remaining life on that account: whether the conditional distribution is roughly memoryless, as for an exponential duration, or whether survival is evidence of durable institutions and should raise the estimate. Tell me what the choice depends on, what could be settled from our own archive, and what to do in the meantime.

6. A rule for the adjustment step: a cap on how far the final number may depart from the class base rate, who may authorise exceeding it, and what must be written down when they do.

7. Stopping rules: the conditions under which the honest output is no defensible number, and how that output should be represented inside a pricing model that requires a number.

8. An explicit list of the points at which the procedure hands the decision to a named person because the question is one of values rather than of fact; for example, whether persistence of a legal persona matters when the population it governed has dispersed.

The procedure is expected to be run in about two hours per case by analysts with graduate training in political science who can run a regression but have no formal training in forecasting. Its output must fit on one page plus a short audit trail, and it must be defensible both to our board and to an external auditor five years later. Please do not propose that we acquire data we do not have.

It must also handle the following, which are drawn from live cases: a federal union in which one member region is likely to secede peacefully, where each successor would probably honour the debt but the original entity would no longer exist; a low-lying island state much of whose territory may be uninhabitable within the horizon, whose government intends to continue in exile and to keep servicing its obligations; a supranational union of sovereign states, for which the archive holds at most a handful of analogues; and a case in which our own guarantee is large enough relative to the entity's economy that publishing a low assumption could itself precipitate the failure it predicts.

Finally, run the finished procedure on three of these cases, showing the audit trail as an analyst would fill it in, and for at least one of them show me where the procedure delivers an answer my desk will find uncongenial and argue why we should accept it. Close by stating plainly what this procedure cannot do. Please reason from the concepts themselves; I do not need citations, sources, or suggestions for further reading.
