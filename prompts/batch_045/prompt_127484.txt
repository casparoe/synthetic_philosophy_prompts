QUESTION (answer all four parts)

A large body of work in experimental philosophy reports that people's answers to philosophical vignettes vary with their cultural background: whether a subject in a Gettier-style case counts as knowing, whether an agent who brings about a side effect counts as having done it intentionally, and so on. Some philosophers claim this should make us stop treating intuitions as evidence.

(a) Set out the strongest version of that skeptical argument. Be explicit about what extra premise is needed to get from "people differ" to "intuitions are not to be trusted," and be explicit about what the conclusion is supposed to be. [7]

(b) Set out and evaluate two replies available to someone who wants to keep using intuitions as evidence. [8]

(c) Now suppose — this is invented, not from the literature — that a large pre-registered replication project finds that culture is actually a weak predictor of people's answers, and that most of the variation is within cultures: individual differences, mood, and the order in which the vignettes are presented do most of the work. Would this help the skeptic or hurt them? Say why, and say whether your answer to (a) needs to change. [6]

(d) The literature tests a few dozen vignettes. Philosophers rely on far more than that. Does the smallness and unrepresentativeness of the tested sample matter for how much weight the literature deserves, and how should a philosopher who wants to keep using intuitions respond to it? [4]

STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) The argument can't just be "people disagree, therefore nobody's intuition is reliable," because there is no argument from disagreement alone to the unreliability of a faculty. If there were, every disagreement in science would show that perception or inference is unreliable, which is absurd. What the skeptic needs is a bridge premise: that the disagreement is systematic, and caused by something irrelevant to the truth of the matter — features of the subject's background rather than features of the case. Once you have that, the intuition's causal history is off-track with respect to the fact it is supposed to be about, and a belief you know to have been produced by an off-track process gives you no reason to think it's true. So variation is only evidence of unreliability when it comes with some account of what is producing the variation.

The conclusion, stated carefully, is not that intuitions are never evidence. It's conditional: intuitions produced by the suspect factors carry no weight for the people whose intuitions they are, and the vignettes in the literature are our evidence for which factors are suspect. This also means the argument is partly empirical and depends on the findings holding up. The fact that some of these studies have failed to replicate is a reason for caution, not a refutation, since a failed replication just shows that that study was badly run.

(b) First reply: the expertise defense. The empirical claim is that the variation in the literature is a feature of untutored, hurried folk responses, and that people with philosophical training — or simply with more time, clearer cases and less pressure — either don't show the variation or show much less of it. The normative claim is that the intuitions that matter for philosophy are the trained ones, so even if the folk data are a mess nothing follows about philosophical practice. This reply is in decent shape empirically, and it has the advantage of being testable. The obvious worry is how we would tell a genuine expert intuition from the shared ideology of a discipline, and whether "expert" is just doing the work of "person who agrees with me."

Second reply: restrictionism. On this reply the variation is real but harmless, because what the studies are really detecting is that different populations are operating with different concepts — the concept of knowledge in play in one population is not the concept in play in another, and the same goes for intentional action. But if that's right there is no disagreement left to explain, only different subject matters, and so nothing for the skeptic's argument to get a grip on. The upshot for practice is that philosophers should restrict the claims they make to their own conceptual scheme and stop presenting results about one community's concepts as if they were universal conceptual truths. I think this is the stronger of the two replies. It explains why the variation shows up precisely in the disputed, borderline cases rather than the easy ones, and it doesn't require us to say that anybody involved is making a mistake. Unlike the expertise defense it also doesn't rest on any contested empirical claim about training, so it can't be knocked over by the next round of studies.

(c) This helps the skeptic, and I think it helps them more than the original finding did. The skeptic's argument was never really about culture in particular; culture was only ever a stand-in for any causal factor that isn't the fact of the matter. So if the variation turns out to be within cultures and driven by mood and order of presentation, the bridge premise from (a) is satisfied just as well — arguably better, since mood and the order the questions come in are even more obviously irrelevant to whether the agent acted intentionally than someone's cultural background is. A general instability is also worse than a local one: if the variation had stayed between cultures you could always say that at least each population is internally consistent and that philosophy should be done relative to a population. If the instability is everywhere, that move is gone. Nothing in my answer to (a) needs to change; the skeptic still needs the bridge premise, but this finding supplies it more cheaply.

(d) This matters a lot and it cuts against the skeptic. The tested vignettes aren't a random sample of the intuitions philosophers actually use — researchers go looking for cases where variation is likely, which is a straight selection effect — so there's no good inference from "these cases show variation" to "intuitions in general are unreliable." The right response for a philosopher who wants to keep using intuitions isn't to dismiss the literature but to make the question local: test the specific intuitions that a specific argument actually leans on, rather than trying to settle the reliability of intuition in the abstract. I'd add that the general question "are intuitions reliable?" is probably ill-posed anyway, since intuitions aren't one faculty with one reliability.
