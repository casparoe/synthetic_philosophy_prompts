A friend and I argued late into the evening about whether Gödel's theorems say anything at all outside of mathematics, and the longer we went on the more I suspected we were using one slogan to mean three different things. The phrase doing all the work was "there are truths that cannot be proved." He used it to defend three claims at once: first, that the human mind cannot be a formal system, because whatever system you model a mathematician with, she can somehow see that its Gödel sentence holds while the system itself cannot derive it — the argument associated with Lucas and later taken up by Penrose; second, that a final theory in physics could never be complete, since any such theory would be a formal system rich enough to encode arithmetic and would therefore have undecidable sentences of its own; and third, that any legal or moral code must be both incomplete and unable to certify itself, which he summarized as "there will always be a case the rules don't settle, and the rulebook can never vouch for its own consistency."

I pushed back with the two half-remembered objections I had. One was that the word "true" in "true but unprovable" seems to mean true in one particular intended structure, and that if you stop privileging the standard model of arithmetic, then for all the first theorem literally says, the negation of the Gödel sentence holds in other perfectly respectable models — so the slogan's appeal to truth is doing more work than the theorem licenses. The other was that the second theorem can't simply mean "consistency can never be established," because I'm fairly confident there are ordinary, accepted consistency proofs for Peano arithmetic that use something like transfinite induction up to ε₀ — proofs that are not formalizable inside the theory but are proofs nonetheless. I also hazarded that the Gödel sentence is not really an artificial self-referential curiosity, being equivalent within the theory to the statement of that theory's own consistency, which makes the first and second theorems feel like one fact in two costumes. He dismissed the model point as "a technicality philosophers invented to dodge the conclusion," and the consistency proof as "changing the subject, since any such proof assumes principles at least as doubtful as what it establishes." When I pressed him on whether you could escape by adding the undecidable sentence as a new axiom and iterating, he said something about a ladder of theories through the computable ordinals, mentioned Turing's ordinal logics, and claimed the whole procedure "breaks down" in a way that reveals something deep about mathematical knowledge. I couldn't follow the last part at all.

So I don't want a verdict on who won; I want the slogan taken apart. Please set out, as a list I can work through, the distinct precise theses "there are truths that cannot be proved" might be expressing: the ones that follow from the first and second incompleteness theorems under their actual hypotheses (say something about consistency versus soundness, and about effective axiomatizability, as you go); the ones that follow from nearby but different results such as undecidability, or the undefinability of arithmetical truth, and so are often confused with Gödel's; and the ones that merely sound like them and are false. For each, tell me whether it is close to trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply wrong. Then take my friend's three applications in turn — the mind, the final physical theory, and the code of rules — and say which precise reading each of them would need in order to go through, whether that reading is actually available, and where exactly the gap opens. Two questions especially: is the model-relativity point a genuine deflation of the "truth" in the first theorem, or is it the technicality he says it is; and is his ordinal ladder, in whatever the correct form of that argument is, a route to some further conclusion about knowledge, or just incompleteness restated for a theory that is no longer computably axiomatizable?
