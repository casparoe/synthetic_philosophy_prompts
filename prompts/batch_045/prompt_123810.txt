My game theory course introduces iterated elimination with the slogan that solving a game is just Sudoku with payoffs: cross out every move a rational player could never make, keep going until nothing more can be crossed out, and whatever survives is the answer. My study group drew two conclusions from it: first, that whenever a single strategy profile survives, rationality plus common knowledge of rationality settles what gets played and no equilibrium reasoning is needed on top; second, that a mixed strategy is never really chosen by anyone, it is only one player's uncertainty about another wearing a costume. I doubt both, partly because the same section tells us that a pure move can be strictly dominated by a mixture of the other moves while being dominated by no single one of them, and that is not what crossing out a digit looks like.

Please audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the grid, the given clues, the candidates for a cell, crossing a candidate out, and the finished solution. Then give the disanalogies, separating the ones that bear on my two conclusions from the harmless ones. I care most about: what dominance by a mixture presupposes about expected utility and about a player being willing or able to randomize, and whether a move can still be legitimately crossed out when the player never randomizes at all; whether the order in which moves are crossed out can matter, or the process throw away equilibria; what the puzzle frame quietly imports about uniqueness, about the independence of what each player believes about the others, and about there being a determinate solution that does not rest on anyone's epistemic state; and how any of this changes once there are three or more players. Then say whether my two conclusions survive the audit and whether there is a better analogy available. Tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the point you are least sure of. Please answer in American English.
