I've been asked to give the send-off at our program's brunch the morning before commencement — about forty graduates from a two-year professional master's in quantitative policy analysis, most of their families, and a dozen or so faculty, of whom maybe half teach decision analysis and will catch every reference while the rest (along with the parents and siblings) have never heard the name I. J. Good. I want the speech to be built around Good's theorem on the value of information, and I don't mean as a flourish: I want it to constrain what I can honestly say to them. Concretely — the standard send-off line here is "go out and gather more evidence before you decide," and as I understand the theorem that's close to empty, since for an expected-utility maximizer who updates by Bayes a free signal is worth exactly nothing unless some realization of it would change what you'd do, and even a free signal that's too noisy to move you across your own threshold can be worth nothing while perfect information about the same question would be worth a great deal. But I also can't fall back on the opposite, tougher-sounding line, "decide on conviction and stop listening," because the theorem's whole point is that free information can't hurt you in expectation — there's no cases where refusing to look is the safer play. And I won't say "knowledge is its own reward," partly because I don't believe the version of that I'd be able to say in seven minutes and partly because the theorem is thoroughly instrumental: it assigns information a value only by way of the decisions it improves. So the speech has to find a third thing to say. The one I keep circling is that the guarantee is ex ante — it's a property of the policy of looking, not of any particular thing you find — and that the framework takes your ends as already settled, so it has nothing at all to say about the kind of information that changes what you want, which is most of what a graduate program actually does to a person. Their class T-shirt says "p < .05 is not a decision," which I'd like to use somewhere, and I'd like the speech to land on something they can carry into a job where they'll spend their lives being asked for one more analysis by people who have already decided.

So: write me the speech, six to eight minutes, in my voice — dry, not sentimental, no opening gambit about what a "commencement" or a "theorem" is, no dictionary definitions, nothing that requires the families to have taken a probability course, and nothing that will make the decision theorists in the back row wince. Take a definite line rather than surveying the attitudes one might sensibly have toward more evidence: I want the graduates told something specific about how to treat information in their working lives, with the reason for it on the table. Then, below the speech, add a short speaker's note (a couple hundred words) in which you commit to a position on two things: how far I can go in suggesting that ex post a particular finding can leave someone worse off than if they'd never looked, and whether it's fair to say the theorem falls silent on information that changes your aims. If I've misstated anything above — about strict positivity, about imperfect signals, or about what the theorem assumes — say so plainly in the note rather than quietly writing around it.
