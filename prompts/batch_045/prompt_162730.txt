I teach a short unit on choice under uncertainty in an undergraduate decision-theory course, and for the last few years I have opened it with an informal version of the Ellsberg urn before the formal lecture — ninety balls, thirty red, the remaining sixty black or yellow in some proportion the students are not told. Most of them bet on red rather than on black, and on black-or-yellow rather than on red-or-yellow, and we take it from there. The wrinkle I cannot place is this. If I tell them the urn's composition was fixed the night before by a machine, printed on a slip, and sealed in an envelope in my desk — nobody has read it, nobody will read it before they choose, and nobody can see which colour they bet on — they show the usual mild avoidance of the ambiguous options. If I describe exactly the same urn, the same machine, the same uniform spread over possible compositions, with the single difference that the machine has not run yet and will not run until after they have committed, the avoidance gets visibly stronger, and several of them say something like "well, in the first case someone could at least tell us." As far as I can see nothing decision-theoretically relevant has changed: their evidence is the same, the second-order distribution over compositions is the same, no one observes their bet, no strategic or adversarial channel exists, and they will learn nothing before choosing. The same pattern turns up with a coin I have already flipped and covered with my hand versus one I have not yet flipped, and, in conversation outside class, with aScan that is sitting unread in a lab versus a sample that has not yet been taken.

I know the genus: ambiguity aversion, the risk-versus-uncertainty gap, the way these cases make the sure-thing principle fail. What I am after is the cut inside it, and I will not be satisfied with a bare label. Is there an established name for sensitivity to whether the unknown quantity is already settled but hidden rather than not yet settled by anything at all — and if there is, how exactly does it differ from the neighbouring notions? Someone in a forecasting seminar once mentioned to me that modellers distinguish uncertainty that comes from missing knowledge from uncertainty that comes from randomness itself, but I suspect that distinction is about something else (whether the uncertainty is in principle reducible, or where the variation comes from), and in both of my urns the chooser's ignorance is equally irreducible and the variation has the same source. If nothing established fits, then say so and give me a term with a definition I could actually use in a lecture.

Three more things I want, and please argue them out rather than point me to a bibliography — I have no library access while I am writing this and I want the reasoning, not references. First, boundary cases: what about a slip that is printed and then destroyed unread, so the composition was settled but exists nowhere; what about a composition drawn after the bet by a machine guaranteed to be independent of the bet; what about a composition fixed in advance and shown to a third party who is forbidden to speak but whose face might give something away? Which side of the line does each fall on, and does that tell us the line is really about time, about whether the information exists somewhere, or about something else? Second, is the asymmetry rationally defensible at all, or is it a bias that a competent chooser should train out of herself — and what consideration or small experiment would tell the two apart? Third, if it is defensible, what would a decision theory that respects it have to give up, given that standard models have no room for a difference between a state that is merely unknown and a state that is not yet fixed?
