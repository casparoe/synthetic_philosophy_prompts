Setup: two philosophers of mind / AI who've just sat through the same workshop and are stuck in a hotel bar with an hour to kill. Dana thinks computational complexity is a genuine constraint on cognition: if a task is NP-hard in general, then no physical system — brains and future AI systems included — gets to do it in general, and that fact should be doing real work in our theory of mind, in what we think evolution could have built, in what we're entitled to expect from an artificial agent. Milos thinks thats a category mistake: NP-hardness is a claim about worst cases over arbitrarily large inputs, and nothing about actual minds in actual worlds follows from it, because the instances the world hands an agent are not adversarial ones.

Here's the wrench I want thrown in, and it's one I'm making up, so don't go looking for it in the literature — call it sourced-instance tractability. Suppose it turns out that across the actual distribution of problems a situated agent faces, whenever an instance has a solution it also carries a short, checkable record of the process that generated it, and that record makes recovering the solution cheap. The terrain is navigable because erosion made it that way and left its marks; the puzzle is solvable because whoever built it left the structure visible. The hard instances exist all right — they're just never the ones that arrive. Dana should grant this. Milos should grant, for his part, that there are complexity results where worst-case and average-case difficulty are known to be tied together, so "it's only worst-case" can't be deployed as a blanket move.

The turn I most want to see is Dana's reply: that an optimizing agent is precisely the sort of thing that makes its own input distribution adversarial. Its earlier choices reshape what it later has to cope with, so the easy natural distribution is exactly what a successful agent destroys, and it winds up facing instances nobody generated for it. And then Milos's answer to that — he needs a real one, not a shrug.

Make them both good at this. No "surely you can see," no "but asymptotics are about limits, and minds are finite" as a conversation-stopper. Each should concede at least one point that actually costs them something, and the dialogue should end with each of them committed: a definite claim about what follows for the theory of mind — a concrete prediction, or a concrete methodological rule they'd adopt in their own work.
