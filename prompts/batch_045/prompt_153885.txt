I retired a few years ago from a biophysics lab — thirty years of protein NMR, which mostly meant arguing with colleagues about whether a bump in a spectrum was a real signal or noise — and a couple of years back I started siting in on a consciousness reading group at the local university. There's one fight there that's been driving me up the wall for about a year and I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

It's the one over integrated information theory, and it goes round in the same circle every time. The IIT side says: take the plain facts about your own experience as axioms, ask what a physical system has to be like to satisfy them, and you get a theory in which consciousness is integrated information, the maximally irreducible cause–effect structure is the one that's conscious, and everything above or below that level drops out — the whole-brain level and the single-neuron level both lose. They also bites the bullets: a big enough grid of simple logic gates wired the right way could in princple be more conscious than you are, and there's no special reason cortex should have a monopoly. The critics say: fine, but computing phi for anything bigger than a handful of elements requires the full transition probabilities over the system and all its parts, it blows up combinatorially, nobody can do it for a real brain, so nothing is ever actually derived or tested; the empirical wins people point to come from proxies like perturbational complexity measured with TMS-EEG, which is a stand-in and not the quantity the theory is about; and the whole machinery looks reverse-engineered from conclusions the authors already wanted, hence the charge that it can't be tested even in principle. The defenders reply that this is a demand for a different sort of theory altogether — they're offering a mapping from physical state to experience, not a causal-mechanistic story of how matter manages to produce experience, and nobody has one of those anyway. Nobody moves. Even the big adversarial collaboration that was supposed to settle part of it seems, from what I've picked up second-hand, to have produced findings that both camps can read as broadly compatible with themselves, which in my old field was always the signature of something being wrong at a level below the data.

So what kind of stalemate is this, actually? My hunch is that it's two different questions sharing one name: one side wants a criterion, a rule that says which physical systems are conscious and which experience they have, while the other wants an explanation, a mechanism; and the complaint that nothing has been tested is really the complaint that the criterion isn't computable for the systems we care about, so it can't do the work a criterion has to do. But I can also see a version where it's a straight argument about what counts as data — whether reports from anaesthesia, dreaming, or split-brain cases are measurements or just anecdotes — or just bedrock disagreement about whether experience could be a fundamental feature of the world at all, which neither side can argue for.

Here's the bit I've been kicking around on my own, and I want to know if it's any good or if I'm importing a prejudice. I've started calling it the continuity requirement: a theory of consciousness owes us a specification of how much the experience changes under a small physical perturbation, and a finite physical change should give a bounded change in what's predicted — not a jump to a completely different experience or an arbitrarily larger one. My worry is that IIT violates this by construction, since which complexes exist and how much integrated information they carry can change discontinuously when you add or rewire a few connections. Is that a real objection, or am I smuggling a physicist's expectation of smooth dependence into a problem where it has no business? And if it is real, would turning it into something checkable count as progress, or is it question-begging against exactly the kind of theory IIT wants to be?

I'd like the diagnosis with reasons, grounded in what each side actually says rather than a survey of positions, and then an honest answer about whether anything at all — a conceptual distinction, a class of experiment, a complexity result about computing phi, or just changing the question — could end this, or whether I should expect it to grind on indefinitely, and why.
