Write a satirical consulting memorandum of 800–900 words, from a fictional heritage-strategy firm (call it Meridian & Voss, practice area: Institutional Memory) to the executive committee of a fictional software company, Palimpsest Systems, which has a fourteen-year memory problem: nothing has ever been deleted. Every commit, incident post-mortem, retro, Slack thread, and filmed offsite is retained and full-text searchable; onboarding now takes nine months; no architectural decision is made before someone locates its precedent; the company ships nothing but revisions to its own record of shipping.

The memo should propose three billable workstreams, and the workstreams should be the three ways of doing history described in Nietzsche's 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, translated into a change-management offering: a Monumental track (curate a canon of founding-era triumphs and re-stage them quarterly, on the argument that what was once possible must therefore be possible again), an Antiquarian track (provenance, preservation, piety toward everything inherited, the tree feeling its roots, the original build server as lineage), and a Critical track (a standing tribunal that puts the inherited architecture on trial and condemns it so that the company can go on). Each should be recognizable to a reader who knows the essay both from its name and from its shape, and legible to a reader who does not: give each track concrete deliverables, an owner, and a fee.

The humor has to come from the tracks failing in the way that mode of history actually fails when it gets the upper hand, not from invented absurdity. The monumental canon should end up burying the present beneath its own monuments and mistaking resemblance for possibility. The antiquarian programme should lose all sense of proportion until everything is equally venerable, and should prove able to preserve but not to create. The critical tribunal should discover that it is condemning its own ancestry, that a second nature has already formed around the condemned inheritance, and that it can clear the ground without being able to build on it. Keep the narrator's register deadpan throughout — no winking, no footnotes admitting the joke — and let one quoted line from the antiquarian head of infrastructure carry the reverence.

Include an executive summary, a diagnosis section that gives the company's condition an invented clinical name, the three track descriptions, a risks table, an implementation schedule, and one footnote from a junior associate who has noticed something the partners have not.

End where the essay ends: the firm's trouble is not that it remembers wrongly but that it cannot stop remembering, and what it lacks is not a better practice of history but the capacity to grow around and past its own record — a horizon to act within rather than a total archive to stand outside of. The reductio is that Meridian & Voss sell this too, as an unlogged room, billed hourly, and that this memo will itself be indexed, cited, and retained forever. Never name Nietzsche, the essay, or its concepts' source, and use no real quotations: if the memo needs an authority, invent one. The satire should target heritage consultancy and institutional memory-management, not the philosophy, which should function as the straight man.
