I spend most of my working life with institutional papers — parish registers, poor-law and court files, mission and colonial correspondence — and there is a pattern in them that I keep running into and have no name for. I would like to know whether the pattern has one, and if it does not, I would like a name and a definition I can actually use, together with the reasons that definition is better than the obvious alternatives.

Two cases first, so I am not asking about an abstraction.

In one county's holdings, the inmates of a single workhouse — never more than two or three hundred people at a time — generate thousands of pages over a decade: admission and discharge books, dietaries, medical case notes, settlement examinations, removal orders. A few miles away, a cluster of families living on a common, working seasonally and moving between parishes, generates perhaps six lines in forty years, and every one of those lines is about a single winter when they applied for relief or were summonsed. A history written strictly from these sources would make the workhouse population look like the centre of local life and the commoners look like a rumour, even though by any ordinary measure the second group lived the more self-directed existence.

In a run of colonial district reports, the only surviving mention I can find of a hillside settlement is the account of the expedition sent against it. Everything I can say about that settlement — roughly how many people it held, how it was laid out, the names of those the report calls its leaders — comes from a document written to justify destroying it. That report is at once the most informative source I have and the least trustworthy one, and I cannot separate the two functions, because there is nothing else to triangulate against.

The generalisation I keep arriving at: the density of the record tracks how thoroughly a person or group was entangled with institutions, not how significant they were. And for the subordinated, that entanglement was compulsory — they appear because they were enumerated, licensed, taxed, tried, baptized, apprenticed, or relieved. The consequence is perverse in a way I find hard to state cleanly: the more successfully a group avoided being governed, the thinner its existence for us, so the archive is richest exactly where people were most controlled, and a history that follows the sources will systematically convert subjugation into historical substance.

I should also flag a complication, because it makes me think I have not got the mechanism right. The relation is not simply documented = subjugated. The largest landowners and the oldest gentry families are the most richly documented people in any English county archive, and nobody would call them subordinate. So what the archive seems to record is institutional transaction of either kind — being an object of control, or being a holder of recognised standing — and what disappears is whatever had no institutional relations at all.

I have read a little about the ways states simplify and standardise populations in order to govern them, and that feels adjacent, but as I understand it, that literature is about what administration does to the governed. What I am after is what administration's paperwork subsequently does to what can be known about them — including by historians who approach the material sympathetically and correct for its slant.

So: does this have an established name or literature? Please do not give me a label on its own. What I want is the carving: what the mechanism is, what has to be true for it to operate, what it is not (it is not merely that archives are selective, and not merely that some documents have been lost, and not merely the familiar advice to read a hostile source against the grain), and where its edges lie. In particular, I would want a definition to handle these cases: a population that was intensely administered but whose papers were later destroyed or never centralised; a community that is richly documented because it recorded itself, through a guild, a congregation, or its own diarists; the same pattern in present-day administrative and platform data, where the record is generated continuously rather than filed; and the elite case above, where archival density marks standing rather than control. If the thing I have described turns out on inspection to be two or three separate effects that I have conflated, say so and pull them apart.

Finally, be explicit about your own confidence. Tell me how confident you are that no established term covers this; tell me, if such a term does exist, in which field and under what description you would expect to find it, and what would count as evidence that it does; and tell me where your proposed definition is most likely to fail — where it collapses into something already named, or where it would misclassify a case of the sort listed above.
