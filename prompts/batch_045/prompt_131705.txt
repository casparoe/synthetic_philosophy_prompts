I wonder whether you can help me with something I keep tripping over. I used to build reliability models for a living and I now read this material for its own sake. I have no library access and no way to chase references, so I am not asking for a bibliography, or for authors' names, or for anything I would have to look up: I want the shape of the thing, and if it needs one, a name I can use.

The pattern is this. I hold two propositions, call them A and B, and I am unsure of both. An informant I trust tells me the rate of one within the other — not a joint frequency, not an overall rate, but a rate restricted to a subclass: among the cases where B holds, A holds in three quarters of them. I know there is a well-known puzzle about what to do with a single announcement of that kind — something about a soldier dropped into unfamiliar territory, though the name escapes me — and that the standard disagreement is between a rule that keeps my confidence in the if-part where it was and lets my overall confidence in the then-part move, and a rule that does the reverse. That is not my difficulty. My difficulty begins when there are two announcements.

First case. I start at even odds on each of A and B and treat them as unrelated. One bulletin says that the rate of A among the B cases is three quarters. A later bulletin says that the rate of B among the A cases is three quarters. Take the first of the two rules: keep my confidence in the if-part fixed, keep fixed the rate of the then-part among the cases where the if-part fails, and let my overall confidence in the then-part move. Taking the bulletins in the order given, I end up at 0.625 for A and roughly 0.594 for B. Taking them in the reverse order I end up at roughly 0.594 for A and 0.625 for B. And whichever order I take them in, the announcement I took in first has stopped being true of my new opinions: the rate it specified has drifted to fifteen nineteenths, about 0.79, rather than the three quarters I was told. The announcement taken in last is respected; the one taken in first is quietly repealed.

Second case. The same two announcements arriving in a single message, where I move to the picture that stays as close as possible to where I began while respecting both at once — under the usual way of measuring that distance, at any rate — gives me 0.6 for each. That is neither of the answers above, though at least both announcements survive.

Third case. Having taken both in, in the first order, I am sent the first bulletin a second time. Nothing new is said. My confidence in A moves from 0.625 to about 0.602.

So: is there an established concept here, and if so how is it marked off from its neighbours? I have three questions I cannot separate. Is the sensitivity to how the total information is parcelled into deliveries one phenomenon or several — the order-sensitivity, the parcel-sensitivity and the repeal of the earlier announcement look related, but I cannot see whether one explains the others? Is this the same complaint that is made about the ordinary kind of uncertain evidence, where I am told something directly about a rate rather than a rate within a subclass, or does it arise for a different reason in this case? And is the repeal a defect of the rule I happen to be using, something a better rule would avoid by construction, or is it forced — so that no rule can take each delivery on board as it arrives and still respect all of them at the end? I genuinely cannot tell whether I have stumbled on a property of the problem or a property of the rule I was taught.

If no established concept fits, I would be grateful for a name and a definition, with the boundary cases: when two announcements of this kind do and do not produce the behaviour, and what a case would look like in which the order of arrival really ought not to matter. A bare label, on either answer, will not be much use to me.

Please reply in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, in the manner of a letter.
