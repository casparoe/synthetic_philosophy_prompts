The talk (about 30 min, then ~20 min of questions, so keep replies to two minutes max): I'm arguing that informal social sanction — deplatforming, disinvitations, boycotts, professional shunning, the swarm — is punishment in the sense that matters for justice, and that the duties that come with punishing (proportionality, a hearing, stated reasons) don't attach to the individuals piling on. They attach to what I'm calling the *aggregator*: the platform, journal, venue, employer or professional body whose single decision turns a lot of small acts into one outcome. Nobody owes you anything for retweeting. The aggregator owes you everything. I'm calling that the aggregator principle.

Second half: the proportionality constraint that applies is not ordinal proportionality (fit the suffering to the desert) but what I'm calling *floor* proportionality — a sanction may not drop anyone below a decent minimum of social standing and audibility. Above the floor, huge amounts of disproportion are tolerable in a way they wouldn't be for the state, because the aggregator's relationship to the target isn't the state's relationship to a citizen.

Moves, so you can aim at them properly:
1. The many-hands move: no individual act is punishment, the composite is; punishment can be a property of a structure the way a market outcome is.
2. The exit-cost move: "start your own podcast" fails because audience is a positional good — I can't have *your* audience, and being removed from a dominant venue isn't losing a benefit, it's losing a rank someone else now occupies.
3. Floor proportionality: two cases — a famous tenured guy with a big following loses one podcast slot and is basically untouched; an adjunct with no savings and no union is dropped by her professional association and can't work in the field again. Same speech, wildly different result; ordinal proportionality can't see the difference, my view can.
4. The drift claim (this is the one I care most about and am least sure of): informal sanction selects its targets partly by how *cheap* they are to sanction — no lawyer, no tenure, no union, no platform of their own — so it systematically over-selects the vulnerable, which is exactly backwards if desert is supposed to be independent of how easy you are to punish.
5. The backfire case: sometimes deplatforming grows the target's audience. I currently say it's still punishment, just an attempted and failed one, and I honestly don't feel great about it.

The room: ~12 legal philosophers, mostly retributivists, several of the communicative/expressive stripe (punishment as addressing the wrongdoer as a responsible agent), two deterrence people who think all of this is vibes; four US First Amendment lawyers, one of whom reduces everything to state action and one of whom reduces everything to public forum doctrine; two trust-and-safety people from a big platform who were invited as "practitioners" and will be annoyed by anything unimplementable; and one person from carceral studies / prison abolition who thinks the whole framing already concedes that punishment is legitimate. The organizer is sympathetic but has already told me he thinks my view collapses into "platforms should have better HR departments."
