I'm a software engineer — backend stuff, mostly — not a philosopher, so apologies if I butcher the terminology. The other day two mates from work, Ollie and Fatima, got into an argument in the office kitchen whilst I was showing them a little browser simulation of a double pendulum. I wrote the sim, so they dragged me in as referee, but I've got no real philosophy background and I don't want to give away who I secretly thought was winning.

They were arguing about whether a deterministic but chaotic system is really predictable 'in principle', or whether there's some distinct kind of unpredictability that isn't just ordinary ignorance.

Ollie started by saying that because the simulation is deterministic, if you knew the exact state to infinite precision — every digit of every number — the entire future would be fixed. So on his view any unpredictability is just our ignorance of those digits, exactly like not knowing the exact forces on a coin toss. It's still the infinite-mind version of determinism; probability is only book-keeping for our uncertainty.

Fatima said that's too quick. She pointed out that no measurement or computer can ever specify a real number to infinite precision — we only ever have finite precision. And in this particular system, if you start with slightly wrong digits, the error doesn't just stay small, it grows really fast, roughly doubling (or worse) every few steps. So for any finite precision you actually have, there's a point beyond which your prediction is garbage. She said that's definately not just 'missing data' like a coin toss: with a coin, more precision would keep helping you steadily, whereas here extra precision only buys you a limited amount of extra prediction time before it falls apart again.

Ollie replied that all of that is still consistent with determinism. He said the rapid error growth is itself a deterministic feature of the system; it just means the system is unstable. Unpredictability caused by instability is still predictability in principle for an infinite mind, so it's misleading to call it a different kind of randomness or to say determinism fails. It's just chaos, plus the boring fact that we aren't infinite.

Fatima came back with the point that 'in principle' is doing all the work there. She said the only thing that could predict the long-term behaviour is something that can store and process an infinite number of digits, which isn't us or any physical computer we could build. She wasn't claiming the universe is random or non-deterministic; she was claiming there's a structural limit on prediction for any finite predictor, because errors blow up exponentially and each extra bit of precision only buys a roughly constant extra amount of forecast time. She said that's a distinctive thing that sits between 'ordinary ignorance' and 'real randomness', and we shouldn't just collapse it into either the coin-toss case or the infinite-mind case.

Ollie then said that sounds like she's redefining 'random' to mean 'deterministic but unstable', and that the real name for the sensitivity bit is chaos, so her 'third thing' is just chaos plus a complaint about infinity. He also said we should keep the two ideas seperate: determinism is a fact about the rules, unpredictability is a fact about us.

At that point I lost track of who was actually right. Can you go through the exchange and adjudicate it move by move? For each of the main moves — Ollie's infinite-precision point, Fatima's finite-precision and error-growth point, Ollie's 'instability is still determinism' reply, Fatima's 'in principle is doing the work' reply, and Ollie's 'that's just chaos' move — say whether the point is clearly right, mostly right, half-right, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, and briefly why. Also say what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please don't give me a reading list, citations, or references — I can't search anything up anyway; I just want the argument itself pulled apart.
