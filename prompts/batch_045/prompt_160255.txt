Write a dialogue between two members of a city commission who disagree about what should be done with a monument, and who are both good enough at this that neither can be dismissed as a slogan. The case is invented, but treat its details as fixed and do not alter them:

The city of Brackenhill (population roughly 180,000) is a port city whose Harbour Square contains an equestrian bronze of General Ambrose Kell, erected in 1898 and paid for by subscription among veterans' associations and the chamber of commerce. Its inscription praises Kell for saving the city during the Rising of 1877, when an insurgent commune seized the harbour district. Kell commanded the militia that retook it; in the course of the suppression he ordered around forty prisoners executed without trial. Brackenhill's charter of self-government and the recovery of the harbour followed directly from that suppression, which is why the subscribers thought they knew what they were honouring.

Kell Street runs through the district where the executions took place. It is now the address of a school, a clinic, and about fourteen hundred households, most of whose residents descend from immigrants who arrived after 1898 and from the families of the executed. In 1898 the deed of gift, and in 1961 a maintenance endowment from a Kell descendant, both require the statue to remain in the square permanently; breach of the condition returns roughly 2.4 million dollars of endowment to a private heritage foundation.

Four options are on the table: remove the statue to the city museum's gallery on the Rising; rename the street Reconciliation Way; do both; or do neither and instead install a large freestanding counter-monument in the square narrating the executions beside Kell's bronze.

The two speakers:

Adele holds what might be called the endorsement view: public honour is a speech act performed by the political community in the present tense, so what matters is not what Kell did but what Brackenhill is now saying, and saying it in the first person plural, on the strength of his name.

Colin holds a custodial view: the monument is a piece of the record, evidence of what Brackenhill chose to praise in 1898 and of the coalition that chose it, and one generation has no licence to edit the record bequeathed to the next on the theory that it knows better.

Give each of them the strongest available version of their position and have them work through, in whatever order arises naturally, the following difficulties:

Inextricability. The act for which Kell is honoured and the act for which he is condemned are the same act, not two separable episodes in one life. Ask what this does to the standard solution of keeping the monument while annotating the disgraceful part, and whether either view can say anything coherent when praise and blame attach to one indivisible deed.

The statue-versus-name asymmetry. A statue can be avoided by walking another way; a street name cannot, because residents must write it on leases, identification, and school forms, and are thus made to utter it. Decide whether this asymmetry cuts for or against each speaker, and whether the name's coerciveness is a reason for haste or for caution.

Semantic drift. After five generations, residents use the name as a bare label the way one uses any other street name, and few could identify Kell. Have them argue whether this bleaching is genuine neutralisation or a laundering that preserves the honour precisely by making it invisible, and whether the drift is something the city may permissibly accelerate or arrest.

Standing and the continuity of the city. Is the Brackenhill that erected the statue the same agent as the Brackenhill that would remove it, and if not, who is entitled to speak: present residents, descendants of the executed, descendants of the subscribers, or the donor's intent?

The conditional gift. Whether a condition attached to a gift binds a later public, and whether the fact that keeping the statue costs the city nothing while removing it costs 2.4 million dollars tells us anything about how serious the objection really is, on either view.

Relocation and counter-monumentation. Whether moving the bronze to a museum stops honouring him or merely changes the register of the honour, whether a museum can show without praising, and whether installing a counter-monument in the square converts the square into a memorial to the executed — and if so, whether that is a solution, a dodge, or a category mistake about what squares are for.

Requirements for the dialogue: both speakers must answer the objection actually put to them rather than the one they prefer, and each must make at least one concession that genuinely costs them something and revise their own recommendation in response, rather than restating it. Do not let the disagreement collapse into compromise: instead, have them locate the precise point at which they diverge and say whether it is a moral disagreement, a disagreement about the likely causal or social effects, or a merely verbal one about what the word honouring covers. If either speaker reaches for the phrases erasing history or judging the past by the standards of the present, the other should be permitted to take it apart rather than to nod along. Aim for roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words of dialogue with only minimal stage directions, and leave the ending open but sharper than it began.
