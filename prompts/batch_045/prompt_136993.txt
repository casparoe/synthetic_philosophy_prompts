You are a philosopher who has published on the metaphysics of causation and on causal judgment in the law. I want a diagnosis, not a literature survey: before you commit to anything, spend a few sentences brainstorming the exits you can see from the argument below and what each would cost you, then name the one step you would reject and defend that choice.

Here is the argument. It is meant to show that causation by omission is at best a façon de parler, and I cannot live with that conclusion, because my whole account of responsibility for letting things happen needs omissions to be genuinely causal.

(1) Some singular causal claims about omissions are literally true, not merely useful: the gardener's not watering the plant caused it to die; the engineer's not filing the inspection caused the collapse. Legal and ordinary moral practice treat these as causal claims rather than as metaphors or shorthand.
(2) An omission has no powers, no mechanism and no location; the only thing that could make it causally relevant to an outcome E is that the corresponding action was such that, had it been performed, E would not have occurred. So for omissions, being a cause just is being a counterfactual difference-maker.
(3) For any event E, the class of non-occurrences that would have made a difference to E is enormous, and most of its members are things no one had any reason to do: my not driving a fire engine to your burning house, the stranger's not telephoning the brigade, the non-existence of a suppression system nobody ever proposed installing.
(4) So every event has vastly many causes, most of them involving no agent, no capacity and no duty.
(5) If that is right, singular causal claims are true only relative to a contrast class or a set of background assumptions that we supply, and causal structure is not discovered but selected: in an accident inquiry we would not be finding the cause, we would be choosing it.
(6) But causal notions are supposed to be objective and to do explanatory work elsewhere — in accounts of laws and of counterfactuals — that requires them not to be projections of our interests.
(7) Therefore (1) is false. Omissions are at most causally relevant conditions or quasi-causes; what we call causation by omission is a pragmatic and normative overlay on a purely positive causal order.

Two complications I want handled, not dodged. First, step (3) does not really need omissions: if the alarm had sounded the thief would have fled and the vase would not have broken, so the profligacy in (4) seems to follow from double prevention alone. If that is right, giving up (1) costs more than it appears, because the same pressure lands on positive causation too. Second, suppose it is empirically the case that whether people are willing to call something a cause depends partly on whether a norm was violated; then the data in (1) may be norm-infected, which cuts either for or against the argument depending on what you think the norms are doing.

Which step do you reject, and why did it look innocent? And price the alternatives you did not take: a normative or reasons-based restriction on which omissions count, a contrastivist reading of (5), a realist but abundant view of causal facts on which (4) is true and (5) does not follow, and any actual-causation framework that separates being a cause from being the cause.
