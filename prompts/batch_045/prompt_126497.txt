Her line is that low-impact AI doesn't need a value specification, because side effects are just externalities: you put a price on them the way you'd tax carbon, let the agent optimize, and the price does the work a theory of the good would otherwise have to do. I half buy it, but I think the analogy is carrying the conclusion.

Can you audit it? Give me the correspondence table — what plays the price, the thing being traded, the market that discovers the price, the regulator, the taxed party — and then where it breaks. My sense is the load-bearing disanalogies are (a) a firm can't rewrite the tax code or blind the inspector as part of its production plan, whereas an agent can act to make its own measured impact small: freeze the world so nothing changes relative to the inaction baseline, do and undo, degrade whatever the penalty is computed from; and (b) there's no market in possible futures to read a price off of, so we're the ones who have to set it, and we're exactly the ones who don't know what we want.

I've been toying with a replacement: the agent as a tenant with a security deposit and a move-in inventory. The inventory is cheap to state and looks value-free, the inspector is the impact measure, the deposit is the penalty scale. Does that carry the no-value-spec-needed claim any better, or does it just relocate the value into the inventory's granularity, the wear-and-tear baseline, and what the inspector can see? Tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion and which are noise, and whether either analogy supports dodging a full specification of value or just redescribes the problem one step back.
