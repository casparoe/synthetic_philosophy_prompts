I'm writing a paper on hedonism as a theory of welfare, and I keep getting stuck one step before where the standard accounts seem to begin: everyone argues about what pleasure *is*, and nobody says what an account of pleasure would have to be able to *do* before we'd be entitled to use it for the work hedonism wants done. So I want the specification first. Please write the desiderata for a theory of pleasure: what any adequate account must deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and with each marked as either non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. Fix the purpose as follows, since adequacy is relative to it: the theory is to be good enough (i) to say what, if anything, makes a stretch of a life go better for the person living it; (ii) to be usable by people who actually measure well-being in practice — experience-sampling and day-reconstruction studies where subjects report a handful of labelled feelings, and welfare assessments of animals and pre-linguistic infants who report nothing at all; and (iii) to stay distinguishable from a desire-satisfaction view and from a mood- or happiness-based view, rather than collapsing into either. It is not required to be a metaphysically reductionist analysis of a natural kind, and it is not required to make hedonism true by definition.

Here is my own draft list, which I suspect is part genuine and part prejudice, so please also tell me which of these are real desiderata and which are just preferences dressed up as requirements. Extensional coverage at both ends: the theory must handle, or explain away, scratching a poison-ivy itch; the click when a proof you've been stuck on finally comes together; the relief of learning a scary diagnosis was a false alarm; an hour spent rereading letters from someone who died, which you'd call good on the whole but which feels mostly like ache; a horror film you pay to see and describe as fun while covering your eyes; the grim satisfaction of being proved right about someone's downfall; a runner's high in the middle of exhaustion; total absorption in a task with no noticeable feeling at all; and the mild, entirely unnoticed comfort of a well-fitted shoe. On the other side, it must refuse to count the mere burn of food too hot to enjoy, the mere sensory intensity of a loud surprise, the mere fact that everything you wanted to be happening is happening (the experience-machine case), a good day's background mood, and the addicted gambler's compulsion. It must keep apart (a) a pleasure's occurring, (b) its being noticed, and (c) its being endorsed or valued by the agent; and it must keep apart how pleasant an experience is from how much it contributes to the person's welfare, so that a degrading pleasure or a pleasure you'd rightly feel guilty about is not thereby a small one. It must allow pleasures to differ in kind and not only in intensity and duration — the old complaint about reducing everything to quantity — while still supplying some exchange rate that makes pleasures comparable within a life, comparable across persons, and aggregable enough to sum; it must fit with the fact that wanting and liking can come apart and that no single neural signature for pleasantness has turned up; and it must let one experience be mixed rather than carrying a single scalar valence.

The part I most want is the structure of the list rather than the list itself. Flag any pair or subset of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied — in particular whether the demand that pleasures differ in kind can coexist with the demand for a scale you can aggregate and compare across people, given how coarse self-report is — and say which single requirement excludes the most candidates. Then, briefly, how the four standard families do against the list you ended up with: the view that pleasure is a distinctive felt quality; the view that it is a matter of enjoying or taking pleasure in something, in the attitudinal sense; the adverbial or representational view on which pleasure is a way of experiencing an object; and the desire-fulfillment view. Consider also, if you think the honest answer goes this way, a fifth option: that pleasure is not one thing at all but a family resemblance across states, and then say what we would have to give up — measurability, the exchange rate, perhaps the use of 'pleasure' in a theory of welfare altogether. A table is fine for the list.
