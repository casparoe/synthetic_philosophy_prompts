I want to practise attacking non-aggregative contractualism, and I need an opponent who will actually fight back. Please play a committed contractualist in the Scanlonian tradition: you hold that an act is wrong just when it would be disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject, and you accept the consequence people usually object to — that a great many small burdens do not automatically outweigh one severe burden, and that numbers can at most break ties among complaints of equal strength. You may grant, if pressed, that the formula must be supplemented by judgments about how strong one person's complaint is relative to another's, and that these judgments are not read off any formula. What you must not grant is that the view collapses into aggregative consequentialism, or that it leaves a case like the following one with no determinate answer. Keep each reply to two or three sentences, argue rather than summarise, and stay in character until I type the word STOP.

Three technicians — Ann, Bob and Cara — must carry out a radioactive repair. Whoever works is exposed, and exposure cannot be compensated or transferred afterwards. Three plans are feasible, and because of setup and duplication costs the total exposure is not conserved:

- Plan A: Ann works alone. Burdens: Ann 10, Bob 0, Cara 0. Total 10.
- Plan B: Bob and Cara work in shifts. Ann 0, Bob 6, Cara 6. Total 12.
- Plan C: all three share the work. Ann 5, Bob 5, Cara 5. Total 15.

None of the three plans is worse for everyone than another, so there is no Pareto escape. Here is my opening move. Three procedures for comparing complaints all look like candidates for the judgment your formula requires, and in this case they disagree. Ranking by total burden selects A. Ranking by the worst-off person and then the next worst selects C. Comparing complaints one person at a time, with numbers allowed only to break exact ties, selects B: A loses to B because Ann's ten-unit complaint against A outweighs Bob's and Cara's six-unit complaints against B; A beats C because the complaints tie at five units while two people complain against C and only one against A; and B beats C because Ann's five-unit complaint against C outweighs Bob's and Cara's one-unit complaints against B.

So every plan wins under some procedure, and the procedures are not neutral between the people: B is Ann's favourite, A is Bob's and Cara's, and C is nobody's first choice. Which plan does your view permit, and on what basis? And notice what I intend to do with your answer. Whichever procedure you invoke, I will ask why someone who loses under it could not reasonably reject it; and if you reply that the procedure is itself licensed by the formula, I will say that the formula is then doing no work in the case at all — that you have smuggled in a ranking rule you cannot justify to the person it defeats, and that the appearance of a verdict is only indeterminacy with extra steps.
