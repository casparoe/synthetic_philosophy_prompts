I've been defending the Dutch book argument for probabilism in a reading group and someone hit me with a version that I think proves way too much, but I can't locate the mistake:

(1) The argument itself: if your credences don't satisfy the probability axioms, there's a set of bets, each at odds you yourself would call fair, that loses you money no matter how things turn out. Being disposed to accept that package is a defect, so rationality requires probabilistic credences.

(2) What the argument is really trading on is that you'd take the bets and then be out money. Nothing in that requires the loss to be one you could have foreseen — a sucker is out the same cash whether or not he spotted the setup. "Exploitable" shouldn't mean "exploitable only by someone you can identify in advance."

(3) Now give the bookie information you lack. Take any true claim you're only, say, 70% on. A bookie who knows it's true sells you a bet paying $1 if it's true, at your fair price of 70 cents. You take it, you lose 30 cents, and he can run that on you over and over at a profit without ever offering you anything unfair by your own lights.

(4) So by the reasoning in (1), you're irrational unless you assign credence 1 to every truth — full empirical and logical omniscience, since the same move works for a theorem you haven't proved yet. Which is absurd.

(5) And it collapses in the other direction too. If the reply is "you couldn't have known," the cure is just: don't bet against people who know more than you. But that cure has nothing to do with coherence — a perfectly coherent agent and a wildly incoherent one are both safe as long as they decline. And the offer is itself evidence: a bookie only offers you a bet he expects to profit from, so once you conditionalize on being offered it, it isn't fair by your lights anymore and you shouldn't take it. So on one reading the argument demands omniscience, and on the other it demands nothing at all.

My attempted fix was that the bookie should have to commit to the whole book before he finds anything out, but I can't tell whether that actually helps or just moves the bump. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd definately rather keep (1)'s conclusion — that incoherent credences really are irrational — than keep the argument for it, but I want to know what I'm paying, and whether whatever survives is still an argument for probabilism or just a representation theorem about fair prices with the normative bite smuggled in somewhere else.
