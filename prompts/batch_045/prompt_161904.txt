THE QUESTION (Q4, 25 marks)

Lexical threshold negative utilitarianism (LTNU) says: there is an intensity threshold T such that (i) for any pain below T in intensity, some finite quantity of pleasure makes pain-plus-pleasure at least as good as a state with neither, and (ii) for any pain at or above T, no finite quantity of pleasure makes a world containing it at least as good as a world without it.

Here is a dilemma against it. Horn 1: if pleasure and pain really are commensurable in the sense of clause (i), the lexical clause is arbitrary, because nothing on a commensurable scale picks out T as the special point. Horn 2: if they are incommensurable, the sub-threshold clause is unmotivated, since the whole point of claiming incommensurability is that pleasure never offsets pain in the first place. Either way LTNU is in trouble.

One reply available to the LTNU defender is the relation-switch reading: T need not be a marked point on a single shared pleasure–pain scale. It can instead be the point at which the comparison relation between pains and pleasures changes kind — trade-off below it, lexical priority at or above it — so Horn 1 has no right to demand that T be readable off a common scale.

Assess the dilemma and the relation-switch reply. Does either refute LTNU? Note, for the purposes of this question: commensurability is a matter of whether some finite quantity of pleasure can offset a given pain. It is not a claim about measurment, about cardinal units, or about interpersonal comparison.

THE ANSWER

Neither horn refutes LTNU, so the dilemma fails.

First the setup. LTNU makes two claims. Below the threshold pain is compensable: there is some finite amount of pleasure which, added to the pain, leaves the package at least as good as a life containing neither. At or above the threshold pain is lexically dominant: no finite amount of pleasure does that job. And "commensurable" shouldn't be over-read. It doesn't say we can put a number on how much a pain hurts, or weigh it against somebody else's pain. It only says that offsetting is possible in principle.

Horn 1 claims that if the scale is commensurable then any threshold drawn on it is arbitrary. But pain intensity is a continuous quantity: mild discomfort shades into moderate pain with no break anywhere along the way. If there is no break in the quantity, then there is no fact of the matter about where the threshold falls, and a cut at a point we could never verify cannot carry the weight the lexical clause puts on it. So the lexical clause is arbitrary, as Horn 1 says.

Horn 2 is weaker than it looks. It assumes that anyone who asserts incommensurability is committed to the global thesis, that no pain whatsoever is offset by any quantity of pleasure. But LTNU only needs a local thesis: pains at or above T are not offset. The familiar arguments for the global thesis — from the urgency of pain, or from the way suffering seems to demand action in a way that an absent pleasure does not — would support the local thesis too if they worked, but the local thesis does not inherit their difficulties, since it happily concedes that small pains are compensated all the time. So the sub-threshold clause is not unmotivated.

An example. A mild itch alongside an equally mild pleasure leaves you better off than an equal stretch of neutral experience, so that pain is commensurable with pleasure. Now take a backache that grinds on for weeks. There is no amount of pleasure you could add that would make that stretch of life better than a neutral stretch; the pain still counts against it. So the backache is incommensurable with pleasure, and LTNU can locate T somewhere in the region of pains like that one.

The relation-switch reply blocks Horn 1 in any case. If what changes at T is the kind of comparison that applies rather than a value on one shared scale, then Horn 1's demand — show me where T sits on the scale — is simply the wrong demand. The defender is not claiming that the scale has a special point marked on it. They are claiming that the trade-off relation gives out at a certain intensity of pain. That is a coherent thing to claim, and Horn 1 offers no argument against it.

Conclusion: Horn 1 works only on the assumption that T must be readable off a single scale, and the relation-switch reply removes that assumption. Horn 2 attacks a stronger thesis than the one LTNU actually holds. The dilemma therefore does not refute LTNU.
