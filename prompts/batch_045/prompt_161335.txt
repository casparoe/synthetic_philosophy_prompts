I am teaching a unit on Confucian role ethics and want to use the novel The Godfather and its best-known film adaptation as a case study, but I do not want to reduce the work to an illustration or distort Confucianism to fit it. Read the story through Confucian role ethics as it is usually presented in English-language discussions: a person is not a detached individual but a nexus of roles, and duties such as filial piety (xiao) and ritual propriety (li) are specified from inside those roles rather than derived from an impartial principle. To keep the reading precise, use a distinction I will introduce between role-inheritance and role-ownership: one inherits a role by occupying a social position, while one owns a role by taking up its constitutive aims and practices, including its ritual forms, as the standards of one's own conduct; Confucian cultivation is supposed to turn inheritance into ownership. Apply this to Michael Corleone's arc: is his movement from reluctant youngest son to avenger in the restaurant scene, to godfather at the baptism while the coordinated murders are carried out, to the finale in which he is acknowledged as don and Kay is shut out best understood as a Confucian achievement of role-ownership, or as a perversion that shows role ethics can consecrate a criminal family as readily as a good one? Where does the film use role-relative duty to make Michael's choices intelligible, and where does it resist the reading — for instance, by suggesting that his decisions are acts of personal ambition rather than the demands of the role, or that the family is more like a modern firm than a Confucian household? Finally, say what the work adds to the philosophical debate about whether Confucian role ethics can criticize a corrupt role from within, or whether its only resources are conformity and loyal remonstrance.
