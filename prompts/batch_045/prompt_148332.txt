I'm on the exhibitions comittee at a mid-sized regional history museum — reconstructed port district, a lot of shipping and quarantine material, a mission statement that goes on about connecting the choices of the past to responsibilities in the present — and we vote next Tuesday on a proposal for our first new permanent gallery in about fifteen years. The curator has been working on this much longer than I have and she'll be in the room, so I'd like to get the argument strait in my head before the meeting rather than voting on vibes.

The proposal is three "case files," each built around a non-human lead. First: smallpox in the Americas after 1492, where the virus is the protagonist. Second: the eruption of Tambora in 1815, the crop failures and cold of the following year across New England and Europe, and our own region's wave of outmigration. Third: a late-nineteenth-century famine in a colonized region, framed around a multi-decade shift in the monsoon, with the colonial administration's grain exports and relief policy also in the record. In each room the non-human gets a first-person placard along the lines of "I arrived on a ship" or "I darkened the sky." The centerpiece is an interactive kiosk called Causal Weight: visitors drag sliders to split responsibility among the listed causes for each case — the pathogen, the volcano, the monsoon on one side, shipping routes, grain exports, quarantine decisions on the other — and the room displays a live bar chart of where visitors put the weight. The curator is explicit that her question is how much difference the non-human factor made, not who's to blame, and that the kiosk exists to surface disagreement rather than settle it.

Two of our education staff ran a focus group and came back saying visitors overwhelmingly read "the volcano did it" as "nobody did it" — attributing an outcome to nature seems to land as an excuse rather than an explanation. One committee member thinks that's tolerable in the first two rooms but that the third is where the design actually does harm: when the case is a famine, giving the monsoon the protagonist's chair is a scientific-looking way of not talking about the administration. Another member says the opposite, and says it harder — that a gallery in which only people act is itself a political choice, that sidelining climate because it's inconvenient is precisely the habit we should be breaking, and that the museum's donor base (old shipping money, mostly) makes human-only history the comfortable option. I also can't shake a worry about the kiosk itself: I teach intro philosophy at the community college, and something feels off about aggregating visitor opinion on a slider, as if "the volcano was 40% responsible" were the sort of thing that could be true or false, or as if blame could just be read off how much difference something made. But I genuinely don't know how much of that is a real objection and how much is professional deformation, and I don't want to be the guy blocking a touchscreen on metaphysical grounds.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate Monday night, roughly 800 words, with an actual bottom line — approve as proposed, approve with named conditions, or send it back for redesign — and with reasons written as changes she could concretely make. Please don't hand me a reading list or a set of theories to go look up; I want the argument, not the syllabus. Two things I especially want in it. First, sort the possible conditions into cosmetic ones (swapping "protagonist" for "factor," adding a disclaimer plaque) and structural ones (changing what the kiosk asks, reordering the rooms, reframing the famine case), and tell me which would actually change what a visitor walks away believing. Second, give me the strongest honest version of the losing side's case in her voice, not a strawman — if you tell me to send it back, I need her best argument for shipping it as it stands. And at the end, say how confident you are in the reasoning overall, and flag the one claim in the memo you'd be least surprised to turn out wrong.
