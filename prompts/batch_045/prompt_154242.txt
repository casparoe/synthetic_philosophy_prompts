I'm in the second year of a BPhil, writing a chapter on exploitation arguments for diachronic rationality requirements — the sort of argument that begins from the observation that an agent with a certain preference structure can be systematically relieved of her money, and concludes that there is something rationally defective about having it. Our reading group has been treating Dutch books and money pumps as a single family of arguments, and one member put the identification bluntly: a money pump is just a Dutch book with the bets spaced out in time. On that basis he argued that whatever defeats a Dutch book — coherence of credences, expected-utility maximisation, whatever the synchronic arguments deliver — should equally defeat the pump, and hence that the whole literature on whether one ought to be a planner, a resolute chooser, or merely sophisticated about one's own future preferences is a footnote to the synchronic debate: get the ordering into the right shape and the diachronic problem dissolves.

I am sceptical, and I would like the analogy audited properly rather than simply denied. Set the pump up concretely, as we did: I currently hold a car-share membership, but I would rather have the train season ticket; given the ticket, I would rather have the bicycle; and given the bicycle, I would rather be back with the car share. For a pound at a time — small enough never to flip any of the three comparisons — someone can sell me the ticket, then the bicycle, then the car-share membership again, leaving me where I started and three pounds poorer, as often as they like. Two wrinkles we came up with have made me doubt that the Dutch-book picture is the right one. First, the cycle is symmetric: a third party with no self-interest could equally pay me a pound at each step to move me round it the other way — car share to bicycle, bicycle to ticket, ticket to car share — and I would end up where I began with three pounds more. If being pumpable is a defect because money flows out, being 'fountainable' ought to be a requirement because money flows in, which is absurd; so the cash flow cannot be doing the normative work alone. Second, the same pump seems to run on someone whose preferences are perfectly transitive at every moment but simply flip between the same two options from one day to the next, and it has nothing at all to say to someone whose cycle is real but whose smallest available payment is larger than her smallest preference gap. Neither wrinkle has an obvious counterpart in the Dutch-book case.

So: give me the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the bookmaker, of the individual bet, of the sure loss, of the agent's own standard of acceptability, of arbitrage, of the single standpoint from which the outcome is assessed — and then tell me which of the disanalogies actually bite on the use he was making of the analogy and which are idle. I especially want to know whether the pump argument, even where it works, is delivering a verdict about a preference ordering at all, or about something else: my suspicion is that the remedy it points to is not coherence but self-coordination, and that a money pump is less like a bookmaker's arbitrage and more like a collective-action problem among my own temporal stages, each able to externalise costs onto the others. If that is overstated, say so; if there is a better analogy, propose it. I would also like you to address whether the cure brings its own exploitability, since a resolute chooser who binds herself to a plan, or a sophisticated chooser who sees the sequence coming and precommits, seems to me to acquire a new vulnerability rather than none. Please write in British English, and be explicit about the epistemic status of what you are telling me: separate what is settled — the mechanics of which sequences of offers are executable against which preference structures — from what is genuinely contested amongst people who work on this (what exploitability arguments can establish, whether diachronic rationality reduces to synchronic rationality, what the pump shows about planning versus resoluteness), and mark clearly where you are handing me your own reconstruction rather than anything like a received view. I don't want a contested position presented as the consensus, and I don't want references — I can find those myself; I want the argumentative structure.
