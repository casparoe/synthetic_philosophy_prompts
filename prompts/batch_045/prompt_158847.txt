I should say at the outset that I have no formal training in philosophy. I work in freight forwarding and have been reading about distributive ethics on my own for about two years, in the evenings, using whatever secondary material I can get hold of. So please explain any term of art you use, and please do not recommend books, articles, or authors — I will do that hunting myself, and a list of names would only tell me what to read, not whether I am thinking straight. What I want from you is an evaluation, not an essay: I have a worry, I have half a dozen candidate ways of dealing with it, and I want you to sort them and tell me honestly which of them are worth anything.

The view, as I understand it: how much moral value a benefit has depends on how big it is and on how well off the recipient is apart from the benefit, with the same benefit counting for more the worse off the recipient is. Total value is then the sum of these weighted benefits across people. Nothing in it says that equality among persons is itself a good.

Here is what I think I can show, and I want it checked before I build on it. If the weighting function is increasing in a person's level — that is, if making someone worse off always lowers their weighted contribution — then the textbook levelling-down case cannot arise: making the better off worse off while benefiting nobody must lower the total, so a pure levelling down can never count as an improvement. On my reading that is why this view was supposed to be immune to an objection that sinks the view that equality is good in itself. Is that right? If the immunity is narrower than I think, or rests on a choice of weighting function that nobody would actually defend, say so.

My difficulty is that the worry seems to me to survive in a different shape, which I have started calling levelling down without a levelling. Two cases.

Case one. Person A is at level 4, person B at level 100, both figures being their level apart from any benefit I might give. I can give A six units or B sixty. If I weight a benefit by roughly the reciprocal of the recipient's level, A's six units are worth 6/4 and B's sixty are worth 60/100, so priority tells me to give A the six and forgo fifty-four units of benefit for no one's sake. Nothing is destroyed, no one is made worse off than they were, and a large possible good is simply not created.

Case two. The same choice a million times over, with a fresh badly off person and a fresh well off person each time, choosing the priority-favoring option every time. Six million units are created instead of sixty million; each badly off person ends up at ten instead of four, still badly off in absolute terms; the well off are exactly where they started; and fifty-four million units of possible good go uncreated. Nobody has been levelled down. It still feels to me like the objection, and I cannot say precisely why — so one of the things I want from you is a verdict on whether case two really is the levelling-down objection in a new costume, or a different objection that happens to produce the same feeling.

Here are the replies I have managed to come up with on my own. Rate each of them.

1. Bite the bullet. If taking the worse off seriously means forgoing fifty-four units to give them six, then forgo the fifty-four; the sense of absurdity is just an undigested utilitarian reflex.
2. Keep priority weighting for the distribution of gains, but add a side-constraint that no option may be chosen which leaves someone worse off than they actually were, or worse off than some feasible alternative would have left them.
3. Impose a sacrifice ceiling: priority may override total benefit only up to a fixed exchange rate of forgone benefit to priority-weighted gain, and beyond that rate total benefit wins.
4. Truncate the weighting. Let the priority weighting be steep below some level at which a person counts as no longer badly off, and let it be flat above that line, so that priority does real work among the badly off and none at all among people who are already doing well.
5. My own idea, and the one I most want tested: change what the weighting is anchored to. On the standard view, as I understand it, the anchor is the recipient's own level apart from the benefit — a baseline. I want to anchor it instead to the range of levels that is actually attainable for that person under the options on the table, so that what counts is how much of the attainable range for them a given option realizes. Under that anchoring, leaving B at 100 when B could have been at 160 is not a neutral omission but a large forgone share of B's attainable range, and it should be weighted accordingly.
6. Debunk the intuition. Treat the pull of these cases as a psychological artifact — our tendency to weight forgone gains as losses, and omissions as less bad than acts — and conclude that it is not evidence about what is morally better.

For each of the six, give me two scores out of ten: one for how much of the worry in cases one and two it actually removes, and one for how much it costs me elsewhere — how much it gives up, how ad hoc it is, how many extra parameters or thresholds it needs before it can be applied. Sort them into three tiers on the basis of the two scores combined, and give me one or two sentences per item saying why it lands where it does. If two of the six are the same move under different descriptions, or if any of them is a position that already has a name and I simply have not encountered it, say so plainly and merge them; I would much rather find out now than after another two years of reading.

Then, if you would, four more things.

First: an overall number between 0 and 1 for how much of the levelling-down worry you think survives against this view, and a second number for how much survives against the view that equality among persons is itself a good. Explain the gap between the two numbers.

Second: whether my fifth idea is genuinely a distinct move or one of the others in disguise, whether it produces anything obviously wrong when you run cases one and two through it, and whether it collapses into something familiar once you press on how the attainable range is to be fixed.

Third: which combination of these replies you would put your own weight behind, scored on the same two scales.

Fourth: the places where the whole question turns on a number I would have to choose — how steep the weighting is, where the truncation line in reply four sits, what exchange rate reply three should adopt. Give me your number in each case and say what would move it. In particular, roughly how steep does the weighting have to be before case one arises at all, and is a weighting that steep independently plausible, or does it look like it was chosen precisely to produce the result I am complaining about?
