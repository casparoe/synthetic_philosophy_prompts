I am a retired plant physiologist who now sits on the animal ethics review committee of a university field station. In retirement I have been attending a philosophy of mind reading group, which is how I became the member who does not regard the following protocol as a mere matter of insect husbandry.

The applicant proposes to train a colony of roughly ten thousand wood ants to a feeder at a fixed compass bearing and distance until the route is stable, and then to divide the colony into two viable fragments. Fragment A receives the queen and about sixty per cent of the workers, among them few of the experienced foragers; Fragment B receives the remaining forty per cent, which contains nearly all the route-experienced foragers, together with a replacement queen. Each fragment is then tested separately for fidelity to the original route. The stated aim is to determine whether the route is stored at the level of the colony and, if so, what becomes of it when the colony divides.

Our counting rules have always defined the experimental subject as an individual organism with a nervous system, so this work has been filed as research on ten thousand insects. Two colleagues take the fission to be unremarkable for that reason. A third argues that if we admit the colony itself as a subject that can learn, remember and survive turnover of its members, then splitting it is a procedure we have never reviewed before and cannot treat as routine.

Please draft the short written opinion I shall circulate ahead of the next meeting. It should state a concrete recommendation — approve, approve with conditions, or defer pending a pilot — with a time frame and a cap on the number of colonies; say what, for the purposes of our review tier, is to be counted as the subject or subjects of this protocol; and give the strongest version of the losing side, which in my view is that this committee has no standing to settle a metaphysical dispute and that whatever rule we write for ants will be quoted back at us the next time someone proposes work on a honeybee colony's dance-derived spatial map or on a termite mound's ventilation geometry. The opinion should also settle a question the applicant has left open: whether the two fragments are the continuation of one colony, two new colonies, or one continuing colony and one mere population of ants, since our rules on the re-use of animals turn on the answer.
