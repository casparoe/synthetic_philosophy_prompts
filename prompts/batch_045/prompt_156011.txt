I do volunteer search-and-rescue in the Cascades, and last February I called a descent in a whiteout off nothing but a feeling, and it was right, and I cant stop chewing on it — someone's podcast put me onto that old question (Meno? that's the one) about why knowledge is supposed to be worth more than just getting it right. Here's where my gut lands on cases, and I want the principle behind them if there is one.

Two of us pick the same gully in a whiteout: I'd hiked it a dozen times, she went on a hunch. Same outcome. I feel mine was worth more — but I also feel like the moment I say "more valuable" I'm smuggling something in.

I glance at my watch, which stopped at 6:00, at 6:00, and leave on time. That true belief seems worth exactly nothing.

Our team lead says "the slope above that cornice is going to go" and can't say why — twenty winters on snow. I want to file that with the knowledge cases, not with the hunch.

My partner gets to the same call from a chain of reasoning in which she misremembers a threshold by five degrees and the error happens to cancel. That feels worse to me than the team lead's hunch, even though she has reasons and can teach me the method.

I ask a chatbot which line to ski and it's right. I act on it, I feel fine about acting on it, I don't think I know, and I'd feel bad passing it on as mine. Somewhere in between.

And: a thing I know that does nothing for me today seems worth no more than a lucky hunch that's actually usable. So whatever the extra worth is, it isn't unconditional.

My tentative principle, which I suspect is broken: knowledge isn't worth more in the case in front of you — I've held that fixed in all of these — it's worth more because it travels, it still holds up in the nearby situations you might have landed in, and you can hand it to somebody else. Two things make me doubt it. The team lead's hunch travels too, in her hands. And knowing a strictly one-off thing (that cornice, right now) is spent the second you act on it, and still looks better than the hunch.

So what principle actually fits, and which of my verdicts does it make me drop? What does it say about the case I keep going back to — I made a call for good reasons in 2019, I've since forgotten the reasons, I remember the call, and it's still true? And give me one case that would separate your principle from its nearest rival, so I can test it myself.
