I run a weekly reading group — about 16 people, half philosophy grad students and half CS PhD students, 80 minutes, small seminar room with a whiteboard and a projector, and I can spend maybe fifteen bucks on snacks — and last week we did a session on the symbol grounding problem (the old worry that a system that only ever manipulates symbols gets its meanings from more symbols and never from the world, so the whole thing spins freely) and it was a disaster in an instructive way. The CS side kept saying the worry is just obsolete: train on enough text written by people who were looking at the world, add a feedback signal from humans, and the world leaks in through the training data, end of story. The philosophy side kept saying that's more symbols all the way down. Nobody moved an inch, and it's personal for me because my flatmate works in ML and this is literally the argument we have over breakfast every weekend and I always lose it. So next week I want to stop arguing about a hypothetical system and make them run into the thing in their own case.

What I want is a game, ideally with real but cheap stakes (candy, snacks, a dumb trophy), where one subgroup of the room gets genuinely fluent in a made-up symbol system — a rulebook for legal strings, a dictionary whose entries are written in the same made-up symbols, a corpus of example strings, whatever works — and can get high accuracy on symbol-to-symbol tasks: continuation, translation symbol-to-symbol, well-formedness judging, maybe even answering questions posed in the symbol language. Then they hit a task where that fluency is visably useless, and another subgroup with the same symbols plus some channel to the actual room — objects they're allowed to handle, or actions whose outcome they actually get to see — can do the thing the first group can't. I don't want anyone to hear the words grounding, symbol, or semantics until the debrief, and I'd rather the failure arrive as a surprise than as a demonstration.

Two things I'd especially like built in, because they're where I think the real argument is. First, I've been toying with a distinction I half invented and I want the activity to test it rather than assume it: the thing that matters isn't causal contact with an object (a camera pointed at a chair is causally connected to it and that means nothing to the camera) but something I've been calling friction — the world being able to correct you in a way that isn't itself just another symbol string telling you that you were wrong. If the rules can make the gap between hooked up to the thing and the thing can push back on me show up as a difference in what people are able to do, great; if the game can't stage that difference, say so and tell me what to say out loud instead. Second, the objection I know is coming: someone will say the first group's failure is just missing information or insufficient training data, not a failure of meaning, and I want the design to make that reply cost something — ideally there's a moment where it's obviously not a data problem, or where the same amount of data delivered through a different channel fixes it.

So: give me the rules, what I need to build or buy beforehand (I have a printer and a weekend), the eight-minute-by-eight-minute timeline, how to split the room and who goes where, the debrief mapping specific moments in the game onto specific claims people were making last week, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (someone will try to cheat by smuggling meaning in, someone else will be annoyingly good at the symbol game and ruin the contrast), and be honest about which part of the problem the activity just can't reproduce so I know which bits I have to lecture on anyway.
