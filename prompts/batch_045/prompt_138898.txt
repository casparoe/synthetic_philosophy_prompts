I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I keep getting handed the same story: some city or country has handed a real decision to a randomly selected citizens' body, and the piece basically writes itself — sortition is hot again. Before I write another one, I want the spec, because I notice every article I read ends up resting on one unexamined requirement ("it's representative!", "it's deliberation!", "juries work, don't they?"). So: what would a randomly selected political body have to actually deliver to count as an improvement on an elected one — good enough, specifically, to justify giving it real power over legislation rather than a purely advisory role? Give me the requirements list with the case or consideration behind each: the cases it has to handle (who draws the pool in the first place, and the regress that invites; what happens when the sample is unrepresentative because the people who most need to be there are the ones who decline; what a body does when an intense minority is right and a deliberative majority is wrong), the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse (descriptive vs. substantive representation; impartiality that comes from randomness vs. competence that comes from something else; legitimacy-by-procedure vs. legitimacy-by-outcomes; representing the people vs. being a sample of them; sortition as selection vs. sortition as decision rule), and the social-science findings it can't contradict — small-group deliberation dynamics, what stratification does and doesn't fix, the fact that a body with no formal link to a decision-maker tends to get ignored (I've been told the Irish assemblies and the French climate convention are the exhibits here; tell me if those are the wrong exhibits).

Then I want the meta-part, which is the part I'd actually build the piece around: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really commitments of one camp dressed up as criteria — "must mirror the population demographically" strikes me as the latter, as does "must beat elections on getting the right answer." And tell me which subset is jointly unsatisfiable, if any. My hunch is there's a three-way squeeze: you want the body small enough that people actually deliberate rather than perform, large and stratified enough to be descriptively representative on several axes at once, and drawn from volunteers because compelling service is politically dead on arrival — and no design gets all three. Say whether that's the real tension or whether I've picked the wrong three horns.

Some context so the list isn't abstract: the occasion for all this is a proposal somebody described to me at a conference that I've been calling the "sortition veto" in my notes — 250 people drawn by stratified lot, one-year non-renewable terms, paid roughly the median wage, each given access to two rival "advocate" teams hired to argue the case both ways, and its only power is to send a piece of legislation back once with a public statement the government must answer within six months. Assume, for the sake of argument, that a (made-up) meta-analysis found minipublics move participants toward the expert consensus about two-thirds of the time but that the downstream effect on actual policy is near zero unless the body has some formal trigger it can pull. Judge that design against your list and tell me where it fails. Also — and this is the thing I keep tripping over — is randomness doing the moral work here (everyone has an equal chance, so no one is subordinated) or is it just doing anti-corruption work (no one can buy a seat in advance)? Those seem like very different justifications and I suspect half the people I quote are using one while thinking of the other. Optional and brief: a scorecard of the three or four main versions on offer — full replacement "lottocracy", a sortition chamber with only delay-and-answer powers, sortition for agenda-setting only, and the one-random-voter-per-district enfranchisement lottery idea — but keep the weight on the criteria, I can do the scoring myself. Sorry this is long, my editor says I do this.
