I'm finishing a novel — a close-third literary book that alternates between a memory clinic and a twenty-year marriage — and my editor has flagged the same problem in three different scenes: she can't tell whether what I've written is a seduction, a bargain, or a consent, and she says the scenes read differently depending on which it is. The cases are these. Marit, fifty-eight, with early-onset Alzheimer's, signs an advance consent form for a research protocol involving lumbar punctures; two years later, still articulate and apparently content, she refuses the same procedure each morning and by lunch has forgotten refusing it. Her son hires a home health aide who tells him she will stop coming unless he raises her hourly rate — a threat she is perfectly entitled to carry out — and he signs the new contract that afternoon. A surgeon mentions the ordinary catalogue of risks to a concert pianist and says nothing about a 0.4% chance of permanently losing fine control in her left fourth finger, judging it immaterial; she consents; it is the single outcome she would have refused the operation for. A character leaves a data-sharing toggle switched on because it shipped switched on, having skimmed the screen. And in the marriage, a woman says yes to her husband out of the wish to keep the evening quiet, to a man who has every reason to want a genuine yes and who is applying no pressure he could name. I don't want to settle any of these by authorial intuition, so before I revise I'd like the specification.

Write me the desiderata an account of valid consent has to meet: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must sort, the distinctions it must respect, the empirical or institutional facts it can't contradict — each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a taste. I want the distinctions treated as genuine desiderata in their own right: consent as against mere permission, waiver, acquiescence, or authorization; the gap between there being no valid consent and there being consent one nonetheless shouldn't act on; the scope problem, that consent attaches to an act under a description and so can be given to something the consenter would withhold it from under another; whether validity depends on the consenter's reasons at all, or is content-independent in the way a moral power is supposed to be; whether competence is a fixed threshold or slides with the stakes of the particular decision; whether a theory owes third parties an answer they can read off from the outside, since someone has to be able to rely on the consent; and what to say about a consent that persists across a self whose values and psychological continuity are eroding, where the person who agreed and the person who undergoes seem to be, to some degree, different people. Say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the prejudices of one camp — I suspect at least one thing I've been assuming is a requirement is really a preference. And if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly and show it, because that would itself be the answer I need.

Two more things. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out otherwise attractive accounts — the one that, if you give it up, you suddenly can't exclude some view you'd rather exclude. And note that my purpose constrains the theory: I need something that lets a close-limited narration render a character's choice as consensual or not from inside her point of view, while still letting another character, and the reader, tell from the outside whether he was permitted to proceed; if those two demands pull the theory apart, that matters more to me than the tidy version would. Then, briefly — a paragraph or a short table, since this isn't the part I'm paying for — score the three families of view I keep circling: the one that makes consent an internal attitude, the one that makes it a public act you perform by expressing it, and the one that moralizes it, holding consent valid only when the terms and surrounding circumstances are themselves permissible. Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases behind them.
