ok so we've been running a nomic-ish game in a group chat for a few months — six players, the rules can amend themselves, you score by getting changes passed. our version departs from the published one in a few places, so please argue from what's below rather than from whatever you remember about the original game.

R1 (immutable): everyone follows the rules in effect.
R2 (immutable): a proposal passes on a majority of yes votes. amendments to immutable rules pass only unanimously. where a mutable rule conflicts with an immutable one, the immutable one wins.
R3 (immutable): the voting window is 48 hours. the Arbiter rotates each round; the Arbiter rules on disputes about what the rules require, and rulings stand until a passed proposal overrides them.
M1 (mutable): whoever proposed a passed change gets a point. first to five wins.
M2 (mutable): proposals go in #proposals and start with PROPOSAL:.

Last round Joon posted, correctly formatted:

PROPOSAL: R2 is repealed and replaced with: "A proposal passes on a majority. Amendments to immutable rules require unanimity — except amendments to this rule, which require a majority."

It got 4 yes, 2 no. Then this happened. I sat the round out and kept my mouth shut; both of them have now asked me to referee and I honestly can't, so I'm outsourcing it.

1. Joon: The self-application is the entire point. R2 never says the threshold is measured at the moment of posting. Once the window closes the rule in effect is the new one, and the new one says a majority is enough for this kind of amendment. And a rule that can only be changed by the procedure it itself specifies makes "immutable" mean "unchangeable," which nobody intended — the whole idea of these games is that everything except the bare minimum is up for grabs.

2. Nadia: "Temporal scope" is a distraction. A proposal has no force until it's enacted, and R2 is the thing that tells you when it's enacted. A rule that does not yet exist cannot authorize the act that brings it into existence — that isn't a gap in the ruleset, it's what a power-conferring rule *is*. And your principle has no stopping point: if a proposal may name its own threshold, then "this passes if I vote yes" is valid too, and every threshold in the game dissolves into whatever the proposer asks for.

3. Joon: There is a stopping point. "Majority" is a threshold the ruleset already recognizes; "one vote" appears nowhere in it. I'm extending an existing threshold to a case R2 sorts badly, not inventing one. Also your "cannot authorize the act that creates it" line proves too much — it's true of every amendment, since the amended rule is never in force when the proposal goes up. You only notice it when the content touches the threshold itself.

4. Nadia: It isn't true of every amendment. In the ordinary case the old R2 authorizes the enactment because you satisfied it, and the content of the new rule does no work in authorizing anything. Here the old R2 doesn't authorize it — 4 of 6 — and the new one isn't in force, so nothing does. You're pointing at a feature all amendments share in order to smuggle past the one that matters. Second: what you wrote isn't really an amendment, it's a demotion. R2's job is to say which rules are harder to move. If a majority can move R2, "immutable" just means "harder until a majority says otherwise," which isn't a kind of rule, it's a mood. Call it what it is: transmuting R2 into an ordinary mutable rule, wearing an amendment as a disguise.

5. Joon: (a) It leaves R2 in the immutable set — still listed as immutable, still overrides mutable rules. Kind unchanged, content changed. Your disguise test would relabel any change to R2 as a transmutation, which renames my move instead of answering it. (b) The ratchet is the real problem with your reading: R2's unanimity requirement is self-perpetuating, so the only way to loosen it is to satisfy it. It can only ever tighten. Give me one reading on which a majority could ever loosen R2, other than mine.

6. Nadia: On procedure: it isn't self-perpetuating, it's entrenched, and entrenchment is the entire function of having two tiers — if any majority could undo it you'd have one tier and a superstition. On your (a): fine, I'll grant R2 stays immutable, and I'll grant that some content changes to R2 are amendments. What I won't grant is that a rule can lower the bar for changing *itself* while remaining the rule that sets the bar. That isn't an amendment to R2; it's R2 voting on its own jurisdiction, and a rule can't be the judge of its own amendment — same reason we don't let the Arbiter rule on his own disputes.

Then Sam, who has a vote and almost never posts: why not do it in two steps — pass a mutable rule M3 saying "amendments to R2 require a majority," which only needs a majority to enact, then amend R2 under it? Costs one round.

Joon: yes exactly, and if the two-step works the one-step should too, since they differ only in how long they take, and in both cases what licenses the amendment is a majority the old R2 didn't authorize.

Nadia: it doesn't work. R2 says immutable beats mutable, so M3 conflicts with R2 and dies the moment it's enacted. The two-step is a placebo.

Also Nadia muttered at one point that Joon gets a point under M1 if his proposal passes, and Joon said that's true of every proposal and is the whole reason M1 exists, so it can't be an objection to this one.
