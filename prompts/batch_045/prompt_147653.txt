I'm a retired plant physiologist — spent forty years looking at how crops cope with drought — and I've recently got into philosophy by sitting in on a reading group on Dewey. We were going through the opening chapter of Dewey's Democracy and Education (it's more than a hundred years old now), and I got stuck on a bit I'd never noticed before. Dewey writes:

"The most notable distinction between living and inanimate things is that the former maintain themselves by renewal. A stone when struck resists. If its resistance is greater than the force of the blow struck, it remains outwardly unchanged. Otherwise, it is shattered into smaller bits. Never does the stone attempt to react in such a way that it may maintain itself against the blow, much less so as to render the blow a contributing factor to its own continued action. While the living thing may easily be crushed by superior force, it none the less tries to turn the energies which act upon it into means of its own further existence. If it cannot do so, it does not just split into smaller pieces (at least in the higher forms of life), but loses its identity as a living thing. As long as it endures, it struggles to use surrounding energies in its own behalf. It uses light, air, moisture, and the material of soil. To say that it uses them is to say that it turns them into means of its own conservation. As long as it is growing, the energy it expends in thus turning the environment to account is more than compensated for by the return it gets: it grows. Understanding the word "control" in this sense, it may be said that a living being is one that subjugates and controls for its own continued activity the energies that would otherwise use it up. Life is a self-renewing process through action upon the environment."

I recieved a bit of a telling-off from a friend (a retired engineer) who says Dewey is just dressing up education in fancy biological metaphors and that none of this has anything to do with democracy or inquiry; but I beleive there's supposed to be a continuity, because Dewey later treats democracy not merely as a set of political procedures but as a shared way of life in which experience is communicated, and he treats inquiry as an experimental reconstruction of a problematic situation rather than a search for certainty. What I can't see is how the Chapter One story is supposed to underwrite that later experimentalism about democracy and inquiry. Is Dewey saying that a democratic community is literally 'alive' in the self-renewing sense, so that the clash of interests and the shocks of social change are the social analogue of the environmental energies an organism turns to its own account, and inquiry is the social analogue of the organismic 'control' that keeps a shock from being merely destructive? Or is the continuity only looser and analogical: organisms maintain themselves by using the environment, and communities should maintain themselves by using criticism and experiment, even though the mechanisms are quite different? I'm especially confused about what would count, in the social case, as the difference between being 'shattered' like the stone and merely losing some equilibrium but continuing to grow — e.g. is a society whose institutions rigidly resist certain kinds of change supposed to be less 'democratic' on this view because it cannot turn the blow into further growth? Please don't give me a bibliography or a list of secondary reading; I only have the passage and my own wits, and I'd like you to work from the quotation itself. Could you explain, in plain terms, what work the stone-versus-living thing contrast is doing for Dewey's later account of democracy as associated living and inquiry as experimental reconstruction, and tell me whether my engineer friend is right that it's all just metaphor? Please answer in British English.
