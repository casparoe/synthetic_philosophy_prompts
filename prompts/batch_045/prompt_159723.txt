I'm a senior in high school, and last term our philosophy elective spent one class on Moore's paradox — the puzzle of why it is absurd to say, of something, that it is so while denying in the same breath that you believe it is so. Since then I have been collecting example sentences with two friends from debate club and writing down how each one sounds to me, and my reactions don't form any neat pattern. I would like to know what principle, if there is one, is behind them.

My verdicts:

a. It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining. Bad. Not self-contradictory — I can cook up a story in which both halves are true — but it sounds like something no one could seriously say.

b. It's raining, but I believe it's not raining. Also bad, and worse than (a).

c. It's not raining, but I believe it is. Nearly fine: it sounds like someone admitting a hang-up they can't shake. This bothers me, because it is the mirror image of (b) and yet doesn't feel nearly as bad.

d. I don't believe it's raining, but it is. Bad as well, but somehow less bad than (a), even though it is the very same conjunction in the opposite order. This is the one I keep going back and forth on.

e. It rained during third period, but I didn't believe it did. Fine — I was in the windowless band room and only saw the video later.

f. Maya believes it's raining, but it isn't. Fine: she is simply wrong.

g. Jonas said it's raining, but he doesn't believe it. Fine: he was lying.

h. It's raining, but I can't believe it. Fine, because there it means I am amazed. Same words, completely different result, which makes me think the principle has to say something about what believe is doing.

i. It's raining, but I'm not certain it is. Fine.

j. It's raining, but I don't know that it is. Bad, and I can't see why (i) and (j) should come apart.

k. It's raining, but I don't believe that I believe it's raining. Odd, but less bad than (a). I would have expected the paradox to repeat itself one level up, and it doesn't seem to.

l. A line in our student council's official statement: The budget is balanced, but we don't believe it is. Not absurd to me; I read it as the majority voting to say this while none of them believes it.

m. An AI assistant answering a question about the weather: It's raining in Cleveland, but I don't believe it. Not absurd either — but I am not sure whether that is because it has no beliefs or because asserting and believing come apart for it.

My own guess is that asserting something is, or at least normally amounts to, an avowal of belief in it, so that tacking on a denial of that belief is a bit like taking back what one has just said. That explains (a) and (b) nicely, and (e) through (g) fall out of it too. But it does not explain why (c) is nearly fine while (b) is not, why (d) is milder than (a), why (h) and (i) survive, why (j) fails, why (k) is intermediate, or why (l) and (m) are fine at all.

Please answer in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the principle you would put in place of mine, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a sentence I had not thought of; (2) which of my cases forces which clause of it; (3) which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, stated honestly; (4) its verdicts on three cases I have not settled — the sentence in (a) but with the disbelief pushed into the recent past (It's raining, but until a minute ago I didn't believe it), the same pattern said by a patient with a neurological condition that leaves them unaware of their own mental states (I'm in pain, but I don't believe I am), and the same pattern written privately in a diary rather than said to anyone; and (5) the single smallest change to my own principle that would do the most work.
