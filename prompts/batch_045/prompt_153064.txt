i'm a research assistant in a lab that does moral-psych / experimental philosophy stuff, and this term I've been piloting a batch of vignettes for a study on whether the Knobe-style intentionality asymmetry still shows up when the agent isn't a single person. I've read our materials so many times that I've completely lost track of whether my own reactions hang together, and my roommate (who does semantics) says the whole thing is just a fact about the word and not about anything mental, which I don't know whether to find helpful or beside the point. So: what principle am I implicitly using?

Here are my gut verdicts, on variants of the classic chairman-and-the-environment setup ("the program" for short). (1) Chairman is told the program will harm the environment, says he doesn't care about the environment at all and only wants profit; program runs; environment harmed: he harmed it intentionally. (2) Same but "help" instead of "harm": he did not help the environment intentionally. (3) Chairman actively wants the environment harmed, that's his secret reason for backing the program, but the mechanism he counts on turns out not to be what causes the damage — the harm comes about through something he never considered: I want to say not intentional, or at least much weaker than in (1), which is weird because he's more blameworthy here than in (1). (4) Omission version: he's told the river gets polluted unless the company spends a million dollars, doesn't care either way, declines to spend it, river gets polluted: intentional, but somehow diluted, like 70% instead of 100%. (5) Mayor backs a bike lane knowing and regretting that it will put a neighborhood bakery out of business; the lane will save lives and she thinks it's obviously worth it: I don't want to say she intentionally put the bakery out of business, even though she foresaw it, the outcome is bad, and she does nothing to prevent it. (6) Neutral version: the program will alter the migratory path of some birds, nobody is benefited or harmed, and the chairman is told and doesn't care: my mind goes blank, and if forced I say not intentional. (7) Committee version: a five-person investment committee votes 3-2 for the program knowing it will harm the environment; all five are indifferent to the harm; the two no-voters know exactly as much as the three yes-voters: the committee intentionally harmed it, the yes-voters did, the no-voters didn't — but then the committee's intention isn't the sum of its members' intentions, and I don't know what to do with that.

My own guess is that "intentionally" isn't doing descriptive work here at all, and that we reach for it when the agent is blameworthy for a foreseen bad outcome, which would explain (1) vs (2) and probably (5). But (3) breaks it — maximal blameworthiness, still no "intentionally" — and (6) is a case where the valence drops out and the word just goes dead, which the blame story predicts without explaining. Can the blame account be repaired (blame for the outcome vs. blame for the agent? something about whether the agent's will was behind it?), or should I drop it for one of the other stories people tell — the assertability/conversational one, or something about whether the agent treated the outcome as a reason for acting? Give me a principle, stated precisely enough that I can test it, tell me which clause each of my verdicts is paying for, and if no single principle gets all seven, tell me which verdict to give up and why. Also: what does yours predict for two cases I can't settle — (a) the agent is a machine-learning system optimizing a profit objective, the harm to the environment is real but no individual human foresaw it; (b) the side effect is probabilistic, a 10% chance of harm, chairman indifferent, and it happens.
