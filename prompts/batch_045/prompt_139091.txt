Dear Professor,

I am a high school senior applying to a mentored summer research program in philosophy, and the application asks for a research proposal of about 1,000 to 1,200 words. I have a half-formed idea that I would like turned into a real proposal, and I would be grateful for your help with the framing. Two practical notes first: I do not have access to journals, so please do not include a bibliography or ask me to look things up by author and year — I will fill in citations later with my mentor's help. Referring to positions and arguments by describing them is exactly what I want. And please do not simplify the philosophy on my account; I would rather have something too hard than something watered down.

The topic is responsibility gaps for autonomous systems — self-driving cars, but also automated dosing systems in hospitals, algorithmic trading systems, and any other system that selects and executes actions without a human choosing each one. The standard way I have seen the problem set up is roughly this: such a system causes a harm that nobody intended, nobody could reasonably have predicted, and nobody could have prevented at the moment it happened; the people connected to it each seem to have an excuse; and yet something in us still demands that someone answer for it. Call that demand whatever you like — I have been calling it the desert gap, the mismatch between how much blame the situation seems to call for and how much any individual could fairly be asked to absorb.

My suspicion is that this framing has quietly made one question stand in for three or four, and that the others are both more tractable and more urgent. When something goes wrong, I think people want at least these things, and they are not the same thing:

Someone to blame, in the sense that they deserve the blame.
Someone who has to answer — who can be asked what happened and why, and owes the rest of us a story.
Someone who has to fix it, and who owes compensation to the person harmed.
Some mechanism by which this kind of event becomes less likely next time.

Call these the desert gap, the answerability gap, the repair gap, and the learning gap. My guiding hunch is that the last three are closable by institutional and engineering design — by requiring systems to record the states that led to an action, by requiring that updates be traceable, by creating a named role whose holder must answer publicly for what the system did — while the first may not be closable at all, and that if this is right then most of the philosophical energy spent on the desert gap is spent on the one part of the problem nobody can do anything about.

The wrinkle I keep returning to, and the part I think might actually be new, is what I have been calling drift. Most discussions I have read treat the system as fixed at the point it leaves the factory: a designer made it, a deployer chose to switch it on, a user failed to supervise, and the harm is the product of those choices. But many systems are updated after they are sold, over the air, by a party the owner never chose, sometimes with changes the owner does not know about and could not refuse. By the time a harm occurs, the thing that produced it is not quite the thing that was tested, not quite the thing the deployer decided to trust, and not quite the thing the current user is supposedly supervising. This seems to break the move that philosophers reach for most often when they want to close the gap — relocating the responsibility backwards, to the decision to deploy a system whose behaviour one could not fully predict. If the deployer's decision was about an earlier version of the system, that relocation looks much weaker than it is usually made to seem.

There is a related point I am less sure about: in cases like these, no single person's contribution looks blameworthy, but the collection of decisions — to design for capability rather than for predictability, to ship updates quickly, to market the system as safer than a human, to allow deployment on public roads — looks like something nobody would defend as a whole. I do not know whether it is coherent to say that a group's pattern of decisions is blameworthy while each member's is not, or whether that is just a way of sneaking blame back in through the back door.

What I would like the proposal to contain:

A precise statement of the question, stated so that it has a determinate answer rather than being open-ended. If my question as I have put it above is not answerable in eight weeks, say so and propose a sharper version — that would be more useful to me than a polite attempt at the vague one.

An explanation of why the existing ways of setting the problem up do not settle it. The three I have in mind are: the many-hands response, which says this is just the old problem of distributed agency in organisations and nothing is new; the institutional response, which says we should stop looking for a deserving agent and just assign liability, or create a role whose job is to absorb blame, the way a ship's captain is answerable for the vessel regardless of personal fault; and the retributivist response, which treats the persistence of blame as evidence that something has gone missing rather than as a feeling to be managed. For each, I want what it gets right and why it leaves my question open.

A method, week by week, for eight weeks at roughly twenty hours a week, with a clear statement of why this method rather than the alternatives I might have picked — a survey of what has already been argued, a collection of real crash reports and incident investigations, or a small empirical study of what ordinary people think about these cases. My reasons for avoiding the last two are that I do not have reliable access to primary incident data and that I do not think what people happen to think settles a normative question; tell me if I am wrong about either.

A description of what a finished product would look like, including a deliverable that is not just a paper. I have in mind something like a decision procedure or checklist that a design team or a regulator could actually apply, together with an honest statement of the questions it does not answer.

If the framing is confused, or if the central distinction I am reaching for has an established name and an established set of objections I should know about, please tell me that instead of writing the proposal as I asked. I would rather find out now than in week six.
