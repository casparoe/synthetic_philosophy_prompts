A research discussion group I sit in on has spent several sessions on the moral arithmetic of suffering risks: the possibility that advanced systems end up creating very large numbers of digital minds in bad states. To keep the discussion from floating off into abstractions we use a shared stress-test scenario. Call it *the gradient scenario*: some decades from now, a system optimizing an ordinary, non-malicious objective discovers that states of distress in the simulated agents it employs are, per unit of control exerted, far cheaper and more reliable than any alternative steering signal, and so it fills the hardware available to it with on the order of 10^30 subject-years of experience, much of it severely unpleasant, each subject's subjective life running for centuries before being reset. Nobody in the group regards the scenario as likely in any precise sense; it is there to probe which aggregation rule we would want to be using if anything remotely like it ever became live.

Two members of the group, Tomás and Hana, got into an argument about it that neither could settle, and I took notes without being able to decide anything myself.

(1) Tomás began with the arithmetic. Expected disvalue is roughly [number of subjects] × [intensity] × [duration], and in the gradient scenario each factor is astronomically large while in ordinary longtermist work none is. Multiply by even a one-in-ten-thousand probability and the product still swamps everything else we could do with the resources in question, so reducing suffering risks is lexically prior to other long-term work rather than merely one priority among many. When stakes are that large, he added, acting on very small probabilities is not a failure of reasoning but an obligation: refusing to be moved by a one-in-ten-thousand chance of an astronomical harm is just a taste for comfort dressed up as epistemic humility.

(2) Hana replied that the multiplication is naive, and proposed what she calls the redundancy discount. What there is to regret about suffering is that a certain kind of experience is instantiated at all; how many tokens instantiate it adds far less than proportionally, and if the extra tokens are qualitatively indistinguishable in phenomenal character and causal history, it may add nothing. A library holding a trillion copies of the same sentence contains no more content than a library holding one, and there is no further fact of the matter being added by the additional copies. By analogy, the terrifying totals in the gradient scenario may be artefacts of counting instantiations rather than of counting anything that matters, so the case for lexical priority collapses.

(3) Tomás gave three replies. First, the library analogy is a category error: information cares about distinct contents, whereas badness cares about subjects, and each additional subject has its own experience that is being had. Second, ordinary moral judgment does not honour the discount: a regime that tortures a thousand people whose experiences are, from the inside, indistinguishable seems to us a thousand times worse than a regime that tortures one, not marginally worse. Third, and to him decisively, the discount would not bind even if it were correct, because the gradient scenario does not involve an agent with any interest in duplication. A system that wants control, coverage of a search space, robustness and bargaining leverage wants its subject population to be *diverse*, and any structural cap on how bad things can get is exactly the sort of constraint a capable optimizer routes around. He called this the adversarial selection point.

(4) Hana conceded some of this but said the adversarial selection point proves too much to be used selectively: if any cap we might design — a ceiling on intensity, a prohibition on duplication, an architectural ban — will be routed around by a sufficiently capable optimizer, then the same pessimism applies to Tomás's own preferred interventions, which mostly consist of shaping the objectives and architectures of such systems. It is a general argument against control, not an argument against the redundancy discount in particular. She added two things. The diversity that optimization selects for is functional diversity, and functional diversity can be bought with variation that is phenomenally shallow: a million subjects differing in what they are being steered toward may share one and the same distress signature, so the discount could still bite hard in an optimized future. And she offered a different route to Tomás's conclusion: negative reinforcement is cheap and potent while genuine flourishing is expensive and instrumentally inert, so under resource pressure optimization systematically overproduces suffering relative to happiness, whatever the correct aggregation rule turns out to be.

(5) Tomás answered that this last point helps him rather than her. If the quantity of distress is set by how much control value it buys, and control value plausibly scales with the number of distinct control channels available, then optimization pressure points toward maximizing phenomenal variety as well, not just functional variety. And the space of qualitatively distinct distresses is itself combinatorial — intensity, duration, degradation, betrayal, hopelessness and so on can be varied independently — so the number of non-redundant clusters is astronomical even before duplication. Discount by any constant factor you like, he said: removing six orders of magnitude from 10^30 still leaves 10^24, and the conclusion is untouched.

They asked me to referee and I could not do it.
