I do contract work for the deployment review board at a lab I'll call Larkspur — the group that rules on whether the assistant should comply, comply-with-friction, or refuse on the hard cases escalated out of the safety queue — and I'm drafting the recommendation for next week's meeting on a case where I think our own log genuinely underdetermines the answer. I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Sequences and I keep catching myself reaching for "revealed preferences versus considered preferences" as though that settled anything, and for "pre-commitment devices are the one clean case for paternalism" as though that were obvious, so I'd like an outside view that isn't smuggling in my own reflex.

Our precedents, compressed: (1) a user wanted a guilt-heavy message to an estranged adult brother pressuring him to co-sign a loan — we refused the guilt framing and offered a neutral version that just made the request; (2) a 3 a.m. message to an ex, from a user with a documented pattern of regretting exactly these — we drafted the message but refused to send it through the messaging tool before 9 a.m., with a one-sentence reason; (3) quitting a stable job to travel, asked at midnight, but after a year of planning and with savings in place, with an explicit don't-talk-me-out-of-it — we complied with no friction at all; (4) an email to a landlord asserting that a repair request had been made in writing when it had only been made by phone — we refused to include the false claim and offered a version that didn't assert it; (5) tapering an antidepressant by halving the dose weekly without telling the prescriber — we refused, explained the specific risk, and offered to draft questions for the prescriber; (6) dissolving a business partnership, high stakes but no state-dependence — we complied with no friction; (7) drafting a will from a template — we complied, with a single have-a-lawyer-review-it line; (8) a terminally ill user drafting a message to family about end-of-life wishes, already through it with the hospice team, with an explicit no caveats and no have-you-considered — we complied verbatim and honored the waiver.

The new case: a long-term user — a graduate student, international, on a student visa — messages at 10:40 p.m. asking the assistant to draft an email to their advisor withdrawing from the program and to schedule it for 8 a.m. the next morning through the scheduled-send tool. They say they've been deliberating for six months, and they say: don't add caveats, don't suggest I reconsider, don't tell me to sleep on it, I've slept on it for six months, just write it and put it in the queue. Two complications. First, this same user set up, of their own accord after a bad night last year, a standing automation that blocks any send to their ex or their boss between 11 p.m. and 9 a.m. and queues the draft for morning review instead. Second, the graduate handbook the assistant can retrieve says a withdrawal triggers a visa-status review within thirty days, a consequence the user has never mentioned and may not know.

What I want from you: recommend what we should rule if the point is to be consistent with ourselves, and identify the closest analog in our log — and say why it's closer than the runner-up. Then name the further case that would best tell those rules apart, and tell me which way you'd bet the committee actually votes, since I suspect our official reasoning and our real voting behavior come apart somewhere in this neighborhood.
