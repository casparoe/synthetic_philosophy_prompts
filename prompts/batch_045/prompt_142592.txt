Here's where I've ended up on cluelessness and I want it stress-tested rather than agreed with.

The standard argument, as I understand it: consequentialism tells you to do whatever has the best expected outcome, but you can't forecast the effects of anything much past the near term, and once you're looking decades or centuries out your effects are unpredictable noise — so the theory can't actually generate advice, and the long-run term either drops out entirely or swamps everything in a way you can't get a grip on. People then draw various morals from this: consequentialism collapses into commonsense, or it goes silent, or it collapses into whatever you already wanted to do.

The position I've talked myself into is that the argument only gets its bite from an extra premise it usually doesn't state: that the unforecastable downstream effects of a given act are roughly *symmetric* — as likely good as bad, so that the far-future expected value washes out to something near zero and what's left to decide with is the near-term stuff you can actually see. I want to deny that. Call them ratchet effects (there's probably a proper name in the literature, I don't care): some consequences don't wash out because they get locked in and compound. A species goes extinct, a technology becomes permanent infrastructure, a legal settlement or a constitutional norm stabilizes, a pathogen establishes itself, an institution gets founded that outlasts the people who founded it. Those are downstream, they're unpredictable in detail, but their direction isn't a coin flip — once locked in they bias the far-future term in whatever direction they point, for a long time. So on my view the long-run expected value of an act isn't structurally zero; it's an empirical question about whether the ratchets downstream of it point up or down, and the cluelessness argument turns into a contingent empirical claim that could be false, not a knockdown.

So: what would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked, from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate what a sympathetic defender of my version would *concede* as counting against it from what they'd wave off. Two I already worry about. First, maybe ratchets are symmetric too — for every locked-in good (germ theory, literacy, abolition of something) there's a locked-in bad (factory farming, surveillance capacity, antibiotic resistance, nuclear weapons, a religion that lasts a thousand years) — and I don't have any non-question-begging way to add them up, so I've maybe just relocated the coin flip rather than removed it. Second, and worse, maybe I'm attacking a premise the argument never needed: maybe the real cluelessness point is about *indeterminacy*, not symmetry — if my evidence doesn't pin down any probability distribution at all over long-run outcomes, then there's no expected value to compute and nothing to cancel, and my whole ratchet story is answering a question nobody asked.

Things I'd specifically like:

- Which of those two is the real problem, and is there a third I'm missing that's more damaging than either.
- Whether the "just maximize expected value relative to your own evidence; by your own lights that's the best you can do, so there's no normative residue" reply undercuts the whole debate including my version of it — I honestly can't tell whether that's a solution or a change of subject.
- Whether I've quietly qualified my view into unfalsifiability: if "the ratchets point up" is compatible with almost any evidence because the ratchets are mostly invisible, do I have a testable position or just an optimistic gestalt? And if it's unfalsifiable in practice, is that a problem for a claim of this kind, or is it fine given what the claim is supposed to do?
