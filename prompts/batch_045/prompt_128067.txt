I'm putting together a short proposal — four months, funded internally by my department, deliverable is one ~8000 word paper — on Kant's claim that we have no direct duties to animals, only duties with regard to them. Don't bother with citations, I'll fill those in myself.

The half-formed idea: this literature always sorts the options into two. Either the duty really is indirect, and Kant routes its force through the effects of cruelty on our own character and on how we then treat other humans; or the indirect-duty story is just a mistake, and animals have to be brought in as ends in themselves somehow. I think there's a third option nobody has developed seperately: the duty is indirect but self-regarding — grounded in what the agent owes herself (not brutalising her own moral sensibility, roughly), not in downstream effects on other people. There's a line somewhere in the late ethics texts or the lecture notes that gestures this way; I'll track it down, but I don't want the project resting on exegetical credit for noticing it.

What I want stated sharply: can the self-regarding reading do three things at once — explain why pointless cruelty is wrong when nobody will ever be affected and nobody will ever know (the last-person cases that sink the standard reading), still distinguish gratuitous cruelty from ordinary painless killing for food, and do both without either smuggling the animal's own good back in or over-generating into duties about how we treat inanimate nature, or about anything at all?

Approach: month 1, a small taxonomy of what "indirect" could mean (causally mediated by effects on others / grounded in the agent's standing under the moral law / self-regarding); month 2 a battery of cases — last human on earth, agent who dies seconds after the act, agent already maximally callous toward people, farmed animal killed painlessly versus tortured for fun, someone wrecking something beautiful and inanimate alone; month 3 run every case against every reading and tabulate where they actually diverge; month 4 write up.

Be honest about what would count as the thing failing: my own guess is that if on every case the self-regarding reading returns the same verdict as one of the other two, or if it needs the animal's welfare to seperate cruelty from use, then the "third option" framing is dead and the paper should just say so. Also flag where you think this framing is most likely to break, and what would convince you I'm asking the wrong question.
