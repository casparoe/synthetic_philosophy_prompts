THE QUESTION

Data: In a large twin study of a continuous trait T in population P, the correlation for monozygotic (MZ) twins is 0.72 and for dizygotic (DZ) twins is 0.44. The correlation between the twins' mothers and fathers — i.e., between spouses — for T is 0.30. Assume no dominance or epistasis, no gene–environment correlation, and that T is measured without error.

(a) Using Falconer's formula, compute the additive genetic (A), shared-environment (C) and non-shared-environment (E) components. In a sentence or two, say what the resulting heritability does and does not license you to conclude. Then comment on whether the spousal correlation of 0.30 should change your numbers and, if so, in which direction.

(b) Populations X and Y are two wild populations of the same species, living in habitats that differ in several respects. Measured within X, the heritability of T is 0.56; measured within Y it is 0.60; and the mean of X exceeds the mean of Y by 9 units. A commentator writes: "Since T is 56–60% heritable, most of the 9-unit gap between X and Y must be genetic." Evaluate this inference; say what kind of evidence would actually settle whether the gap is genetic; and say what would happen to the heritability if you pooled X and Y into a single sample.

(c) A school-board member says: "T is 56% heritable in our population, so it's determined by genes and there's no point spending money on an environmental intervention." Respond, being precise about what the 56% does and does not settle. Would your answer change if there were gene–environment correlation — that is, if people's genotypes partly caused them to end up in the environments that affect T?

(d) A commentator proposes a "personal heritability index." For each person i, take their additive genotypic (breeding) value g_i, expressed on the same scale as T but measured from zero rather than as a deviation from the mean, and divide it by their observed score P_i. He says the average of these personal heritabilities across the population should come out close to the heritability of T, and that where it doesn't, that shows the published heritability estimate is bogus. Explain what is wrong with this proposal.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) A = 2(r_MZ − r_DZ) = 2(0.72 − 0.44) = 0.56. C = 2r_DZ − r_MZ = 0.88 − 0.72 = 0.16. E = 1 − r_MZ = 0.28. These sum to 1, so that checks out. So h² = 0.56: 56% of the variance in T in this population — i.e. of the differences between people — is statistically associated with additive genetic differences between them, in this population, in the range of environments that actually occurs there. It does not mean 56% of any one person's T is produced by their genes, and it doesn't tell you anything about what causes the population mean or about causes that don't vary in this population. It's also not the same as the SNP heritability from a GWAS chip, which usually comes out lower because common SNPs only tag causal variants imperfectly. A useful way to read the number: if someone's T is one standard deviation above the population mean, their expected breeding value is 0.56 SD above the mean. On the spousal correlation: yes, it matters, because 0.30 shows there is assortative mating for T, which means parents are more similar to each other than random pairs. That raises both twin correlations, but it raises the MZ correlation more than the DZ one, since MZ twins share all their genes while DZ twins only share half, so the gap r_MZ − r_DZ gets inflated, and since Falconer's formula doubles that gap, the estimate comes out too high. So 0.56 is an overestimate and the true additive heritability is probably something like 0.45.

(b) The inference is invalid. Heritability is calculated within a population from the variance within it, and it is silent about the cause of a difference between two population means. The classic illustration: take seed from one genetically variable maize variety and sow half in poor soil and half in rich soil. Within each field the differences in height are almost entirely genetic, so heritability within fields is very high, and yet the difference between the field averages is entirely environmental. Same structure here. To actually settle whether the 9 units are genetic you'd need to equalize the environments — a common-garden or reciprocal-transplant design, or cross-fostering in animals — or randomize genotypes across the two habitats and see whether the gap persists. Also, the fact that the estimate comes out 0.56 in X and 0.60 in Y already shows that heritability isn't a fixed property of the trait; it's a property of a trait-in-a-population-in-a-range-of-environments, and some of that gap could just be sampling error. If you pooled X and Y into one sample, the heritability would not be the weighted average of 0.56 and 0.60: the pooled phenotypic variance now includes the between-population variance as well, so the denominator changes. If the 9-unit gap is environmental, the pooled heritability would come out below 0.56; if the gap is genetic, it could come out above 0.60.

(c) The board member is confusing "heritable" with "unchangeable." A heritability coefficient is a ratio of variances in one population exposed to one range of environments; it says nothing about what would happen under an environmental change that isn't represented in that range. Height is highly heritable in well-nourished populations, but average height rose substantially over the twentieth century as nutrition improved, and PKU is a single-gene cause of cognitive impairment that is almost entirely preventable by diet. So high heritability is perfectly compatible with an intervention moving the whole distribution. Note also that the 44% that isn't A isn't all "environment" in a causal sense either: C in a twin design is confounded with the fact that MZ twins are treated more similarly than DZ twins. As for gene–environment correlation, it makes the case against the board member stronger, not weaker: if a genotype leads people to select into or end up in the environment that produces the trait, MZ twins will resemble each other more than DZ twins because they end up in more similar environments, and the design books that resemblance as genetic even though the causal chain runs entirely through the environment. Such a trait could have a very high measured heritability and still be completely preventable by changing the environment. But there is a limit, and this is where the board member is half right: because only 44% of the variance in this population is non-genetic, no environmental intervention could move the population mean by more than about sqrt(0.44) ≈ 0.66 standard deviations, and in practice far less.

(d) Three things go wrong. First, the index rests on a category mistake: heritability is a property of a population of differences, not of a single score. The variance decomposition tells you how differences between individuals covary with genetic differences; there is no fact of the matter about what "fraction" of one person's T is genetic, because you can't look inside an individual's trait value and find a genetic part sitting next to an environmental part — both are needed to produce any value at all. Second, the ratio g_i/P_i isn't even well-defined independently of how the scale is set. If T were a temperature, switching from Celsius to Fahrenheit or Kelvin would change every person's index while leaving the heritability untouched; and on the zero-point version the index blows up for anyone whose score happens to be zero. (To be fair, h² isn't invariant under every rescaling either — a nonlinear transformation like taking logs would change it — only under linear ones.) Third, the average of a bunch of individual ratios isn't the ratio of the variances: even with the scale fixed, there's no reason for the mean of g_i/P_i to land on V_A/V_P. So a mismatch between the index and the heritability wouldn't show that the heritability estimate is bogus; it would only show that the index was the wrong statistic.
