Setup: I moderated a panel last month at one of those quantative-history workshops, on whether you can forecast social instability. Afterwards three of us went for dinner: Marcus, who does computational macrosociology and is broadly sympathetic to cliodynamics, and Hannah, a historian of nineteenth-century state finance who thinks the whole field is phrenology with better PR. They went at each other over whether the structural-demographic / secular-cycle people actually called the mess of the last few years. I want a point-by-point ruling, not a general essay.

Move 1 — Marcus: Around 2010 Turchin published a short forecast saying political instability in the US and Western Europe would climb through the 2010s and peak in the 2020s. That was fitted on historical indicators and published a decade ahead of the fact. Since then: polarization, mass protest, a mob in the Capitol, fiscal gridlock, and an epidemic of elite-on-elite nastiness. And it wasn't a lucky calendar guess — the model says instability comes out of popular immiseration plus elite overproduction plus state fiscal strain, and he flagged stagnating wages and the swelling pool of credentialed young people with nowhere to go as the drivers, not just the date. Name me another dated, mechanistic, ahead-of-time call in the social sciences.

Move 2 — Hannah: Two problems. First, the target, "socio-political instability," is a composite of riots, assassinations, demonstrations, constitutional crises, defaults — pick your index. With that many dials, and the choice of index made after the fact, any turbulent decade can be booked as a hit. Second, more important: the parameters were calibrated on the same country's history up to roughly 2010, and that history already contains the previous instability spikes. If the machinery has a roughly fifty-year rhythm in it, then "next spike around 2020" isn't a prediction, it's the extrapolation of a curve drawn through the very point you're now pretending to forecast. It's a postdiction with a press release.

Move 3 — Marcus: The sine-wave jab is wrong and it matters. It's a dynamical model with separate state variables — population pressure, real wages, elite numbers and intra-elite competition, state fiscal capacity — coupled through lags. Nobody types in "fifty years"; the periodicity is emergent from the feedbacks. That's why it gets the ordering right, not just the timing: living standards flatten first, then the elite pool swells and turns on itself, then state finances buckle, then open instability, each step trailing the last by roughly a generation. A curve-fitting exercise doesn't reproduce a causal sequence it was never given. And on your index complaint: fair, the outcome measure is loose — which is exactly why he and a collaborator later published their own retrospective scorecard, where they had to commit to specific series in advance of scoring them.

Move 4 — Hannah: Emergent or not, for the US you have maybe two and a half cycles of decent data, so an effective sample size of about two — the same data that fixes the period also fixes where the peaks sit, so scoring the next one isn't an independent test. And the "causal sequence" impresses me less than it impresses you: those state variables are all reconstructed from overlapping source bases. Grain prices, wage series, tax rolls and officeholding records feed several of your variables at once, so of course they move in a tidy order — the coupling is baked into how the variables were built, not discovered. Even granting the timing, timing doesn't identify a mechanism: inequality, social media, deindustrialization and climate all "predicted" the 2020s too, and they can't all be the one true cause.

Then I jumped in with something I'd half-remembered, and I want that adjudicated too:

Move 5 — me: Someone described a test I can't stop thinking about. Build "patched" or Frankenstein chronologies: take a real polity's two-century series and splice its first half onto the second half of a completely unrelated polity's series, interpolating across the join so nothing looks obviously broken. Then run the standard fitting procedure on the patched series. Apparently a substantial fraction of them produce clean-looking secular cycles with the same lead-lag ordering, because short, noisy, heavily interpolated series plus smoothing will manufacture oscillation. If that's right, the base rate of apparent cycles in this kind of data is high, and the US fit is weaker evidence than it looks.

Move 6 — Marcus: That's the right kind of test and I'd take a bad result seriously — but the right comparison isn't to zero, it's to the real data. If real chronologies show the pattern at, say, twice the rate the patched ones do, the model is tracking something real, just less than advertised. Also be careful: splicing creates a discontinuity, and if your procedure smooths, it will find cycles — so the test partly measures your smoothing rather than the theory. The real out-of-sample test is time and place: fit on one region's history, call the timing for another, before the fact.

Until someone runs the version where the sources don't overlap, the honest summary of the 2010 call is: one intriguing, ambiguously scored hit by a model with a lot of knobs.
