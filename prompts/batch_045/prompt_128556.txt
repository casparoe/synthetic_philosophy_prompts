I've been stuck on a version of the Buridan's ass problem for about two years — in a reading group, in two abandoned draft papers, and in arguments with two friends who disagree with each other and with me — and I've reached the point where I care less about who is right than about why nothing ever moves. Stripped down, the dispute is about whether an agent can make a genuine choice when her reasons are exactly balanced: two options, each equally good by her own lights, she knows they are equally good, she must pick one now, and no further consideration is even apparently relevant to the choice. One side — call them the differentiators — says no. Intentional action has to be explained by what the agent took to favor the option she took; a consideration that favors both equally cannot explain why this one rather than that one; so either there was really some asymmetry after all, in which case the described case never arises, or whatever breaks the symmetry is a coin flip, a whim, a mood, a neural twitch — something that happens to her rather than something she does, and so not full-blooded agency at all. The other side — call them the permissivists — says that responding to the fact that these are equally good is itself a rational response: when a norm permits either, the agent can take the permission rather than a preference as her guide, and no arbitrariness worth worrying about follows. They also press a regress: the differentiator tells her to break the tie by deferring to a coin, but choosing to defer to a coin is itself a choice among equally good randomizers, and choosing which randomizer to use is one too, and so on up. I should add that there is a third, dismissive camp, which holds that exact ties are a measure-zero pathology: real agents'Utilities are never precisely equal, or where they are, the balance is broken by arbitrarily small perturbations, so the puzzle is a curiosity about an empty case.

That last move looks to me like it fails for a large and interesting class of cases, and I'd like your reaction to this before anything else. In a discrete or digital decision space — a game with exactly symmetric moves, two items indistinguishable by any available test, an agent whose deliberation returns fixed-precision values, an agent choosing under a hard deadline with no time to look for further asymmetries — ties are not the knife-edge limit of near-ties but generic, and no epsilon small enough to break them exists. Call these non-deflatable ties: ties that cannot be dissolved by perturbation and cannot be escaped by postulating an unnoticed asymmetry, because in the described case there is nothing left to notice. My friend who works on animal decision-making keeps insisting that real animals placed between two equal food sources do not starve, they pick, which I take to be beside the point — but it does raise the question of where, if anywhere, the empirical facts about how agents actually behave in symmetric-choice tasks are supposed to enter, and whether either side is answerable to them. My own hypothesis is that the standing dispute is three disputes under one label: a verbal-ish question about what a preference is (a state the agent is in prior to acting, versus whatever her choice reveals), a substantive question about what an explanation of an action has to look like (must the reason that explains a choice be difference-making with respect to the alternatives, or is that really a thesis about causation smuggled in under the guise of a thesis about reasons?), and an empirical question about whether agents ever face non-deflatable ties. If that is right, the differentiators and the permissivists may largely be answering different questions under one name.

I want this pushed rather than confirmed. Diagnose the impasse using what each side actually says: is it verbal, substantive, a clash of bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, a hidden empirical question, or several questions sharing a heading? Tell me which of those is doing the real work, whether the regress argument and the difference-making principle are as strong as each side takes them to be, and what each side would itself concede as counting against it — not just what its critics say. I'd also like to know if there's a framing I've missed. And then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a formal result about tie-breaking, a distinction that dissolves the case, a finding about how agents actually behave in symmetric choices, or a change of question — and if your honest answer is that no progress should be expected here, say why. I'm not after citations or a survey of the literature; I want the structure of the disagreement, argued for.
