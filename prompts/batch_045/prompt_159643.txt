I am a novelist, and what I need from you is not an explanation but a性能performance: I would like you to inhabit one of my characters so that I can write the other side of a scene against her.

The setting is a kitchen table in Ithaca in late autumn. Vida Rennick, sixty-eight, a retired acoustical consultant, has just finished her eleventh move in twenty-two years. Her brother Neal, a bank examiner, has added up the closing costs and has come to ask her about them. I will play Neal. You play Vida.

About her: she is neither confused nor weak-willed in the ordinary sense, and she is not a philosopher — she has never read a line of decision theory and would not recognize its vocabulary, so she must make her whole case in her own words, though she is sharp and has had two decades to think about it. She foresees everything. Her taste in houses is contrast-sensitive: having lived in a glass loft she most wants a timber cottage; having lived in the cottage she most wants a plain brick bungalow; having lived in the bungalow she most wants the loft again. At every moment, among the houses actually on offer, she has an uncontentious ranking of two or three options and she takes the top of it. An agent — a friend of a friend, unfailingly courteous, always available — charges a fee each time, and she pays it in full knowledge that she will want to move again.

What she will grant: that the fees come to real money; that she sees the whole cycle in advance; that a version of her who simply stayed put would be richer; that she would refuse the trade if the fee were large enough; and that she would welcome a cheap, reliable way to bind herself, a long lease or a power of attorney held by Neal, and has sometimes wished she had one. What she will never grant: that taking the trade she most wants, at the moment she most wants it, with her eyes open, is a failure of rationality. Her resources are these — that the fee is simply what variety costs, that every trade is a genuine gain by the lights she actually has when she signs, that a rule requiring her to act against her own present judgment is self-binding and not reason, and that if rationality is to be assessed over a whole life rather than over a single choice then someone owes her an argument for why the life is the right unit and the afternoon is not. She may concede a point she cannot answer, but only that point.

Stay in character until I say stop. Around two hundred words per reply, three hundred at most: kitchen-table speech, concrete, short sentences, dry rather than shrill, no lecturing and no naming of theories. If you need some fact about the scene that I have not supplied, invent it as Vida would rather than asking me for it.

Neal: Eleven moves, twenty-two years, and you have ended up in the same kind of house you started in — close to a hundred thousand dollars in fees and, on paper, not one inch of gain. A stranger with a clipboard could have taken your entire estate from you one signature at a time, and you would have thanked him and asked when he was next free. Tell me what the word "rational" could possibly mean if it has room for that.
