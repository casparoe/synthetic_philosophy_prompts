Hi — I'm the convenor of a tiny methods reading group (five of us, mostly early-career historians plus one philosopher of social science who keeps derailing us), and I need your help producing a document. Two members of the group have been arguing past each other for something like two years: Dr. Mireia Fontcuberta, who does history from below in the microhistorical vein and has spent eight years on one tenant miller, Josep Ribalta, and the forty-odd notarial protocols he turns up in; and Prof. Dieter Wexler, who works on fiscal-military state formation and works almost entirely from aggregate series — tax yields, militia rolls, correspondence between intendants — across three provinces. Please act as an experienced philosopher of history who has been asked to serve as neutral scribe for an adversarial collaboration between them, and think carefully before you write: do a bit of quiet brainstorming first on where these two genuinely differ and where they're just using words differently, and only then draft. Don't give me a reading list or tell me to go look things up — you've got no web access and the document should stand on its own. Same for citations: no bibliography, no block quotes from anyone, invented names only for invented material.

Here's the shared setup I want you to assume, because I think the only way to keep them honest is to pin everything to one body of material they've both agreed to work on. Call the town Vilanova de Serra, a mid-sized market town in the Catalan interior; the material is the hearth-tax and militia rolls for 1788–1795, the Ribalta protocols, and the correspondence of the local tax receiver. And please make use of four gadgets the group has already hammered out, rather than generalities: (1) the substitution test — if you swapped Ribalta for another tenant miller in the same tax decile, would the shape of the story change, and so what; (2) the scale-drift audit — take a mechanism Mireia identifies at the level of the household and re-run it at the level of the parish, then the province, and record where it stops being identifiable; (3) the averaging objection — Dieter's point that a case selected for documentary richness is selected on the dependent variable, since rich records track litigation, wealth and proximity to power, so the best-documented case is systematically the least typical one; and (4) index-case privilege — Mireia's reply that a single well-documented case can be revelatory in a way a sample can't, because averaging across cases destroys the mechanism you were trying to see. A fifth thing is in the background and I want you to place it somewhere: whether a historical explanation is supposed to yield portable causal knowledge, or orientation, or the recovery of what a life was like, and whether those demands can be traded off.

Deliverable, roughly 1100–1400 words: a short header stating the shared object of study; then a numbered agreed-claims section of 8 to 12 claims that both of them could actually sign — each one substantive enough that some historian could coherently deny it, so nothing like "evidence matters" or "context is important", and nothing either of them would read as having been written by the other side's graduate student; then a disagreements table with one row per residual disagreement, columns for Mireia's preferred wording of her own view, Dieter's preferred wording of his, and a verdict on whether the disagreement is verbal or substantive (with a sentence on how the verbal ones would be dissolved); then three cruxes, where for each you give the sub-question, the specific test or case on the Vilanova material both would accept as bearing on it, Mireia's prediction in advance, and Dieter's prediction in advance; and finally a short closing note on which parts of the old argument they should now concede were never about anything. Where you think one of their disagreements is really about what history is for, say so explicitly instead of papering over it.
