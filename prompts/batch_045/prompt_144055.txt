I have no formal training in philosophy — I read translations of Indian philosophical texts in the evenings, mostly Nyāya material about what counts as a source of knowledge — and I have been keeping a list of ordinary cases with my own gut verdicts, hoping a principle will fall out of them. The list is below.

As I understand the framework: a cognition is genuine knowledge (pramā) only if it is produced by one of a short list of pramāṇas — perception, inference, comparison, and testimony — and the interesting questions are which source was at work in a given case, and whether a candidate source is really a separate one or just a special case of another.

My cases:

1. Dusk, a coil on the path ahead. I take it for a rope, and it is a rope. In that light I would equally have taken a snake for a rope. Verdict: I know it is a rope. The fragility does not bother me.

2. Grey stuff drifting over a ridge; I take it for smoke, infer fire, go to raise the alarm. There is a fire beyond the ridge, but the grey was mist from a stream and the fire is a camp stove in a hollow I could not have seen or smelled. Verdict: I did not know there was a fire, though I was right and though anyone would have reasoned as I did.

3. My mechanic of nine years tells me the alternator is dead. It is. Verdict: knowledge. A stranger on the bus who happens to be right says the same thing and I believe him. Verdict: not knowledge, or not the same thing. What bothers me: if the difference is his track record, my knowledge looks like an inference from past cases, and testimony stops being its own source. If it is just that he is reliable whether or not I know he is, then the stranger may be reliable too, and I have to say I knew.

4. A friend tells me a gavaya is a beast shaped roughly like a cow. Weeks later at a sanctuary I see one and think, so that is a gavaya. Verdict: knowledge, and neither inference nor testimony — my friend never mentioned this animal, and I am not reasoning from a mark. It feels like its own source.

5. I read a field-guide description of a wombat — thick-set burrower, short legs, waddling gait — see one at a zoo and think, that is a wombat. Verdict: this is not the same kind of thing as 4; it feels closer to recognition, or to memory of what I read. But I cannot say what the difference is, and 4 and 5 look structurally identical to me.

6. I remember where I parked and I am right. Verdict: not pramā, since nothing new was produced, only a copy of what I already knew. But I also remember that seven eights are fifty-six and use it to check a supplier's invoice, and there the memory seems to be doing real work.

7. I glance at the coil, feel fear, look again, and now think: that is a rope, not a snake. Verdict: knowledge. But of what sort? The rope part looks perceptual; the not-a-snake part is not something my eyes delivered, and it is not inference either.

The principle I have been toying with: a cognition is pramā when it is true, is not a copy of something I already knew, and was produced by a source whose normal procedure actually succeeded here rather than failing and getting lucky; and a source is a distinct pramāṇa only if it can deliver something the others could not. Test that against the list and repair it. Cases 1 and 2 are where it strains: the procedure in 2 looks like the same procedure that works in ordinary smoke-and-fire cases, so I am not sure failed versus succeeded is doing any work.

Please give me: the principle in one sentence, then each clause with the case that motivates it; your verdicts on two cases of your own construction that I have not listed; an honest statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which one you would overturn if you had to drop one; and a note on where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what would change your mind. A friend who did take a philosophy degree says 1 and 2 are the Gettier problem in different clothes — if that is right, say so and say whether it matters here. Lead with the one-sentence principle before any discussion, and keep the whole reply under 700 words.
