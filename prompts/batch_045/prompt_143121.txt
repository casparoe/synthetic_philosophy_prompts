I work in root cause analysis at a freight company, and me and two colleagues have started noticing something we keep doing, and I want to know whether it has a name.

Our whole job is basically "what's the best story that explains this mess" — a friend who did a philosophy minor says that's got a fancy name, inference to the best explanation or abduction or something. Anyway. What we've noticed is that our best explanation tends to get *better* over time, and we don't think it's because we learned anything new. We think it's because the list of stuff the explanation is supposed to cover keeps getting quietly edited.

Two cases.

1. A run of late deliveries in the north region. The explanation we settled on was that the nightly batch job in the sorting software was finishing late, so vans were going out with half their loads. That covered maybe seventy percent of the incidents right away. The rest: over the next couple of months they one by one stopped being the kind of thing we were explaining. A depot that doesn't even use the batch job had late vans — that got called a dispatch problem, different failure mode. Late arrivals where the van actually left on time became transit delays, not a depot thing. Nobody ever said ignore those, and honestly each reclassification was reasonable on its own, some of them were probably right. But by the end the batch job story explained everything on the list, and the list had been trimmed to fit the story.

2. Not work, family history. Why did my great-uncle's shop go under in the sixties? The family answer is that he was undercapitalized after the expansion. The bits that don't fit — the fire, the year his brother ran it, the near-identical shop two streets over that survived on less capital — have all, over about forty years of retelling, been reclassified as separate stories. The fire was after he'd already lost it. That year was really my grandmother's business. Again: each one plausible, maybe even correct, and the explanation now fits perfectly.

I also think there's a mirror image. Once we'd committed to the batch job story, new complaints started getting *folded in* as extra successes — the routing complaints, the driver overtime — where before we'd committed they'd have been logged as unrelated grumbling.

So the thing has two directions, and that might be one phenomenon or two, I can't tell.

I had a look around and it isn't anything I could put a name to. It isn't bolting an extra assumption onto a theory to save it; nothing gets added to the batch job story. It isn't the thing where a theory explains everything and therefore explains nothing — the story has real content, it's the target that moved. I thought of the Texas sharpshooter, but as I understand it that's drawing the circle around your bullet holes after you've fired; here the target was drawn up front and then narrowed. Moving the goalposts isn't right either, that's about changing what counts as success, not about what's on the inventory.

So: is there an established concept for this — the cumulative, individually defensible redrawing of what an explanation is answerable for, in both directions? And I don't just want a label. If there's a literature I'd want to know what the nearest concepts are and where exactly this sits relative to each. If there isn't one, then: what's the right way to carve it, when is "that's actually a different phenomenon" a legitimate move and when is it this thing, and does any of it matter for whether best-explanation reasoning can work at all, or is it just sloppy bookkeeping that a careful person could avoid?
