I work in trust and safety at a virtual-reality platform that also runs a sizeable in-world economy: users design and sell clothing, furniture and decorative objects to one another for real currency, and we take a commission on each sale. I've been asked to write the internal document that sets out what our enforcement rules actually presuppose, and the more carefully I list them, the more they seem to pull in different directions. So before I draft it: what theory of virtual objects, and of virtual harm, does a practice like ours presuppose? I want the conceptual structure rather than a literature survey or anything that would need a library — our lawyers handle the legal question, and I'm not asking which jurisdictions have prosecuted what.

Three parts of the practice seem to me to be doing the real work. First, on ownership: our terms say that the user owns nothing, that every item is a revocable licence, that we may seize or delete anything at will, and that when a long-standing player dies there is nothing to pass on, so we refuse the family's request to transfer the contents of the account to an heir — but we also permanently ban players for taking another player's items, we describe this in public statements as theft, and where the taking involved breaking into an account we have sometimes referred the matter outward instead of simply closing the ticket. Second, on identity: after a crash destroyed a batch of items last year we rolled the database back and told people their items had been restored — the same items, we said, not replacements; then a bug duplicated a limited-edition object several thousand times, and although the copies were byte-for-byte indistinguishable from the originals we deleted every copy and left only the originals with whoever had held them before the bug. Players reasonably pointed out that we cannot have both: either the item just is its state in our database, in which case the copies were as genuine as the originals, or identity depends on how an item came into existence, in which case a rollback does not give you back the same item either. We had no reply ready. Third, on harm: we hand out permanent bans for non-consensual sexual touching of another person's avatar, and we ship a default personal-space bubble that prevents other avatars from coming within arm's reach; but we host combat experiences in which avatars are dismembered by the thousand, and we do not treat avatar death as a harm at all, even when one player deliberately targets another repeatedly. Our own staff disagree about whether the line is drawn by consent, by what an avatar stands for, by downstream effects on the person, or merely by what revolts the team.

So I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed and then interrogated. What must be true for these rules to make sense, as opposed to what merely fits comfortably with them? Where are the three parts mutually consistent, and where do they commit us to incompatible things — in particular, is there any coherent account of what an in-world item is that simultaneously makes it unownable and uninheritable, makes a duplicate not the same item, and makes taking it from someone a genuine harm? And on harms: is a virtual harm a real harm, or is the harm always to a person, with the virtual event merely its occasion? Then the practical part of the answer, which is what my document needs: if we replaced the view we are implicitly working with either with the view that in-world items are genuine objects with genuine owners, or with the view that they are nothing at all and the only real facts are facts about people's experiences and about our servers, which of our rules would have to change, and how? And, relatedly, what rationale for the touching-versus-killing line would make it look like more than an accident of our team's sensibilities — or is the line genuinely underdetermined, in the sense that several different accounts of the wrong involved would each license exactly the enforcement policy we already have?
