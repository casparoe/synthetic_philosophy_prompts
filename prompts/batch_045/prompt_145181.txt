I'm defending my dissertation prospectus in three weeks — a twenty-five minute talk followed by forty minutes of questions. The thesis is that the margin-for-error argument against luminosity succeeds against perceptual and phenomenal conditions but leaves a restricted KK principle standing, because that argument's sorites construction needs a condition that can vary across arbitrarily small, subject-indiscriminable differences, whereas what KK demands is not the detection of a condition but an all-or-nothing endorsement of the first-order judgment: a second-order state whose correctness is secured by default epistemic entitlement rather than by any evidence about one's own condition, and to which the adjacent-case series therefore has no grip. I call this the granularity reply.

The committee is four people, none of whom works on KK directly, and I know roughly where each of them stands. The first is a Williamsonian who takes anti-luminosity to have settled the fate of KK outright and will suspect that my restricted principle concedes the interesting claim while keeping the words. The second works formally, will want the principle stated as a constraint on an accessibility relation in a model with a finite agent, and will notice at once that the granularity reply threatens to prove too much: run the same move on being in pain and luminosity comes back, which I do not want. The third works on introspection from a phenomenal-concept angle, is happy to grant that self-knowledge of attitudes is special, and will press on what the supposed asymmetry between attitudes and experiences is meant to rest on. The outside member is a cognitive scientist who studies metacognitive monitoring and will assume, unless I stop him, that my principle is an empirical claim about how confident people are when they happen to be right.

Write me the prep sheet: seven or eight objections in the order they will actually arrive, each phrased the way that particular person would put it rather than in my vocabulary, with a two-minute reply to each and an explicit note on where the reply is softest. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes of the question period and what I have to hold no matter what. And include the one question I am hoping nobody asks — my guess is some version of the charge that if the second-order endorsement is entitled by default and grounded in nothing I can detect, then knowing that one knows is not a cognitive achievement at all and my KK does no work the words alone wouldn't do, but tell me who asks it and how it is phrased.
