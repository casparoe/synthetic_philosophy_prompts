I'm a third-year PhD student writing a paper on material constitution, and I keep coming back to an exchange I overheard after a works-in-progress talk last month, partly because I can't referee it and partly because my supervisor's only comment — that the whole debate is verbal and I should say so and move on — doesn't feel like an answer. The speaker had run the standard case: a figure is made from a single lump of clay, the statue and the lump share all their parts at every moment at which they both exist, but the lump would survive being rolled into a ball while the statue would not, so by Leibniz's Law they differ in properties, are not identical, and we have two coincident material objects. Two people in the audience, Marta and Dov, stayed behind afterwards and argued for about half an hour. Both made claims I find hard to dismiss, and I honestly cannot tell who has the better of it.

(1) Marta opened by saying the standard case is rigged by being described as a finished product, and substituted what she called the Threshold case: a sculptor works subtractively from a two-hundred-kilo block of clay over six weeks, removing chips and then larger pieces, and the result is catalogued as a work titled Half-Sleeper. If the statue is a second object that begins to exist somewhere in that process, she said, there ought to be a determinate moment at which it appears, and there plainly isn't one: any candidate threshold chip is arbitrary, and she is not willing to posit an object whose coming into existence has no determinate time. Her positive view is that there is one object, the clay, which acquires a shape, and that statue talk is talk about a phase or a manner of describing that object rather than about a second thing. (2) Dov replied that the Threshold case is a sorites for the predicate is a statue and nothing more — we already know how to handle vagueness in predicates, and nobody thinks the vagueness of is bald shows that a new object pops into existence — and then reasserted the modal argument: there is a change the lump survives and the statue does not, so they differ, so they are distinct. He added that Marta needs the same apparatus he does, since she too must say when is a statue starts to be correctly predicable of the clay. (3) Marta came back with two points. First, in her own case we are happy to let the finished work survive the loss of a third of its matter and a drastic change of shape, so the bare premise that the statue cannot survive extreme deformation cannot be doing the work on its own; what seems to matter is whether the change is gradual and internally driven or sudden and imposed — and if persistence conditions are sensitive to the rate and causal history of change, a single object can have such conditions too. Second, she said the modal argument proves too much: if any divergence in modal profile spawns a new object, then we also owe ourselves something coincident with both that survives squashing only if the squashing happens before noon, and she wanted to know where Dov stops and on what principle. (4) Dov said he would accept the historical point gladly rather than as a concession, on the grounds that the identity over time of an artifact is partly a matter of provenance, so a statue's surviving slow carving while not surviving a sudden squash is not ad hoc stipulation but what artifact identity is like. On proliferation he said he would rather accept many coincident objects than give up the modal argument, and that the question how many objects are on this plinth is well formed only relative to a sortal: one lump of clay, one statue, one portion of matter, and so on. Then he turned a case against her: if the finished figure is fired in a kiln, the clay is gone — it is ceramic now — yet the work survives and travels to exhibitions; on Marta's view, he said, the museum's centrepiece went out of existence in the kiln. (5) Marta answered that the kiln shows only that the sortals under which we trace one and the same thing can come apart: the clay stops applying and the statue goes on applying, which is just what one should expect if there is a single thing and several ways of keeping track of it — the same thing is graceful qua statue and heavy qua lump, and survives firing qua statue while ceasing to be clay qua clay. She then pressed what she took to be the decisive point: coincidence requires that two material objects can share their matter, their form and their location and differ only in modal and historical respects, and Dov has never argued for that — he infers it from modal premises that already presuppose it. (6) Dov said the modal premises are not stipulations but answerable to practice: conservators replace matter grain by grain and the catalogue still lists the same work; someone who buys Half-Sleeper has not thereby acquired a heap of clay; and if Marta is right, he said, there is no distinction left between restoring a work and replacing it. At that point somebody had to catch a train and it fizzled out.

Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — for each of the six moves, what is right in it, what is mistaken or question-begging or merely rhetorical in it, and whether it actually advances that speaker's side — and then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them were missing? I'd also like your view on two things neither of them made explicit: whether the argument changes shape if you assume a perdurance rather than an endurance view of persistence, and whether there is a stable object for the expression the clay to refer to in the first place, given that lumps have vague boundaries and shed pieces. And if my supervisor is right that the disagreement is merely verbal, I'd like to know what would have to be true for that to be the right diagnosis, since I don't think either Marta or Dov would accept it.
