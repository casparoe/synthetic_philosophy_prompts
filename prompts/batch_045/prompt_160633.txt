I'm teaching a seminar this term on analogy and conceptual fluidity (upper-level undergrads plus a few first-years grads), and at the same time I've been throwing together a small set of analogy items to use as an eval for a tiny model we're prototyping at work. The two projects have collided and I need help turning the collision into a take-home.

The eval items are letter-string analogies in the classic style — abc → abd, ijk → ? — except I moved them onto rings, because I wanted to see what happens when a domain loses its ends. Concretely:

- Objects are necklaces: beads on a closed loop, each bead carrying a letter from a, b, c, d. I write them ⟨a b c⟩ for beads a, b, c going clockwise. There's no clasp and no first bead, so two necklaces count as the same object when one is a rotation of the other: ⟨a b c⟩ = ⟨b c a⟩ = ⟨c a b⟩.
- Successor wraps: a→b, b→c, c→d, d→a.
- An item gives you one or two example pairs and asks for a third.

Here's where it fell apart. I paid two friends to label 20 items and they agreed on 11. Then I tried to adjudicate and found I couldn't, but not because they were being sloppy — the disagreements seem to be of different kinds.

⟨a b c⟩ → ⟨a b d⟩, so ⟨d a b⟩ → ?
One of them says ⟨d a c⟩: the bead where the clockwise successor-chain breaks is b (a→b holds, b→d doesn't), so advance that bead. The other says ⟨a a b⟩: the alphabetically greatest bead is d, advance it, and with the wrap d→a. Both fit the example pair, because on ⟨a b c⟩ those two descriptions pick out the same bead.

⟨a b c⟩ → ⟨a b d⟩, so ⟨b c d⟩ → ?
Both of them say ⟨b c a⟩, and for different reasons. So this one looked clean in the answer key and isn't clean at all.

⟨a b c d⟩ → ?
The successor-chain never breaks, so "the bead at the end" has no referent. One friend advanced every bead and got ⟨b c d a⟩, which is the same necklace he started with — I only noticed because I was checking by hand and thought he'd made a copy-paste error. The other wrote "no answer". A third possibility someone floated is advancing the alphabetically greatest bead, giving ⟨a b c a⟩.

And when I tried to fix things by adding a third example pair, it sometimes helped and sometimes didn't, and I can't articulate the difference.

So: write me one take-home question for the seminar out of this. 48 hours, 1200 word limit, 25 marks total. Constraints on the question itself:

- It should be unanswerable by reciting the vocabulary — nobody should get credit for saying "slippage", "fluid concepts", "the slipnet", "temperature", or "Copycat" without doing work in the ring domain.
- It should force students to tell apart three things I keep conflating: a concept genuinely slipping into a neighbour; the same transformation described two different ways; and an item that simply doesn't determine an answer.
- It should make them say what "the right answer" is here and what my answer key should do (one key? several? grade the rule instead of the string? report a distribution of answers?), and what follows if a set like this is used to score a model.
- It should make them handle the ⟨a b c d⟩ case, where applying the rule seems to change nothing, and say whether "no answer" is a legitimate response to an analogy item.
- It should make them say when adding another example pair disambiguates and when it doesn't.

Then give me a model answer at about 900 words, and a marking scheme: five or six must-hit points with marks adding to 25, the specific move each one requires, and one line each on how students normally blow it. I want the A/B split to be crisp.

Finally — and this matters to me because I'm going to get argued with in office hours — add a short paragraph at the end on how confident you are in the diagnosis your own model answer gives, and single out the one claim in it you're least sure of. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I've got the syllabus, and I'd rather have the argument then the bibliography.
