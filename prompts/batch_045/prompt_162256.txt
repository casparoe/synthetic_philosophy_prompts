Hey — I write a column on science and ideas, and on thursday I'm giving a 25-minute talk to a political psychology reading group and then taking questions for 40 minutes. The room is roughly twenty social and personality psychologists who actually run studies with the moral foundations questionnaire (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, liberty — the usual six), six or seven political philosophers who work on public reason and deliberation, and a couple of journalist friends who showed up for the wine.

My thesis has two halves. First: the foundation-profile findings (liberals loading mostly on care and fairness, conservatives spreading more evenly across the binding foundations too) are real enough, but the interesting fact isn't how many foundations each side draws on, it's an asymmetry I want to call the audibility gap — the difference between hearing some consideration in some foundation's vocabulary as a moral reason at all, and hearing it and then rejecting it. Progressive audiences seem to register loyalty, authority and sanctity talk as moralized but wrong, i.e. as a reason they decline; conservative audiences seem to register care-and-fairness-only arguments as thin or incomplete rather than as a reason they decline, which is a different failure mode and makes translation between the two sides asymmetric in difficulty. Second: none of that tells you who is right about policy, but it does generate one real norm for reporting — you owe readers the other side's strongest argument rendered in the vocabulary that side's own audience counts as reason-giving, not just the version that lands hardest in your readers' vocabulary. That second half is what I actually care about and where I think I'll get hit.

Be my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I'll really get from that specific room — the psychologists' ones (self-report and measurement, sampling, wether the audibility gap is just the old data relabeled, wether it's post-hoc rationalization all the way down) and the philosophers' ones (the is-ought gap, why isn't your norm just strategic framing, and what happens to public reason if reasons aren't mutually audible) — ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way that person would actually say it out loud in a seminar, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Also: what should I concede up front so I don't look like I'm overselling, what do I have to hold, and what's the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Keep it punchy, and in American English please.
