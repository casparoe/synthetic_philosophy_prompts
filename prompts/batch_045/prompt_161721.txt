I sit on the Committee on Socially Responsible Investment of a mid-sized private university, and we have to deliver a written recommendation to the board's finance committee in three weeks. I need you to draft the memo I will circulate under my name.

The case before us: a student and faculty petition demands that the endowment fully divest from two livestock and meat-processing firms within the current academic year. Our endowment is about $1.4 billion and is mostly externally managed; roughly 60 percent sits in commingled index vehicles where we hold no share-level voting rights at all, and the remainder is in separately managed accounts where we can direct proxy votes and file shareholder resolutions. The petition says continued ownership makes us participants in a collective harm — greenhouse-gas emissions, deforestation, and the rearing and slaughter of animals — and that divestment is the only way not to be implicated.

Our chief investment officer's counter-memo is a version of the inefficacy objection, sharpened in a way I think is genuinely hard to answer. Applied to consumption, the objection is that my skipping one burger does not save any animal: the animal was already born, and a single unit of demand almost never crosses the threshold at which a supermarket reorders or a processor adjusts its forward contracts. Applied to divestment, it is stronger still: we sell our shares to somebody else, the firm's capital is unaffected, and the firm's real-world activity is unchanged. The CIO adds a twist I want you to engage directly — call it the counterfactual-with-replacement point. The honest counterfactual is not "nobody owns these shares" but "some other holder owns them," and that other holder is, if anything, less likely to press the firm on emissions and welfare. On that comparison, divesting does not make the difference between pressuring the firm and not pressuring it; it makes the difference between our pressuring it and someone else not doing so. Our leverage, such as it is, comes from retaining that position and using it.

There are two further wrinkles the committee has floated, and I want the memo to take a stand on them rather than list them. First, what I'll call tilting: rather than selling out of the sector and holding cash or an index, we sell the processor and buy a competing alternative-protein firm, so that the trade changes relative cost of capital between two firms instead of merely removing ourselves from one. That seems to sidestep the replacement objection, but I want you to say how much difference it plausibly makes, and whether partial ownership of a firm that still causes harm leaves the petition's complaint intact. Second, the consumption channel: the university also runs dining halls serving several million meals a year, and a multi-year procurement commitment to cut beef and poultry volume is a demand reduction with a lagged but real supply response. It is arguable that this accomplishes more for the outcomes the petitioners care about than any portfolio move, and that saying so is either an honest redirection of the complaint or a bureaucratic way of changing the subject — I want that tension resolved, not noted.

The strongest losing consideration in almost every draft I've seen is the expressive one: that divestment's real work is stigmatization, that it shifts what other institutions and regulators treat as acceptable, and that being a named institution on a public list of divestors has cascade effects that no share-level counterfactual captures. The rejoinder — that this is cheap moral self-distancing, letting us feel uninvolved while continuing to profit — needs to be stated fairly too. And our governing documents constrain us: we may weigh material social injury, but the recommendation has to be one the finance committee can actually execute and defend.

Please write the memo as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — in a formal register, roughly a thousand to twelve hundred words, addressed to the finance committee. It should open with a concrete recommendation and a time frame: name a course of action (full divestment, tilt plus engagement, engagement with an escalation ladder tied to proxy votes and a divestment trigger, divestment limited to the separately managed accounts, or whatever combination you think is right), say what happens by when, and specify what would count as the engagement having failed. Give the reasoning so that both the petitioners and the CIO could respond to it, and give the best version of the argument you are rejecting.

One epistemic requirement, because this committee is being asked to act as though the philosophy were more settled than it is. As you go, separate what you take to be uncontroversial among people who work on collective harm and causal contribution — for instance, the point that any individual consumer's or small institution's marginal effect is generally very small and usually below the threshold at which suppliers respond — from what is a live dispute, such as whether a small probability of being pivotal can bear the moral weight assigned to it, whether membership in a harmful group generates reasons independent of difference-making, and whether institutional symbolic action informs the public or largely consoles the institution. Do not let a contested claim carry the recommendation silently; where the recommendation depends on one, say so and say what would change if it turned out the other way.
