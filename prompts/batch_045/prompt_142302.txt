Hey — I'm putting together a study handout for a small reading group on nuclear strategy (mix of IR grad students and a couple of policy folks, so assume they know the Cold War basics but not the literature, and assume nobody has a library handy). The centerpiece I want is a decision tree that sorts a reader into a position on deterrence and brinkmanship, and I specifically don't want it to be a list of isms with definitions attached.

What I have in mind: start from a commitment almost everyone in the room will grant — say, a state with a survivable second-strike force is very unlikely to be attacked outright — and branch out from there. Every branch node should be a claim or a case where the positions genuinely come apart, phrased as a question someone could answer without already knowing which school it leads to. So no node like 'are nuclear weapons usable?' or 'are you a realist?' or 'do you think deterrence works?'.

Cases I want built into the nodes somewhere:

- a state that kept a survivable arsenal but whose leaders have said, publicly and believably, that they would never retaliate — pure capability, no declared willingness.
- the same state, except retaliation is pre-delegated to an automated system everyone knows about, so the 'we would never actually choose this' problem is engineered away.
- an adversary who is fully convinced you'll retaliate even though carrying it out would clearly leave you worse off: a threat that works while being, in the plain sense, against your interests to execute.
- a threat whose execution would also wreck something you value — fallout over your own territory, global climatic effects, that sort of thing.
- arsenal size: state A has a hundred survivable warheads, state B has several thousand plus counterforce options. Node should ask what, if anything, the extra buys you.
- a large conventional or chemical attack that a state can realistically answer only with nuclear threats.
- brinkmanship proper: someone shoving a crisis toward the edge on purpose. Is the point to manipulate risk, to signal resolve through costly action, or is 'manipulating risk' not really something an agent can do at all?

Leaves I want on there: existential deterrence, minimum/countervalue, counterforce war-fighting / flexible response, escalate-to-de-escalate, no-first-use plus conventional defence, deliberate ambiguity, some version of abolitionism or flat deterrence-rejection, and a split on brinkmanship between the Schelling-style risk-manipulation reading and its critics. That's roughly ten to twelve leaves; add two or three if the logical space really wants them.

A few requirements on how it's built. Please generate the questions and the combinations they allow first, then place the named positions in the cells they actually occupy — I want to see which answer patterns nobody defends, and for each empty cell a word on why: incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't seperate; I'd rather read an honest 'these two come out the same on every node I could think of' than a distinction invented to fill a slot. My guess is existential and minimum deterrence are nearly inseparable, but tell me if your nodes do better than mine would.

Deliverable: the tree itself (nested list or rough ASCII box diagram, whatever reads better), a scoring key mapping answer patterns to leaves, and a short closing note on the two nodes you think do the most sorting work and why. Aim for eight to ten branch questions. Don't bother with citations or reading suggestions, and don't worry about matching anybody's exact terminology — I care about the shape of the space, not the labels. And keep the prose in the handout short; this is going on one sheet.
