I've believed some version of epiphenomenalism since I encountered Huxley's version of the view as a first-year, and I've decided to try to defend it in my master's thesis rather than keep writing about it from the outside. My supervisor's reaction was that the self-stultification objection — the one about how, if experiences cause nothing, then my reports about them aren't really about them and my beliefs about them aren't really warranted — had been dealt with years ago and wasn't worth a chapter. I can't see that it was, so I've written the objection out as carefully as I can, in the hope that it either stands or visibly breaks. Here it is, with the steps separated so they can be attacked one at a time.

(1) Tracking: S knows that p only if, in the nearest possible worlds in which p is false, S does not believe that p. (I mean this in the ordinary counterfactual style associated with Nozick; I'm not assuming closure in either direction, and I'm not assuming it is the whole of knowledge — I only need it as a necessary condition.)
(2) Epiphenomenalism: phenomenal states are effects of physical states and causes of nothing physical; in particular they cause none of my beliefs, utterances, or diary entries.
(3) So for any phenomenal fact F about me — say, that I am in pain now — there are possible worlds in which F does not obtain, my entire physical history is exactly as it actually is, and I believe that F obtains just as I actually do.
(4) Those worlds differ from actuality in strictly fewer respects than any world in which my physical history changes as well, so the nearest worlds where F is false are among them.
(5) So my belief that I am in pain does not track the fact that I am in pain. I do not know that I am in pain, and by repetition I know no phenomenal fact about myself at all.
(6) But the arguments that moved me to the view in the first place — the knowledge argument, the conceivability of a physically identical being with no experience, the explanatory-gap argument — are all arguments from what my experience is like, and each of them presupposes that I know that. If (5) is right, the view saws off the branch it is sitting on: I cannot so much as know that there are experiences for it to be true of that they do nothing.
(7) And here is the part that actually worries me, more than (6) does. Nothing in (1)–(5) uses the word phenomenal in any essential way. Substitute any class of states whose obtaining is said to make no causal difference to what I believe. Most of the physicalists I argue with say exactly that about semantic properties — that whether my noise means that snow is white makes no difference to what my neurons do — and about the rational relation between premises and conclusion, which they take to be implemented by, and to be nothing over and above, the neurophysical story. Run (1)–(5) with those substituted and I get that I do not know what I believe and do not know that my conclusions follow from my premises, hence that I do not know that (1)–(5) are sound. That is too much. An argument that refutes every position in the philosophy of mind at once, including my opponents', has refuted nothing.

So: which step goes, and what does giving it up cost me? I would rather keep (2) than (1), obviously, since the view is what I am trying to defend, but I would like to know whether I am being sold a lemon. Two repairs I have attempted, in case they are the answer or in case they are themselves the mistake. First, the shadow reply: suppose phenomenal facts, while causally inert, are nomologically glued to the physical facts tightly enough that every nomologically possible world in which I am not in pain is one in which my physical state, and hence my belief, differs; then tracking is restored without giving up (2), and the objection gets its force only by smuggling in metaphysically possible worlds that break the laws. I distrust this, because a correlation law that strong looks either like identity under another name or like a brute, and if the phenomenal is tied to the physical that tightly I do not see why it is not doing causal work through its correlate. Second, a friend points out that epiphenomenalists are generally epiphenomenalists about qualitative character only and not about intentional content, even though the argument that motivates epiphenomenalism — causal closure plus the worry about overdetermination — looks as though it should swallow both; if that asymmetry is unstable, then perhaps (2) has to be restated as a thesis about properties rather than about events or tokens, and perhaps that restatement is precisely what breaks the inference to (3). I do not trust either of my own fixes. What I want is the actual diagnosis: the step that fails, the reason it looked like a good step, and the price of each alternative — in particular, whether any of the repairs leaves me able to say, from inside the view, that I know what my own experience is like.
