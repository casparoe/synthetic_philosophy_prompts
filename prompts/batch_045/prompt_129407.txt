I have no philosophy training — I left school at eighteen, spent fifteen years as a logistics supervisor in a hospital outside Lyon, then did a two-year secondment inside a Japanese supplier of ours, where I watched people my age fall asleep upright at their desks and read in the paper about a man in his forties who died at work and was said to have been a victim of karoshi. Since coming back I have been reading about this on my own in the evenings, which is why I would rather you not point me at books, papers or authors: I have no library access, cannot check anything, and a set of names and titles would just be something I would have to take on faith. What I want instead is a specification. Before I read any more people arguing about how much we ought to work — the old Protestant idea that idleness is a moral failure, France's thirty-five hour week, the Japanese overwork deaths — I want to know what I should be demanding of an answer. Say the theory is supposed to be good enough for two jobs: telling me how many hours I myself should work, and telling me whether a rule like the thirty-five hour week should be law.

Here is the raw material I keep going in circles with, which you can organize, add to, or throw out as you see fit. First, the willing workaholic: someone who genuinely likes seventy hours, is not coerced, suffers no obvious ill health, and would be made worse off by being stopped. Second, the fact that hours are not a purely private matter: if I stay late, I move the bar for everyone on my team, so a theory has to be able to object to a choice that is good for the chooser. Third, the karoshi cases, where as I understand it the person was often working on paper voluntarily and sometimes resented being sent home, which makes me doubt whether consent can do the work most theories want it to do. Fourth, a thing I have noticed and never seen discussed: free time seems to be a capacity that atrophies. The colleague who retired and came back part-time within six months, the Sunday-afternoon dread, the way the first two weeks of a long holiday are spent being miserable — the same fifteen extra hours a week appear to be worth much less to someone who has never had them, and possibly worth nothing at all. That worries me in both directions: it means maximise free time cannot be a raw requirement, but it also means a theory that counts free time only when it is used well has quietly reinstalled the work ethic under a different name. Fifth, the currency problem: forty hours in a call centre and forty hours in a workshop where you build something you can point at are not the same thing, so hours alone cannot be what we are measuring — but if not hours, then what, effort, autonomy, exhaustion, output, and why is a cap on hours the right lever on whichever it is? Sixth, the person whose hours protect other people: the nurse, the only electrician in a village, the father who covers a shift nobody else will take. Seventh, the question of whether we are even arguing about the right variable, since if the real point of the French law was to spread a fixed amount of work across more people rather than to give anyone more leisure, then judging working-time rules by how much free time they produce may be aimed at the wrong target entirely. Eighth, and this is the one I feel strongest about: the guilt many of us feel when idle is itself something the work ethic produced, so a theory has to decide whether that guilt is evidence about the value of work or merely a symptom to be explained away.

Do not give me a neutral menu. I want you to commit. Sort the list into requirements you take to be non-negotiable and demands you think are just the prejudices of one side — say the thirty-five hour week is not self-evidently the right instrument, or that the workaholic does have to be accommodated, if that is where you land — and give your reason in each case. Say which two requirements you think most likely cannot be satisfied together, since if the list is unsatisfiable that is the most useful thing you can tell me. Say which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the popular answers. And take a position on the currency question: either hours can be the thing we regulate or they cannot, and I want to hear the argument for whichever you pick. Where you are speculating rather than confident, mark it as such rather than hedging the whole answer into a survey.
