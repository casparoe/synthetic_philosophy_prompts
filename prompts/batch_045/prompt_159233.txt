I keep trying to apply Dennett's phrase about the strange inversion of reasoning — Darwin's version and Turing's version — to actual cases, and my gut verdicts won't line up with the slogan. The slogan says competence comes first and comprehension is a late, expensive add-on that we should be slow to assume. Fine. But then here's what I say case by case (verdict = there's comprehension here vs. this is just competence without it):

- a thermostat holding a setpoint: just competence
- a slime mold grown between oat flakes that ends up tracing something close to Tokyo's rail network: just competence, and I'm comfortable saying so
- the digger wasp that re-runs its whole prey-dragging routine if you move the prey while it's inside inspecting the nest: just competence, paradigm case
- a termite mound holding its internal temperature within a degree by opening and closing vents: just competence ... but I'm uneasy, because it's responding to something it has no sensor pointed at, and that's the shape I'd normally describe as tracking a fact about airflow
- a rat that takes a shortcut through a maze when the route it was trained on gets blocked: comprehension, or at least a glimmer of it
- a three-year-old who says "two mouses" and later switches to "mice" without anyone correcting her: comprehension
- the Deep Blue move in the 1997 rematch that Kasparov couldn't account for: I used to say competence only; now I'm not sure, and I suspect that's just fashion
- Clever Hans tapping out sums: competence, no comprehension of arithmetic — the behavior was real, the subject matter wasn't there
- an octopus matching a checkerboard it can't see by color: it's tracking something, but not the thing I'd name if I had to say what it understands
- a sommelier who nails the vintage and can't explain how: comprehension, I want to say, even though there's nothing she could hand me as a reason
- an LLM that produces a clean proof and then, asked why step three follows, produces a justification: this one I go back and fourth on weekly
- a grandmaster who plays a move in two seconds and reconstructs the reasoning afterward: comprehension, no hesitation

The pair I can't reconcile is the last two. The grandmaster's after-the-fact reasons and the model's after-the-fact reasons look like the same observable behavior, and I call one comprehension and the other a coin flip, which feels like cheating.

My own candidate principle: comprehension is competence that is counterfactually robust in the right way — the system keeps doing the right thing under perturbations that don't change what the task demands, and fails in the way you'd predict when they do — plus a re-recruitment clause, that the same capacity has to be usable in a different task without being rebuilt from scratch. But I think that makes the termite mound, and maybe the slime mold, into comprehenders, which I definately don't want.

So: is there a principle, or a couple of candidates, that gets most of my verdicts? Please test my candidate against the cases and repair it rather than just replacing it, and tell me which clause is doing the work on which case. Then say what the repaired version predicts for cases I haven't listed: a honeybee following a waggle dance (does the follower understand where the nectar is?), a system trained only on video and robot data with no text anywhere, and a hospital scheduling program that allocates beds better than any of its designers can explain. And be honest about which of my verdicts I should drop. One last thing: does the same principle have to cover both inversions, or is Darwin's — competence with no comprehender anywhere in the history — a different test from Turing's — competence from a mindless procedure that a mind designed?
