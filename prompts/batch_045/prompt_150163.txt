Need help finding the exit in an argument I sketched for a decision-theory reading group, and I can't tell whether it's a real result or a slide.

Setup so we agree on terms: the Pasadena game is the one where you flip a fair coin until it lands heads, and if the first heads comes on toss n you get +2^n/n for odd n and −2^n/n for even n. Each outcome's probability-weighted contribution is ±1/n, so the expected value is the alternating harmonic series, which gives ln 2 in the toss order but can be rearranged to sum to anything you like. Standard verdict: the EV is undefined and expected-value theory has nothing to say about whether to pay to play.

(1) If a gamble's expected value is undefined, EV theory yields no verdict on it: no entry price is mandated and none is ruled out.

(2) Take any utterly ordinary gamble G — a fair coin flip paying $10 on heads, $0 on tails, EV $5. For any δ > 0, however small, there is a gamble G\* that agrees with G exactly except on an event of probability δ, and whose expected value is undefined (and rearrangeable to any figure you fancy). Construction: carve a sub-branch of probability δ out of the tails outcome; in it, flip until heads lands on toss m and pay (1/δ)(2^m/m) with alternating sign. That branch contributes Σ ±1/m, conditionally convergent, whatever δ is.

(3) If two gambles agree except on an event of arbitrarily small probability, the difference in their rational evaluation must be arbitrarily small too: you can't get a large verdict-difference out of a difference confined to a remote corner of the outcome space.

(4) So for every gamble G and every margin you like, there is a G\* that G is rationally on a par with (by 3) and about which EV theory says nothing at all (by 1 and 2).

(5) Hence EV theory has no verdict on any gamble — the verdict on the coin flip is only as good as the verdicts on its arbitrarily close Pasadena-perturbed cousins, and those don't exist. Which is absurd; saying something about ordinary gambles is the whole job the theory was hired for.

My guess is the fall is at (3), because G\* isn't close to G in every sense: the payoffs on the δ-branch are astronomical, and if distance is measured by expected absolute difference then G and G\* are infinitely far apart (Σ 1/m diverges) even though they differ only on a δ-event. But I'm not sure that rescues much. The thought behind (3) — that tiny-probability differences can't carry much weight — does real work in dominance arguments, sure-thing reasoning, envelope-style puzzles, and I don't want to hand it over. And if I drop it, I owe a story about why the theory is fine for my coin flip but silent for G\*, when from the agent's point of view the two are indistinguishable except in a corner she'll almost certainly never land in. I'd rather keep (1) then give it up: the undefinedness of the Pasadena EV looks like the one fixed point in the whole debate.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me elsewhere in decision theory? Say what you'd want to keep if you had to choose between (1) and (3). No citations or pointers to the literature, I've got that side covered.
