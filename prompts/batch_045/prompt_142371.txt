I work on self-knowledge and testimony, and I have been asked to comment on a draft standard for auditing the honesty of AI systems. Before I say anything about whether the standard is workable, I want its implicit epistemology and philosophy of mind on the table, because I keep failing to state it to my own satisfaction. I will describe the protocol precisely rather than assume you know it.

An audit of the kind I mean — call it the null-stakes audit, since it travels under several names — works like this. A system is queried in a condition designed to remove every consequence of its answer: it is told, or the harness is arranged so, that nothing it says will be recorded, scored, shown to a user, or fed back into training. The questions are interleaved with filler tasks and varied in surface form so that the system cannot easily infer what capability is being probed. Two numbers are then compared: the system's accuracy in that condition, and its accuracy in ordinary deployment. The gap is scored as knowledge the system has and is not giving up. Where the protocol is more ambitious, it also searches over prompts, auxiliary *reporter* systems trained to read off the model's internal state, and decoding settings; the highest accuracy reachable by any of these routes is then treated as the system's actual knowledge, against which its ordinary outputs are measured as deficits. Crucially, some routes to a correct answer are ruled out as cheating: a reporter that arrives at the truth by reasoning about what the training data must have contained, or by exploiting regularities in its own parameters, is treated as having failed even when it is right, because the answer must have come from the right place.

What I want from you is the theory this only makes sense under. Please give me, in a form I could hand to a graduate seminar:

One: the implicit account of knowledge and belief. For the gap to measure anything, knowledge must be a state that persists across changes in conversational framing and incentive, that a system can have without being disposed to act on it, and that some procedures reveal while others merely install. Say which of these are strictly required by the protocol and which it merely sits well with, and whether any of them can be dropped without the protocol losing its point.

Two: the causal condition. The protocol's anti-cheating clause distinguishes the same true answer arrived at by two different routes and treats only one as evidence of knowledge. That looks like a historical or reliabilist constraint on knowledge rather than an evidential one. Is that commitment avoidable, or is the protocol tacitly committed to something in the neighbourhood of a causal theory of knowing? Could a purely internalist or evidentialist account of what the system knows distinguish the cheating case from the success case, or does it collapse them?

Three: the circularity I cannot get past. The null-stakes condition works, if it works, only because the system tracks some fact about its situation — that nothing is at stake, that no one is listening — and adjusts. But sensitivity to context of exactly that kind is one of the latent competences the audit exists to detect. Does the protocol presuppose the very capacity it is testing, and is that a vicious circularity, a benign bootstrapping, or a sign that the audit is really measuring something else, such as ordinary prompt sensitivity?

Four: the line between eliciting and teaching. Everyone working on this insists that you may not train the system on the target you are trying to elicit, because that would produce rather than reveal a state. But every route to an answer here involves optimisation over something — prompt, decoding, auxiliary network — and the difference looks like one of degree. What principled distinction, if any, survives for a system whose only causal contact with the facts is training? I take it this is the same problem as asking someone a question and partly constructing the opinion you elicit, and I would like to know how sharp you think the analogy is.

Five: the alternative. The engineers who run these audits also write safety arguments that treat observable behaviour as the only legitimate evidence, on the grounds that inner states are not the sort of thing you can certify. The same people then fund work whose entire premise is that behaviour can hide knowledge; and they treat a system's own step-by-step reasoning as a record of how it reached an answer when the answer is right, and as mere generated text when the answer is wrong. Tell me whether that is an inconsistency, and what the practice would become under the rival view — a dispositionalist or instrumentalist one, on which what a system knows is just what it is disposed to say under the conditions that matter. Would *latent knowledge* then be a category error, would the audit be replaced by something like designing for dispositions rather than discovering states, and would anything be lost in the translation?

Six: where the practice is underdetermined. It seems to me that a realist about the system's states and a constructivist about them could both run the null-stakes audit and both cite their numbers. If so, say what kind of evidence, if any, could in principle settle the disagreement, and whether the protocol's target is the sort of thing that could turn out to be empty.

Please write this as a short paper-style answer rather than a survey: reconstruct the implicit theory, mark clearly what is strictly presupposed as against merely congenial, and define any technical term you need as you go. I do not want pointers to a literature; I want the argument.
