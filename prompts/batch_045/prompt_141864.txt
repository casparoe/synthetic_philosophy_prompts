I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long piece on the Fermi paradox. Before I commit a conclusion to print I'd like it stress-tested, and I'd rather be embarrassed now than in the piece.

The position I've arrived at is this. The silence is not, on its own, a puzzle that requires a story about what civilizations are like. The best-supported explanation is that at least one step in the chain from a habitable planet to a civilization detectable across interstellar distances has a per-unit-time probability so low that it typically fails to occur within the window a biosphere is granted; Earth won a lottery that is usually not won. Call it the rare-transition picture. Two things make me prefer it to the alternatives. First, a timing consideration: the span our own sequence of major evolutionary transitions occupied is not trivially short relative to the span over which Earth will remain habitable, which is roughly what one would expect if those transitions are hard steps with long expected waiting times rather than quick consequences of a suitable environment. Second, something structural: the rare-transition picture is what I'll call 'contracting' — every improvement in what we can detect, whether sensitivity, sky and wavelength coverage, the sample of characterized atmospheres, or in situ chemistry in the outer Solar System, removes some of the room the picture needs, so that in the limit it becomes a claim we could actually fail to confirm. Most sociological explanations strike me as 'accommodating' instead: the aliens are quiet, or they withdraw, or they lose interest in expansion, and each fresh null result is absorbed as further confirmation rather than paid for.

I already have the two obvious counterarguments in hand, so don't restate them: the selection-effect worry that we can only ever observe a sequence of transitions completed before observers existed, and the environmental-trigger alternative, on which the transitions merely look hard because each was waiting on a change in conditions rather than on an intrinsically improbable event. What I want to know is which precise versions of those arguments bite and which a defender can absorb, plus everything I haven't thought of.

Specifically: give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for each, say whether a committed rare-transition theorist would concede it as counting against them or dismiss it, and why. Tell me which observations plausibly available in the coming decades would discriminate, and be careful to separate a detection of simple extraterrestrial life, a detection of independent complex life, a technosignature, and something like the residue of a civilization that no longer exists — I take it these do very different work, and I want to know which of them only looks decisive. Say what the picture predicts that its rivals don't, beyond the absence of signals. Then give me the honest part: does the move of saying the hard step might be any step in the chain, at any point, insulate the picture so thoroughly that it has stopped risking anything, and is that a defect in a view of this kind or an acceptable feature of an argument from a single data point? Finally, tell me whether my contracting/accommodating contrast is a fair way to compare solutions or whether it rigs the game — whether a sociological solution can also be contracting, and whether the rare-transition picture has an accommodating core of its own.

The thing I expect to find most persuasive, if it turns out to be strong, is the suggestion that a transition we treat as hard is not hard at all, evidenced by genuine independent convergence on complex organization here on Earth. Assess how much that would really move a defender, and what the best reply to it is.
