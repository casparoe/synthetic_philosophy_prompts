I'm a novelist and I've written myself into a corner, and I'm hoping someone finds it fun rather than annoying.

Setup, short version. A couple of centuries out in my book there's life on another planet — not microbes, a whole biosphere — and the plot hangs on a legal question: there's a conservation instrument that protects kinds of organism, and somebody has to certify how many kinds there are before a mining concession goes through. So my characters need a species concept. Which means I need to know what a species concept is even supposed to do, because right now every argument in my draft is people reciting defintions at each other and it reads like a wikipedia page with dialogue.

So: give me the requirements list, not the answer. What does a species concept have to deliver to count as adequate — and adequate for what, since I get the sense that's half the fight. The cases it has to get right, the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse, whatever bits of actual biology it can't flatly contradict. For each requirement I want the case or consideration behind it, i.e. why it's a requirement and not just something one camp happens to like. And mark which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular schools.

Three different jobs are pulling on the concept in my book, and I suspect they pull different ways:

1. The lawyers need a number and a list, and the list has to stay still long enough to be litigated.
2. The mining company's scientist says none of this matters, all anyone needs is to know which organisms will respond the same way to what they're about to do — so the concept has to license inductive generalizations, not just label things.
3. My point-of-view character is a field naturalist who has to make the call in the field, with what's in her pack and a slow handheld sequencer, on an organism she may only ever see once.

Tell me which requirements come from which job, and whether any of those jobs is making a demand that a species concept shouldn't be asked to meet at all.

The biosphere I've given them, which is were I think I've made it hard on myself:

- Nothing has sexes. Individuals bud off propagules, but two grown individuals can also fuse, and their genomes recombine into one organism.
- Fusion isn't confined within anything recognisable. A sessile filter-feeding mat and a motile grazer that look like they belong to different phyla fuse as a matter of course, and the fused product can bud offspring of either body plan.
- There's a virus-like shuttle that moves whole packets of chromosomes between organisms that never fused with anything. So different genes tell different family trees, and the trees disagree.
- Development is switched by diet and temperature: one genome can produce three or four adult forms that don't look related, and these aren't life stages — they don't turn into each other. Which one you get depends on what you ate as a propagule.
- The ecologically dominant organism on the planet is a persistent swarm of intermediates between two otherwise perfectly recognisable forms. It has been stable for as long as anyone has been watching, it isn't a transient at a contact zone, and it is exactly the thing that would be wiped out by the mine.
- And they can't rule out that the mats and the grazers had separate origins in the first place — more than one tree of life on the planet — with the shuttles moving genes between the trees anyway.

Two things I especially want. First: which of the requirements on your list are really just terran parochialism, things that look mandatory only because Earth life happens to be built a certain way, versus which any concept would have to meet anywhere? Second: which two requirements on the list can't be had together? If the honest answer is that my biosphere makes the full list unsatisfiable, that's a gift, I can build a scene around it — so say so explicitly rather than papering over it.

There also seem to be two questions tangled together in every argument I've drafted — how many kinds there are, and which kind this particular animal belongs to — and I want to know whether a concept has to answer both, and whether settling one settles the other.

At the very end, a one-line scorecard is fine — the interbreeding one, the look-alike one, the family-tree one, the niche one, and whatever the lineage-first people call theirs — but only one line each, and only if it doesn't crowd out the criteria. The criteria are what I'm paying for.
