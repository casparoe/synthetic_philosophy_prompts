I sat in on a meeting where two colleagues from our model-evaluation group argued about the training regime for a system we are building, and I have not been able to settle it myself, so I would like you to adjudicate it move by move rather than tell me who wins overall.

The system reviews municipal bond offering documents for compliance with a state code. The outcome signal is whether a court or regulator later finds a violation. It has three awkward properties: it resolves in three to seven years; it is censored, since a violation nobody litigates is indistinguishable from compliance and litigation is more likely when a violation was flagrant or when some party had an incentive to sue; and of roughly fourteen hundred documents a year, perhaps thirty ever get resolved. The process signal is a former bond counsel who can supervise the model step by step — identify the governing section, check the particular clause, recompute the debt-service schedule — approving or rejecting each step. He is slower than the model and his routine omits two classes of violation (continuing-disclosure undertakings, and some of the rebate computations) that account for a disproportionate share of enforcement actions. With that in mind, here is how the exchange went.

(1) Nadia opened by saying that outcomes are the only signal that tracks the thing we actually care about, and that since the expert's checklist is demonstrably incomplete, process supervision would install his omissions as though they were correct. (2) Ellis replied that the outcome variable is censored in an adversarial direction — it registers violations only after they have already been caught, so training on it pushes the model toward the cases that were easy — and that at thirty resolutions a year we get about one training round per decade. (3) Nadia said those are measurement problems rather than target problems: reweight the litigated sample, buy labels on a targeted subset, apply a correction for the censoring, since a biased measurement of the right quantity beats an unbiased measurement of the wrong one. (4) Ellis said that even with clean outcomes the feedback is one scalar per document and says nothing about which of forty-odd steps failed, so credit assignment across a long chain is hopeless, whereas process supervision lets you repair step nineteen specifically. (5) Nadia proposed what she called checkpoint supervision: insert intermediate verifiable waypoints — did the memo cite the governing subsection, does the stated debt-service total equal the sum of its own components — and supervise those, on the grounds that a waypoint is a checkable fact rather than a style, so you get process supervision's gradient without inheriting the expert's reasoning. (6) Ellis said this relocates the problem: every waypoint needs a verifier, and for the steps that matter the verifier is either the human expert, which reinstates process supervision with extra machinery, or an automated checker, which is a proxy outcome and inherits the Goodhart pressure that scalar optimization always carries — there is no third kind of check that is both cheap and genuinely tracking the target. (7) Nadia answered that the gaming pressure differs in degree: a waypoint can be satisfied only by the artifact coming out a certain way, while process approval can be satisfied by making the supervisor nod, and optimizing against a judge's approval selects for the appearance of good reasoning, which is reachable without the substance. (8) Ellis said the same is true of outcome supervision, since a model can reach a clean verdict by routes that will not generalize, and added that compliance is a counterfactual target — would a court find a violation — not an actual one, so the outcome variable was never the right quantity in the first place; both signals are proxies, and hers is at least inspectable. (9) Nadia said inspectability is a property of the deployment interface, not of the training signal: train on outcomes, then at deployment require a rationale and have a human check it, because how we grade a system and how it presents its work need not match. (10) Ellis said that if nothing in training ever rewards the rationale for being causally upstream of the verdict, the deployment rationale is decoration, and the reviewer's confidence comes loose from the system's reliability, which is worse than honest ignorance. (11) Nadia closed by saying that a decoration which occasionally catches an error beats a rationale that systematically misleads by imitating a flawed checklist, because process supervision's failure modes are concentrated in the supervisor's blind spots, which is exactly where the rare catastrophic misses live. They were interrupted there.

Please go through the eleven moves and say, for each, whether it is clearly right, partly right, or mistaken, and why; then say who is more right overall and on what specific point. I am especially interested in what both of them are missing — in particular any assumption they share that is doing more work than either of them notices, and any option the framing of the argument as a choice between these two signals has ruled out in advance. If you think one of them quietly changed the subject somewhere, say where.

Two further requests. Say how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and close by naming the single claim in your adjudication that you are least confident in and what would change your mind about it. And write your reply as continuous prose in the manner of a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, numbered lists, or tables, and roughly seven hundred words.
