I work as a site reliability engineer — nine years, most of it running incident review for a large distributed system — and my philosophical vocabulary is thin: one undergraduate ethics course decades ago, plus a few years of arguments with our legal and policy teams. I write postmortems, own an error budget, and spend my days deciding what to roll back. I would like to understand the responsibility-gap debate about autonomous and learning systems by having it restated in the vocabulary I actually reason in, and then to see what the restatement cannot carry.

The terms I have, more or less: incident; blast radius; trigger versus contributing cause versus root cause; error budget and budget burn; kill switch and rollback; on-call owner and code owner; action items with named owners; blameless postmortem; a system that updates its own behavior under feedback, so that no one can write its state trajectory in advance; monitoring for failure modes that were never enumerated at design time; graceful degradation when the human on the loop is out of the loop more often than the design assumed.

To make the translation concrete, here is a mechanism my team has been drafting for an autonomous remediation service. Call it a pre-registered mandate: before the service is enabled, a named person of record writes down what the service is for, the metric it optimizes, and which other metrics it is permitted to sacrifice to a stated degree; that person must hold, and periodically test, the authority to disable it. After a harmful incident they are explicitly not answerable for the action the system took, but they are answerable for the quality and honesty of the document they wrote beforehand and for whether the kill switch actually worked when pulled.

Please do the following.

1. Restate, as faithfully as you can, these four moves in the debate, using only vocabulary from my list above (translate the vocabulary itself if you must, but flag when you do):

   (a) the gap claim itself: for a system that learns from its environment, no individual or collective agent satisfies the conditions normally required for responsibility for a specific harmful output, because no one had the relevant combination of control, foresight, and understanding at the moment the output was produced;
   (b) the diffusion reply: responsibility is spread across designers, data curators, deployers, operators, and regulators, and the appearance of a gap is an artifact of insisting on a single responsible agent;
   (c) the design-time reply: responsibility properly attaches to the decision to field the system under uncertainty, not to the individual output, and this is enough to fill the gap;
   (d) the retribution complaint: even if forward-looking accountability can be allocated, something backward-looking is left unsatisfied, because no one is an appropriate target of blame or punishment for the harm, and that residue destabilizes the surrounding practice.

2. Give me a residue report: for each of (a)–(d), say whether it translates cleanly into my vocabulary, translates only with distortion, or cannot be expressed in it at all, and name precisely what had to be fudged or dropped.

3. Tell me what my framework has no word for that the debate needs — desert, culpability, intention, the difference between doing and allowing, whatever it turns out to be — and, separately, whether any of (a)–(d) is doing less work than it appears to be doing, such that my inability to express it is not a defect in my vocabulary.

4. Run the pre-registered mandate through your translation: for each of (a)–(d), does my mechanism close the gap it names, partly close it, or fail to engage with it? Where it fails, say whether the failure is one my vocabulary can represent.

Format: a four-row table with columns for the restated claim, the fit verdict, and what was lost or distorted; then at most 250 words of prose for the residue judgment, the closure verdicts, and your view on which move is the weakest. Total response under 800 words. Keep table cells to two sentences.
