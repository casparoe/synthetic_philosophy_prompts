I'm TAing a second-year course on rational choice for philosophy students — 24 of them, one 80-minute seminar, no budget for real money but I can do chocolate or bonus credit as stakes. I want them to actually feel the force of the backward induction argument in the centipede game rather than nod along with it. Last year I ran the textbook version: I drew the payoff tree on the board, told them the prediction is that the first mover grabs at the very first node, then had them play it in pairs on paper — most pairs passed a few times, everyone concluded "people trust each other, so rational choice theory is just wrong about humans", and the seminar kind of petered out. I don't want that again. The thing I want them to walk into on their own is the opposite conclusion: that the argument is strangely strong, and that what it leans on is the ability to say what you would do at a node that the argument itself says will never be reached, and to know that everyone else can do that too. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the name "backward induction" until the debrief.

Two gimmicks I've been toying with that I can't quite get to work, use either, both, or throw them out. First: the number of legs isn't on the board. I shuffle five cards (6, 7, 8, 9, 10 legs), seal one in an envelope, and the students know the set of cards but not the draw — so nobody ever knows whether the node they're at is the last one, which means the usual starting move of the induction ("at the final node, grabbing beats passing, so...") isn't available to them. My payoff sketch was: pot starts at 4, grows by 3 on every pass, a taker gets 60% of the pot and the other player 40%, and if the last leg is reached and the mover passes it splits 50/50 — but tell me if that's broken or if it accidentally makes passing obviously right for dumb reasons. Second: before anyone plays, I hand each student a slip asking something like "suppose that, somehow, the game got to node 5 and it was your move — what would you expect your partner to do if you passed there?" They fill it in privately and hand it over before play starts, and we compare it with what actually happened. The idea is that they'll have committed themselves to a claim about a node that the argument predicts will never be reached, and then have to deal with that. I also thought about letting half the pairs exchange one non-binding public sentence beforehand ("I'll pass until node 4, I swear") and keeping the other half silent, to see whether cheap talk changes anything.

What I need from you: exact rules and materials, a timeline for the 80 minutes that leaves real time for the debrief, and the debrief itself mapping specific moments in play onto the parts of the argument — including how you'd handle the student who says the whole thing just shows rationality isn't the same as selfishness. Also be honest about what will predictably go wrong when I run it (I'm worried about students treating it as a trust exercise, about the stakes being too small to care about, and about someone pointing out that with one shot per pair there's no room for reputation to do work), and frank about which feature of the paradox the game simply can't stage, so that bit has to come from me talking at the end.
