I could do with some help designing one session.

Background: I'm 71 and retired — 35 years in X-ray crystallography — and the last couple of years I've been going to a philosophy reading group in the back room of the library. We're 11 to 14, mostly retired engineers and science teachers, one ex-vicar who will get God into it somehow, and one woman from IT who says "it's just abstraction layers" about absolutely everything. I'm leading the session in three weeks: 90 minutes, and the tea break reliably eats 15 of them. Tables pushed together, a whiteboard on a stand, and a projector that nobody can ever get working. Two of us are in wheelchairs and several of the others have hands that arent steady any more, so anything fiddly, or anything that depends on people hearing a whisper, is out.

The topic: whether symbols push the substrate around. There's a thing in Hofstadter's big book — I think it's in Gödel, Escher, Bach somewhere in the middle, I'm going from memory and I may have the levels the wrong way round — about a planet made mostly of a stuff called careenium, with things called simmballs, and higher-level patterns called simms, and the question gets put roughly as: do the simms push the simmballs around, or do the simmballs push the simms? In terms I'd use: when stuff gets arranged into a pattern that means something or does something, is the pattern an extra cause, or is every last move made by the stuff? I care because in crystallography everyone talks about the lattice doing things to the atoms and nobody thinks that's spooky, but it starts to sound spooky if you press on it.

What I want is an activity — a game, a staged thing with real choices — where the group walks into the problem themselves. Not a talk with examples. I've already failed once: last month I handed out printed cellular-automaton grids and had them step it by hand, and it died in ten minutes when somebody said "well, it's deterministic". So nothing that can be disposed of with "it's deterministic". And I don't want them hearing the word supervenience from me before the tea break.

The half-baked idea I can't get to work, which you're welcome to bin: two trays that end up disc-for-disc identical. Tray A — someone arranges discs to match a template only they can see, a letter or a shape. Tray B — nobody looks at a template; you shake a tray of loose discs repeatedly and keep it only on the occasions when it happens to match the target, and throw the rest back. Same end state, wildly different history. Then, and this is the bit where I think the philosophy is, you nudge one disc in each tray and see what happens: in one the pattern comes back, in the other it just stays nudged. My suspicion is that this is where the real content of "the pattern is a cause" lives — not in an extra force at the micro level, but in which counterfactuals hold, and in the pattern acting as a filter on outcomes rather than as a shover of stuff. But I can't turn it into something eleven people can do with their hands in a library back room, and I can't work out what the payoff is. We do like a small flutter (chocolate, nothing serious), so there's room for a wager, and I want the scoring to punish "well, it's a bit of both", which is what this group says every single week.

So, what I'd like:

- Actual rules, in a form I can photocopy onto one side of A4, what I need to bring, and a rough minute-by-minute shape for the 90 minutes including setup and tea.
- Roles. I was thinking: someone who can see the template but not touch, someone who can touch but only by following local rules they're allowed to see, a blind shaker, and someone whose job is to nudge one disc per round. Fix that if it's wrong.
- Where the betting goes and how it's scored.
- A debrief that maps specific things that will have happened in the room onto the parts of the question: that the macro state is nothing over and above the micro state; that it nonetheless earns its keep in explanation and in intervention; the filter-versus-shover contrast; and what to do when someone points out that the person holding the template is just more simmballs, so it's simmballs all the way down. I think that's the strongest objection and I don't want to swerve it.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and what I do about it.
- An honest note on what the activity can't show and I'll just have to say out loud myself.

No reading list, please — I've already got more unread books than I have years. What I want them walking out with is this: unable to say "obviously the pattern is just the discs" as easily as they'd say it at the start, and equally unable to say "obviously the pattern is an extra thing doing the pushing". If they go home arguing about whether a sieve is a cause, I'll count it a win.
