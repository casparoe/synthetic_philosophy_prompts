I work with a small research group that has just finished analyzing a multi-site study of ordinary responses to philosophical thought experiments. Before deciding what to do next, I want to get clear on what our results would actually show if they held up. Please treat the following pattern as given, stipulating that all the usual alternative explanations were tested for and ruled out.

We ran two families of cases at twenty-four sites. The first is the side-effect family: an agent adopts a plan he cares about, is told that the plan will also bring about some further outcome, says he does not care about that outcome, and the outcome then occurs. When the outcome is bad, participants overwhelmingly say he brought it about intentionally; when it is good, they overwhelmingly say he did not. This asymmetry appeared at every site, at nearly the same magnitude, and the differences between sites were negligible.

The second is the Gettier family, including both the classic inference-from-a-justified-falsehood structure and the case of the driver who looks at the one real barn in a landscape of barn façades. Here the proportion of participants willing to attribute knowledge ranged from roughly a quarter to roughly four fifths across sites. Three features of that spread matter. It was not reduced by comprehension checks, by presenting the target case within subjects alongside a clear case of knowledge and a clear case of ignorance, or by asking whether the protagonist really knows rather than whether he knows. It did not cluster by region, language family, religion, or years of formal schooling. And within each site the disagreement between individuals was about as wide as the disagreement between site means, with participants at every site reporting high confidence that their own verdict was simply the correct one.

Please describe the most important implications of this overall pattern — near-universal and tightly clustered for one family of cases, wide and structurally unexplained for the other — for whether philosophers' reliance on case-based judgments needs methodological reform and for what that reform should consist in. In your answer, please address specifically whether the fact that the variation is as large within sites as between them, and tracks none of the cultural variables we recorded, should change how the variation is interpreted, and whether the contrast with the side-effect family shows that no single diagnosis can cover both.
