You are a professional metaphysician who has taught the problem of universals to first-year undergraduates many times and who is unusually good at writing diagnostic questions that sort people by what they say about cases rather than by which labels they recognise. Before you write anything for the reader, spend a moment sketching, in two or three sentences, the logical space you intend to carve up: which separate questions a position must answer, and which combinations of answers the space allows. I convene a fortnightly metaphysics reading group at a small liberal-arts college, and about half the regulars are mathematics and computer-science majors who distrust abstract objects; the other half are philosophy majors who have already skimmed the encyclopedia entries and can recite the list of standard views. What neither group has is an instrument, so please build me a decision tree rather than a survey: a tree of at most five branching questions, each node phrased as a concrete case with two or three answer options, that takes someone from no view at all to one of ten to twelve leaves. I have specific cases in mind and I want them used rather than the usual canonical ones: two paint chips produced with identical reflectance profiles; byte-identical copies of a file sitting on two machines; two performances of the same score at the same tempo; two organisms sharing an allele; a quantity like temperature that physics later identifies with mean molecular kinetic energy; a predicate like being a non-tennis-ball, or being either cubical or charitable, that nobody is tempted to treat as carving nature at a joint.

Three constraints on how the questions are phrased. First, no node may simply ask whether the respondent believes in universals, or name any position at all: every node must be answerable by someone who has never heard the word trope and who cannot tell realism from nominalism. Second, build the tree from the logical space rather than backwards from a list of names: state the separating questions and the combinations they permit, then locate the actual views inside that space, and mark the unoccupied combinations with a one-line diagnosis of why nobody lives there, whether because the combination is incoherent or merely unattractive or simply unexplored. Third, I want two things the standard presentations leave out. One branch must lead to a quietist leaf for the person who thinks the whole question is ill-posed, and it should be reachable from more than one node, because in my experience people go quietist at very different points in the conversation. And there must be a node that probes whether the respondent wants one uniform answer across categories or a category-by-category one, contrasting the repeatability of a colour shade, of a biological trait type, and of the value of a banknote; if your tree cannot represent that category-relative option as a single leaf, say so explicitly and explain what shape of instrument would be needed instead.

The leaves must include Platonic realism ante rem, Aristotelian realism in re, trope theory split into its primitive-resemblance and resemblance-class varieties if your tree can separate them, trope nominalism, class nominalism, resemblance nominalism, ostrich nominalism, and conceptualism, alongside whatever unnamed options the space allows; each leaf carries a name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, such as in re realism against certain trope views or class against resemblance nominalism, and name the extra case that would separate them. Also tell me what to do with a reader who refuses to answer a node. Assume the reader has no web access and no library: no citations, no bibliography, no reading suggestions, and no quoted passages from any author. Keep the entire reply under 800 words; if you must cut something, cut the leaf commentary rather than the branch questions.
