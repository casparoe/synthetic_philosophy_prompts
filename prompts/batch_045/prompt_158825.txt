I run the outpatient mental health service at a hospital trust, and from next quarter we begin using a newly approved diagnostic category at routine intake — call it Compulsive Digital Engagement, CDE. I have to audit how the category is behaving every quarter, and those audits decide real things: whether to narrow or widen the criterion set, whether to keep screening for it at intake, and how many therapy sessions get funded, because the code is what unlocks funding. I know Ian Hacking's work on the looping effects of human kinds and want it turned into something my team can run.

Write me the procedure: an ordered checklist or flowchart in words that a clinician with no philosophy background can work through in a twenty-minute review, using only data we actually have — coded prevalence, referral letters, free-text intake notes, waitlist and session counts. Give the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point where the procedure stops with a no and refuses to revise the criteria on the evidence available, and the points where it must hand the decision to a person because it is a question of values rather than fact. Preserve the one distinction that does the real work: between a category that tracks a condition indifferent to being classified and one whose introduction changes how the people classified experience, present, and describe themselves, and so changes the very evidence you would use to check it. Say which further distinctions you are keeping and which you are dropping, and why.

Two loops here are not in the standard cases. An automated screener suggests the code at intake; it was trained on our own notes from last year, so it learns whatever the label has already done to how we write. And the funding rule is itself a loop, since clinicians know that coding CDE is what gets a patient sessions.

Then apply the procedure to three quarters, in under 800 words in total: intake narratives increasingly reproducing the criterion set's own wording; prevalence up forty percent with referrals and waitlists flat and notes saying the code was used to secure sessions; and the screener flagging patients whose notes contain none of the criterion symptoms but who resemble earlier flagged patients in demographics and service history. If in the second case the honest result is that prevalence has stopped being evidence and that narrowing the criteria would cut some patients off from funded sessions, say so plainly. Close with what the procedure cannot do.
