I've been trying to get my head around the debate about the "norm of assertion" and I feel like I must be missing something, because there looks to me like there's a short argument that settles the whole thing, and I never see anyone actually use it.

Here goes. Everyone agrees that it's incoherent to come out with something like "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining." The obvious explanation is that assertion is governed by a rule along the lines of assert only what you believe: you're not permitted to put forward a claim you don't believe, so doing it in a single breath trips over the rule in a way you can hear. That looks to me like the belief norm, established more or less directly. But then the knowledge norm and the truth norm should both be off the table, since they're stricter than belief and would prohibit all sorts of perfectly ordinary things people say. So what is left to argue about?

Second thing, which seems to nail the coffin shut for the knowledge version: lotteries. If I buy one ticket in a huge lottery, obvously I can say "I'm not going to win." People say that sort of thing constantly. But I don't know that I'll lose, since it's genuinely possible that I'll win. So the knowledge rule declares something plainly permissible impermissable. Someone told me the knowledge people just bite the bullet here and insist you really shouldn't flat-out assert that you lost, which sounds like pure stubborness to me.

And seperately: if the norm really were knowledge, I'd have to be certain of pretty much everything before opening my mouth, which would make normal conversation impossible. So is the belief norm basically a done deal, and why hasn't everyone packed up and gone home? Where does my argument go wrong?
