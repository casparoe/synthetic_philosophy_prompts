I've been arguing with a friend about how much weight Arrow's theorem really carries, and I've ended up somewhere I don't want to be. Her argument runs like this:

(1) The theorem is a purely formal result about any mapping from a profile of individual orderings over a fixed set of options to a single social ordering over those options. Nothing in the proof cares whether the inputs come from persons, so any three voters will do.

(2) Now take a single agent choosing between three laptops, X, Y and Z, on three criteria: purchase cost, portability and processing speed. Each criterion induces a weak ordering of the machines. Suppose cost orders them X above Y above Z, portability orders them Y above Z above X, and speed orders them Z above X above Y.

(3) Suppose that agent's all-things-considered ranking is produced by some rule that takes those three criterion orderings as input and returns a weak ordering of the laptops.

(4) Suppose the rule satisfies Arrow's conditions: it is defined for every logically possible triple of criterion orderings; it respects unanimity, so that if every criterion strictly prefers one machine to another the overall ranking does too; the relative position of any two machines depends only on how the criteria rank those two against each other; and no single criterion is a dictator.

(5) By Arrow's theorem no such rule exists. She adds that plain majority over the three criteria makes this vivid: X beats Y two to one, Y beats Z two to one, and Z beats X two to one, so majority rule is not even transitive here. The only rules left are dictatorial ones, on which the agent is really ranking by cost alone, or portability alone, or speed alone, and the appearance of trade-off is an illusion.

(6) But ordinary agents plainly do weigh several criteria at once, and the profile in (2) is not exotic. So at least one of Arrow's conditions is not a requirement of good aggregation at all.

(7) And since those very conditions do all the work in the social case, the theorem tells us nothing distinctive about democracy; it is an artefact of an over-strict formalisation, and every impossibility result built on top of it inherits the artefact.

I want to keep both (6) and the thought that Arrow's result is a real constraint on social choice, so (7) seems to me to prove far too much. Which premise or step should I reject, and why did it look innocuous when I granted it? Please price the alternatives for me: if I give up independence, what exactly do I lose, and does moving to positional information simply smuggle cardinality in through the back door; if I restrict the domain, is the laptop case genuinely a restricted domain, and does that escape survive in the social case; if I reject (3), on the grounds that a rule only ever sees ordinal inputs while a real agent sees intensities, what does that concession cost when the voters are separate people whose intensities are precisely what is contested?
