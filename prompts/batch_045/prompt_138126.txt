I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and for the past few months I have been sitting in on a small evening reading group on persistence — endurantism versus perdurantism — convened by a postdoc in the philosophy department. I can follow the arguments now, but what strikes me is how little anything moves. The endurantist says that ordinary objects are wholly present at every moment at which they exist, so persistence is one and the same three-dimensional thing showing up at different times with different properties, and my intuition that I am entirely here right now is taken as data. The perdurantist says that persisting objects are extended in time the way they are extended in space, by having a temporal part at every instant at which they exist, so that I am a long four-dimensional whole whose earlier and later stages are related the way my head and feet are. Inside the four-dimensional camp there is a further split between worm theorists, who say I am that whole long thing, and stage theorists, who say I am a momentary stage and that my talk about my past and future is made true by other stages bearing some identity-like relation of temporal counterpart to me. Every week the same handful of arguments gets wheeled out — the problem of change or temporary intrinsics, how one thing can be bent at one time and straight at another; the coincidence puzzles, the statue and the clay, the ship and its replaced planks; fission and fusion cases; and the claim that modern physics favors the four-dimensional picture — and every week both sides produce replies the other side has already heard. Nobody has shifted in four months, and from what I can tell the published debate has had roughly this shape since Lewis's discussion in the mid-1980s and Sider's book at the start of the 2000s.

What confuses me specifically comes from my day job, where I spend a lot of time on schemas for records that change. I tried to get traction the only way I know how: I wrote both theories down as explicit models, one whose primitives are three-dimensional objects plus a relation of being wholly present at a time, and one whose primitives are momentary stages plus a temporal-part relation, with ordinary talk about tables and people defined over fusions of stages. Then, in a proof assistant, I built a translation in both directions — every sentence about ordinary objects, properties and times provable in one model is provable, translated, in the other, and vice versa. As engineering the two schemas are equivalent; they differ only in which predicates are primitive. So my hypothesis is that a large part of the dispute cannot be a first-order disagreement about objects at all, and that whatever is really at issue sits elsewhere: in a question about which structure is metaphysically fundamental, which my translation is silent about by construction; in a disagreement about what counts as evidence, whether the data are the way we ordinarily talk and count things or the four-dimensional picture we get from physics; or in a disagreement about whether what really exists is a factual matter at all rather than a choice of framework. The postdoc in the group says something stronger, that the two views are not even cleanly defined, because nobody has an account of what a temporal part is supposed to be that does not collapse into something an endurantist could accept. A physicist friend says the whole thing is settled by special relativity, which the group waved away by pointing out that you can combine either theory of persistence with either a block-universe or a growing-block view of time.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict or a survey of the positions. What kind of disagreement is this, actually: is my intertranslatability observation evidence that it is verbal, or does it only show that the disagreement is not located where I went looking? Is the physicist right that an empirical question is doing the work, or is the link to the metaphysics of time looser than that? Does the three-way split — worm theory, stage theory, endurance — change the shape of the dispute, or is it a third question sharing the name? Please ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says, including the replies I sketched, and then tell me honestly what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a sharper definition, a formal result about the limits of translation, an argument from physics, splitting one question into two, or nothing at all. If nothing at all, I want to know whether that is because the question is very hard or because it is ill-posed, and how I would tell the difference.
