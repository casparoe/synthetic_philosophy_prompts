I TA a 200-level course called Technology, Power, and Society at a big state school — about 26 students, mostly sophomores, maybe a third of them engineering and CS majors who are only there for the requirement and are pretty resistant, rest poli sci, sociology, a couple of philosophy majors. One 75-minute session, room with movable tables, no AV I'd want to depend on. Last week they read Winner's Do Artifacts Have Politics? — the bit about technical arrangements settling the affairs of a community in a way that isn't quite intended or unintended, the distinction between artifacts that require and artifacts that are merely compatible with a certain kind of social order, and the parkway overpasses built too low for buses. It went ok but I could tell half of them filed it under the-designer-was-prejudiced-got-it and the other half under fine-but-a-bridge-is-still-just-a-bridge-it-depends-who-uses-it.

So I want them to feel something instead of sorting it into a box. Design me a runnable in-class activity — a game, or a build-it-then-live-under-it exercise — where the politics shows up in what happens to them and not in anything I say first; Winner's name shouldn't come up until the debrief. My rough idea, which you should improve or replace: split them into groups of 4 or 5, hand everyone identical cheap materials, and have each group design a small artifact or rule-machine that allocates one scarce, mildly desirable thing in the room (the good seats, the one whiteboard, snacks, a participation-point bonus, who presents first). They design without knowing who they'll be afterward; then I hand out roles — someone who has to leave exactly on the hour for a bus, someone with a back injury, someone with a loud straightforward preference, someone whose job is setting the room up before class — and then every group has to actually live under another group's machine for the rest of the session. No real money, obviously.

The part I really want, and where I'd like you to get concrete, is a third phase that produces what I've started calling orphaned politics: the artifact's political effect outlives its reason, the people who built it are gone or no longer in charge, nobody currently wills that effect, and it still quietly distributes who can do what. I want them to arrive at that on their own and notice that both of the stories from the reading run out there — the one that says the thing itself has politics doesn't say whose, and the one that says look at the people who chose it can't find anyone still choosing. Design whatever twist gets them there (redeploy the machine into a changed setting, swap the population, change what the scarce thing is) without me announcing anything up front. Give me materials and prep, the rules, any payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, the roles, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto those claims, including how to handle the engineering majors who'll say well-then-just-redesign-it. Also tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time I run this — last year I did a dumber version where I showed photos of stairs next to ramps and everyone just nodded and nothing landed — and, just as important, which feature of the real phenomenon the activity structurally cannot reproduce, so I know what I have to supply myself in the last ten minutes. My guess is it's something about time scale, sunk cost, and the fact that concrete doesn't get redesigned when the reasons for it expire, but I'd like you to say it better then that.
