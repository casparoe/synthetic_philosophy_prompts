hey — I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on Kant's ethics and I'm putting together this year's final, and I need one essay question on Kant on animals that a student can't pass by writing "Kant thinks we only have indirect duties to animals, because being cruel to animals hardens you toward humans." Everyone knows that line and I want the question to force them past it.

The exam is 90 minutes, closed book, one essay chosen out of three, students write about 1200 words. What I want from you: the question itself, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 built around five must-hit points (5 marks each), with a one-line note for each on how students typically botch it. Also a short paragraph at the end contrasting an A answer with a B answer by naming the moves that separate them, and — because I want the model answer to actually take a side rather than survey — a brief note on where it commits to a contested reading.

Here's the material I want the question built around. Three cases I've been sketching:

- the Hermit: someone who will never interact with another human being again (last person alive, or permanently isolated), who tortures animals for amusement. No spillover onto human treatment is possible.
- the Offsetter: someone who is cruel to animals in private but reliably kind to humans — moreover, suppose the empirical psychology is settled and this person's cruelty has zero hardening effect on how they treat people, and they compensate with extra beneficence. On the spillover story they seem to come out morally ahead of a normal person.
- the Rational Non-Human: a creature with full rational agency and autonomy but not a member of our species. Does anything in Kant's framework generate a duty to it? What does the answer tell us about whether the relevant property in the humanity formulation is species membership or the capacity for rational agency?

And I want the question to lean on a distinction I've been using in lectures that isn't standard, so please slot it in: a duty can be indirect in its content (it forbids or requires behaviour involving a being that is not itself a moral patient) or indirect in its ground (its bindingness derives entirely from some other duty — e.g., to persons, or to oneself). Kant is standardly read as committed to both, but the interesting question is whether a Kantian can hold that duties regarding animals are indirect in content while direct in ground, which would make the usual "indirect duty" label actively misleading.

The three routes I have in mind for them to work through are: the spillover/hardening route; a character or expressive route (the maxim of cruelty is itself corrupt — a failure of self-cultivation or a maxim that can't be willed as universal — and that's what grounds the duty); and a direct-duty route on which animals count as ends in themselves via some capacity other than autonomy. The question should require them to pick one and defend it, and to say what picking it costs. I'd also like them to have to explain how their preferred route handles the asymmetry Kant seems to accept — that we may kill and eat animals but may not torture them — since that asymmetry is easy on one of the routes and very awkward on another.

One more thing: I'm deliberately not asking them for citations or passage references, so please don't build the question around locating anything in a specific text, and keep the mode l answer free of block quotes — paraphrase the doctrine instead. And make the model answer genuinely committed: it should say which route a Kantian should take, and why, not lay out the three and shrug.
