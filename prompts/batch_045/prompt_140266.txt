I've been reading about origin essentialism — the view that a person could not have originated from a different sperm and egg — and I keep noticing a pattern in how that idea gets applied that I can't find discussed. I'd like to know whether it has a name, and if not, how exactly it should be carved up.

The pattern has two halves. First: when people are asked what they could have been like, what seems to matter is causal route rather than magnitude of difference. Asked whether they could have been born to different parents, in a different country, with an utterly different life, most people say no — that would be someone else. Asked whether they could have been born a different sex, or raised by an aunt instead of by their parents, or with a severe disability, most say yes, even though the last of these arguably makes for a bigger difference in the resulting person than a change of parents would.

Second: change the question from what could have been to what was. Someone discovers at forty that he was switched at birth, or that the couple who raised him were not his biological parents. The usual reaction is that he is the same person he always was and that only the story about his origins has turned out false; nobody concludes that he never existed and that someone else did. Yet the difference between this case and the previous one is only whether the alternative history is presented as the actual past that everyone was mistaken about, or as a way things might have gone.

Does this asymmetry have a name? The accounts of personal identity I've come across — ones that stress psychological continuity, ones that stress bodily or causal origins, ones that make identity a matter of which candidate best fits the description — all look like accounts of what identity consists in, and none of them obviously predicts that the truth-conditions for "same person" should shift with the mood of the question. So I'd want to know whether this is best handled as a distinction between what identity consists in and what one would be entitled to conclude if the facts turned out differently; whether there are established notions in the neighbourhood that it resembles and needs separating from (counterpart-style or best-candidate moves, the contrast between metaphysical and epistemic modality, something about the pragmatics of modal language); or whether the appearance is an artifact of how the two questions are posed. If nothing established fits, I'd like a term coined and defined tightly enough that I can tell which cases would be boundary cases, and told what kind of finding would show the asymmetry is real rather than merely rhetorical.
