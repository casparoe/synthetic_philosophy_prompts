I work at a chemicals regulator, and two features of our own practice puzzle me. First, an asymmetry of burden: a firm that wants to place a new substance on the market must submit a full safety dossier and wait for approval before it may sell, while a substance that was already on the market when the inventory was compiled may simply remain, often on evidence decades old, until we get around to reviewing it one at a time — even where the older substance's known toxicity is worse than the new one's suspected toxicity. Second, an asymmetry of framing: when we assess a proposed substitute for a hazardous substance already in use, we assess the substitute's risks but treat the harm from continued use of the incumbent as a background condition rather than as something our decision produces, even when our own numbers say that delay in approving the substitute is the larger expected harm.

I suspect that neither asymmetry is really a view about uncertainty, and I want the alternative tested. My candidate: the practice rests on what I call an *authorship of risk* norm, on which whoever proposes to introduce a hazard is answerable for it in a way that no one is answerable for a hazard that merely persists — so the precautionary principle is triggered by a proposed change in the world with an identifiable proponent, not by the probability or magnitude of the harm.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory the practice needs: what view of responsibility, risk, or evidence it strictly presupposes, as opposed to what it merely sits well with; which rival views it quietly rules out; and whether the two asymmetries above presuppose the same thing or different things that happen to be compatible. Say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license it. In particular, can the authorship reading be cashed out epistemically — we have lived with the incumbent hazard and so know something of its shape, whereas the new one is untested — rather than morally, and if it can, does the practice then owe us something it currently doesn't deliver, such as a duty to generate evidence about incumbents or to review them on a fixed schedule? Finally, what would a symmetric practice look like: if the risks of the status quo were treated as newly authorised each year, what in our procedure would have to change, and what, if anything, would be lost by the change?

Two constraints: keep it short — this is a memo I can act on, not a literature review, and I will supply the citations to the precautionary principle literature myself — and be explicit about which parts of the reconstruction hold for any precautionary practice of this shape and which depend on details I have described.
