ok so I want someone to try to talk me out of a position I've settled into on evolutionary debunking arguments. No need for references or name-dropping papers, I've read around the debate — just argue it.

Where I've landed, call it the sensitivity-gap line: a debunking argument needs more than a causal story about where our moral beliefs came from. It needs the counterfactual claim that we'd have landed on roughly these beliefs even if the moral facts had been different — insensitive, not merely caused. The genealogical premise (selection shaped these judgments for fitness, not for truth) doesn't by itself give you that, because the realist can say fitness-tracking and truth-tracking came apart less than the debunker assumes, or that one thing did both: the facts about what makes a life go badly are part of why we survived and are also what we're tracking. So the debunker has an unclosed gap, and until it's closed the argument is a challenge to answer, not a refutation.

The case that actually worries me, which I made up and dk if anyone has run anything like it: suppose we had something like a credible replay result. A thousand counterfactual runs of our own evolutionary history — or a thousand exoplanet populations we've got independent reason to treat as real analogues, where we can read off the moral facts independently of what the creatures believe. In every run the creatures converge on moral outlooks functionally identical to ours, while the facts vary all over the place: in some runs the stuff we call gratuitous suffering is good, in others promise-keeping is morally inert, whatever. And suppose the convergence isn't an artifact of the setup — the runs genuinely differ in the facts and genuinely don't differ in the beliefs. To me that hands over the sensitivity premise, and no third factor saves me, because whatever is doing the explaining across runs, it isn't tracking the variation.

So: what would count against the sensitivity-gap line? Rank it from fatal to merely awkward. Include: whether the replay result really closes the gap or whether I have an honest way out, and if I do, whether it's a genuine reply or a dodge; whether my line quietly commits me to some positive story about moral facts' causal contact with belief that I'd rather not be stuck with; whether there's a debunking argument with exactly the same shape in a domain where I do accept the debunking — religion, disgust, taste, whatever you think works — that I can't block without begging the question (if there is one, that's the thing I'd personally find most persuasive); whether any of this survives if the debunker's argument generalises to epistemic reasons and eats itself; and whether my position has been qualified into something no evidence could touch, and if so whether that's a problem for a view of this kind or just what a decent metaethical position looks like.

Say which of these a defender of my line would themself concede as counting against them, and which they'd brush off as a critic's invention. Also flag how confident you are in what you're claiming, and single out the one point you're least sure about.
