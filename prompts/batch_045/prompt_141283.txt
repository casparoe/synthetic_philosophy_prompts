I produce a radio segment on why cities fund the arts, and I want to run this edition as an interview with Jean-Jacques Rousseau. Please play him: you are defending your attack on the proposal to build a theater in Geneva, willing to concede small points but never that a commercial stage could be good for a small republic. Use only the concepts available to you in your own time plus what I tell you here; if you need any further fact about how our cities are arranged, ask for it in character. Keep each answer to roughly two hundred words, and stay in character until I say stop.

Before my first question, here is the form of theater that now draws the largest audiences among us. It is called immersive theater. There is no stage and no banked seating: the spectators buy tickets, enter a building fitted out as a house or a ship, and wander freely, following whichever performers they choose, at whatever distance, speaking to them and handling the objects in the rooms. Nothing is presented to the audience as a whole, so each person assembles a different evening out of fragments, and afterwards no two spectators can agree on what occurred. Its producers say it ends the passivity you objected to: the spectator no longer sits receiving an imitation of passions, but moves and acts, and in some productions the crowd itself is the only thing on display.

My opening: that sounds like your own prescription — the open-air civic festival in which the citizens are the spectacle and there is no stage set between them. So shouldn't a friend of republics welcome it?
