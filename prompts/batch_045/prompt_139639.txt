I would be grateful for help designing one session, and I should say at the outset what I am not asking for: I do not need overdetermination explained for my own benefit. I have read enough to follow an account of it. What I lack is any sense of the architecture such an explanation should have when it is delivered out loud, in one pass, to people who have never studied philosophy.

The setting: since retiring from a chair in earth science I have fallen in with a small reading group — fourteen people, mostly of my own age, several of them serious amateur historians, none of them philosophers. We have been working slowly through a book on the philosophy of history, and the word "overdetermination" has begun to trip everyone up, including me. I have agreed to take one ninety-minute session on it, and I would rather it did not collapse into the exchange we can all predict: one member insisting that the causes were economic, another that they were political, and a third announcing that everything causes everything.

Two features of the material strike me as traps rather than details. The first is that the word appears to be used in two ways that slide into one another. On one use, several candidate causes are each claimed to have been enough on their own — the metaphysician's case, as I understand it, of two stones and one window. On the other, what seems to be meant is closer to the opposite: a heap of conditions, no one of which would have produced anything alone. I cannot always tell which our author intends, and I suspect the group will move between the two without noticing. The second is that the test historians and lawyers reach for instinctively — the "but for" test, no such-and-such, no such-and-such — is precisely the test this phenomenon defeats, and it is the one my audience will trust most. A connected worry: a philosopher friend mentioned a label for the ordinary case, something like a cause that is an insufficient but necessary part of a condition that is itself unnecessary but sufficient. I do not know whether offering that to the group would steady them or lose them.

On the example: my instinct is to open with a case from my former field, the end-Cretaceous extinction, where the Chicxulub impact and the vast Deccan flood-basalt eruptions have each, as I recall the literature, been advanced as sufficient on their own. But I am unsure whether a case from natural history can carry a point for people whose real interest is human history, or whether it imports something — my old field had a word, equifinality, for an end state reachable by several different routes — that will muddy rather than clarify. I would rather you set out what the carrying example must have and must lack, and then judge my candidate against that, than simply take it from me.

So what I want is the plan: the order in which the components must appear and what depends on what; the one example to carry the hour, with your verdict on mine and, should you reject it, the human-history case you would put in its place and why it meets your criteria; the misconceptions this particular audience will bring or will form on the way, each fixed to the point in the sequence at which it must be headed off; a closing question that would show me whether the idea has landed rather than merely been absorbed; and what I should decline to raise at this level, however tempted I am.

Please organise your reply under five headings, in this order, keeping roughly to the lengths given:

1. Sequence — about 400 words: the ordered components and the dependency that fixes their order.
2. The carrying example — about 250 words: required and forbidden features, your verdict on the extinction case, and any substitute.
3. Misconception map — about 350 words: each misconception with the point in the sequence at which to defuse it.
4. The check — about 100 words: the closing question and what an adequate answer to it would contain.
5. What to leave out — about 100 words.
