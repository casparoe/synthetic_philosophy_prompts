Hey — I'm one of the clinicians on my med school's student accommodations committee, and we've got a case that's split us basically down the middle. I need to circulate something before we vote on Friday, and I'm definately out of my depth on the philosophy side of it.

Short version: a fourth-year has asked to be excused from two specific procedures in the ob/gyn clerkship on religious grounds. What's making this hard isn't the usual stuff. When we asked her to explain the basis of the objection, she declined — not "I'd rather not," she said outright that she can't give us a reason we'd find convincing, that she isn't claiming the procedures are wrong in general or that anyone else shouldn't do them, that she knows exactly how it sounds, and that she'd rather fail the rotation and repeat the year than either do them or produce a rationale. She's also offered to substitute a longer, harder alternative block, so nobody seriously thinks she's gaming us.

Our policy lets us recommend an exception only where a student demonstrates that the requirement substantially burdens a sincerely held religious belief. Half the committee reads "demonstrates" as: you actually have to say what the belief is and why the burden follows, otherwise there's nothing for us to assess and anyone can get out of anything by just going quiet. One member, who teaches philosophy of religion part time, brought up Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling and this thing about a teleological suspension of the ethical; he said the whole structure of a claim like hers is that it can't be cashed out in public reasons, so our demanding them is demanding the wrong kind of thing. Someone else shot back that if that's right, then any student who goes silent gets whatever they want, which can't be right either.

Could you draft the memo I'll send round? It needs a bottom line — grant, deny, or grant with conditions — plus a concrete timeline, and it has to take the "no reasons, no accommodation" position seriously rather than writing it off as heartless or as a lawyer's move (our counsel did tell us the legal bar is basically sincerity and that we shouldn't be grading how sensible the belief is, which I take as a separate point). And I'd like it to be honest about what we're actually doing whichever way we go, because I think both halves of the committee are quietly pretending they can tell the difference between faith and self-deception.
