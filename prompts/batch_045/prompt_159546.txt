For an independent project in my school's senior philosophy elective, I am writing about Sidney Lumet's 1957 film Twelve Angry Men through the Nyāya theory of the pramāṇas: the four recognised sources of knowledge (perception, inference, comparison, testimony), together with the disputed candidates such as non-cognition of absence. I am after close criticism rather than a plot summary, so please read the film as though it were a case in Nyāya epistemology and tell me what it shows that the theory does not.

Three things I would like taken seriously. First, the reconstruction scenes — the duplicated switchblade, the timed walk to the door, the passing train that swallows the downstairs witness's evidence, the marks on the woman's nose. Are these best understood as one pramāṇa defeating another, a perception or inference that blocks a piece of testimony? Does Nyāya have a principled rule about which source wins such a conflict, or is it settled case by case?

Second, my own thesis, about which I am genuinely unsure: Juror 8 almost never accuses anyone of lying. He attacks the perception the witness's words report. That suggests the film treats testimony as a dependent source, only as strong as the cognition behind it — which sounds to me like the reduction of testimony to the other pramāṇas that Nyāya famously resists. Is the film quietly taking a side in that dispute, or does my reading distort it?

Third, a mismatch I cannot resolve: a Nyāya pramā is a cognition that gets things right, whereas what the jury is asked about is reasonable doubt, a standard for acting and not for knowing. Does the film dramatise a standpoint Nyāya has no vocabulary for, or do its accounts of doubt and of hypothetical reasoning used to settle doubt cover the case after all?

Please also say where the film resists the reading — where it is doing something other than epistemology — and point me to the two or three scenes that carry the most weight for it.
