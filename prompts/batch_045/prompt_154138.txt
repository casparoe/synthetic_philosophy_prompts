I'm giving a 20-minute paper next week to the science staff of a large land trust: mostly field ecologists and conservation planners who use habitat extent and native-species richness as their working proxies for value, who are uneasy about welfare talk, and whose institutional mission is acquiring and restoring habitat. My thesis is that for a nontrivial class of temperate ecosystems, conversion of habitat to low-productivity human use reduces total wild-animal suffering, and that conservationists should therefore stop treating every hectare protected as a welfare gain and should pair biodiversity accounting with an expected-suffering estimate.

The argument I'll present has three steps: (1) most wild suffering is concentrated in early juvenile mortality among high-fecundity, low-parental-investment species — the doomed surplus of offspring that die before reproducing, which I'll argue dominates the welfare ledger; (2) habitat loss removes breeding and recruitment capacity, so it shrinks that surplus far more than it degrades the lives of the adults that remain — my "averted-births dividend"; (3) the "compensation threshold," the rise in per-capita suffering among survivors needed to cancel the dividend, is rarely met in the systems I discuss.
