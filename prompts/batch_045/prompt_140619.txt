I have been trying to work out how much I am rationally required to move my confidence in response to someone disagreeing with me, and Aumann's agreement theorem keeps bothering me. As I understand it: if two agents share a common prior and each one's posterior is common knowledge between them, their posteriors must be identical; and there is the companion result that if they simply announce their posteriors to each other and revise, round after round, then in a finite setting the announcements converge to a common value after finitely many rounds. My actual verdicts on cases do not seem to line up with any clean version of that, so here they are.

Case 1, same evidence and a peer, with full exchange. We both read the same spreadsheet of two hundred trial results, we exchange our estimates, each sees the other's, each has time to revise and re-announce, and we still differ: I say the coin is biased with probability 0.7, she says 0.55. My verdict: I am not permitted to stand pat; something has gone wrong somewhere. But I do not feel required to go to 0.55, and the midpoint feels arbitrary to me, so I end up thinking that one of us has made an error and that whoever reasons more carefully should win outright.

Case 2, different priors. Two people who share all their evidence but were raised in different religious communities disagree about whether one of those traditions is true. My verdict: no requirement to move at all; the disagreement can be fully rational and permanent.

Case 3, unshareable superior evidence. A colleague has read the entire clinical file; I have read the abstract. She puts the probability that the drug works at 0.8, I would have said 0.5. My verdict: I should move most of the way to her number, near-total deference.

Case 4, known directional bias. The same colleague, except that I know she holds stock in the manufacturer and I know from her past work that her estimates in this area run about ten points too favourable. My verdict: I should move partway, and I should discount in a way I can estimate; but her saying 0.8 is not evidence against the drug working.

Case 5, a view held for signalling reasons. A group holds view X, and I have good reason to think they hold it mainly because holding X signals loyalty, and that their internal discussion is entirely about signalling. My verdict: I should not move toward them. Whether I should move away from X is the case I keep going back and forth on: half the time I think their holding X is evidence that X is the sort of claim that spreads for non-truth-tracking reasons; half the time I think a corrupted source is simply inert.

Case 6, a crowd with unknown dependence. Ten thousand people online answer a factual question the opposite way from me, each apparently knowing less about it than I do, and I cannot tell whether they read one another. My verdict: I move hardly at all, even though if each of them were even slightly better than chance and their errors independent the majority would be near-certain to be right.

Case 7, my own copy. An exact copy of me, who has seen everything I have seen plus one clean randomized experiment, comes out at 0.9 where I am at 0.4. My verdict: I should move essentially all the way, and I trust this more than I trust the colleague in Case 3, who has a far larger evidence advantage.

Case 8, a broken channel. We exchange estimates by a channel either of us might have reason to think dropped the other's message. My verdict: I move a little, but nothing requires convergence and nothing is irrational if we still differ.

Cases 6 and 7 are the pair I cannot reconcile: I defer almost completely to one extra study I can reconstruct, and almost not at all to ten thousand opinions I cannot.

My tentative principle is: I move toward another person's view only to the extent that I can specify the mechanism that produced it and judge that mechanism to be truth-tracking, so the size of my move tracks how much of their advantage I can reconstruct rather than how much advantage they have, and a corrupted source is inert in both directions. This seems to sit well with 3, 4, 6 and 7, seems silent on 1, where I can specify her mechanism perfectly and it still disagrees with mine, and seems to settle 5 only by fiat.

Please test that principle against the cases, repair it or replace it, and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Where it is silent, say what has to be added. Also give its verdicts on at least two cases I have not listed, of your own choosing, including at least one where the disagreement is not about a matter of fact.
