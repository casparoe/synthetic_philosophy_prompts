I've just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether someone can intend to do something they know they will have no reason to do when the time comes, and I'd like an audit of the version of the argument he was working from. He has read Gregory Kavka's original paper on the toxin puzzle; I have only met it second-hand. His summary, as nearly as I can reconstruct it, ran like this:

"A wealthy eccentric offers you a million pounds, paid tonight and yours to keep whatever happens, on condition that by midnight you have formed the sincere intention to drink, tomorrow morning, a vial of toxin that will make you wretchedly ill for a day and do you no good at all. Kavka's puzzle is a one-person version of the prisoner's dilemma: you cannot form the intention, because you know that tomorrow, with the money already banked, drinking would be nothing but gratuitous suffering, and no rational person would do it. So the puzzle shows that an intention is really just a prediction of your own future action, and that you cannot intend what you do not believe you will do. That is also why the only way to collect the money is to deceive yourself into thinking you will drink. The moral is that this is a problem about weakness of will, and that a fully rational agent does worse here than a slightly irrational one."

He then applied it to commitment devices: since, on his reading, you cannot intend what you will later have no reason to do, he says that paying now to be locked out of your phone during exam week is only ever a way of rigging your future incentives, never an exercise of intention.

Something in this rings false to me — the little I have read connects the puzzle to nuclear deterrence and to the rationality of intention rather than to akrasia — but I cannot tell where it goes wrong, and I have no way of checking.

Please go through the summary and tell me what someone working on this would accept, what is a defensible simplification of a contested point, and what is a genuine misrepresentation. In particular: are "intentions are just predictions" and "you cannot intend what you will not do" things the puzzle establishes, or one side of a live dispute? And does the conclusion about commitment devices follow even from the parts of the summary that are right?

One wrinkle that is the case I actually care about. Suppose the toxin is not purely bad: it will also clear up a chronic but mild sinus complaint, so tomorrow morning you would have a real though small reason to drink, far outweighed by a day of illness. Does the argument that the intention cannot be formed collapse as soon as any residual reason exists, or does it survive — and does that turn on whether intending must track the balance of reasons or merely not be contrary to them?

Please answer in British English, and flag where your own verdict is least secure: say which further argument would most plausibly move you to a different reading, and where your reconstruction is most likely to fail.
