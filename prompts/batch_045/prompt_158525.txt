I'm drafting a paper on hinge epistemology — the reading of On Certainty on which any epistemic practice rests on propositions that are not held on the basis of evidence, are not the sort of thing one could turn out to be wrong about from inside the practice, and instead form the frame within which doubting, checking and justifying get their sense — and I want to test it against a case rather than keep turning it over in the abstract. The case I keep returning to is the reception of continental drift. As I understand the episode: Wegener proposed in the 1910s that the continents had once been joined, pointing to the fit of the Atlantic coastlines, to matching rock formations and fossil assemblages on now widely separated shores, and to glacial deposits whose directions make no sense on present-day geography; the geological establishment rejected it, largely on the ground that no mechanism of remotely adequate strength was available, and explained much of the same evidence away using sunken land bridges; by the late 1960s, seafloor spreading, paleomagnetic directions and the symmetrical magnetic striping of the ocean floors had produced a plate-tectonic picture in which continents in motion had become ordinary and much of Wegener's evidence counted as confirmation. I don't need citations or a reading list — I know that literature — what I want is the analysis.

So: map the episode onto hinge epistemology carefully, and then tell me where the mapping breaks. Which propositions in geological practice were actually functioning as hinges — the fixity of the continents, the requirement that a proposed mechanism's forces be quantitatively adequate, the standing preference for causes observable in operation now — and which were ordinary empirical claims that merely happened to be hard to test? What does the view predict about how a competent community should have responded to the evidence it had, and is that prediction borne out, or does the case make the view look like a device for insulating any recalcitrant belief from criticism? Be disciplined about the mapping and say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. And engage the strongest thing on the other side: that the episode is fully accounted for by mundane confirmation theory — no mechanism, therefore no serious prior probability to be raised — or by a Kuhnian change in what counts as a problem and a respectable solution, with hinge epistemology contributing nothing but vocabulary.

The part I'm least confident about is what the case does to the theory itself, and I want your judgment on the sub-questions it opens up rather than a survey of them. I've been working with a rough distinction between hinge replacement, where the frame changes and the old proposition simply ceases to be intelligible as a candidate for belief, and hinge erosion, where a proposition that stands fast is worn down by accumulated anomalies across generations; and with the thought that many hinges travel with buffer propositions, auxiliary claims that absorb apparent counterevidence so that the hinge itself never has to be doubted, which would make the interesting question not why hinges resist evidence but how a buffer gets used up. On this case: does the drift episode illustrate erosion, replacement, or neither? Does it tell in favour of the un-Wittgensteinian idea that a hinge can rationally be abandoned for evidential reasons? If hinge status is a matter of what a community's practice actually treats as standing fast, what would settle from the historical record which reading is correct — or is the case simply underdetermined? Rank the two or three questions this episode makes most worth answering, and say what would move them up or down your ranking.
