I sit on a small commission drafting a governance proposal for a regional assembly of about four million people, and we are close to recommending a second chamber chosen by lot. Our draft gives members a single non-renewable six-year term, with a third of the seats refilled every two years — a rolling lot, as one of my colleagues has taken to calling it — and the chamber would share power with an elected one rather than merely advise it.

Nearly all the persuasive work in the draft is being done by a single analogy: a chamber chosen by lot is a jury. It is used twice. First, to defuse the objection that random selection has no democratic mandate: we already accept this sort of thing in criminal trials, so — the brief says — lotteries are not the alien procedure they sound like. Second, and to my mind where the real weight falls, to justify the absolute ban on reselection: we do not allow career jurors, we excuse people after one case and send them home, so a chamber chosen by lot must also be a chamber of one-term amateurs. I want that second use audited in particular, because it is quietly doing the work that arguments about term limits are normally asked to do, and it carries a claim about career politicians that I do not think the analogy has earned.

What I want from you:

Set out the mapping explicitly — a table of correspondences is fine. What plays the role of the voir dire, the judge's charge, the verdict form, the sequestered deliberation room, the twelve, the unanimity requirement, the excusal, the return to ordinary life the next morning. Say which of these are load-bearing for the jury's legitimacy and which are incidentals of criminal procedure that no one would think to copy.

Then the disanalogies, sorted by whether they damage the first use or the second rather than by how obvious they are. Three I keep circling and cannot settle:

The charge problem. A jury is handed the question it must answer by an authority that is not itself the jury; a chamber sets its own agenda. Whatever supplies the charge to a sortition chamber — permanent staff, the elected chamber, a standing rule — will hold most of the real power, and the analogy is silent about this because in a trial the division of labour is inherited and never remarked on. Does a chamber with nothing playing the judge's role stop being a jury at all, and if so does the analogy still do any work for us?

The filter runs the wrong way. Jury selection removes exactly the people a sortition chamber is supposed to include: those with relevant knowledge, strong prior opinions, or a stake in the outcome are struck for cause. If the case for sortition rests on descriptive representation and lived experience, then the jury is the one existing lottery whose point is to be unrepresentative in precisely that respect. Does this sink the first use even if the second survives?

The return problem. A juror goes home to anonymity the next morning. Someone who spends six years in a chamber, knows they cannot be reselected, and must re-enter a labour market at the end is in the position that debates about term limits usually worry about: a fixed horizon plus a need for a subsequent employer or client. Taken seriously, the analogy seems to me to argue for much shorter service than six years, or for some funded transition out of office, rather than for the term we have drafted. If that is right, say so plainly.

There is also a rival version in circulation on the commission: a career legislator is a judge, a term-limited one is a juror, and the design choice is a choice between the two. I suspect this is the same analogy doing double duty and quietly assuming that what a judge has is the same kind of thing that a long-serving politician has. Deal with it if it deserves dealing with.

Finally: does the conclusion the brief wants — no reselection, ever — actually survive? If it does not, is there a better analogy for a non-renewable chamber chosen by lot, or is the honest answer that we should stop defending the ban by analogy and defend it directly on its own grounds?

Write it for a room of moderately sceptical people who have to vote on the draft next month. No jokes at the brief's expense, and no citations or references to the literature — I will add those myself.
