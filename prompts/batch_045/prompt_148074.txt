I convene a small reading group that works through classic popular books on logic and mind. Last week we did the chapter built around the "MU puzzle," and two members — a formal-verification engineer and a philosopher of mathematics — got into an argument that I could not settle and that they asked me to referee. Below are the rules of the system exactly as I handed them out, then the exchange as close to verbatim as I could reconstruct it. I have deliberately left out my own view.

**The system (call it MIU).** Alphabet: M, I, U. Axiom: MI. Four rules:
1. If a string ends in I, you may append a U. (xI → xIU)
2. If a string begins with M, you may double everything after the M. (Mx → Mxx)
3. Any occurrence of III may be replaced by a U. (xIIIy → xUy)
4. Any occurrence of UU may be deleted. (xUUy → xy)

The puzzle is whether MU is a theorem. It is not, and the usual argument is that no rule changes the number of I's except rule 3, which reduces it by three, so that the I-count of any theorem is never divisible by three — while MU has none. What the two of them then argued about is what, if anything, that argument shows.

**Dana:** I don't see what's deep here. Finding an invariant — a quantity the rules preserve and the target lacks — is completely standard technique. It is what a model checker does before breakfast: abstract the state space, look for predicates preserved by the transition relation, rule out the bad state. Engineers do this on hardware designs with more states than there are grains of sand. If a machine can get there by search-and-abstract, then calling the result "jumping out of the system," and then comparing it to Gödel, is romanticizing a routine proof.

**Theo:** Difficulty isn't the point; *level* is. Every rule application is a move inside MIU. But the sentence "MU is not a theorem" is not expressible in MIU at all: the vocabulary is M, I and U, and there is no negation, no quantifier, no predicate for theoremhood. So by reasoning about the system we establish something the system cannot even formulate, let alone produce. That is the structural feature the chapter uses the puzzle to isolate. Your model checker doesn't touch it, because someone hands the checker a formal description of the transition system: the jump happened in whoever wrote that description, before the program ran.

**Dana:** Two problems. First, "the system can't state it" is far too weak a test. MIU also can't state that it has four rules, or that MIU contains three letters; nobody thinks those are transcendental discoveries. And crucially there is nothing *self-referential* about MU's underivability — it's a fact about an external object, established from a comfortable external standpoint. Gödel's sentence is different in kind: it lies in the language of arithmetic and says of itself that it is unprovable, and the coding is what gives the result its bite. Calling the MU result a miniature Gödel theorem is loose talk. Second, the "outside" is not a fixed location. Extend MIU with a little syntax for strings, a derivability predicate and enough arithmetic, and the invariance argument becomes a derivation *inside* the extended system. The gap you are pointing at is an artifact of how narrow the original formalism is. And notice that the book concedes this later on: in a subsequent chapter it maps the letters to digits — M, I and U become 3, 1 and 0, so that MI is the number 31 and the rules become operations on integers. That arithmetization is precisely this kind of internalization, and it is what makes the Gödel analogy work at all.

**Theo:** On the loose talk: agreed, and I'll grant that the chapter's version of it is a parable rather than a theorem. But a parable can isolate something real — namely that some questions about a formal system can only be settled by taking the system as an object, and that no amount of rule application settles them. That distinction doesn't need self-reference to be real. On internalization: it doesn't close the gap, it relocates it. Enlarge MIU to a system E that proves "MU is not an MIU-theorem"; then there are truths about E that E cannot prove, and to see those you step outside E. You never land outside all systems; you only ever land in a bigger one. And your own arithmetization example supports me: reinterpreting the symbols as numbers in order to gain a vantage point on them *is* the meta-level move, not an escape from it.

**Dana:** "You always land in a bigger system" is a good line, but as an argument it is unfalsifiable — no amount of internalizing would ever count against it. And here is the case that actually convinced me the insight is thinner than you think. Add to MIU a single rule: any occurrence of U may be replaced by II. Now MU is a theorem in three steps — MI, MIU, MIII, MU — and the entire "deep" discovery evaporates. If the invariant argument were a cognitive achievement of the sort the chapter describes, it wouldn't be so brittle. What it uncovered was a contingent property of a stipulated rule set, not a structural feature of the kind Gödel's argument exposes.

**Theo:** Brittleness is the wrong test; plenty of genuine mathematics is sensitive to which axioms you adopt. And your example cuts against you. Look at what you had to do: you had to notice that the new rule changes the I-count by two — the very quantity the old argument tracked — and that this breaks the old invariant, and only then did you go looking for a derivation and find one. That is reasoning about the system again, and it is answerable to the system in a way that rule application is not. Add instead the rule UUU → III: now the I-count changes by three, the old invariant survives untouched, and MU stays underivable. What separates the two cases is a fact about the rules, discovered from outside. That is the whole phenomenon, at whatever scale you like.

**Dana:** One more thing, then I'll stop. For MIU there is a complete decidable criterion: a string is a theorem just in case it consists of one M and nothing but M's, I's and U's, it begins with M, and its I-count is not divisible by three. So for this system the "outside" standpoint can be compiled into a machine that settles theoremhood for every string, with no residue. If the jump can be fully mechanized after the fact, and leaves nothing undecided behind, I want to know in what sense it was a jump rather than a piece of mathematics that the formalism happened to be too poor to state.
