Write a parody of a UK government consultation document, 800–1,000 words, issued by a fictional Office for National Commemoration and inviting local authorities, heritage bodies and devolved administrations to apply to have a local defeat registered as a Designated Lost Cause under the (equally fictional) Commemorative Balance Act. Write it in the deadpan register of an actual civil-service call for evidence: numbered clauses, sub-clauses, defined terms in bold, a short scoring matrix at Appendix A, and two anonymised worked assessments at Appendix B. Use British English and civil-service idiom throughout — "the Department does not propose to", "stakeholders will wish to note", "for the avoidance of doubt" — and never break character to explain the joke.

The target is not any historical episode but the practice of converting a defeat into a national myth, and in particular the two moves that make the conversion work: that a community can insist the cause was lost beyond doubt while also insisting it was never really surrendered, and that the sacrifice can be honoured while the cause is formally disowned. The humour must come from real structural features of that practice, exaggerated rather than invented: that the myth is retrospective and typically crystallises generations after the event; that it trades on selective amnesia about what the cause was actually for; that it requires a fixed repertoire of stock elements (the last stand, the betrayal by an ally, the weather, the round number of the fallen); that the dead are conscripted as silent endorsers of a settlement they did not live to see; and that the more morally indefensible the original cause, the more strenuous the insistence that the monuments are about the sacrifices and not the politics.

Build the following apparatus into the clauses, each of which should do recognisable satirical work:

- the Simultaneity Requirement, under which an applicant must concede that the cause is lost and maintain that it was never abandoned; applications are scored on how little cognitive strain this causes in the local press;
- the Commemorative Leverage Ratio, the ratio of commemorative infrastructure to surviving participants, in respect of which a higher figure is treated as evidence of a deeper communal need;
- the Dunkirk provision, under which a defeat so successfully reframed as deliverance that it no longer functions as a loss is reclassified as a Retained Moral Victory and therefore disqualified;
- the Masada clause, under which near-total mortality among participants is accepted as conclusive evidence of sincerity and waives any requirement to show that the cause was ever widely supported;
- the Kosovo Field rule, under which, where the passage of centuries has rendered the factual record unusable, the Department assesses the myth on its present usefulness alone and expressly declines to inquire into what happened;
- a provision for the retrospective redesignation of a victory as a loss where this would improve community cohesion, included without any acknowledgement that it is odd.

Let the apparatus run to a reductio at least once. Award maximum points under the matrix to causes whose aims no living person would endorse, on the stated ground that only such causes can be commemorated without controversy, so that the Department's highest honour is reserved for defeats whose point has been entirely forgotten — and let the Department draw that conclusion with evident satisfaction.

Historical material may be used only as anonymised case studies inside Appendix B ("Case 41(B): a fourteenth-century field in the Balkans"; "Case 7: a desert fortress in the eastern Mediterranean") or drawn from classical antiquity, and must never be the butt of a joke in itself. Close with standard consultation boilerplate, including a closing sentence in the "How to respond" section in which the Department quietly notes that it will not be publishing its own assessment of which defeats actually occurred. A specialist in memory studies should find the satire fair; a reader with no background should be able to tell what is being ironised.
