Hey — I keep having this argument with my office mate about whether computational hardness is a fact about the world or just a fact about how we happen to write things down, and I want your number with the derivation, not "it depends what you mean by efficient."

Here's the proposition I want a credence on, as precisely as I can state it: when a concrete physical system (a logistics network, a folding protein, a compiler, a cell's regulatory network) is faced with a task whose general form is NP-hard, there is a mind-independent fact about the minimum physical resources any process that system could actually run would need in order to return exact answers, and that fact — not our choice of encoding, model of computation, or asymptotic framing — is what's doing the explanatory work when we say the system "can't" solve it. Call that claim H. I'm at about 0.55 on H; she's at about 0.15 and thinks it's all representation.

Give me your own number, but mostly give me the decomposition. The sub-questions I think it rides on:

1. Is polynomial time a real joint in nature, or a convention that the "the machine model doesn't matter up to polynomials" result just relocates one level up? And does it cut against H that plenty of polynomial-time algorithms are useless at real sizes while plenty of exponential ones are fine there?

2. The redescription test: if the same physical task can be re-described — unary instead of binary numbers, some parameter held fixed and moved into the complexity accounting, a preprocessing/kernelization step, or an encoding where part of the answer is already baked into the representation — so that the hardness disappears, does that show the hardness was never in the world to begin with? How much of H survives that?

3. Worst case vs. what actually shows up: hardness is a statement about the worst instance at each size, but real systems throw structured instances at themselves. Is there a principled reason the instances nature generates sit in the hard region, and how much of H is really a bet about instance distributions?

4. Physics: how much rides on whether the physical Church–Turing thesis holds, i.e. on whether the universe has any reliable shortcut for hard instances (quantum is presumably not enough for NP-hard ones, but analog, thermodynamic, or cosmological exotica might be)? Is H in part an empirical claim about physics, or is it insulated from physics entirely?

5. Asymptotics vs. the finite: H talks about scaling in the limit, but every real system is finite and any finite instance can in principle be brute-forced. Does a claim about n → ∞ constrain any actual physical system, or only our models of them?

Tell me which of these you read H as asserting, whether H even has a determinate truth value under (i) and (iii), and how much of the 0.55/0.15 spread is just us picking different readings.
