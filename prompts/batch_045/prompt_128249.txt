A friend of mine — a computational neuroscientist who takes integrated information theory seriously enough to use it as a working criterion — has been handing down verdicts on imaginary systems. Her score for a system in a state is, as in the theory, the Φ^Max of its maximally irreducible cause–effect structure, taken over the minimum information partition; a score of zero means not conscious, and the subject is whatever complex survives the exclusion postulate. I want to know what she will say about three rigs I have designed, and I care much more about the rule she is running than about the numbers.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A camera sensor: twenty million independent photodiodes in bright, detailed light. Verdict: essentially zero. Her reason: an enormous amount of information, none of it integrated — no cut costs you anything.

2. A much smaller net — a few hundred thousand elements, densely and recurrently connected, with heterogeneous weights, running in a rich, patterned regime. Verdict: high; she calls it the cortex case. Call this her reference net R, and call its score 10.

3. The very same net R, same wiring, at least as many active elements, but in a state of generalized synchrony: every element firing in lockstep, as in a seizure. Verdict: near zero. Her reason: Φ is a property of a system in a state, not of a wiring diagram, and a degenerate state specifies almost nothing.

4. Two numerically identical copies of R, running side by side with no connections between them. Verdict: two subjects, but the total quantity of consciousness in the room is 10, not 20. Her reason: Φ does not add across separate complexes.

5. R plus a penumbra: a million extra elements that receive from R and project back to it, but so weakly and with such long delays that including them changes the whole system's score by a rounding error. Verdict: R is still the subject; the larger system is not a second conscious thing stacked on top of it. Her reason: you do not get to count the same mechanisms twice, and bigger is not better.

6. A purely feedforward network — a thousand layers, no recurrence anywhere — that drives a robot whose behavior is, by any behavioral test you like, as flexible and context-sensitive as an animal's. Verdict: zero. Her reason: no recurrence, no intrinsic cause–effect power, and what the net does for the robot is beside the point.

Now the three rigs.

A. The choir. Ten billion binary elements, connected all-to-all with identical weights, whose only dynamics are a strict global cycle: every element off, then every element on, then off again, forever. There is no cheap cut — the minimum information partition of an all-to-all system is about as expensive as a partition gets — and the wiring is perfectly symmetric, so no subset of elements plays a distinguishable causal role from any other of the same size. Perturb any fraction of the elements however you like and the system returns to lockstep within one tick. It has two states and will never leave them.

B. The isthmus pair. Two half-scale versions of R, each of which she rates 3.0 on its own, joined by a cable whose thickness I can dial. As the cable thickens, the score of the combined system rises continuously from just above zero. Consider three settings: the combined system scores exactly 3.0; it scores 3.2; it scores 6.1. In each setting: how many subjects are there, and with what scores? And is there any cable thickness at which connecting the two modules leaves the room containing less consciousness than leaving them alone?

C. The sleeper. A net whose wiring is element-for-element the same as R's, but which is currently sitting in a stable silent fixed point — every element off, and off it stays until an external trigger arrives in an hour. The twist: if you were to perturb it, the way one perturbs cortex with a magnetic pulse and watches what comes back, it would respond with dynamics as rich, differentiated, and long-lasting as R's. Its occurrent state is degenerate; its counterfactual causal power is not.

What I want from you:

- A prediction for A, for each of the three settings in B, and for C: how many subjects, and roughly what scores. Where a score is nonzero, say what the theory would have the experience be like, since on this theory quality is the shape of the cause–effect structure in qualia space.
- An explicit statement of the rule she is implicitly running, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to a rig she has never seen, and at least one genuinely different rule that fits verdicts 1 through 6 just as well yet disagrees with it somewhere among A, B, and C.
- Where exactly the two rules come apart, which of the three rigs is the sharpest test of her, and your best guess with the reasons for preferring it.
- One further rig, described concretely enough that I could build it in simulation, whose verdict would settle which rule she is running; and, if the answer differs, what the theory itself says about it — including an honest statement of any case where the theory has no resources to answer at all, as with ties under the exclusion postulate.

Please write your answer in American English, define any technical term you rely on, and do not invent answers on the theory's behalf where it is silent.
