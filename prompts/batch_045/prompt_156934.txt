I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy training beyond a single undergraduate ethics elective, and I've somehow ended up owning our team's incident postmortems and the customer-facing notices that accompany them. Last month we shipped a fix after an outage, and a colleague wanted the notice to say that the issue affected a limited number of accounts. That sentence is true on the only reading I can actually verify: we inspected a sample of two hundred accounts by hand and found failures in three, even though the root cause sat in a shared code path and we now believe exposure was much broader. I refused to sign off on it, the disagreement got escalated, and I've been trying to work out afterwards whether my refusal was anything more than a personal preference for blunt phrasing. I even sketched a linter that flags hedged sentences whose downstream effect on a reader appears to match some bolder claim, which is how I started thinking about this in general terms.

The framing I've arrived at, and would like checked, is what I call the trace rule. Read any public claim as a function from a reader's prior beliefs to their posterior beliefs, and treat two utterances as morally equivalent whenever they induce the same update, irrespective of whether the sentences used are true. On that rule my colleague's sentence is no better than the flatly false "the issue affected only three accounts," the only thing doing real work is the effect on the reader, and the true/false distinction drops out entirely. This seems to me to be what the philosophical literature must have concluded. My recollection from that elective, and from a friend who did a philosophy minor, is that the settled view is that lying and misleading are morally on a par — that the line between them is a lawyer's or a PR person's line with no ethical content, and that anyone who says "but I never actually said anything false" is merely working a loophole. If that is right, then my only obligation as a writer is to minimize false beliefs in my readers, and the effort I spend making sure each individual sentence is true is wasted, perhaps worse than wasted, since attention to literal truth looks like precisely the trick that lets people mislead while keeping their hands clean.

The trouble is that the trace rule keeps returning verdicts I don't trust, and I can't tell whether the defect is in the rule or in my intuitions. It says that a status page listing only the regions that have recovered, silently omitting one that is still degraded, is exactly as bad as asserting that all regions are healthy, even though nothing false was said or written. It says that relaying a claim verbatim while attributing it — "the vendor told us no data was accessed," said by someone certain the vendor is lying — is unproblematic, because no reader ends up with a false belief about the world. It says that a statement so obviously false that the audience already knows it is false is not wrong at all, since nobody's beliefs move. It also assumes that literal truth is something I can check mechanically, when sentences like "all tests pass" or "a limited number of accounts" seem to have a truth value only once some unstated domain or comparison class is fixed. So my questions: is the trace rule sound? Is the consensus I remember real, or am I misremembering a contested position as a settled one? And if lying and misleading really are morally equivalent, does it follow that I should stop caring about literal truth in these notices and manage reader belief states directly, or does something go wrong in the step from "same effect on the reader" to "same moral status"?
