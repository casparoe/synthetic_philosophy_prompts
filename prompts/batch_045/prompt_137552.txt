I work on a small team drafting legislation that would move possession of small quantities of illicit drugs out of the criminal courts, and we are deadlocked over which of two designs to put in the bill. The model everyone invokes is Portugal's, as I understand it: since the early 2000s, a person the police find with up to roughly a ten-day supply is not prosecuted but referred to a regional Dissuasion Commission — a small panel with legal, medical and social-work members rather than a judge — which may impose a non-criminal sanction such as a fine or community service, and which may suspend that sanction if the person engages with treatment or health services; possession remains formally unlawful, but there is no prison exposure and no criminal record. What I cannot get straight is what this arrangement is actually saying. Its own name advertises deterrence, yet its instruments are therapeutic, its personnel are not judges, and the coercive element is a sanction you can make go away by accepting help. It also applies to everyone caught below the threshold, dependent or not, which makes me doubt that the design rests on the illness reading of addiction that people usually attach to it. And it leans on a quantity threshold that looks like an evidentiary proxy for intent to supply rather than a moral boundary, plus a treatment system that in our jurisdiction simply does not exist in three of our districts — so the health route would in practice be an urban privilege.

Our own draft splits the single track in two. Under the version my colleague prefers, only people assessed by a clinician as having a substance use disorder would be routed to the health-oriented panel; everyone else — the weekend user, the student, the visitor — would keep a criminal liability for possession but could escape a record by completing an awareness course and paying a fee. She says this is simply the honest version of the Portuguese idea: illness excuses, recreation does not. I think that cannot be right, because the Portuguese scheme does not sort people by diagnosis at all, and if the justification were excuse, the design would have to look very different. But I also cannot say what the single-track design is committed to instead: that self-regarding conduct is none of the criminal law's business, that the harm of prohibition exceeds the harm of use, that criminalisation deters people from seeking help, that threats are the wrong motivational tool for this behaviour, or something more deflationary about enforcement cost and administrative convenience.

Can you reconstruct what each design presupposes, and separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with? I would like the implicit theories laid out rather than a verdict, and I do not need citations or comparative statistics — I need the structure of the arguments. Specifically: what view of addiction and of responsibility for one's own actions does the single-track scheme require, and is it compatible with a view on which dependent users are fully responsible? What theory of punishment, and of what makes a state response punitive, distinguishes suspending a fine in exchange for treatment from simply offering treatment — is the suspended sanction coercion, an improper condition on a benefit, or an ordinary exercise of prosecutorial leniency, and does the answer depend on how hard the underlying sanction bites? What does the two-track version commit us to about the difference between the dependent and the non-dependent possessor, and would adopting it force us into claims we would not want to make elsewhere — about excuse, about diagnostic gatekeeping, about the person who is dependent but declines to be assessed, or about the prisoner already convicted of possession for conduct that would now be routed to a clinic? Finally, tell me where each design is philosophically underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would license the same institutions, and tell me what would concretely have to change in the bill if we built it on the rival theory instead.
