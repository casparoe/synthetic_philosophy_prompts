I'm a journalist — I write about science, environment and ideas — and I'm working on a piece about what happens after everyone agrees a shared thing is being overused: a fish stock, an aquifer, the atmosphere, a pasture. The arguing is never about whether to cap it anymore, it's about who gets what under the cap, and I keep running into the same move and I can't tell what it's committed to.

Here's the pattern I keep seeing. A government decides the resource can only take so much total pressure, it caps it, and then it hands out the new rights for free, in proportion to how much each party was already taking before the cap existed. Fishery quotas worked out from catch history. Emissions permits worked out from past emissions. The biggest historical users get the biggest share of the thing that is now scarce and valuable, and it's often worth a fortune within a few years.

Two things bug me about it. First, the same governments auction off other public assets — spectrum, mineral leases — to whoever pays most, and their own environmental law otherwise runs on the principle that the person causing the damage pays for it. Here the biggest takers are the ones who get the asset. Second: the story everyone tells about why the commons gets wrecked treats the users as completely interchangeable. A bunch of herders, each adding one animal, none with any more claim than the others, each doing a bit of harm spread over everybody. But the fix has to *rank* those users — 30% to this fleet, 2% to that one — and nothing in the diagnosis says anything that could justify a ranking. So the remedy seems to be smuggling in a theory of who's entitled to what that the story of the problem doesn't contain.

And it's not the only historical rule around. In parts of the American West, water rights are ranked by who got there first — "first in time, first in right" — the right only covers water actually put to some recognised beneficial use, and it lapses if you stop using it. That's also an appeal to history, but it seems to be appealing to something quite different from "you took the most, so you get the most."

So: what does grandfathering actually presuppose? Does it presuppose that taking from an unowned thing generates some real claim on the taker's side, so that the person who took more has more of a right? Or the opposite — that nobody had any claim at all, so the state is simply free to create rights in whatever pattern it likes and history is just the allocation that causes the least shouting? Or is it not really about entitlement but about what property is *for*: stable expectations, investment, peace, so that who holds the rights is philosophically beside the point?

I also want to know how much of that the practice strictly needs versus what it merely goes along with. Could someone who thinks taking from the commons was straightforwardly wrong still endorse grandfathering — on feasibility grounds, or because it's unfair to punish people for what wasn't against the rules at the time, or because newcomers haven't relied on anything? If several different theories would all license the same rule, say so and tell me where they'd come apart. And then: if you swapped the theory — equal shares per person, or auction everything off — what would actually change about how the scheme runs and who turns up angry?

Please don't give me citations or a reading list, I can't use them in a news piece. I've put this to economists and they just say "efficiency" or "political feasibility," which feels like dodging. I want the implicit theory: what view of entitlement, property or responsibility this rule only makes sense under, and which rival views it quietly rules out.
