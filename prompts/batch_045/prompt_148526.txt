I am writing a novel, and I need your help with the document that stands at its centre.

The setting is the Marlund Reserve Authority, the central bank of a country of my own invention. For twelve years the Authority has set its capital-buffer requirements using an internal model of interbank contagion known, after its architect, as the Halden model. The model assumes that a failing bank's assets can be liquidated instantaneously at prevailing prices, that counterparties locate one another at no cost, and that the Authority's own intervention is anticipated with certainty by every participant. For eleven years the model's quarterly stress numbers fell inside the Authority's tolerance band. In the twelfth year a mid-sized lender failed, liquidation took nineteen days, prices moved against the sellers, and the model's numbers were wrong by a margin large enough to end careers. A review panel has now ordered Maya Halden and the internal critic who spent a decade writing memoranda against her, Joseph Ahlgren, to produce a single joint document, signed by both, on what this episode does and does not show about unrealistic assumptions in scientific and policy models. The panel has ruled that the memo must be intelligible to a board of governors with no training in the field: no footnotes, no bibliography, no citation of any authority, and no technical term that is not defined on the page.

The two characters hold the positions I want you to write from.

Halden speaks for the instrumentalist line descended from Friedman's essay on positive economics — the notorious argument that the falsity of an assumption is not itself a defect, that the only test of an assumption is the empirical performance of the whole model at the tasks it was built for, and, in its strong form, that a theory's fruitfulness and the unrealism of its assumptions tend to rise together. She adds that the demand to make assumptions realistic is not even well formed, since every description of anything is selective.

Ahlgren speaks for the line that treats an idealization as a controlled omission which the modeller is obliged, in principle and eventually in practice, to be able to reverse: you must know what you removed and be able to put it back, and a model's credit with you depends on there being a ladder of successively less idealized variants that converges on the phenomenon rather than jumping to a different one. (This is the tradition that talks of Galilean idealization, of isolations and de-isolation, of models as surrogate systems; the names need not appear in the memo.) He adds that unrealized obligations of this kind accumulate as a debt, and that the debt falls due not when predictions fail but when the model is carried into a neighbouring domain.

Please write the memo. I would like it to use, and define in passing, three pieces of vocabulary that the panel's own regulations have introduced: *surrogate credit*, meaning the share of a model's predictive success that a particular false assumption may claim as opposed to the share belonging to the calibration procedure; *de-idealization debt*, the standing backlog of omitted factors that no one has a scheduled obligation to reinsert; and the *convergence ladder*, the ordered family of model variants obtained by reinserting those factors one at a time.

On substance, these are the matters I most want the document to settle or locate precisely. When the Halden model produced correct numbers for eleven years, did the liquidation assumption earn any surrogate credit at all, or did the credit belong entirely to calibration? Suppose the frictionless-liquidation assumption were replaced by a different, incompatible false assumption that reproduces the same eleven years of numbers: does such a tie discredit the content of the assumption, or does it only show that the target behaviour is multiply realized and that the model was never describing the mechanism? What should be expected of the convergence ladder — a sequence of predictions that steadily approaches the observed behaviour, or a qualitative change at some rung, and what number on which rung would each of them predict in advance? Does eleven years of success in Marlund lend any credence to the same model in a jurisdiction with a different settlement structure, and does the answer differ between them? And the panel has required that any retained assumption be *auditable*: is the dispute over what auditable means a real disagreement or merely a verbal one, and likewise for calling an assumption, as opposed to a whole model, unrealistic?

Format the memo as follows: a short preamble in its own institutional voice; a numbered section of claims both can sign, where I want nothing vacuous — no aphorisms about models being simplifications, and no agreed claim that a committed partisan of either camp would not find mildly uncomfortable; then a table of residual disagreements whose columns give the neutral statement of the disagreement, the formulation Halden would sign, the formulation Ahlgren would sign, the crux, the case or computation both accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance; then a short list of the disputes the two of them now concede were about words; and finally a closing paragraph, under both signatures, conceding something each of their own camps would find treasonous — the document is meant to change the people who sign it. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words, in prose dense enough to be read into a hearing record and plain enough that my board of governors could follow it.
