I work in post-training at a large lab. My day job is turning human preference data into reward models and running RL against them: recruiting and screening annotator pools, writing and iterating the annotation spec, Bradley–Terry fits, inter-annotator agreement, curation of held-out and out-of-distribution eval sets, reward hacking and its patches, and social-choice rules for aggregating conflicting labels. Use that vocabulary freely and at full technical register — I do not need an introduction to any of it.

What I want translated into that vocabulary is a philosophical dispute I have been reading around, and I want the translation to be strict rather than suggestive.

The dispute: survey and experimental work on folk moral judgment has been dominated by subject pools drawn overwhelmingly from Western, educated, industrialised, rich, democratic societies, and on a number of moral and economic measures those pools sit toward the extremes of the human distribution. The commonly reported pattern is that American university samples weight harm- and fairness-type considerations heavily and purity-, loyalty- and authority-type considerations lightly, while many small-scale and non-industrial samples reverse or substantially redistribute that weighting. The question is what this variation implies for whether moral judgment is objective. I am stipulating the empirical picture rather than vouching for it: please do not spend words on whether it replicates, and do not try to look anything up. I also do not want names attached to the positions or any reading list — I have seen them attributed variously and I do not want the answer to turn on who said what. Reason from the structure of the arguments.

Three positions to translate, one at a time:

1. Robust universalism: the variation is superficial. There is a shared underlying moral competence or a single set of moral considerations that all populations respond to; cultures differ in which considerations they weight, in the situations they apply them to, or in what they take the non-moral facts to be, not in the basic structure of moral response. Moral facts are what they are independently of anyone's attitudes, and the variation is noise, measurement artifact, or diversity of application.

2. The debunking argument: the variation is best explained by local cultural learning rather than by tracking mind-independent moral facts. If we can give a complete causal story about why a population makes the judgments it makes — and that story never mentions the moral facts as causes — then the judgments are not justified as beliefs about such facts. In its evolutionary version the same point is made by noting that the forces shaping our moral psychology were not forces sensitive to moral truth.

3. The methodological reply: what variation indicts is our sampling and our methods, not the existence of moral facts. It shows that a literature built on a narrow slice of humanity has been over-generalising, and it licenses no conclusion about objectivity either way.

Two distinctions I want you to honour, because I suspect the literature slides over them and I suspect my vocabulary handles them very differently:

(a) Sampling failure versus constitutive failure. Is the complaint that the pool is unrepresentative of a target that exists anyway — in which case better sampling would converge on it — or that for some moral questions the target is constituted by whatever the pool says, so that there is nothing for a better sample to converge on? These seem to me to be completely different engineering situations, and I want to know whether my vocabulary can tell them apart from the inside.

(b) Weight divergence versus reason-set divergence. Populations may assign different weights to the same reason-giving features, or they may not share a feature space at all: what counts as a candidate consideration in one population is not even a candidate consideration in another, so that the same vignette is not the same input. Tell me whether I have any operational grip on this beyond cross-population transfer — whether a reward model fitted on one pool predicts the other pool's judgments better than chance — and be explicit if that lever cannot distinguish the two failure modes.

Finally, the word objective is doing at least four jobs in this debate, and I want them kept apart rather than merged: (i) there is a true target function independent of any annotator, of which labels are noisy measurements; (ii) there is a canonical rule for aggregating annotators, so that disagreement has a right answer even if no single annotator has it; (iii) labels converge under idealisation — more information, more reflection, less affect — so that actual disagreement is a symptom of impairment; (iv) a fitted model transfers out of distribution instead of merely interpolating its training pool. Say which of these each position needs, and whether needing one buys you any of the others.
