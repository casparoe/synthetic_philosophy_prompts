I'm running a seventy-minute session on the two-envelope paradox for about fifteen first-years — mostly philosophy and PPE students in a "Paradoxes" taster series, plus two or three who did further maths. They can compute an expected value and that is about it: no conditional probability, no Bayes, nothing about priors. I don't want the paradox explained to me; I've read the usual diagnoses and I'm unconvinced by the order in which they're normally presented. I want a plan for putting it in front of this group.

Here is my one fixed decision and the material I'm working with. I'm going to run the whole session on a bounded version rather than the usual open-ended one, because every time I've seen it taught with an unbounded set-up the discussion collapses into "the expected value is infinite, that's the trick," which seems to me a dead end that leaves them with nothing they can carry anywhere else. So: a fair coin decides whether the envelopes hold £10 and £20 or £20 and £40; you're handed one of the two at random; you may look inside; then you keep it or swap. As far as I can tell, staying is worth £22.5 in expectation, always swapping is also worth £22.5, and looking first and swapping unless you see £40 is worth £27.5 — so on this version the switching intuition is actually correct, and correct for a reason that survives every cleanup of the algebra. Correct me if any of those numbers is wrong, since the plan depends on them.

What I want from you is the plan, and specifically these five things. First, the order: does the closed case (no peeking) come before the open one, or after? My worry about opening with the closed case is that the symmetry argument — the envelopes are indistinguishable, so swapping is worth nothing in expectation — cements precisely the misconception that blocks the open case, namely that if the set-up is symmetric then looking inside cannot possibly give you a reason to switch. My worry about opening with the peeking case is that they have no machinery for conditioning on what they see. Which order, and what does the second case need to have been established in the first? Second, the carrying example: is my three-value ladder the right single case to hang everything on, what must a carrying example have, and what must it lack? I'm aware the overlap is doing a lot of work — £20 occurs in both pairs — and I suspect a non-overlapping variant (£10/£20 versus £40/£80) would make peeking worthless and mislead them in the opposite direction; if you think I should stage both, tell me which is the decoy and which carries the session. Third, the misconception map with a point in the sequence attached to each: (a) "it's just an algebra slip — the X in 'the other envelope contains 2X or X/2' is standing for two different amounts"; (b) the symmetry objection above; (c) "infinite expectations are the culprit," which I'd rather never come up at all — and when it does, what is the two-sentence defusal that doesn't open that door; (d) "if the paradox were real you'd pay for the right to peek before peeking, which is absurd," which I think is actually correct here and want them to see, except that I fear it lands as a cheap trick; and (e) the one the clever ones will form immediately after the notation fix — "so it was a fallacy all along and there's nothing left" — which is false on my version. Which of these should I let them hold for a while before taking it away, and where exactly does each get defused? Fourth, the check: one closing question that separates someone who has the structure from someone who has only learned the slogan "it depends on the prior" — my current candidate is to re-run the same game with the peek put behind a fee they name themselves, but I'm not sure that distinguishes understanding from number-crunching. Fifth, what should I openly refuse to explain at this level, and say out loud that I'm refusing?
