I am writing the framing chapter of a dissertation on inductive bias in machine learning, and before I commit to a position in print I want an audit of the package I have been defending. Here it is, as seven theses.

(1) Hume's problem of induction has no solution. There is no non-circular argument from the observed to the unobserved, and every proposed vindication, whether pragmatic, meta-inductive, or coherence-theoretic, either assumes what it was supposed to establish or changes the subject.

(2) The no-free-lunch theorems are formally correct but epistemically inert. They average over a uniform, or in the generalizations symmetry-invariant, distribution across a space of problems that no real inductive situation instantiates, so they threaten science no more than the observation that averaging over all possible worlds washes out every hypothesis.

(3) What licenses induction in practice is a contingent fit between our inductive biases and the structure of the world. That the fit obtains is an empirical discovery about the world, not an a priori guarantee.

(4) Goodman's lesson generalizes: projectibility is not a formal property of predicates, and no purely syntactic simplicity ordering, Kolmogorov complexity included, is a priori truth-conducive. Any such ordering earns its keep in the way (3) describes, if it earns it at all.

(5) Still, the Solomonoff-style convergence results are the strongest thing on offer, and in seminars I lean on them when pressed: a universal mixture predictor merges with the truth for every computable data-generating process, with total expected loss bounded by the complexity of that process. I present this as the nearest thing to a vindication of induction that we have.

(6) I reject pragmatic vindications of the straight rule as too weak to matter. Roughly: if any method succeeds, the straight rule succeeds too. But that promises only convergence in the limit, says nothing about how long we wait, and so cannot guide what to believe now.

(7) Which is why I have started to worry about (5). As far as I can tell, the complexity budget in (5) bounds the total loss, not the schedule on which it is paid. Given a deadline T of any size, I think I can write down a process of modest complexity — mimic one very simple process up to T, then switch to another — such that the mixture predictor spends essentially its whole loss budget after T. If that is right, then (5) is in the same boat as (6): convergence guaranteed, no deadline respected, nothing said about what to believe now.

What I want from you is an audit, not a tutorial.

First, tell me which of these are genuinely in tension and which only look that way. I would rather you discriminate than flag everything; I expect at least one of the apparent conflicts to dissolve on inspection, and I want to know which and why.

Second, rank the real tensions by severity. For each, say what exactly would have to be given up, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the set.

Third, check (7) as a piece of mathematical philosophy. Does the construction work as described? Does it overstate? Is there an asymmetry between (5) and (6) that survives it — for instance, does a bound on total expected loss give me something that a bare convergence-in-the-limit result does not?

Fourth, I have two further suspicions I cannot adjudicate, and you should say whether either is a real conflict. The first: (2) dismisses the no-free-lunch results because their uniformity assumption is unrealistic, while (5) advertises a guarantee holding uniformly over all computable environments, which looks like the sort of something-for-nothing that (2) says is unavailable. The second: I have been told that (4) and (5) flatly contradict each other, and I have been brushing this off on the grounds that the invariance theorem makes the choice of reference machine irrelevant. I am no longer confident that reply is adequate, since the invariance I am appealing to looks asymptotic while the objection looks like it concerns the short run.

Fifth, suppose I make the single revision that does the most work. Which is it, and is the package that survives still recognizably the skeptical position I set out in (1), or is it a quiet retreat from it?
