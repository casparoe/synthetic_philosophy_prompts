I direct a small municipal archive and museum in a former company town, and I want to stop triaging by instinct. A committee of three — me, a part-time archivist, and a rotating trustee with no training in anything — meets for ninety minutes a month and moves roughly twenty proposals through one of four dispositions: acquire and preserve, memorialize (display, plaque, named room, monument), restrict (seal for a fixed term), or let lapse and deaccession. Legal retention schedules are outside what I'm asking about; assume everything in front of us is legally ours to keep or discard.

Please turn Nietzsche's early essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life — the monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes, and the condition he places on all three, the power to transform and incorporate what one inherits — into a triage procedure my committee can actually run. I need the steps in order, each with an explicit STOP or HAND-OFF tag, so a trustee can be told on which questions a wrong answer kills the proposal and where the question is one of values and must come back to the whole board. Add an honest list of the steps that only look like procedure: distinctions Nietzsche depends on that will not survive being written as a question a volunteer can answer.

Three complications the procedure must handle rather than dodge. First: items plausibly claimed by two modes at once, where each claim is correct. Second: items that fit none of the three — and here I especially mean a category we invented in-house and use constantly, holdings we keep not because of what they record but because some future party we cannot now identify may need them to press a claim we cannot anticipate, a land title, a reparations case, a paternity record. Third: our collection is mostly the founding family's papers, and the family both built the town and broke its largest strike, so nearly every item is at once an origin and an indictment.

Work the procedure on three live proposals, in under 1,000 words total for the whole answer: the founder's ledgers, which are the only record of the town's founding payrolls and also the hiring list used to recruit strikebreakers; a sealed oral-history recording from the 1980s dispute, given on a promise of anonymity, which one participant now wants opened to correct the record; and a monument to the strike that I have been pushing unsuccessfully for three years and that I expect your procedure will tell me to drop. Tell me plainly in each case what it decides and where it stalls. Close by saying what this procedure cannot do — what the essay knows that a checklist loses.
