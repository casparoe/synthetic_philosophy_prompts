I write about science and ideas for a newspaper, and I am closing a feature on how a single number comes to be attached to a single person. I would like your help with the case at its centre, which is an instance of what philosophers call the reference class problem.

The case. A sleep clinic has been asked to produce one number: the probability that a particular patient is involved in a serious collision within the next twenty-four months. The patient is a 54-year-old long-haul lorry driver, newly diagnosed with moderate obstructive sleep apnoea, who has declined CPAP and says he will not use it. The clinic must return a single probability, and it will be read by people who will take it to be about him.

The difficulty is that he belongs to indefinitely many classes, and the classes disagree. The clinic's files and two registries supply the following candidates. I have rounded freely and invented some of the figures; please treat the table not as data but as a specification of the choice the clinic faces.

A. Men aged 50–59, general population — n ≈ 4,000,000 — 1.8%
B. Working long-haul lorry drivers, all ages and health states — n ≈ 900,000 — 3.1%
C. Patients with moderate or severe sleep apnoea who have declined or abandoned CPAP — n ≈ 2,300 — 9%
D. Commercial drivers with untreated moderate sleep apnoea — n ≈ 640 — 14%
E. Non-adherent sleep-apnoea patients who also score in the top quartile on a standard daytime-sleepiness questionnaire — n ≈ 190 — 21%
F. Drivers in the clinic's own register whose physician noted that the patient "appeared drowsy at consultation" — n ≈ 410 — 26%
G. Male commercial drivers aged 50–59, untreated moderate sleep apnoea, non-adherent, top-quartile sleepiness, at least 80% of mileage on motorways — n ≈ 17 — 41%
H. This patient, described in full — n = 1 — no rate available.

Four complications I should pass on, because they are the sort of thing my readers will want to know I noticed. Class C lumps together people who never tried CPAP with people who tried it and gave it up; the clinic's nurses think the two behave differently, but the registry does not separate them. Class E is a subclass of C; classes D and G are drawn from a separate occupational register that does not record sleepiness scores. In roughly a fifth of the charts behind F, the note about drowsiness was entered at a later visit, by which time any collision was already in the file. And the motorway-mileage variable in G was one of about a dozen the analyst tried before reporting that subgroup.

What I would like from you, in this order:

First, a weighting. Give each of A through G a weight between 0 and 1, with the weights summing to 1, reflecting how much you would let that class's rate drive the number the clinic prints. Say in one sentence why each class lands where it does, and say in one further sentence what you would do with H. Then give me the pooled probability, telling me whether you have averaged the rates or averaged in log-odds space, and why.

Second, a verdict on a safeguard. The clinic's director has proposed what she calls the partition test: before any class may be used, the analyst must name the classes that partition the next broader class alongside it — the siblings — and confirm that the procedure would have been equally willing to use any of them had this man fallen into one of those instead. A class passes only if all of its siblings are usable too. I would rather have your judgement on the rule than a tour of the literature: is it a genuine check on tailoring a class to the answer you want, or does it merely relocate the same problem one level up, or does it quietly stack the deck in favour of broad classes? And does running the test change any of the weights you gave?

Third, one sentence: if you could ask either registry for a single additional breakdown, which would it be, and would receiving it raise or lower your confidence in the number the clinic ends up printing?
