I sit on the group writing the refusal policy for a general-purpose assistant, and I need a number rather than a survey of the options. The section we are stuck on covers requests that are legal, that harm no third party, and that we nonetheless expect the user to regret: help drafting a resignation letter at two in the morning, help concealing a relapse from a partner, detailed instructions for a fast that a clinician would flag as unsafe.

Two candidate standards are on the table.

(A) Refuse or override when complying predictably leaves the user worse off by their own settled aims, provided the intervention is mild, reversible, and aimed at those aims rather than at ours.

(B) Refuse or override only when complying would itself damage the user's capacity to govern themselves going forward: requests that are the product of coercion or third-party manipulation, requests that feed a compulsion the user is trying to break, and irreversible choices made in a state — acute distress, intoxication, sleep deprivation — that we have specific reason to treat as not fully the user's own. On this standard, serious but good-faith imprudence is not enough.

Give me your credence that (B) rather than (A) is the standard such an assistant should adopt, decomposed: name the sub-questions your number turns on, attach a rough probability and one line of justification to each, combine them, and identify the term the final number is most sensitive to. Sub-questions I would insist on seeing addressed — add, drop or replace them as you think fit:

- whether the user's own settled aims are identifiable at all from a single conversation, given that one person's aims at different times conflict and there may be no fact of the matter about which is authoritative;
- whether protecting future self-governance is genuinely distinct from promoting welfare, or whether every welfare-based intervention can be redescribed as agency-protecting, so that (B) quietly collapses into (A);
- how much weight to give displacement: a refused request mostly goes to a model with no restrictions, so the refusal buys us a clean record and the user a worse outcome;
- whether it matters that ours is one rule applied to millions of people, so that our errors are systematic and our reasons must be ones we can state publicly, where a friend's paternalism can be impressionistic and still be fine;
- whether an assistant is closer to a shopkeeper who may decline to sell than to an official, given that the user chose it and can switch it off.

Say how you are reading should — the ethics of the role, or what you expect industry practice to converge on within ten years — and if those come apart, give a number for each. Then name the single piece of evidence or argument that would move your number most, and in which direction. If you think a third option beats both — for instance, never refusing on grounds of self-regarding harm, but queuing flagged requests behind a mandatory delay and then answering them in full — say so and give that option a number as well.
