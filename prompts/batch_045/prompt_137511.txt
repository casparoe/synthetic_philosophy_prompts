I have no academic background — I process insurance claims and read history and philosophy on the bus — but for a couple of years now I've been chewing on the argument between the historians who explain Nazi perpetrators as ordinary men shaped by their situation and the one who argued they were willing executioners moved by a shared eliminationist antisemitism. I know the rough shape of it: Browning's 1992 book about Reserve Police Battalion 101, middle-aged working-class men from Hamburg, at one point told by their commander that anyone who didn't feel up to shooting could step aside, with only about a dozen of roughly five hundred taking him up on it; men who wept and got drunk and then carried on; and Goldhagen's 1996 reply that this only tells you how the killing was organised, not why men who could have refused went on killing anyway, and that the answer is a murderous German political culture rather than obedience or peer pressure. There also seems to be a third option people reach for — that some people have cruel characters and the situation only licenses them — and I can't tell whether that's a real alternative or just a way of giving up on explanation. I reread Shirley Jackson's 1948 story "The Lottery" recently and it has lodged itself next to all this. As I remember it: a village of about three hundred holds an annual lottery whose loser is stoned to death by the whole town; the children spend the morning piling up stones; the ritual is run by a coal merchant and a postmaster out of a shabby black box; Old Man Warner repeats that "Lottery in June, corn be heavy soon"; other villages have already given up the lottery and are dismissed as crazy; Tessie Hutchinson turns up late and joking and ends up screaming that it isn't fair while her own little son is handed stones to throw at her.

What I want is a reading that runs in both directions: the story read through this debate, and the debate read through the story. A few things I'd like you to take seriously rather than skate past. First, a distinction I've been trying out on my own and haven't found in anything I've read: a situation can matter in two quite different ways — by supplying the agent with a reason he takes to be good (the lottery's supposed link to a good harvest, the belief that one is doing something necessary and hard) or merely by applying pressure he does not endorse (conformity, obedience, not wanting to be the one who steps out). Browning's men are usually described in the second way, but the fact that they seem to have believed they were doing something required arguably puts them in the first. So which kind of situation is Jackson's lottery, and what does the answer do to a Browning-style reading of it? Second, the lottery's victim is chosen at random and nobody has any grievance against her: there is no enemy, no threat, no propaganda, only the rite itself. Is that a harder case for a culture-and-ideology explanation than the massacres are, or an easier one? Third, the children: if even small children are gathering stones and throwing them, does the story support a character-and-disposition account, an enculturation account, or does it break the character-versus-situation framing altogether? Fourth, the story contains a kind of natural experiment the historians don't have — some villages have abandoned the lottery, and this one knows it and mocks them for it. Say what each of the two explanations would predict about which villages quit and why this one hasn't, and then say whether the story's answer, such as it is, tells for or against either view.

Two constraints and then I'll stop. Be disciplined about the mapping and honest about where it is loose: the story was published in 1948, decades before any of this social science, it isn't about Germans or Jews, and Jackson said her target was pointless violence and inhumanity in ordinary American life — so tell me plainly if pressing it into a Holocaust-historiography argument is an anachronism, and I'd much rather you say the debate has nothing to say about some feature than stretch it to fit. And where the story shows something neither explanation captures — my hunch is that it does, something about how a practice can keep going after nobody can say why they're doing it, and about what it costs the people who are only compliant — I want that spelled out rather than smoothed over. Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list; I can't get to a university library, and I'm after your argument, not a study plan.
