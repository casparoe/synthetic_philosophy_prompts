I am a novelist, and in three weeks I am giving the keynote at a small interdisciplinary workshop on the measurement of well-being. The room will be mostly well-being researchers who design and defend positive-affect, life-satisfaction, and eudaimonic scales; there will also be two scholars of Buddhist thought and one psychiatrist whose clinical work is on addiction and anhedonia. My thesis is that a life goes well for its subject just to the extent that it is free of suffering and of craving, and that pleasant experience contributes nothing of its own — and that this has an uncomfortable consequence for my own profession, which I intend to draw out rather than dodge.

My main support is what I call the anticlimax argument: in fiction the moment of satisfied desire is notoriously unwritable — writers reach for it and find nothing there to render, while anticipation, frustration, and loss are inexhaustible material. I read this as evidence that a satisfied desire leaves no positive phenomenology behind; it removes a discomfort and then there is simply nothing, which is what my view predicts and what anyone who thinks positive affect is intrinsically valuable should find puzzling. I also lean on a distinction between occurrent craving and what I call quiet craving, a standing disposition that never surfaces as a conscious wanting but still organises attention and memory, and on a distinction between tranquility and emotional blunting that I cash out in terms of retained responsiveness to reasons.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I should actually expect, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way the person raising it would really phrase it, with the reply I can give in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes, which single reply has to land if the talk is to survive, and give me the question I am hoping nobody asks.
