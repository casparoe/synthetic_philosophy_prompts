Below are an exam question from my third-year seminar on consciousness, and one student's answer to it. Please grade the answer out of 20 and give point-by-point comments that justify the mark: note what the answer gets right, where it goes subtly wrong (any conflation, misattributed commitment, or step that does not follow), and what a fully correct answer would have said instead.

**Question.** Ned Block distinguishes access consciousness from phenomenal consciousness, and has argued that global workspace theory (GWT) is best read as a theory of the former rather than of the latter.

(a) State the distinction as Block draws it.

(b) Explain why, on Block's view, an account of access is not automatically an account of phenomenal consciousness.

(c) Now consider a variant I will call *multiplex* GWT. On this variant there is no single global workspace; there are several partially independent workspaces, each broadcasting to a distinct set of consumer systems (motor planning, verbal report, memory consolidation, and so on), and no state is broadcast to all of them at once. Assess the following argument: *Multiplex GWT makes the access/phenomenal distinction collapse. Once access is consumer-relative there is no single property of being access-conscious, so a theory like GWT cannot be said to be about access consciousness at all, and Block's reading of GWT is refuted.* Is this argument sound?

**Student's answer.**

(a) A mental state is phenomenally conscious just in case there is something it is like for the subject to be in it — the qualitative, subjective character of experience, the feature that Chalmers later called the hard problem. A state is access-conscious just in case the subject can report it, that is, can say that she is in it. Access is therefore a matter of what the subject can say, while phenomenal consciousness is a matter of how things seem to her.

(b) GWT holds that a state becomes conscious when, instead of remaining confined to a local processor, it is broadcast to a global workspace and thereby made available to many downstream consumer systems — memory, attention, motor control, language. That is a story about the availability and circulation of information, and it says nothing about why any of that broadcasting should feel like anything. So a theory can be a good theory of access and still say nothing about phenomenal character: it explains how a content gets around in the system, not why there is something it is like to have it. This is why Block allows that phenomenal consciousness may overflow access — a subject may have more phenomenally conscious content at a time than she can report or bring to bear on reasoning and action.

(c) The argument is sound. If multiplex GWT is true, a single state can be broadcast to the motor-planning workspace but not to the verbal-report workspace, and so there is no one thing that being access-conscious is: it splinters into as many properties as there are consumer systems. Block's reading of GWT presupposes a single workspace, so once the workspace is multiplexed his claim that GWT is a theory of access loses its content. And since the whole point of the contrast was to split consciousness into two properties, showing that one of them is not a single property at all collapses the distinction. We are left only with the question of which workspaces are the neural correlates of phenomenal consciousness. Multiplex GWT is therefore a counterexample to Block's framing, and he would have to give it up.
