Please write a piece of sustained satire in the form of a mock government white paper.

Setting and document: A near-future state has taken quantitative macrohistory at its word and made it an arm of domestic policy. The document is issued by a fictional Bureau of Structural-Demographic Stabilization, with a title in the manner of White Paper No. 7: On the Economical Management of Surplus Elites, dated roughly a decade from now, signed by an invented under-secretary.

What the piece has to get right. The humor must come from the actual content, not from generic dystopia. The literature in view is the cliodynamic / structural-demographic tradition associated with Peter Turchin: long "secular cycles" with an integrative phase followed by a disintegrative one; population growth pressing on resources; elite overproduction, in which the supply of credentialed aspirants outruns the number of positions able to absorb them; popular immiseration as real incomes stagnate while elite incomes and elite numbers rise; fiscal strain on the state; and the widely noted forecast, published around 2010, that the 2020s would be a period of growing instability in the United States and Western Europe. The models were built and calibrated largely on preindustrial agrarian cases, using coarse historical series — population, real wages, prices, state revenues — and the cycles they describe run for roughly a couple of centuries.

Form and voice:
- Deadpan civil-service prose: passive constructions, euphemism, cost-benefit arithmetic, gently circular official reasoning, footnotes that concede everything while conceding nothing.
- Length 800–1000 words, formatted as numbered clauses grouped under four to six section headings, with three to five mock footnotes and one annex containing a small table of invented indicators.
- It should be legible and funny to a reader who knows the literature, and merely readable to one who does not. A reader should be able to tell what is being ironized without the author winking: no punchlines that announce themselves, no aside that admits the joke, no quotation marks around the euphemisms.

Target: not any person, living or dead, but the practice of reading quantitative macrohistory as a policy manual — the technocratic move from "these variables moved together in past cycles" to "the state should therefore manage those variables." Exaggerate real features of that style of reasoning; do not invent features it does not have. Someone who works in this tradition should be able to judge the satire unfair-but-fair.

The reductio: follow the model's own logic to a conclusion the reader recoils from. If surplus credentialed aspirants are a leading indicator of political violence, then the state acquires a rational interest in managing the supply of elites and the distribution of prestige as a matter of public safety — caps, licensing, deferral schemes, honorific sinecures, means-tested access to ambition. The paper should propose such measures in perfect bureaucratic good faith, at a level of administrative specificity that makes the horror concrete (who is counted, who is deferred, what happens to the deferred, what the program costs per averted riot).

Two wrinkles the paper must handle without ever naming them as problems:
1. Proxy measurement. Because the model was calibrated on agrarian empires, the Bureau must defend modern substitutes for its variables — e.g., holders of advanced degrees in fields without corresponding posts as the measure of elite aspirants, rent-to-income ratios as the measure of immiseration — and must defend them in the same confident register it uses for the original variables.
2. Temporal mismatch and reflexivity. Because a cycle is far longer than any electoral or budgetary horizon, the Bureau needs an accelerated or "sub-cycle" instrument for officials who require discord within their own term; and one footnote should register, in perfect innocence, both that publishing the forecast changes the behavior being forecast and that the cycles on which the model was fitted were observable only because nobody was managing them.

Constraint on criticism: every standard objection to this style of work — a small number of observable cycles, fitting rather than forecasting, the gap between historical variables and modern proxies, self-defeating prophecy, and the moral cost of the policy the model invites — must be shown rather than stated. Do not use the vocabulary of the critics ("overfitting," "unfalsifiable," "proxy problem," "reflexivity," "technocracy") anywhere in the prose of the white paper.

After the piece, on a line beginning APPENDIX, output nothing further in prose — only a single JSON object with exactly this schema:

{"title": string, "word_count": integer, "setting_gloss": string (one sentence on the fictional premise the reader must accept), "indicators": [{"name": string, "official_definition": string, "real_variable_it_stands_in_for": string}], "features_exploited": [{"feature_of_the_literature": string, "clause_number": string, "exaggeration": string}], "objections_shown": [{"objection": string, "where_the_reader_infers_it": string}], "reductio": {"premise_taken_from_the_literature": string, "policy_conclusion_drawn": string, "why_a_reader_recoils": string}, "deadpan_audit": {"least_deadpan_line": string, "most_legible_sentence": string, "verdict_on_fairness": string}}

Use the real vocabulary of the literature inside the JSON (elite overproduction, immiseration, secular cycle, disintegrative phase) even though the white paper itself should keep it euphemistic.
