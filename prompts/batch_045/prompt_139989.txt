I teach the final session of a graduate reading group on morality and nonhuman animals: 16 participants — mostly first- and second-year philosophy students, plus two visitors from biology — one 100-minute meeting, no internet access in the room. We have already worked through the argument from marginal cases, the epistemic problem of other minds, and the vertebrate-biased welfare literature. What I want from this last session is for people to *feel* the force of the scale argument about insects — that an astronomically large number of candidate individuals can swamp even a very low probability that any of them is sentient — rather than to hear me assert it and then discuss it.

Please design a runnable activity for that session. My intended frame: participants serve as a small funding committee with a fixed budget, deciding how to divide it between an intervention whose case rests on insects and one whose case rests on vertebrates. The department will actually transfer a trivial sum — say 100 units of local currency — according to the committee's decision, so the choice is not purely hypothetical, though nothing else of consequence rides on it. There should be no lecture, definition, or literature summary up front; the ideas should reach participants through what they have to do. Please do not cite or recommend any readings: I already have the syllabus.

Arrange the roles, rules, staged information, and payoffs so that participants collide with the following five complications on their own. The expressions 'expected value', 'fan-out', 'scaling exponent', 'replacement', and 'proxy transfer' should not be spoken until the debrief.

1. **Fan-out rather than diversification.** Uncertainty about insect sentience is not spread across individuals; it is very nearly one bet. An enormous number of insects multiplied by a small probability is not a near-certainty of a large amount of welfare — it is a single coin flip with an enormous upside. Two allocations can share an expected value and differ completely in how that value is distributed across outcomes, and I want participants to feel that difference in their gut before anyone names it.

2. **A hidden scaling exponent.** Suppose a creature's welfare capacity scales with its neuron count as n raised to some power α, where α is unknown: α = 0 for a threshold or any-mind view, α = 1 for a linear view, 0 < α < 1 for steeply diminishing returns, and possibly something other than neuron count doing the work. The comparison between very many very small nervous systems and few large ones flips depending on α, and nobody in the room knows α. The activity should make allocation decisions sensitive to α without ever letting participants settle it.

3. **Replacement.** If insect populations are limited by resources rather than by deaths, then averting insect deaths may barely reduce the number of insects that ever live: prevented deaths make room for replacements. The headline number of individuals affected can therefore be enormous while the counterfactual difference is near zero. For farmed vertebrates the population is created by demand, so the counterfactual has a different structure altogether. I want participants to discover that "number of individuals helped" and "difference made" can come apart, and to discover it by being shown the mechanism rather than being told.

4. **Proxy transfer.** Our evidence for sentience — flexible trade-off behaviour, learned avoidance, analgesic modulation, and the like — was calibrated on vertebrates. Applying it to insects assumes the link from proxy to sentience transfers across an enormous phylogenetic distance, which is a reference-class or base-rate problem rather than a simple evidential gap. Give participants evidence packets that look strong in isolation and let them notice that the strength depends on a transfer assumption they cannot check.

5. **Cheapness is ambiguous.** Insect-directed interventions are cheap per individual affected, and that cheapness is what makes the scale argument bite. But cheapness is also some evidence about what is being measured, and about neglectedness rather than about welfare. Participants should be able to feel the pull of "the numbers are bigger" and also feel, later, that the cheapness was doing part of the work that the sentience probability was supposed to do.

Practical constraints: stipulate every empirical figure yourself as an order of magnitude and hand it to participants as given — I do not want the session spent arguing about real entomology, neuron counts, or population estimates, and I do not want anything to depend on their being accurate. Supply a lookup table or pre-computed cards so that nobody stalls on arithmetic. Assign role cards carrying different prior credences about insect sentience, so that the committee contains both people who take insect sentience to be near-certain and people who take it to be near-zero, and require the committee to produce a single allocation (with provision for a recorded dissent that the scoring still tracks). No one should be able to sit out: every participant must make at least one binding choice. Assume no one present has any decision-theoretic training beyond the obvious.
