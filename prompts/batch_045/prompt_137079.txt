I am one of two student members of the Discipline Committee at my school, a small independent school of about 350 students, and next week we vote on a proposed addition to the student handbook. I have to cast a vote and I have roughly three minutes to speak, and I have not been able to work out what I think.

Here is what happened. Last semester a group of about fifteen upperclassmen started a betting club: they run a paid fantasy football league, they took bets on the student council election, and once a month they hold a poker night at someone's house. All of it is with their own money, all of it is consensual, nobody has been cheated or pressured, and nobody who was not already looking for it has been bothered by it. Two of them put up a sign in the hallway.

The dean would like a rule. What makes this difficult is that her argument is not that anyone was harmed. Her argument is that a school is held together by a shared understanding of what sort of conduct is acceptable here; that this understanding is what makes our rules against cheating, hazing and cruelty work at all; that a norm stops binding once people can violate it openly without consequence; and that fifteen students publicly treating gambling as a normal thing to do is therefore a threat to the whole fabric even though no individual is hurt. Several parents have said much the same, and two have said they will withdraw their children if nothing is done.

The version of the rule that will actually be voted on does not ban gambling. It bans open and unapologetic violations of community standards: a student who quietly bets with friends off campus is left alone, while a student who recruits, posts about it, or signs people up in the hallway can be suspended. It was drafted that way deliberately, on the grounds that what damages the shared understanding is the publicness rather than the betting.

The things I cannot sort out are these. If the publicness is doing all the work, then either the quiet betting is not really corrosive at all, in which case I do not see why the school has any business with it and the rule is only about visibility, which seems to punish candour; or the quiet betting is equally corrosive, in which case the line the committee has drawn is arbitrary and the honest version of the rule would be a flat ban, which I also do not like.

Second, the dean's argument sounds as though it were built for an entire country, and for an entire country I do not believe it: people have said this about all kinds of conduct and nothing collapsed. But my school has 350 people in it. It may be that the argument is simply stronger here, because in a group this size what people can see one another doing really does move the norm. I cannot tell whether that should make me more sympathetic to her or more suspicious of her.

Third, whose standards are these? Students are close to evenly split, teachers are mostly against, parents are loudly against, and the wider world we all go home to is full of betting advertisements. Every family signed the handbook on enrolment, which one member of the committee thinks settles the matter, but I did not choose this school and I could not realistically leave it. A friend on the committee also says the real reason we are voting on this is that two families who donate a great deal complained, and that the language of shared morality is what we say instead; I do not know whether that is true, and I am not sure it should change the argument even if it is.

There is a fourth option on the table, which is that the committee issues a statement saying the community disapproves of the club and stops there, with no punishment attached.

I looked some of this up, and I gather there is a well-known version of this argument, but everything I found was about national law and about sex, and none of it told me how to vote. Please tell me, in your first sentence, how I should vote: for the rule as written, for a broader ban, against the rule, or for the statement without punishment. Then give me the reasoning. Do not open with background, do not restate what I have written, and do not spend three paragraphs hedging before committing. If it is not too much to ask, end with the two or three sentences I should actually say at the meeting, and tell me which part of my own thinking is weakest.
