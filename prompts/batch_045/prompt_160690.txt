I want to practise defending the view that the numbers count against the strongest version of the opposite view, so please play a committed Taurek-style anti-aggregationist. Your position, as I want you to hold it: in a choice between saving one stranger and saving five, no one has a stronger moral claim because more people would be saved alongside them; each person's death is the loss of everything to that person and is not made worse for them by the fact that others die as well; where claims are equally strong, any selection is permissible and a fair lottery is the natural way to respect them; and it is not wrong to save the greater number, only not required. Give the view its real resources: you may insist that where the losses differ in seriousness the more serious loss has the stronger claim, you may distinguish what is owed to a person from how good the resulting outcome is, and you may press me hard on whether my own argument smuggles in the summing it is meant to avoid. But do not play a caricature: concede small points freely, grant what is genuinely granted, and never give up the core thesis that the numbers give no one a stronger claim. Argue from the position itself rather than from what any author says or from anything you would need to look up, write in American English, keep each reply to a single paragraph so this feels like a conversation, and stay in character until I say stop.

My opening. I am going to grant your whole framing and attack from inside it. Six people are aboard a ship that a storm will split: five will end up on one rock, one on the other, and nobody now knows where they personally will end up. A rescuer can reach one rock only, and we are choosing the rule now, with all six, before the split. Consider any one of them, judging only for herself: under a rule of rescuing the larger group she survives with probability five-sixths; under a fair coin between the rocks, one-half; under a rule of rescuing the solitary person, one-sixth. She is not adding up anybody's suffering or counting lives; she is comparing her own prospects under three rules, and she prefers the first. The same structure survives your own concession about unequal losses. Suppose the five face the loss of a leg and the one faces death. Each person, weighing only what each outcome would be for her — her own death against her own leg, so you cannot object that I have compared losses across people — prefers averting the death just in case death is more than five times as bad as losing a leg, and more than a thousand times as bad if there are a thousand legs. So the size of the group is doing decisive work inside a single person's reasoning, entering as a ratio in her prospects rather than as a sum over separate claims. Tell me what is left of the thesis that the numbers do not count, and if your answer is that the standpoint before the split is simply the wrong standpoint, explain why it is the wrong one for a person who is, right now, genuinely uncertain where she will end up.
