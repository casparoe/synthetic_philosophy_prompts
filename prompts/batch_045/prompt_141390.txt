I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of history, and I sat in on a departmental colloquium last week where a familiar tag was made to do a lot of argumentative work, and I cannot pin down what it was supposed to mean.

A graduate student asked whether two accounts of the same episode — a famine reconstructed from the archival record of grain shipments and prices, and one reconstructed from household diaries and later oral testimony — could both be objective, neither being an error about, or a distortion of, the other. A senior colleague answered that the discipline's task is simply to show how it actually was, the Rankean tag, "wie es eigentlich gewesen," usually rendered "as it really happened," and treated that as closing the question: if the accounts conflict, at most one can be how it actually was.

I want to bring the student a disambiguation rather than a verdict, so please lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could be asserting and assess each. My own suspicion is that it trades on an unmarked contrast between two different ideals: correctness in what one says, and fineness of grain — how much one says. An account can be accurate at low resolution; but if the demand is for maximal resolution, I do not see how it is satisfiable even in principle, since at finer and finer grain there appears to be no canonical inventory of events for a complete chronicle to be complete about. I would also guess there is a reading on which the eigentlich is doing essentialist work — the real nature of the episode as against its appearances — and further readings on which the demand is instead one of independence from the inquirer's standpoint, or of what any well-run inquiry would converge on.

Give me the menu, with for each reading a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, or false or unstatable. Then say which reading, if any, would actually entitle my colleague to treat the student's question as settled, which readings a Carr- or White-style constructivist could cheerfully accept, and which reading you suspect is doing the work in most of the arguments where the phrase gets wheeled out.
