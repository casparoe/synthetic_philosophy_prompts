I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas in which a comparative psychologist I'll call Miriam assigns every creature our readers send in one of the four grades of mind that run from Darwinian through Skinnerian and Popperian to Gregorian — the vocabulary borrowed from Dennett's *Kinds of Minds* (1996), which Miriam uses freely but insists is only a ladder, not a ranking of intelligence. I draft each instalment and she signs off on it, so I need to guess her verdict correctly in advance, with a one-sentence rationale that a scientifically literate reader who has never opened a philosophy book can follow; the piece is due Thursday at 900 words and cannot lean on the technical literature. Her track record so far: a moth at a candle flame, a mayfly, and a slime mold that traces the shortest path through a maze to reach oat flakes are all Darwinian, on the grounds that clever performance is not the currency, learning is; a bacterial population that evolves antibiotic resistance over generations is Darwinian too, because the design change is phylogenetic and no individual learns anything; a pigeon pecking a key, a laboratory rat pressing a lever, and a dog shaped by clicker training are Skinnerian; a honeybee that learns a colour–reward pairing and then waggle-dances for nestmates is Skinnerian, with the dance ruled hard-wired signalling rather than culture; a rat in a water maze that takes a novel short-cut when its usual route is blocked is Popperian, because it had a map and rejected the detour before swimming it; a chimpanzee that solves a puzzle box and then goes straight to the matching latch on a second box among decoys is Popperian; a hypothetical human raised with no language and no artefacts is Popperian and not Gregorian, because it is the equipment and not the species that matters; a crow in a population where the local shape of a probing tool is passed along is Gregorian; a chimpanzee using a few hundred visual symbols to ask for absent things is Gregorian by courtesy of our tools; and an adult human doing long division on paper is Gregorian, with Miriam adding that part of the cognizing is being done by the paper.

The two cases due this week are these. The first, submitted by a reader who calls it Orion-7: a system whose capacities were produced over weeks by an external optimisation process — next-token prediction over a large corpus of human text and code, followed by reinforcement from human feedback — and which, when given a hard problem at run time, produces many candidate solutions in tokens, scores them against a learned verifier, writes and executes code to check its own answers, keeps a scratchpad, calls a calculator, and emits only the best candidate; nothing that happens in a session changes its weights afterwards, and two copies of it share nothing one of them worked out. The second: a fleet of warehouse robots that keep updating their own internal models from their own experience over months and can ship what they have learned to new units, which then build on it. My own hunch is Popperian for the first and Gregorian for the second; my editor thinks both are Gregorian because both borrow human-made tools; a persistent reader says the first is Darwinian at run time, since nothing is learned once the weights freeze and the whole scheme is supposed to be about how an individual acquires its design rather than about how impressive it looks while it performs.

What I need from you is the philosophy, not the reporting. Extract the two or three rules that fit Miriam's whole track record equally well — I take it they will differ over whether a grade is earned by the mechanism an individual can deploy right now, by the process that installed that mechanism (genes, its own experience, or culture), or by whether the mechanism is acquired and passed on within an individual lifetime — and say what each predicts for Orion-7 and for the fleet, where they diverge, and why. Give me your best guess at her verdict on each with the reasons for preferring it, identify which of her earlier verdicts each guess leans on most heavily and which one it sits least comfortably with, and tell me what she should say for consistency if she wants to stay faithful to her own precedents, including what principle she would be adopting with either choice and which earlier verdict she would then be implicitly demoting to an exception. Then name one further case — beyond the two I have — that would cleanly separate the surviving rules: I have in mind something like a creature born with a rich innate model it never updates but does use to reject candidate actions before performing them, or a population of identical systems that transmit acquired weights to each other, but I would rather have your candidate than mine. Finally, tell me honestly whether consistency here might require refusing to grade an artefact at all, or adding a second axis — a design-time grade and a run-time grade, or a fifth grade between Skinnerian and Popperian for creatures whose selective environment is internal but not self-built — rather than forcing one of the four; if that is the real answer, say what it would cost the column, which is built on giving readers a single word per creature.
