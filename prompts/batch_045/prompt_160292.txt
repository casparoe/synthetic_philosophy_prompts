I teach a graduate seminar on prognosis in social and political philosophy — how much confidence large-scale claims about the direction of history can possibly deserve — and I'm drafting a paper on the epistemology of such claims, using Fukuyama's end-of-history thesis (the 1989 essay and the 1992 book that expanded it) as my central case. I want a number from you, and I want the derivation. Here is the proposition, with its resolution conditions fixed as tightly as I can manage: by 2100, no form of political organization will have arisen that (i) governs a single state or an integrated bloc of at least three hundred million people, (ii) endures for at least twenty-five consecutive years, (iii) is not a liberal democracy in a recognizably Fukuyaman sense — competitive elections with genuine turnover, broad protections for individuals against the state, and a largely market-organized economy — and (iv) is offered by its own defenders and widely received elsewhere as a universal alternative, something other societies ought to adopt, rather than as a local, civilizational, or emergency exception. My own credence in that proposition is about 0.4. I don't want 'it depends what you mean by history' as an answer: I have fixed the referent, and one of the sub-questions I want you to price is whether my fix leaves something genuinely indeterminate.

Decompose it. I suggest, though you should add or substitute freely, at least these terms: the ideological-exhaustion claim, that no coherent rival universal doctrine is available to be believed (as opposed to merely being professed); the institutional-convergence claim, that the socioeconomic requirements of a modern economy push toward one institutional form; and the anthropological or 'last man' strand, that a regime securing equal recognition settles the demand for recognition well enough to be stable, while the residue of the desire to be recognized as superior remains a permanent source of turbulence without generating an alternative. Give me a number and one line of justification for each, then combine them — and be explicit about whether you are treating them as independent, since I suspect the second and third pull in opposite directions and the first may be nearly entailed by the third. Two further terms matter to me and are not in the standard discussion. Call the first the decoupling hypothesis: the mechanism behind the convergence claim seems to run through mass literacy, mass mobilization, and a workforce whose cooperation must be secured, and it is at least conceivable that advanced economies will grow while shedding that dependence, so that a wealthy, modern, high-surveillance regime remains stable without the participatory pressures the thesis expects; price how much of the convergence term rides on that mechanism being load-bearing. Call the second the vacancy condition: a rival may not need to win any argument, only to be credibly available, so that the absence of a doctrine is compatible with the thesis being satisfied in letter while failing in spirit — price how much of my credence should be discounted for that possibility. Finally, give me a term for the thesis's own vocabulary: how likely is it that 'end point of ideological evolution' and its cognates have no determinate truth conditions at all, so that the honest answer to my question is refusal rather than a low number?

Then the diagnostics. A colleague in international relations puts the same proposition at 0.15, on the grounds that democratic backsliding inside established liberal democracies and the evident durability of authoritarian capitalism have already done the falsifying work; tell me which of the terms above most plausibly separates me from her, since we agree on most of the surface facts. Tell me which single sub-question my final number is most sensitive to — the one where a shift from 0.5 to 0.8 or 0.2 would move the total most — and say what observable development between now and 2050 would be most diagnostic for that term, with an indication of how you would update in each direction. And state plainly, at the end, whether you think the whole exercise is misplaced: whether a thesis of this shape is the kind of thing one should hold a credence about at all. No citations or bibliographic pointers, please; I know that literature, and I am interested in the structure of the reasoning, not in who has said what.
