My brother and I got into it again over Thanksgiving about voting. I said I basically don't bother with national elections any more, for the bog-standard reason people call the paradox of voting: the chance my one vote changes the outcome is essentially nil, voting costs me a couple of hours, so on straight expected-value terms it isn't worth it. (And I'm not convinced by the reply that the stakes are huge so even a one-in-a-million shot pays — by that logic I should be spending those two hours on almost anything else.) He comes back with: "if everyone thought like that, nobody would vote." Then he acted like that settled it, and like I was some kind of free rider.

I keep going back and forth on whether that's an actual reply or just a way of making me feel bad. So: what are the different precise things that one sentence could be claiming? I want the whole menu, from "trivially true and irrelevant" to "substantive and defensible" to "just false", and for each one whether it would really answer my argument or just quietly change the subject — from "should I vote?" to "should the practice be kept alive?" — plus which reading my brother was probably reaching for, given he used it to end the conversation. Does it matter that it says *thought* like that rather than *acted* like that?

Two things I've been chewing on that I'd like folded in, because they're where I cant tell if I'm onto something or just confusing myself:

1. The rule I'm appealing to — vote only if your vote decides something — seems to have no stable everyone-follows-it state. If literally nobody votes it's 0–0, so my single vote does decide it, so by my own rule I should vote. But if everybody votes, mine decides nothing, so by my rule I shouldn't. It flip-flops. Is that instability doing any real work inside the "if everyone thought like that" reply, or is it a parlor trick about a rule nobody literally follows?

2. I made up two variants to test it. In the first, my staying home is by stipulation perfectly correlated with three million other people staying home — say we all read the same post and all act the same way. In the second there's no causal link at all, just evidence: my not voting is a strong signal that lots of similar people also didn't. The slogan seems to have real pull in both, but for different reasons, and I can't tell whether either one is anything like the actual situation on election day.

Anyway — lay the readings out as a list, say which are platitudes, which are defensible, which are plain wrong, and which (if any, honestly) actually defeat the "my vote makes no difference" argument rather than gesturing at it. And tell me if any of them is the one that would make "free rider" stick.
