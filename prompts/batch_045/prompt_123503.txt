hey — I'm on my department's curriculum committee (mid-sized philosophy dept, first rewrite of our assessment policy since generative AI showed up) and we've got a faculty meeting next thursday where we're supposed to 'discuss assessment in the age of AI.' The discussion so far has been genuinely useless: half the room wants blue books and invigilated exams again, the other half thinks that's cargo-culting and we should just accept that everyone is going to delegate and grade the transcript rather than the mind. I think both camps are circling the actual worry, which isn't cheating — it's that delegation might quietly destroy the very capacity we're certifying, and that the checks we'd use to notice are themselves delegatable. You can only sanity-check output in a domain to the extent you already understand that domain, so each round of offloading makes the next round's audit weaker, and the audit of the audit weaker still; meanwhile borrowed explanations run through your own phrasing and come out feeling like your own understanding (there's probably already a name for that phenomenon — the illusion-of-explanatory-depth stuff — but call it comprehension laundering for my purposes), and the cost only comes due in genuinely novel situations where the model has no training signal and no one in the loop has ever performed the task unaided. I want to break the deadlock by making the pro-delegation position as ridiculous as I think it is, so: write me a satirical internal document I can read out loud at the meeting, 700–900 words.

Form: a 'Programme Specification and Assessment Framework' — the kind of numbered-clause administrative prose a university issues when it has fully made peace with something — for a taught graduate programme that has formally replaced understanding with a defined substitute (call it something bureaucratically cheerful: verified output-readiness, epistemic liquidity, just-in-time comprehension, your pick). Venue/voice: deadpan, an internal circular from an invented Director of an Office or Institute for Cognitive Infrastructure, addressed to colleagues, entirely sincere about its own project — no winking at the reader, no 'as if that weren't horrible' asides. The jokes have to come from getting the epistemology right: build the standard objections into the machinery rather than stating them. So I want clauses on how all verification of AI output is itself to be AI-assisted and efficiency-rated; on who validates the validator (and who validates them, and where the chain is allowed to stop, and on what grounds); on the grade penalty for students who persist in understanding things unnecessarily slowly; on the handling of genuinely unprecedented cases, where the framework must have some quiet way of declaring them out of scope; on what happens to the department's own research output once no one can referee a paper without assistance; and ideally something about the training-data problem — the programme can't be evaluated by anyone who hasn't been through it, and eventually there is no unaided human performance left to calibrate against. End with an assessment rubric with grade bands (first-class work should sound genuinely indistinguishable from the real thing, and the top band should reward something hollow). Invent whatever committee names, consultation deadlines and pilot-cohort details you need. One constraint for fairness: somewhere in there the document should accidentally say something true and defensible about real cognitive offloading — writing things down, calculators, external memory, the fact that nobody ever understood anything entirely on their own — so that the people in the room who think delegation is fine hear a version of themselves being caricatured rather than a strawman, and can laugh without feeling railroaded.

After the document, add a brief note (150 words or so, and written straight, not in character) on where the satire is taking liberties: which of the pro-delegation arguments it's actually engaging and which it's dodging by exaggeration, what the strongest defender of the policy would say in reply, and — since this is going to be read out as if it's an argument — what evidence or consideration would change your mind that the erosion is real rather than the latest episode of moral panic about a new technology. Don't cite anything or point me to reading; I want the piece, not a bibliography.
