I'm giving a twenty-five minute talk with fifteen minutes of Q&A at my city's effective-altruism-plus-rationalist meetup next month, and I'd like to show up with prepared answers rather than just a slide deck. The position I'm defending I've been calling *restructuring, not dampening*, and it has three parts.

First: taking the simulation argument seriously does not lower the expected value of far-future-focused work. Most of the probability mass I'd put on this run being terminated before anything astronomically valuable happens is independent of what we do, so it enters every longtermist calculation as roughly the same multiplicative constant, and constants cancel when you compare longtermist interventions to one another. Second: where simulation considerations genuinely bite is not *within* longtermism but at the boundary between near-term and long-term causes — and there the sign is ambiguous rather than deflationary, because a simulator with any ordinary motive (entertainment, research, training data, moral curiosity) plausibly cares about what happens inside the run over ordinary human timescales, which pushes value back toward the near term. Third, and this is the part I care most about: there is a neglected category of intervention I don't have a good name for, call it *continuation value* — actions whose main expected effect is to raise the probability that this run is kept going, archived, or rerun, or that its recorded history is worth something to whoever is running it. Under a nontrivial range of simulator motives, that category rewards things like being legible and interesting, and like passing through a civilizational transition without destroying the dataset, none of which any existing cause-priority framework tracks.

The room matters, so let me describe it. Roughly half are longtermists, several of them employed on AI existential risk, who have internalized the astronomical-waste framing and get nervous about anything that looks like it shrinks their numbers. There are two or three near-termist global-health people who think longtermism is unfalsifiable and will happily use simulation skepticism as a club against it. There is one condensed-matter physicist and one mathematician, both of whom will refuse to let me say the word probability until I've said what measure I'm using over the space of simulations. There are a handful of general rationalist-blog readers who will apply the and-then-what-do-you-do-on-Monday test and who flinch at anything Pascalian. And there will be at least one person who already believes we are in a simulation and has concluded that this licenses cheerful nihilism.

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from that specific room, ordered by how likely they are combined with how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way its source would phrase it — the physicists' objection should read like a physicist wrote it and the near-termists' like a grant officer wrote it. For each, a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, plus an honest note on where that reply is soft, quietly question-begging, or dependent on an assumption I haven't argued for. Tell me which objections I should concede in the talk itself before anyone raises them, and identify the single reply that has to land for the position to survive the session. Then give me the question I'm hoping nobody asks, why it is the dangerous one, and what the least-bad answer to it looks like.

For calibration on where I'm weak: my credence that we are in a simulation of some kind sits around fifteen to twenty percent; I do not think we can know simulators' motives in any detail; I am aware that my third claim most resembles a machine for generating post hoc justifications; and the piece I have least worked out is the nesting question — simulations containing simulations, and whether cheap, short, low-fidelity runs so dominate the population of simulations that my constant multiplier is very small and my conclusion becomes technically correct but practically indistinguishable from the nihilists'. Aim your objections at the strongest version of the argument, not at the flustered version I'd produce under time pressure. Please don't pad the answer with citations or suggested reading; I want the argument, not the literature.
