I am working on the question of whether Malthus's population principle can fairly be called 'right but early' rather than falsified, and I have become stuck on a passage in the third chapter of the first (1798) edition of Thomas Malthus's An Essay on the Principle of Population, the chapter that reviews the savage or hunter state and the shepherd state. Malthus writes:

"It remains to inquire, whether this power can be checked, and its effects kept equal to the means of subsistence, without vice, or misery.

The North American Indians, considered as a people, cannot justly be called free and equal. In all the accounts we have of them, and, indeed, of most other savage nations, the women are represented as much more completely in a state of slavery to the men, than the poor are to the rich in civilized countries. One half the nation appears to act as Helots to the other half: and the misery that checks population falls chiefly, as it always must do, upon that part whose condition is lowest in the scale of society. The infancy of man in the simplest state requires considerable attention; but this necessary attention the women cannot give, condemned as they are to the inconveniences and hardships of frequent change of place and to the constant and unremitting drudgery of preparing every thing for the reception of their tyrannic lords. These exertions, sometimes, during pregnancy, or with children at their backs, must occasion frequent miscarriages, and prevent any but the most robust infants from growing to maturity."

I am finding several things hard to follow, and I would like help with all of them. First, what exactly is the logical work of the sentence 'It remains to inquire, whether this power can be checked, and its effects kept equal to the means of subsistence, without vice, or misery'? Is Malthus there assuming that any effective check must be either vice or misery, or is he leaving open the logical possibility of a third kind of check? Second, how should I understand the claim that 'the misery that checks population falls chiefly, as it always must do, upon that part whose condition is lowest in the scale of society'? Why does the ranking of society matter to where the check falls, and how does that claim sit with his later comparison of savage warriors to gentlemen and of savage women, children, and aged to the lower classes of civilised states? Third, I want to relate this to a distinction I will introduce for the purposes of my question: call a 'benign preventive check' any limitation on population growth that works through people voluntarily choosing to have fewer children as their standard of living rises, and that involves neither 'vice' in Malthus's sense nor 'misery'—no starvation, war, exposure, or crushing drudgery, and no resort to what he would count as immoral or degrading sexual conduct. Modern defenders of Malthus sometimes say that the demographic transition, in which fertility falls voluntarily with rising income, shows that his underlying mechanism was correct and that the prediction was merely early; critics reply that this is a 'retrofit rescue', because a first-edition text that recognises only vice and misery as checks has to be rewritten to make room for a benign preventive check. Read closely, does the passage itself give Malthus the resources to perform that rescue in a principled way, or does his own dichotomy and his emphasis on misery falling on the lowest ranks mean that a benign voluntary check would have counted, for him, as no check at all? In other words: is the 'right but early' verdict continuous with the argument of this passage, or does it depend on replacing the dichotomy the passage sets up?
