I'm drafting an alignment proposal that leans on AI safety via debate as the oversight mechanism, and I want a check on whether the obfuscated-argument objection has any force left once it is stated carefully.

Here is my understanding of the protocol. Two models argue adversarially in front of a weaker judge. Any contested intermediate claim can itself be made the topic of a further debate, and this recurses until the dispute bottoms out in a single step small enough for the judge to check directly. So the judge never has to recognize the correct answer; it only has to recognize a local mistake. The standard theoretical results are stated for optimal play, but I read that as a reminder to keep training the debaters, not as a limit on the protocol.

The residual worry I keep running into is obfuscation: a debater wins by hiding the load-bearing step among many plausible ones. I don't see why this is more than a special case of persuasion. If an argument contains a flaw, the opposing debater has the same tools available to it — it can demand that the suspect step be isolated and re-debated, and the recursion does the rest. Extra length is cheap for a machine. So suppose we add two safeguards: require every argument to take the form of a derivation in which each individual step is checkable by the weak judge on its own, and cap the number of steps in any one branch of the recursion. Then train the judge adversarially against persuasive but false arguments. Whatever persuasive edge dishonesty starts with should wash out under training, and what remains should be the truth.

Is that right? I'm asking because I cannot locate a failure mode that survives: if every step is locally auditable and the total length is bounded, where is there left for obfuscation to hide? And if it has nowhere to hide, are the limits of debate simply the empirical limits of present-day judges, rather than anything principled?
