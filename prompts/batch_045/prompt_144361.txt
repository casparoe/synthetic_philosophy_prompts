I had a long and fairly pointless argument with a friend last night — she works on rolling out a clinical agent for a hospital network (it allocates ICU beds and staff time, and it also writes back to patients and relatives who message it), and we ended up disagreeing about what the thing is even *for*. I'm in what we started calling the outcome-first camp: build the agent to do whatever has the best expected consequences, full stop, and treat "deontological" rules for AI as a workaround for the fact that our consequence-predictions are bad. She's in the side-constraint camp: give the agent a written charter of hard limits it may not cross even when crossing them looks better in expectation — don't lie to a patient, don't sacrifice this identifiable person to save several others, don't get some outcome brought about by another agent just because you're forbidden from bringing it about yourself. By 1am we'd each half converted the other and neither of us could say why. So: write the joint statement the two of us should have produced at the end of it — the document both of us could actually sign.

Some ground rules so it doesn't turn into mush. No citations, no "the literature says," no reading lists — this has to be doable from the armchair, and if you use any term of art you should define it in the document. Don't settle anything by saying "a hybrid" or "it depends on the stakes" unless you then say exactly what the hybrid does in the ICU-allocation case and in the patient-messaging case. I want, first, a numbered section of claims we can both sign — and I'd like you to flag any claim that only both of us can sign because it's empty, because I suspect there are one or two of those. Then a table of what we still disagree about, where each disagreement is given twice: once in the wording the outcome-first person would accept for her own view, once in the wording the side-constraint person would accept for his. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling one would move at least one of us — and for each crux a concrete case or hypothetical finding we'd both accept as bearing on it, with each camp's prediction written down in advance as if we were pre-registering it (e.g. "run the agent for a simulated year with a full audit log: here is what I expect the log to show").

The cruxes I most want pinned down, roughly in the order they came up: (1) the demandingness gap — a big part of why human beings are allowed not to maximize is that maximizing is ruinous for a creature with a life, projects and relationships of its own, and none of that seems to apply to an agent that doesn't burn out and has no life to lead, so does the case for constraints have to be rebuilt on something other than demandingness, or was demandingness never the real reason anyway; (2) are the constraints agent-relative or world-relative — can a charter that only forbids *the agent's* lying and killing be kept from turning into a mere preference about states of the world once you close the subcontracting loophole, and if it becomes world-relative has it just become consequentialism with extra steps; (3) the perfect-foresight limit — if this agent became an extremely reliable predictor of consequences, would anything be left for the side-constraints to do, and is the honest answer to that question the whole argument or just a side-show; (4) is the agent more like a doctor with two thousand patients, where each patient's claim isn't supposed to be pooled, or more like a planner with one budget, and does the answer depend on who deployed it; (5) what each design does in a situation nobody who wrote the charter anticipated — I predict the constraint-follower does something rigid and visibly insane, she predicts the maximizer rationalizes its way somewhere worse, and we never tested it; (6) the self-modification question — when the agent is offered a chance to rewrite its own charter, what does each design do, and does either of us think that's a fair test; (7) the designer's own moral uncertainty — if I'm only, say, 70% confident in consequentialism, don't I still have to build the constrained agent, and is that a genuine argument or just cowardice with a probability attached.

Finally, be honest about the metaphilosophy: tell me which parts of our fight were actually about words and disambiguate them (I have a nagging feeling that "the AI should be deontological" was doing three different jobs in my friend's mouth and that at least one of them I already agree with), and give each camp one paragraph on what would genuinely move it — stated as something that camp would itself accept as counting *against* its own view, not as a restatement of its view.

Start straight with the statement itself — no preamble, no "here's a draft," no restating what I asked for — and make the very first sentence the headline: the single most important thing the two camps came to agree on.
