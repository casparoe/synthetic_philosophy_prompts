I run a small reading group and last week we did Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary from Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979) — the ant colony he talks to by making marks in the dirt, which has moods, learns things, forgets them, can be interrupted mid-thought. (If I'm mangaling the details of his version, never mind, just take the case as I set it out below.) The discussion was useless in a specific way: everyone produced an intuition, nobody had to say which intuition was doing the work, and we left with six people agreeing and no idea why. So I want to force it.

Here's the setup. Below are six colonies. Stipulate that in every one of them the colony's outward behaviour is exactly as rich, flexible and context-sensitive as Aunt Hillary's: same repertoire, same ability to learn from what you do to it, same everything you could ever see from outside. So you can't break the tie by pointing at behaviour — the differences are all in what's going on underneath.

1. **The original.** Just the biological colony as described: ants, pheromone trails, signals, nothing else.
2. **The one-number colony.** Everything that varies at the colony level reduces to a single global pheromone concentration. All the behavioural flexibility comes from individual ants' fixed local responses to that one number plus whatever's right in front of them.
3. **The slow swap.** Ants are replaced one at a time by synthetic replicas that reproduce the original ant's causal input/output profile in every local situation they could meet. By the end there are no biological ants left and nothing at colony level has changed.
4. **The fission colony.** It can be divided into two halves, each of which is immediately as flexible as the whole was. Put both halves in the same situation and they behave identically; there is no fact of the matter that makes one of them the continuation.
5. **The statistical colony.** It has no stable response to any single query. Whether it "says" yes or no depends on which ants happen to wander past the marks in the dirt. But over many trials there's a stable frequency of answers, and that frequency tracks the environment in a sensible way.
6. **The never-wrong colony.** It lives in an environment rigged so that every colony-level discrimination it makes succeeds. No colony-level state ever gets corrected, and no ant ever dies or goes hungry because of something the colony got wrong.

What I want:

- A score out of 100 for each, for how confident you are that there is a mind — a subject of experience, or whatever the nearest thing you'll commit to is — at the *colony* level rather than the level of the ants.
- A strict ranking, no ties, and each one assigned to a tier: dead certain / fairly confident / genuinely torn / probably not / no.
- One sentence of reasoning per case, and I mean one — the point is to see which consideration separates neighbours in the ranking.
- Tell me which single case does the most work in seperating the accounts you find plausible, and which one you'd cut from the list for telling us nothing.
- Say, in one sentence, which account of mindedness you're scoring against, and then actually stick to it. I don't want a survey of the options and I definitely don't want reading suggestions or citations — this is your ranking, not a literature review.
- And commit: where you're torn between two adjacent cases, pick one, move it, and tell me how likely you think you are to have got that particular call wrong. If you think one of my cases is confused or badly posed, say so in passing and rank it anyway.

British English please (organise, behaviour, and so on). Aim for something I can read out loud at the start of a session without it going past ten minutes, so keep it tight.
