I'm giving a forty-minute paper with half an hour of questions at a one-day workshop on institutional reform at a public policy school next week, and what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view. The thesis is this: the familiar Chesterton rule — don't tear a fence down until you know why it was put up — is best read not as a cautionary slogan and not as a dressed-up status quo bias, but as a burden-of-proof rule responding to a specific and largely irreducible kind of ignorance. The reasons a long-standing practice carries are mostly inarticulate; they accumulate by what I've been calling functional layering, where a rule introduced for one purpose quietly comes to do two others; and they are invisible above all to the people with the standing to change the rule. From that I make three moves. First, the usual satisfaction condition is too weak: being able to tell the causal story of how a practice arose does not license abolishing it, because practices accrete functions, so abolition should require a two-key showing — a genealogy plus an affirmative demonstration that no function has migrated into the practice since. Second, the part I actually care about: a novitiate requirement, on the model of apprenticing to a trade, according to which anyone arguing to abolish a practice must first spend a fixed period operating under it, because what practitioners know is not extractable by interview or survey and is transmitted, if at all, by participation. Third, what I've been calling the articulacy trap: reform arguments are articulate, the case for the existing practice is not, and any deliberative forum that trades in stated reasons will therefore systematically underweight the practice no matter how good the reasons on the other side are — so the fence is best understood as a correction for an articulacy asymmetry in the forum rather than as a claim about the wisdom of the ancestors. My worked example throughout is a hospital rule requiring the senior clinician to be physically present on the ward to sign off discharge summaries: introduced for one reason, retained for another (it is the corridor conversation, not the signature, in which medication errors get caught), and now slated for abolition on the grounds that the signing can be done electronically from anywhere.

The room will be about twenty-five people, and the composition matters more than the size. There is a political theorist who works on Burke and Oakeshott and will be broadly sympathetic, but who will press me on whether the novitiate requirement smuggles in an epistemic privilege for insiders that Burke would not have extended to just any practitioner, and on whether I have quietly turned a claim about tacit knowledge into a claim about who gets to speak. There is a liberal egalitarian philosopher who thinks this entire tradition is status quo bias in better tailoring and who will want to know what any of this says about practices that were unjust from the outset. There are three economists — two institutional, one behavioral — who will ask what would even count as evidence for functional layering, how I'd distinguish a genuine second function from a just-so story told by people whose jobs depend on the rule, and whether the novitiate requirement is anything more than an unusually expensive pilot study. There is a law-and-economics person who will try to translate the whole thing into option value and ask why I don't simply say irreversibility and stop. There is a medical director, and the discharge-summary example comes from her hospital, so she has opinions I haven't heard yet. And there are several practitioner types, one of whom spent an evening arguing to me that software engineering solved this problem decades ago: you delete the fence, you keep the history, you revert if something breaks, so Chesterton's fence is just what you get when institutions don't have version control. I expect that last one to land well with the economists, and I don't yet have a reply to it that I like.

So: give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones I'd get from a room of Burke specialists — ordered by how much damage they'd do to the three moves rather than by the order they'll arrive in, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it (I should be able to tell from the wording who is talking), each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and one line on where that reply is soft, begs the question, or should just be conceded. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes of the talk, before anyone asks anything, to take the sting out of the questions I'm certain to get — I'd rather give something up cheaply than have it taken from me. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks; my own guess is that it's some version of which of the three moves is doing the real work if the other two fail, but tell me what you think it actually is. And, because I would much rather over-prepare the wrong thing than under-prepare the right one: put a confidence number out of ten on each prediction that the objection really does come in roughly that form, and close with two or three sentences identifying the one suggested reply you are least confident would survive a follow-up question, and why. I have the literature, so no citations or reading lists — what I need is the room.
