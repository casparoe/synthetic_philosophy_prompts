I am writing a seminar paper on the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism, and a friend in the department — a utilitarian, and an unhelpfully patient one — handed me the following as a tu quoque. I am fairly confident it goes wrong somewhere, because it seems to prove far more than even a utilitarian should want, but I cannot put my finger on the step that fails, and I do not want to answer it by simply stipulating that persons, and only persons, are morally special. I would be grateful for a diagnosis of the argument rather than a survey of the literature.

(1) A gain can count as a moral reason only if it is a gain for someone, that is, only if there is some point of view from which things thereby go better. Improvements that are improvements from no one's point of view are morally inert.
(2) Persons are separate in the sense that there is no point of view which is the point of view of a collection of persons: when the sum of benefits across two lives goes up, there is no one whose life thereby goes better. The aggregate is not anyone's good.
(3) So a loss imposed on one person is not made right by a larger net gain to others. Trading magnitudes across the boundary between persons is a bookkeeping error: the cost and the benefit never appear on the same ledger, and no one stands on both sides of the trade.
(4) What makes persons separate is not some further fact, not a soul or a Cartesian ego, but the holding of certain relations: overlapping chains of memory, intention, character and causal dependence. These relations admit of degrees and can hold partially, so the relation between my earlier and my later stages differs from the relation between me and a neighbouring stage of someone else's life only in how strongly and densely it holds.
(5) If the separateness that carries the moral weight in (2) and (3) just is the holding of these relations, then a mere difference in degree cannot bear that weight, and the argument should run within a single life as well: the twenty-year-old who bears a cost and the fifty-year-old who enjoys a gain are related in exactly the way two distinct persons are, only more so.
(6) So prudence collapses along with utilitarianism. Saving for old age, sitting through chemotherapy, or accepting any small present cost for a much larger future benefit would be mere aggregation of precisely the kind (3) condemns: a benefit to one stage purchased by a cost imposed on another, with no standpoint from which the trade is an improvement.
(7) Diachronic prudence is not irrational; a view that entails otherwise has proved too much.
(8) Therefore the separateness objection either fails outright, or it must be restated so that the distinction between a person and a temporal stage of a person, rather than the degree of psychological connectedness, carries the normative load.

Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (3), and I am even willing to pay for it by giving up reductionism about personal identity and accepting that identity consists in some further fact, if that is genuinely what it takes — but I want to know the size of that bill, and whether it is really the only way to stop (5). I am also unsure how to cash out the alternative: if I weaken (3) to the claim that aggregation generates reasons that can nevertheless be defeated by claims of justice, rights, or fairness, I cannot see what distinguishes that from a utilitarianism with side-constraints, which is roughly what I was trying to argue against. If the argument instead contains a slide or an equivocation somewhere between (2) and (6), I would like it identified precisely, along with what the separateness objection looks like once the slide is removed.
