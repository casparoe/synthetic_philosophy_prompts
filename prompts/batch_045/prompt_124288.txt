I am a journalist writing a feature on cross-cultural variation in moral judgment and what that variation should do to a reader's confidence that morality is objective — the familiar territory of the WEIRD-samples critique (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic), the handful of vignettes that produce very different answers in different populations, and the argument from disagreement that philosophers built on that kind of data long before anyone ran a survey. My readers are intelligent generalists: most will have read one or two popular books on moral psychology, will have met the claim that the most-sampled populations are also the least representative ones, and may have met the idea that moral judgment tracks several distinct concerns at once rather than a single one. Almost none of them will have read a paper on it.

For the online version I want an interactive companion piece: a decision tree that takes a reader from a handful of concrete cases to a named position on what the variation means. It has to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they find attractive, so no node may ask anything like "do you think morality is objective?" or "are you a realist?" Every branch point should be a case or a claim a reader can answer without knowing where it leads. I would also like a scoring key at the end, so that the same instrument can run as a quiz.

Please include at least these splitting questions, and add three or four of your own where the logical space demands it:

1. The harmless taboo. A family butchers and eats a pet dog that was killed by a car; nobody else is affected, nobody who would be distressed finds out. Many respondents condemn it while conceding that no one was harmed. Is that a false moral belief, a non-moral disgust response misreported as moral, a correct response to a property others are insensitive to, or the right principle applied against a different background belief about what the act means?

2. Weight, not kind. Two communities both accept that helping kin matters and that fair treatment of strangers matters; they disagree about which wins when the two conflict — hiring a less qualified relative over a more qualified outsider. Is that a moral disagreement, a disagreement about non-moral facts, or no disagreement at all?

3. The false-embedding case. A community condemns a practice because its members believe the practice causes a harm invisible to outsiders. Given that the belief is false, is the condemnation mistaken, correct but for the wrong reason, or not in the business of being correct?

4. Reverse-WEIRD. Suppose the widely replicated finding is that the heavily sampled populations are also the statistical outliers. Does outlier status give a reader any reason to distrust those populations' moral judgments, any reason to trust them more (because education, literacy, or market integration might improve judgment), or no evidence either way? I want this node to actually separate positions rather than to be rhetorical.

5. The elicitation-frame test. Does a population's answer survive a change in how the question is asked — third-person (is it wrong), first-person (would you do it), reputational (what would your neighbors think of someone who did), or religious (does your tradition forbid it)? A reader who treats frame-sensitivity as evidence that there is no single stable judgment being measured should land somewhere different from a reader who treats it as a measurement problem only.

6. Necessary-looking claims that go missing. Some claim that looks to my readers like a candidate moral universal — that deliberate harm is worse than the same harm caused accidentally, or that it is wrong to punish someone for what they could not control — fails to show up reliably somewhere. Does that count against the claim, against the reliability of the responses, or against the whole practice of treating intuition as evidence?

Required leaves, under whatever names you think accurate: a robust objectivism that cheerfully accepts a large error theory about folk judgment; an objectivism that concedes deep unity only at the level of a narrow core and treats the rest as surface variation; a constructivist position on which the variation is variation in the facts that generate reasons; cultural or indexical relativism; expressivism or a similar non-cognitivism; the quietist or negative-program view that folk intuitions were never evidence in the first place, so the variation data are beside the point; and a view on which moral psychology comprises several distinct concerns whose relative weights are ecologically calibrated, so that the variation is adaptive tuning rather than error. That is seven; add leaves only where a genuine combination of answers has nowhere to go, and cap the total at fifteen.

Two things matter to me more than completeness. First, note the unoccupied cells: combinations of answers that nothing on my list occupies. For each, say in a sentence or two whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unmapped. Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot honestly separate rather than inventing a distinction to pry them apart — I would rather publish an admission that two positions converge on every case I can pose than a fake fork.

Because the piece is about how these findings get deformed in popular coverage, give each leaf a short line on uptake: what a writer holding that position may responsibly say in a headline about whether science has settled the objectivity question. Keep the whole thing to roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words plus the tree and the leaf table, in plain text — an indented or numbered structure, not a diagram I cannot reproduce. No citations, no bibliography, no reading list: I cannot check them and my readers will not follow them. Please flag briefly any term you feel you have to use that a general reader will not know.

On confidence: mark each branch you added yourself with whether the empirical claim under it is well replicated, actively contested, or something you are stipulating because I asked for it. I would rather run a tree with three admitted gaps than a smooth one that implies the data are settled. And if the framing itself is weak — if, for instance, the measured variation is too small, or too entangled with what the experimenter chose to ask about, to put any pressure at all on the objectivity question — say that plainly at the top rather than producing the instrument anyway. I can still file the piece around that.
