I work in ML infrastructure and have no philosophy training. A colleague and I have been arguing about whether a reward model fitted to human behavior can ever be said to have learned anyone's preferences. I wrote the argument below to show that it cannot, and now I think it proves too much, but I can't locate the bad step. Please tell me which step you reject, why that step looked fine when I wrote it down, and what I have to pay for rejecting it.

(1) Inverse reinforcement learning infers a reward function from behavior by finding rewards under which the observed behavior looks rational: either optimal, or probable under a Boltzmann-style model in which higher-reward behavior is more likely.

(2) For any finite body of observed behavior there are infinitely many reward functions that rationalize it perfectly. The all-zero reward makes every behavior equally optimal; and beyond that, given any reward that fits the data, one can add a term of the form γΦ(s′) − Φ(s), which cancels between the endpoints of any complete trajectory and so leaves every ranking of whole trajectories untouched, while changing the reward assigned to states and actions the demonstrator never visited.

(3) If, for every observation a learner could possibly make, some hypothesis in the class fits that observation perfectly, then no observation can discriminate among the hypotheses in that class: the posterior over the class equals the prior, the data carry no information, and what the learner outputs is only its prior re-described.

(4) The class of rewards an IRL system searches over is unrestricted, or at least as expressive as a neural network, so (2) guarantees a perfect-fitting hypothesis for every possible observation, and (3) applies.

(5) What the inference does pin down is the reward only up to the transformations in (2); and what those transformations leave free is exactly what one wanted — how the reward behaves off the observed distribution, which is precisely where a new policy optimized against the learned reward will go. So even the information that survives is not the information that matters.

(6) Therefore a reward model fitted to human behavior carries no information about human preferences, and any system trained against it is, at bottom, being optimized against the assumptions its designers built into the prior and the hypothesis class.

(7) But (1)–(4) generalize. The model class in ordinary large-scale supervised learning is far more flexible than any reward class in (4), so by (3) nothing is learned from data there either, and no trained model carries information about the process that generated its training set.

(8) That last claim is absurd. Something is learned, well or badly, and it shows up as better-than-chance performance on held-out data. So at least one of (1)–(6) is false.

The step I am least willing to give up is (2): it is constructive, I can implement it, and it is the thing that made me suspicious in the first place. Beyond that I would rather not prejudge the diagnosis. If the flaw is at (3) or (4), please say precisely what the difference is between the hypothesis class being too flexible to be falsified and the data being uninformative, since I don't have the vocabulary for that distinction and my colleague insists they are the same claim. And if (5) survives even when the argument fails, tell me how much of my original suspicion it actually supports.
