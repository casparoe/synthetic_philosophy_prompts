I sit on the nine-member advisory board that allocates our county's Community Reentry Fund — about $400,000 a year, most of it from an opioid-settlement stream, disbursed to nonprofits that work with people coming out of the county jail. I am not an academic by any stretch: I ran a regional freight operation for thirty years and got put on this board by the county commission because I know how to read a budget. But I've been reading and thinking about the religion-and-morality question on my own ever since two of this cycle's applicants forced it on me, and I'm the one who has to draft the written opinion we circulate before the vote in three weeks.

Applicant A is a faith-based mentoring program, twelve months per participant: a matched volunteer mentor, a required weekly one-to-one meeting, larger gatherings on a roughly monthly cadence, an explicit expectation that the participant attend the mentor's congregation, a signing ceremony at which the participant and mentor both read a written commitment aloud in front of the participant's family, and a rule that a mentor can be removed by the other mentors. Their self-reported numbers on re-employment and on not being reconvicted within a year are the best of anything we currently fund. Applicant B is a two-year-old secular program with what looks to me like almost the same skeleton — twelve months, matched mentor, weekly one-to-one, monthly cohort dinner — but no congregation and no prayer; instead the mentors must complete a demanding training course with two weekend intensives, they wear a jacket with the program's mark when they're on program business, they read the same kind of written commitment aloud in front of family at an induction ceremony, and they can be removed by a panel of their peer mentors. B has no outcome data yet and is asking for a one-year pilot grant. I should also tell you the board is split in a way that maps onto nothing useful: two members think funding A at all is a church-state problem, two think refusing A is culture war posturing, and the rest mostly want to split the money and go home. I don't want to split the money.

Here is what I actually can't work out, and what I want the memo to address rather than dodge. First: I can't tell whether A's better numbers have anything to do with religion as such, or whether they're mostly a recruitment artifact — A draws almost entirely out of existing congregations, so its participants arrive already embedded in a dense network of people who see them every week, know their mother, and will notice if they stop showing up, whereas B has to recruit through probation officers and a Facebook page and starts with strangers. One board member says we should fund what works and stop caring why; another says that if the mechanism is just pre-existing social embeddedness, we are paying a premium to rent a network these people already had, and the money would do more good aimed at the people who have no network at all. Second, and this is what I keep coming back to: in A's exit interviews participants very often describe their change in terms of being watched over, and a striking number of them talk about people outside the congregation in a way I would call less trusting than when they started, not more. So there may be two different things in play here — conduct that is measurably better by our metrics, and a narrowing of who counts as someone you owe anything to — and I do not know which of those our mandate is about, or whether a program that buys the first by selling the second has made anybody more moral in a sense I'd be willing to defend out loud. My own half-formed thought, which you should feel free to dismantle, is that A's practices seem to work by making commitment expensive, public, and monitored by peers, and I can't see why any of that needs the supernatural part; but I also wonder whether the supernatural part is what makes the monitoring feel inescapable — you can skip a meeting and lie about it, but you cannot step outside being watched by a god — and so whether any secular substitute really closes that gap or just relocates it somewhere we can audit. And the version of this that worries me as a trustee of public money: if that gap is real and unclosable, is a secular county like ours structurally stuck paying other people's congregations to do this work, or is there something we could build?

A few constraints, please. Do not give me citations, study names, author names, or a reading list: I have no library access and neither does most of this board, so anything I can't check will just make the memo weaker. Reason from the structure of the case and say plainly where you are guessing. Give me a bottom line I can put to a vote, with a time frame and a specific trigger for revisiting it. Give me an operational definition of what "made participants more moral" should mean for a fund like ours, and say what that definition leaves out. Give the genuinely strongest version of the case for whichever applicant you recommend against — the version that might make me change my vote, not the easy one. And on measurement: I've been toying with replacing reconviction and program completion as our main metrics, since both strike me as mostly measuring whether somebody stayed inside a system that was watching them, with some small unobserved test of honesty built into follow-up — for instance overpaying the final stipend by a known amount and seeing who reports it, or leaving something of value where a participant can return it without anyone knowing. Tell me whether that is a better or a worse proxy than what we use now, whether it is ethical for a public body to run, and precisely what it would and would not tell us.
