I work as a communications coach for engineers, and for about eight years I have been running the same two-day workshop on giving critical feedback for a Dutch engineering firm and for its Japanese partner company — the same material, translated, delivered to both sides. Three things keep happening, and I have started to think they are all one thing that I do not have a word for. (The examples below are paraphrases of things I have watched happen, not transcripts.)

First: a Dutch project lead sends a Japanese counterpart a one-line message to the effect that the specification will not work and should be changed. He means it as pure technical content, and in Utrecht it would land as pure technical content. In Nagoya it lands as a statement about the relationship — roughly, that he thinks little of them, or is picking a fight. The striking part is how hard this is to undo. The recipients knew he was Dutch, knew the Dutch are famously direct, had sat through my slide saying exactly that, and could recite it back to me afterwards; and they still flinched, and afterwards said things like 'I know it is not meant that way, but it still feels like an insult.' There is apparently a layer of meaning here that survives full common knowledge and even survives the hearer's own judgment that it is unwarranted. Second, the mirror case: a Japanese manager says of a proposal that it is a little difficult. At home that is a clear no, and everyone treats it as one; the Dutch team read it as an opening bid and replied with a counter-proposal, which was itself then heard as bulldozing. Third, the same thing inside one language: when a Dutch manager who is normally blunt suddenly wraps a routine request in apologies and hedges — sorry to bother you, might it possibly be possible to — his team assume something is seriously wrong or that a large favour is coming; and in Japanese, switching from the polite forms to the plain forms while saying the very same thing is itself an event, heard either as an offer of closeness or as a deliberate insult depending on who does it.

The pattern I think I am seeing is this: there is a dial controlling how much packaging goes around a message, each community has a resting position for that dial, and what carries meaning is not the setting but the distance from the local resting position — so the identical sentence is information-free in one place and loaded in another. A linguist friend, when I described this, muttered something about 'markedness', but what I found when I looked that up seemed to be about the shape and frequency of linguistic forms rather than about this, and it did not explain the part that interests me most, namely that the extra content arrives whether or not the speaker put it there and does not switch off when everyone knows better. That makes me doubt it is the ordinary Gricean kind of implied content, which is supposed to be speaker-intended and to evaporate when the hearer is told otherwise; and it is not just 'different cultures have different norms' either, which is a way of stopping the conversation rather than of saying what the mechanism is.

So: does this have an established name, and if it does not, can you give me a term with a definition and, more importantly, the boundaries? Specifically I want to know (a) what the nearest established notions are and exactly where each one stops short of what I have described — including whatever handles the fact that the inference is not intention-dependent and not cancelled by common knowledge; (b) whether my 'deviation from a local default' formula over-generates, because a foreign accent, a non-native grammatical slip, or an unusual word choice also deviate and also seem to carry social meaning, and if those are the same phenomenon then maybe my concept is too wide to do any work, while if they are different I need to know where the line goes; and (c) what any of this implies for the ethics of polite falsehoods. That last part is what I actually need for the workshop. If a Japanese manager's ritualised 'I will consider it' is understood by everyone involved as a refusal and by nobody as a prediction about his future mental states, it hardly seems to be a lie at all; but then, by my own formula, the same softening emitted in a low-politeness environment like the Netherlands becomes genuinely informative, and insincere softening there should count as real deception rather than as harmless social lubricant — which is a much stronger and stranger conclusion than 'white lies are fine in context'. Is that where the view leads, is it a reductio of the view, or is there a distinction I am missing between content that a context generates and content that a speaker asserts? I would rather you argue it through than send me to a reading list, since I have no easy way to chase up references from where I work.
