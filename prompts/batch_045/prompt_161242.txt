I'm the TA for an upper-division seminar on formal social choice, and I'm marking the second problem set, which covers judgment aggregation. The students have seen the three-judge contract example, the contrast between the premise-based and conclusion-based procedures, and the statement (without proof) of the result that generalises the discursive dilemma to any aggregation rule meeting certain natural conditions. I've left the answer below unmarked on purpose: I've found that I'm unreliable at noticing a confident-sounding step that doesn't actually follow, or a distinction that gets quietly collapsed mid-argument, especially when the conclusion the student reaches is the one I expected. So I'd like an independent read. Please give the answer a mark out of twenty and then justify it point by point in continuous prose, the way I would write feedback in an email to the student: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Where the reasoning is right, say so; where it goes wrong, say exactly what the mistake is and how much it should cost; and distinguish claims that are merely loose or under-explained from claims that are actually false. I've reproduced the question first so you can see what was being asked.

The question I set was this. A five-member faculty committee must make three determinations about a proposed curriculum reform: A, that the reform would improve student outcomes; B, that the reform is affordable within the current budget; and C, that the reform should be adopted. The committee's mandate requires that C hold if and only if both A and B hold. Each member records a yes-or-no judgment on all three propositions, and every member respects the mandate. (a) Show that taking the majority verdict on each proposition separately can yield a committee judgment that violates the mandate, by giving an explicit profile of five individual judgment sets, and say where the inconsistency comes from given that no member is inconsistent. (b) Suppose instead that the committee took a majority vote on C alone and derived nothing further. Would the difficulty be avoided? Say precisely what would be gained and what would be lost. (c) Is there some other aggregation rule that avoids the difficulty altogether — one that is defined for every possible profile of consistent individual judgment sets, always returns a complete and consistent collective judgment, treats all five members alike, and settles each proposition by one and the same procedure applied only to the members' judgments on that proposition? Answer yes or no and explain. Parts (a), (b) and (c) were worth six, six and eight marks.

The answer I'm grading reads as follows. (a) Here is a profile. Members 1 and 2 accept A, B and C; member 3 accepts A only; member 4 accepts B only; member 5 accepts none of them. With five members, three votes carry any proposition: A has members 1, 2 and 3; B has members 1, 2 and 4; C has only members 1 and 2, so three members reject it. The committee therefore accepts A and B while rejecting C, contrary to its own mandate. Nobody is at fault, since each of the five has recorded a fully consistent judgment set, so the inconsistency is produced by the aggregation and not by anyone's reasoning; and this is not a cycle or an intransitivity of preferences, since no ranking of alternatives appears anywhere in the setup. The pattern is unavoidable whenever A and B each attract three votes but their supporter sets do not fully overlap, because the supporters of C are exactly the members who accept both A and B; correspondingly, the paradox cannot arise when the two sets are nested one inside the other, since then the majority verdict on C is just the verdict on the smaller set and all three verdicts line up. (b) Voting on C alone avoids the difficulty entirely. A majority verdict on a single proposition cannot conflict with anything, because a single proposition imposes no constraint on itself, so the outcome is guaranteed to be consistent. What we lose is only the record of reasons: the members' judgments on A and B are not carried into the outcome, so the minutes will not explain why the reform was rejected. That is a genuine loss for transparency and for anyone wishing to appeal, but it is not a logical defect, and it is a cheap price to pay. The point is fully general: a procedure that takes majority votes on conclusions only and never on premises cannot produce inconsistent collective judgments whatever the agenda looks like, and the inconsistency in part (a) arises precisely because the premises were voted on as well. (c) No, there is no such rule. The four requirements — defined for every profile of consistent individual judgment sets, always complete and consistent, anonymous, and settling each proposition by one and the same procedure applied only to the judgments on that proposition — cannot be met together, and this is the generalisation of the discursive dilemma: propositionwise majority voting is the only rule that is anonymous and applies one and the same procedure to every proposition, since any other rule would have to single out some member as more authoritative than the rest or some proposition as more important than the rest, and part (a) shows that majority voting fails. So no rule satisfies all four, and the committee must abandon one of them; I would abandon the last, because the members' expertise is about the premises rather than the conclusion.
