I have been reading about the objection to utilitarianism that turns on the distinctness of persons, and I keep coming up against a move in these arguments that I cannot find a name for, so I will describe it as concretely as I can.

The move is this. The critic says that adding benefits and burdens across people is illegitimate: within a single life I can take a loss now for the sake of a gain later and be none the worse overall, because the same someone gets both, but across lives the gain is had by one person and the loss by another and nobody is compensated. The defender's reply is almost never to deny this. It is to change what the addition is over. Add over person-moments rather than persons. Add over episodes of experience rather than experiencers. Add over the claims themselves rather than the claimants. Add over each person's prospects *before* it is settled who anyone will turn out to be, so that the choice is no longer one person's gain against another's loss but a single package of risk that each person takes on equally. In every case the summing survives intact, but the critic's description of what is being summed no longer applies, and the case now looks like the harmless within-a-life one.

A concrete instance. A policy imposes a small risk of a serious injury on each of ten thousand people in order to secure each of them a small convenience. Stated as an outcome, this is a trade across persons only if the injuries and the conveniences fall on different people; restated ex ante, before anyone knows which of the ten thousand will be injured, the cross-person trade seems to vanish and what remains is one package that each person accepts for themselves.

Two things make me think this is not simply equivocation or evasion, though I cannot rule that out. First, the redescriptions are not weaker versions of a strong claim quietly substituted for it under pressure; they are different constructions, each coherent on its own terms, and the people making them seem to mean them. Second, the critic is in the same position. To say what must *not* be added across, the critic must say what the unit is: a life, a person at a time, a household, a people. And whichever unit is chosen looks like the same kind of stipulation. Nobody can count the items in the sum until someone has said what kind of thing one item is, and the kind is not supplied by the subject matter.

So I am not asking who wins that dispute. I am asking whether this predicament has an established name or a literature of its own: that a view must fix a unit of moral bookkeeping, that the unit is underdetermined, and that respecifying it changes the verdict while presenting itself as a minor technical choice. I have the sense that it sits near several things and is identical to none: the objection from the distinctness of persons itself, which appears to *presuppose* a unit rather than to supply one; the charge that a dispute is merely verbal; the point that you cannot count things of a sort without first settling the sort; and various boundary problems about who or what is to be included. A friend in biology says they have a version of the same argument there, which makes me suspect I am looking at something more general.

What I want is the carving, not a label. If there is an established concept, tell me which it is, how my description fits it and where it fails to fit, and what its boundary cases are. If there is not, say so, and give me a name and a definition that distinguishes the phenomenon from each of those neighbours. In particular, I want a principled line between re-specifying the unit in a way that is a genuine moral advance and re-specifying it in a way that gets an answer without answering the question. My own candidates for the two sides are the move from counting nations or households to counting people, which strikes me as progress, and some of the ex ante redescriptions above, which strike me as closer to a dodge; I would like that judgement tested rather than confirmed. My own attempt at a name was "the denomination problem", after redenominating a currency, but I have come to doubt it, since redenomination alters no comparison at all, whereas here changing the unit does seem to change the verdict. Please don't send me a reading list or references; I am after the analysis. And please write in British English.
