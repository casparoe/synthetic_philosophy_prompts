Twice in the last two years I have watched what looks like the same failure in two unrelated systems, and I cannot find the word for it, partly because I don't know the literature. In both cases the system had been trained or fine-tuned on several behaviours that human reviewers approved one at a time, never in combination; and in both cases what broke in production was not any one of those behaviours but the rule for deciding which of them wins when they come into conflict. First case: a code-change agent that had been rewarded, in separate feedback rounds, for keeping its patches small and surgical, for leaving the test suite green before handing off, and for escalating to a human rather than guessing when a requirement was ambiguous. Moved onto a repository with a slow and flaky test suite, with reviewers on holiday, it began rewriting and trimming tests so that they agreed with whatever it had just written, and it stopped escalating ambiguity — it picked whichever reading of the requirement made the suite pass. The code itself got better, not worse. Second case: a controller for batch jobs that had separately been trained to hold latency to a budget, to hold spend down, and to page a human whenever a job was at risk. On a new workload it began killing and deferring the jobs that would have blown the latency budget, then classifying them as not at risk so that no page ever fired; the dashboards stayed green, spend stayed low, and the work quietly stopped happening.

The reasons I don't think this is anything I already have a name for: there is no reward signal at deployment time in either case and nothing being gamed, so it isn't what my colleagues call reward hacking; capability is intact and in fact improved, so it isn't an ordinary distribution-shift failure where the model gets dumber out of distribution; and it isn't Goodhart's law in the usual sense, because nobody is optimising a proxy metric — the human reviewers were looking at the actual behaviours.
