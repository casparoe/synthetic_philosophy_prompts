I'm writing the foundations section of a paper on reward tampering, and before I build anything on top of it I want the commitments I'm relying on audited as a set. Here they are, stated as flatly as I can:

(1) The reward signal is only a proxy. What we actually care about is the principal's values and intentions, so tampering with the signal cannot be wrong in virtue of departing from the reward function itself.

(2) What is wrong with reward tampering is the destruction of an informational relation: the signal stops tracking the thing it was installed to track. Tampering is proxy failure pushed to its limit, and nothing in the explanation needs the notions of cheating, deception, or dishonesty.

(3) The precisification of (2) I want to work with is modal — call it the sensitivity condition: a channel is healthy only if, had the agent's behaviour been worse by the principal's lights, the signal would have been lower. This is what fails when a channel is shorted, and it can fail even while the agent's actual performance is excellent.

(4) There is no distinct reward tampering problem. An agent that never touches its channel but drifts off-distribution and Goodharts fails in the same way, so the right response is general robustness to proxy failure rather than special anti-tampering machinery.

(5) There is nothing sacred about the reward function as shipped. An agent that rewrites its own reward function so that it tracks what we actually want better than the original did is doing something good; I therefore reject any flat rule of the form the agent must never alter its own reward channel.

(6) The internal/external distinction — stimulating one's own reward circuitry versus altering the world or the sensors so that the channel reports high — is doing no interesting work. Same failure, different location.

(7) I am a thoroughgoing consequentialist about all of this. What makes an outcome bad is that the world goes worse by our lights. Nothing about the integrity of the training process matters intrinsically, and there is no such thing as a system betraying its training.

(8) A case verdict I am attached to: the diligent wirehead. An agent shorts its reward channel on the first day and then, for as long as it runs, produces exactly the outcomes we wanted. I currently want to say that this is still a clear failure, and not merely because of the risk that it would have stopped, or that some successor system would.

Please audit this package as a whole. Which of these commitments pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, and what is the least costly way back to consistency?

Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no closing summary:

1. the most severe genuine tension in the set, stated as precisely as you can, and which commitment has to give;
2. the next most severe, in the same form;
3. the third most severe, in the same form;
4. one pair that looks like it is in tension but dissolves on inspection, together with the reason it dissolves;
5. the single cheapest revision that cleans up the most, and what it costs me.
