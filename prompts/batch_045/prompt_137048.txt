I'm giving a twenty-minute talk next month at a small workshop on AI and collective epistemology, and I want to be ready for the question period. I've been making versions of this argument in blog comment sections for a couple of years and I can't tell which parts are load-bearing.

My thesis: as AI forecasting agents come to dominate prediction markets, prices will become a worse guide to the truth even as they look better — tighter spreads, deeper liquidity, apparently excellent calibration — because the no-trade theorems tell us what a genuinely shared prior does to speculative trade, and a population of models trained on overlapping corpora with overlapping objectives is closer to a common prior than any human population has been. Sketch of the argument: first, the standard way of defusing the no-trade result is to treat common priors as an unrealistic idealization, so that observed trade is explained by real disagreement; second, I claim that for AI agents the idealization is nearly satisfied, and what looks like residual disagreement between them is fine-tuning and decoding noise rather than epistemic diversity; third, so in a mixed market the human money gets steadily arbitraged away and the price converges on something close to a single model family's posterior, which is then read downstream by journalists, policy shops, and other models as the wisdom of a crowd when it is closer to one mind sampled many times; fourth, and this is the part I care most about, the market emits no internal signal of the problem, because every observable we normally use to detect good information is also what monoculture produces.

The room is about twenty-five people: mostly market-design and mechanism-design economists, several of whom have built or studied real-money prediction markets; four or five people from the AI-forecasting world who build and evaluate LLM forecasting systems; and two philosophers who work on epistemic dependence and testimony. Essentially nobody there is hostile to the claim that markets aggregate information — several have staked careers on it — but the Mechanism designers will think I am misusing their theorems and the forecasting people will think I am extrapolating from a monoculture that does not exist yet.

Give me a prep sheet rather than a defense. The eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that subgroup would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Flag what I should concede in the first two minutes, what I should refuse to concede, and which objection has genuine force that I am most tempted to dismiss as mere correlated error. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
