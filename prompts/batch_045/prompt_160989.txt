Over the past year or so I've drifted into accepting the strange-loop account of the self: the view that what each of us calls I is not an inner object, a soul, or a further fact about the world, but a self-referential symbol that a sufficiently complex physical system comes to carry in its own repertoire — a symbol whose referent is the very system that hosts it, which is produced by that system's activity and in turn feeds back into it, so that the self is a tangled hierarchy rather than a thing, and the question of what I am is answered by describing the loop's structure rather than by pointing at anything underneath it. I find it attractive mainly because of what it does with gradations: it seems to predict that selfhood comes in degrees, that it fades rather than switching off, that a person's self can be partly lodged in other people's brains, and that there is no sharp fact of the matter about when it begins. I'm now trying to write up why I hold it, and I'd rather know how exposed it is before I commit.

So: what would count against it? I want the evidence, arguments, cases and formal results that should move someone in my position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want it made explicit which of them a defender of the view would herself concede as counting against it and which she would dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding of what the view claims. Some of the things I'm worried about: (i) the bolted-on loop — imagine engineers take a system that is richly organized but has no self-representation and add a module whose sole job is to maintain, update and act on a symbol whose referent is the system as a whole, so that the symbol is causally efficacious and the system behaves exactly as if it had a self. If the reply is that this fails because the loop must be grown rather than installed, or must reach some threshold of complexity, or must be entangled with the system's other concepts, then what exactly is the extra condition, and is it specifiable independently of the intuition we were trying to explain? (ii) the split or shared loop — one system maintaining two rival self-symbols, or one self-symbol maintained jointly across two systems: what does the account predict, and is any answer it can give here a cost rather than a bonus? (iii) the question of what fixes the reference of a merely formal symbol to the whole system rather than to a subsystem or to nothing at all, especially given that self-reference in formal systems is achieved through an encoding supplied from outside; is the self-symbol's reference similarly extrinsic, and does that matter? (iv) reports of states in which the sense of I goes quiet while consciousness plainly continues — deep absorption, certain冥想-like states, drug-induced ego dissolution — and whether these are predicted by the view or are cases it cannot describe. (v) systems that now use the first-person pronoun fluently and maintain elaborate self-models while striking nearly everyone as having no self at all: how decisive is that, if at all?

A few further things I'd like folded in. First, whether the view is insulated: are clauses like sufficient complexity, the right kind of level-crossing feedback, or organically grown doing enough work that the account has become unfalsifiable, and if so whether that is a genuine defect for a claim of this kind or an acceptable feature of a metaphysical thesis. Second, what the account predicts that its rivals do not — substance dualism, animalism, psychological-continuity views, the idea that the self is simply an illusion — and whether any such differential prediction could in principle be checked rather than merely asserted. Third, an honest verdict on the consciousness question: whether defenders should treat the fact that a structural story about the self-symbol says little about why there is something it is like to be a self as outside the view's scope, as a problem it inherits from every physicalist account, or as a defect specific to it. Personally I expect to be moved most by whatever shows the view is either too cheap — generating selves wherever there is self-representation — or unable to say which loop, among several candidates in one system, is the one that is me; but tell me if those are the wrong things to weigh most heavily.
