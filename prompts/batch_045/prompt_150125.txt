I'm running a discussion group on Scanlon next month and I want to build the aggregation session around Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" instead of yet another trolley variant, but I'm worried I'm forcing it.

The thing that bugs me: as far as I remember, the stakes on the many side aren't small per-person benefits at all. The idea is that the whole cities happiness and prosperity would collapse if the child were let out — so every citizen stands to lose everything, not a trifle. But the aggregation problem people wheel Omelas out for is about summing many tiny gains against one person's serious loss, which is exactly where the individualist restriction does its work. If the per-person stakes here are enormous, isn't the story just the wrong shape to test that? Or does the fact that it's one against thousands still make it the case everyone takes it for?

The other thing is the ending. The people who walk away look like they're refusing the trade, which sounds like the contractualist verdict — but contractualism asks what the child could reasonably reject, and walking away does nothing at all for the child. Is the story quietly swapping the child's complaint for the citizens' own clean conscience? And does it matter that Le Guin writes the child as damaged and dependent, so it can't really press its own case?

I'd like to close the session with whatever the story adds that the pairwise-comparison machinery doesn't — but I'd rather hear it if the honest answer is that Le Guin is stacking the deck and this is only ever an intuition pump.
