were doing a session on sortition and anti-career-politician stuff for a political theory reading group and i want to open with Shirley Jackson's The Lottery. On paper that towns lottery is basically the reformers wish list: universal eligibility, random selection, annual rotation, a one-year non-renewable turn, no campaigns, no factions, no incumbency, nobody can make a career of it, and everybody carries the same ex ante risk of being picked — which is the usual reason given for why you'd consent to a lottery you might lose under. And its a horror story.

So read it as a thought experiment about selection by lot. Take the standard case for sortition seriously (it removes ambition and capture, it doesnt select for the kind of person who wants power, it mirrors the community descriptively) and also the old Aristotelian line that election picks aristocrats while the lot is democratic. Where does the mechanism's selling point flip — is it that the very fact that nobody chose the winner is also what lets nobody be answerable for whats done to her, so a stoning gets laundered through a fair procedure? And where should i admit the reading breaks down: Jackson isnt doing institutional design, Tessie isnt an officeholder, theres no term, no deliberation, no aftermath in which anyone is accountable. Where does the sortition lens actually illuminate the story and where does it just distort it, and is there anything the story says back to the reformers that the papers dont?
