hey — I'm a novelist, and what I need is a teaching plan, not the explanation itself.

Sunday I've got my critique group: five other novelists, coffee shop back room, maybe forty minutes before we start tearing into each other's chapters, napkins only, no whiteboard. None of them have math past high school and two of them actively resent it. But they're scary good at character psychology, which I think is going to be its own problem here.

The thing I need them to get is the single idea my new book rests on. Roughly how I understand it: my protagonist is an AI that wants to rewrite itself into a better version, and before it lets the rewrite run it wants a guarantee that the rewritten version is still safe. And apparently it can't have one. Because a reasoner that could certify its own reasoning — "if I proved it, it's true," pointed at itself — ends up certifying anything at all, including nonsense and including the claim that a bad rewrite is fine. I've seen this called the Löbian obstacle. I want the group to understand it well enough to tell me whether my third act is broken.

So plan how I explain it, and please don't explain it to me. What has to land first, in what order, and why — what depends on what. My guess is something about a certificate being checked by the very system it certifies, and about "I proved this" being a different claim from "this is true," but I don't trust my guess about the sequence.

Then: the one example that has to carry the whole thing. It should come from our world — writing, publishing, editing. My instinct is an author handing a series off to a ghostwriter who is a copy of herself, wanting a guarantee the next book is any good, with herself as the only judge. Tell me what that example has to have, and especially what it has to *avoid*, because I can feel it sliding into two things it isn't: "who checks the checker" infinite regress, and ordinary human self-doubt. If it slides into either, my group will think they already know what I'm talking about, and they'll be wrong in a way I won't notice until later.

Misconceptions, and where in the sequence to kill each one. The ones I'm expecting: that this is just Gödel incompleteness repackaged; that "smarter" fixes it (my beta readers keep saying a sufficiently advanced AI would obviously be able to check its own work); that it's really about the successor lying to it; that the answer is to stop proving things and just test the successor empirically; and the one I'm most worried about from *this* audience, which is that they'll hear a formal limit and rewrite it as a feeling — the AI's anxiety about changing itself, daddy issues, etc. There's probably one I'm not seeing.

Two wrinkles that are mine specifically, so please fold them into the plan rather than answering around them. One: my third act has the AI solving this by deliberately building a successor that reasons *more weakly*, so that it can certify it, and the characters treat that as a noble sacrifice. I've also half-heard that in a long chain of self-rewrites each generation has to accept a weaker check than the last, so the lineage sort of grinds down — that's the emotional spine of the book. Is that the right shape, and where does it belong in the sequence? Two: my AI isn't a theorem prover, it's a big learned thing that predicts and updates. It never proves anything. So why can't it just A/B test its successor, and does this obstacle have any teeth at all for an agent like that, or have I built my book on a problem that only applies to a kind of mind I'm not writing about?

Finally: one question I can ask at the end that tells me whether it landed, including what a wrong-but-confident answer would sound like, and what I should openly refuse to explain at this level (and how to say so without sounding like I'm dodging). No readings, no links — just the plan.
