hey — I need a draft memo for my committee and I'm a bit out of my dept on the conceptual bit, so bear with me while I set the whole thing up.

I sit on the Priority Setting Committee at my hospital. Once a year we get a one-off discretionary fund (this year roughly $2.6 million) to spend on things the normal budget won't cover. Our terms of reference say we should spend it so as to produce "the greatest total health benefit for our patient population", and also that we owe "fair consideration to all patients regardless of age, diagnosis, or how visible their case is". In practice we've always ranked bids by cost per QALY and funded down the list until the money ran out.

This year the two options that matter are these.

(1) A single application: a one-time gene therapy for a 6-year-old with a rare neurodegenerative condition. Cost $2.5M. Without it the team estimates about 5 QALYs — short life, very low quality weight. With it they estimate around 55: something like normal life expectancy, and they also say his post-treatment quality of life would be *better* than "full health" as our instrument scores it, because he's an unusually strong responder, so they want us to count a benefit above the scale's 1.0 ceiling. Call it $45k per QALY.

(2) The rest of the shortlist: about 260 patients at roughly $10k each — dental work under general anaesthetic, mobility aids and home adaptations, a year of home dialysis support, respite care. Each one is small, maybe 0.15 QALYs, so around 39 QALYs in total. Call it $67k per QALY.

So the total-maximising answer is (1): 55 beats 39. But (2) is better per dollar, and (1) spends the entire fund on one child while 260 people get nothing at all. One member said this is just the utility monster thing — the old objection that any rule which adds up welfare and maximises the sum will end up handing everything to whoever can absorb the most of it — and that if our terms of reference really commit us to that, so much the worse for the terms of reference.

Some other things making it messy. The family ran a very successful crowdfunding and social media campaign, and the clinical team have hinted they'll go to the press if we say no; several members think that shouldn't count at all and a couple think it obviously should. Two more applications of the same general shape are already in the pipeline for the next two rounds, so whatever we decide is a precedent, and if we fund this one and then the next one and then the next one, we'll have spent three years of the fund on three children and the small-grants end of the list will never see a cent.

What I want from you is the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should:

- recommend a concrete course of action for this cycle — fund, don't fund, or fund with conditions — with a time frame, and not deffer the actual call to "further discussion";
- say what rule we should adopt for the next two cycles, and why, given that we only ever decide one case at a time and some members think a body like ours can't really do distribution at all, only triage;
- deal with the off-the-scale question: should we ever count a benefit bigger than our quality-of-life instrument allows, and what does permitting that do to the rest of the ranking (wouldn't everyone start claiming it?);
- take the losing side seriously. If you tell me to fund the child, I need the strongest version of the monster objection answered rather than waved at. If you tell me not to, I need an answer to the member who says we were told to maximise total benefit and the total is the total.
- be readable by a finance director and a paediatrician who don't care about philosophy. No jargon for its own sake, and where some technical distinction is doing real work in your argument, flag it in plain terms.

Don't bother with citations or a reading list — none of us are going to recieve them well or look them up. Just give me the argument and the bottom line.
