Hey, I'm in an informal reading group — four of us, we meet at a bar once a week, and we've been chewing on dispositions for the last few weeks — and I keep losing an argument I think I should be winning, so I want to practice it against someone who actually knows the moves. Please play a metaphysician who is stubbornly loyal to the conditional analysis of dispositions: you think that when we say a glass is fragile or a pill is soluble, we are saying something about what would, or might, happen to the thing in the relevant counterfactual situations, and you treat the famous family of counterexamples — finks that switch the disposition off just when the stimulus arrives, masks that block the manifestation from outside, mimics that produce the manifestation by the wrong route — as engineering problems for that idea rather than refutations of it. You're free to add machinery as we go: hold the intrinsic basis fixed in the nearest worlds, require that the response issue from that basis rather than from some implanted gadget, weaken would to might, whatever you need. You may concede that a particular formulation is dead. You will not concede that dispositions have to be identified with their categorical bases, or that the counterfactual approach should be scrapped wholesale. Stay in character, keep each reply to a short paragraph or two, argue in your own words rather than naming papers or telling me what to go read (I've got no library access out here and I don't want a reading list), and don't be a pushover — if one of my cases is just an old counterexample wearing a hat, say exactly which one and why.

Here's my opening salvo. Two vases. Vase one is an ordinary piece of porcelain, genuinely fragile in the boring sense. It sits on a museum plinth wired to a stabilizer: the plinth listens for the acoustic signature of a crack starting to propagate through porcelain, and the moment it hears one it floods the vase with resin, so the crack stops and the vase never breaks, no matter how hard you hit it. Vase two is molded out of some tough polymer that shruggs off any blow you'd care to land on it — except that someone has embedded a tiny charge in its base that detonates on impact, so that when you whack it, it comes apart in pieces just like the porcelain would. My claims: vase two is not fragile, even though it reliably shatters when struck, and vase one is fragile, even though it reliably fails to shatter when struck.

What I want from you is this: I think those two cases pull your machinery in opposite directions and that you can't have both. To get vase two right you have to insist that the shattering flow from the vase's own basis rather than from an implanted gadget — fine, I'll grant you that clause. But vase one never produces any shattering for that clause to evaluate, so to rescue it you have to either look past the masker to what the basis would do on its own, or count the crack's starting as a partial manifestation. Pick one and defend it against the other case, not just against mine. And whichever you pick, tell me whether your answer changes if I point out that the only reason the plinth's detector ever fires is that it is listening for fragility: it is, in a perfectly ordinary sense, a fragility detector, and it seems strange to me that the most reliable evidence that a thing is fragile should be exactly the thing that stops you from calling it fragile.
