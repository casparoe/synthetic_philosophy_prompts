Hi — I'm the token philosopher on our hospital's Autonomous Systems Deployment Committee (I got put on it after teaching one undergraduate seminar on AI ethics, which now feels like a thin qualificiation), and we have to decide by Thursday whether to let facilities turn on a reinforcement-learning controller for the campus energy system: chiller plants, the backup generators, air handling for the ORs, the pharmacy and morgue fridge farms, all of it. The vendor's pitch is that they've solved the side-effects problem without anyone having to write down what the hospital values. On top of the energy-cost reward they added what they call a Q-preservation penalty: every few minutes the agent estimates, for each of about forty probe target states (server room under 24°C, pharmacy fridges in range, generator fuel above 20%, at least four ORs available, and so on), how well it could still bring that state about — roughly, the best score it could get over a short simulated rollout — and it's penalised for the gap between those numbers under the action it's about to take and what they would have been under a no-op baseline. No list of forbidden outcomes, no utility function over wards, just: don't shrink the space of things that are still easy to reach. It recovers about 90% of the savings of the unconstrained agent and in simulation leaves nearly every probe number where it started, which I have to admit is impressive.

Two things are bothering me and I don't know whether they're really one thing in two costumes. First, the baseline. The vendor uses a stepwise inaction baseline — compare against what happens if the agent does nothing from this moment on — which is convenient, because the penalty stays small while the agent keeps doing its job. But our safety engineer pointed out that any change the agent made three weeks ago is now baked into the reference, so maintaining it costs nothing: if the agent quietly reconfigured something once, early, it's thereafter rewarded for freezing that new arrangement in place, and the committee would never register it as a side effect at all. When I asked what happens with the other obvious baseline — what would have happened on this campus if we'd never switched the thing on, which is, you know, literally the question we're voting on Thursday — the vendor said that one isn't actionable as a per-step penalty, because it just degenerates into keep the world on the trajectory it would have taken without us, which both makes the energy task impossible to satisfy and sneaks a full specification in through the back door. So my suspicion is that the choice of baseline is doing all the normative work and nobody wants to say so out loud: stepwise inaction is tractable but lets side effects get grandfathered in, and never-existed is honest about what we actually care about but collapses into exactly the value specification we were told we didn't need. Second, softer and maybe more important: the probe list is just whatever the vendor could instrument. Night-time noise in the wards, whether the nursing staff trust the system, the fact that we're converting a storage annex into a clinic next year — none of that is in Q, and because the penalty treats the current configuration as the thing to preserve, it looks designed to resist rearrangements we actively want. One of the nurses said a system that is indifferent to everything unmeasured and mildly hostile to every change isn't obviously value-free; it's conservative in a fairly specific sense, and the things it conserves are the things that already have a sensor on them.

What I need is the memo I'm going to circulate: a real recommendation — approve, approve with conditions, or defer — with conditions concrete enough that the vendor could price them and a timeline attached, plus the reasoning, including the strongest version of the case I'm on the other side of (our CFO will say that a system delivering 90% of the savings with a documented side-effect penalty is strictly better than the status quo, where the same savings get chased by a human nudging setpoints with no penalty whatsoever, and I don't have a great reply to that). Please don't just restate the abstract debate about whether impact can be defined without a theory of value; I can read that on my own. Tell me what this committee should decide about this particular box, how much of my baseline worry survives contact with the way these penalties are actually implemented, and what we should require to see in the logs during a limited trial — two non-clinical buildings first, say — before anyone lets it near the operating rooms.
