My friend — a solid-state chemist — and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether chemistry has been reduced to quantum mechanics, and we got exactly nowhere, partly because I came to think we were using "reduced" to mean three different things and "emergent" to mean four. Rather than ask you to settle the general question, I'd like you to work a single case we both agreed we knew: the quantum-mechanical explanation of the periodic table.

The episode, as I'll stipulate it (correct me only where a fact is actually wrong, not where you'd phrase it differently): Mendeleev's table of the 1860s ordered the elements by atomic weight, left gaps for elements not yet found, and predicted their properties; later X-ray work established that the true ordering parameter is nuclear charge rather than weight; and once quantum mechanics arrived, the row lengths (2, 8, 18, 32) fell out of the shell structure of atomic orbitals together with the exclusion principle for fermions. The clean version is also partly a textbook artifact: the simple filling-order rule has familiar exceptions (chromium and copper being the standard ones, with the lanthanides and actinides messier still), ground states of heavier atoms are in practice obtained by heavy numerical approximation rather than derived in closed form, and for the heaviest elements relativistic corrections visibly shift the chemistry.

Please analyze this case through reduction and emergence, using the following distinctions, which I'm defining myself rather than borrowing from anyone:

1. Derivational reduction: the higher-level generalization can be recovered from the lower-level theory plus bridge principles, at least to good approximation.
2. Predictive surrogacy: for any question the higher-level theory can answer about a system, a lower-level model could answer it too without using higher-level vocabulary.
3. Explanatory priority: the lower-level story is the one you would give when asked why the higher-level pattern holds, so that the higher-level story is description rather than explanation.
4. Reverse constraint: the historical datum that the higher-level pattern was in hand first and helped fix the lower-level theory — the gaps did real evidential work, and spectroscopic regularities were evidence for shell structure before they were consequences of it. Does reduction as standardly understood have room for this direction of flow, or does the bottom-up asymmetry carry genuine weight in the concept?

Then let the case push back on the framework. In particular: is the periodic table emergent in any sense worth caring about? Separate at least these candidates — emergent as not derivable in closed form; emergent as multiply realizable, i.e. insensitive to which lower-level details realize it; emergent as requiring vocabulary that does not translate term by term; emergent as involving novel causal powers — and say which hold, which fail, and what would have to be true for the strongest candidate to hold. Does the fact that the pattern seems to follow from little more than a Coulomb potential, Fermi statistics, and three dimensions make it a paradigm of reduction, or does that very insensitivity to microphysical detail make it a paradigm of the autonomy that anti-reductionists care about — and are those two verdicts possibly compatible?

My friend's line was that in-principle computability settles it and the exceptions are just numerical untidiness. Mine was that the exceptions, and the fact that chemists still get predictive work out of molecular-orbital and electronegativity talk that is not part of the fundamental description at all, show something the reduction/emergence vocabulary doesn't reach. Tell me who is more nearly right, and where each of us is asking the framework a question it has no answer to.

Be disciplined about the mapping: say what the reducing and reduced items are here — laws, models, or something else — what counts as a bridge principle, and which features of the episode the framework genuinely explains rather than merely restates in new words. Don't give me a reading list, citations, or an account of who holds which position; I've read the standard material and I want the argument. Finally, attach a confidence to each of your main claims, and single out the one you are least sure of, ideally with a note on what sort of evidence would settle it.
