Hi — I'm one of five people on the commemoration review panel my city set up after the last statue fight. We get maybe ten or twelve referrals a year: a statue, a school name, a couple of streets, a mural, someone's name on a park bench. We meet once a month, we get about two weeks per case, no research staff, and whatever we write goes to council (who nearly always rubber-stamp it) and sometimes to the local paper. So I need something we can actually run.

What I want is a decision procedure, not a set of principles. Ordered questions, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, the points where the procedure just stops and says leave it alone, and the points where it has to stop and hand the call over to council because it's a values question and not a factual one.

Here's the half-formed theory I keep coming back to, which I want you to operationalize (correct it if it's wrong): a commemoration does three separable things — it honors (ranks someone as deserving public esteem), it testifies (asserts something about what happened), and it occupies (takes up shared space, or, in the case of a street or school name, gets said out loud by everyone who lives there, on every form and every 911 call, whether they want to say it or not). And the disqualifying thing about a person is either load-bearing — part of, or a causal input into, the thing they're being honored for — or merely incidental, something else that also happens to be true of them.

Things it has to handle: what our evidence standard is for a claim about someone long dead (we are not convicting anybody); the remedy ladder from plaque through co-naming, relocation and removal, and which rung each finding buys you; and whether we are revoking an honor for misconduct versus registering that the reason for it has expired, because I think those need different arguments and I can never keep them straight in the meeting.

Then run it on three: (1) a statue of a nineteenth-century mill owner who endowed the public library and the hospital, where the fortune came from a labor system that used children and the endowment is downstream of that; (2) a street named for a much-loved local organizer where the disqualifying thing is a private letter containing a slur, found in an archive last year; (3) a school named after the city's first Black councilman, honored for that, who also, on the record, voted against a fair-housing ordinance. At least one of these should come out in a way I definately won't like — I'd rather find that out here than at the council meeting. And tell me honestly what the procedure can't do.
