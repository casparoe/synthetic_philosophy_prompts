I am writing a paper on whether fanaticism is a problem for expected-value maximization in practice rather than merely in principle, and I want to use the decision to bring the Large Hadron Collider into operation as my central case. Do not give me a survey of the literature or a map of positions with names attached; I have that. I want the case worked, in both directions: what the fanatic should say the decision-makers faced and should have done, and what the case in turn does to the theory.

The facts as I take them to be, which you should treat as given rather than as something to check: the machine was built by CERN near Geneva, in a tunnel beneath the French–Swiss border, to collide protons at energies no terrestrial accelerator had reached. Before and during start-up, a small number of physicists and commentators raised the possibility that collision products — a microscopic black hole that failed to evaporate, or a strangelet capable of catalyzing the conversion of ordinary matter — could destroy the planet. CERN commissioned safety reviews, one in 2003 and a further review in 2008, which concluded that there was no danger. The arguments were of two kinds: theoretical grounds for thinking that any microscopic black hole would evaporate harmlessly, and an observational argument to the effect that collisions at far higher energies than the collider can produce occur continuously and naturally, when cosmic rays strike the Earth and other astronomical bodies, and have done so for billions of years without catastrophe. The machine first circulated beams in 2008 and then suffered a magnet failure; the first collisions came in 2010 at 3.5 TeV per beam, roughly four times the previous record; it was shut down for upgrade between 2013 and 2015 and afterwards reached 6.5 TeV per beam.

What I want from you is, first, a disciplined mapping. Identify the agent and say why the identification is itself contested: the decision-maker was an international organization and its member-state funders, the beneficiaries were mainly a scientific community, and the risk-bearers were, on the worst-case scenario, everyone alive and everyone who would otherwise have lived. Set out the actual option set — not to run; to run and ramp up through increasing energies; to run at design energy; to defer pending further review — and say what the fanatic's decision rule returns for each, and at what probability of catastrophe the ranking flips. Then say where the mapping breaks: which features of the episode the expected-value frame captures, and which it does not represent at all.

Second, four problems that I think this case presses on fanaticism specifically, and on which I want you to take a position rather than to catalogue options.

The first concerns the status of the probability bound. The cosmic-ray argument is usually presented as ordinary empirical evidence that the risk is negligible. But the outcome at issue is an extinction-type outcome, and the evidence is that no catastrophe has been observed — evidence available only to observers who survived. Is the argument therefore an observational-selection argument rather than a frequency argument, and does a selection-derived bound do the same work for an expected-value calculation that an empirically measured one does? If not, what remains of the safety case?

The second concerns learning by doing. If the agent assigns any nonzero probability to catastrophe from a collision, then running the machine at any energy, including an energy chosen in order to learn whether higher energies are safe, carries an expected cost dominated by the catastrophic term. The ramping procedure — run low, learn, run higher — appears to be exactly what a fanatic should refuse. Yet ramping is what was done, and it is what any sensible institution would do. Is this a reductio of fanaticism as a decision procedure even for someone who accepts it as a criterion of rightness, or is there a reading on which the low-energy run was held to carry probability zero and so posed no fanatic problem? Say which, and defend it.

The third concerns where the burden fell. The episode suggests the practical response to a fanatical worry is not to do an expected-value sum but to press the science hard enough to turn a small positive probability into a structural zero. Assess this as a general strategy: how often is a structural zero available, and what should a fanatic do when it is not? Distinguish uncertainty about parameters within a physical model from uncertainty about whether the model is right, and tell me whether fanaticism's bite differs between the two and where this case sits.

The fourth concerns imposition. Fanaticism as usually stated has no place for the difference between a risk I run for myself and a risk I impose on people who did not consent to it, nor for the fact that the disvalue here is aggregated across billions of risk-bearers while the benefit accrues to others. Is the needed supplement — a consent or legitimacy condition — coherent as a modification of fanaticism, or is it an independent side constraint that reveals the view to be silent on the question a case like this actually turns on?

Third, the verdict on the theory. I can see three readings and I want you to adjudicate among them: that the case confirms fanaticism, since the participants behaved as though only a zero probability would settle the matter and were willing to delay and to commission reviews at all; that it undermines fanaticism, since no expected-value calculation was ever performed and the reasoning was qualitative and structural; or that it never tests fanaticism, because the probability was argued to zero and so the fanatical term never entered. If the third, say what kind of case would test it. And if some other model of the decision — precautionary reasoning, a lexical threshold, robustness or satisficing under model uncertainty, or a political-legitimacy account of who may impose catastrophic risk on whom — explains the episode better, say so and say what it explains that the fanatical frame does not.

Finally, be explicit about the limits. State the assumptions you need about the size of the stakes — in particular, whether the loss of all future human value is to be treated as finite, and what happens to the case if it is not. State whether you are treating the epistemic benefits of the experiment as commensurable with prudential ones, and flag if fanaticism here silently presupposes that they are. And mark every point at which your analysis depends on a fact I have supplied rather than on the structure of the argument. I would rather have a short, exact account with honest gaps than a long one that forces the theory to speak where it is silent.
