I am a retired experimental physicist who has been reading my way into formal epistemology, and I have become stuck on a point that I suspect is more delicate than the textbook presentations admit. Please take the role of a formal epistemologist working on the pragmatic vindications of probabilism, and think the problem through properly before you write: open with a short section headed Scratch work, in which you list the assumptions your explanation will depend on and mark any you think are doing hidden work, then give the explanation itself in 700 to 900 words, defining technical vocabulary as you introduce it.

Here is the set-up. In the version I have been reading: an agent assigns degrees of belief to the members of a finite partition; a bookie is assumed to know them; the agent is required to accept either side of any bet priced at her own fair odds; and the bookie may choose how many contracts to write and how large to make them. Two theorems then go through: if the degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, some finite package of bets guarantees a net loss whatever happens, and if they satisfy the axioms no such package exists. I follow all of that.

What I want explained is what survives once two familiar frictions are put back in. The agent's liability is bounded, there is a ceiling on the stake per contract, and each contract bought or written carries a fixed charge, so an exploitative strategy must overcome the charges as well as the discrepancy in odds. Exploitability then looks graded and thresholded rather than all-or-nothing: only some incoherences can be converted into a guaranteed loss. Please explain:

(a) Whether the argument, so modified, vindicates the probability axioms at all, or only something like approximate satisfaction of them. If the latter, approximate in what sense: is there a privileged measure of distance from coherence that the argument singles out, or does the threshold depend on details of the bookie's strategy, such as the number of contracts used, so that there is no single number answering how incoherent one must be before one is exploitable?

(b) Whether the modified argument can rank different kinds of departure from the axioms, for instance a small violation of finite additivity smeared across many cells of the partition, one large violation concentrated in a single cell, and a degree of belief outside the unit interval; or whether it collapses them into a single quantity, the largest guaranteed loss per unit of capital, and so treats them as equally serious whenever they are equally exploitable.

(c) Whether this shows that vulnerability to a sure-loss package was never the substance of the argument but only a vivid way of detecting something else, namely that credences violating the axioms cannot jointly guide action. If it is only a detector, is there a formulation of the vindication that the frictions leave untouched? Add a sentence or two on whether the diachronic argument for updating by conditioning is more or less exposed to the same treatment, given that there the bookie's strategy must span time as well as states.

Where my set-up has smuggled in an assumption, such as the bookie's knowledge of my credences, the obligation to take either side of a bet at my own odds, or the linear scaling of pay-off with stake size, say so explicitly rather than quietly repairing it. Quantitative illustrations are welcome; I am at home with error budgets and significant figures. Please do not hand me a reading list or a set of references. I want the reasoning.
