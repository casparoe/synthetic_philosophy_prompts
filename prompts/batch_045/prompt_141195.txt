Write a short essay on the following controversy, reaching a definite verdict and defending it.

The case. In the familiar teletransporter thought experiment, a machine scans your brain and body, destroys the original, and builds an exact replica on Mars. Those who hold that this preserves what matters in survival usually say it does so because the replica is psychologically continuous and strongly connected with you — overlapping memories, character, desires and ongoing projects — even though it is arguably not the same person. Now consider a variant I will call the relay. The direct link to Mars is broken, and the only available route is a chain of fifty short hops through relay stations. The scanner at each station is slightly lossy: each hop produces a replica that overlaps with its immediate predecessor to roughly the degree to which you overlap with yourself after an ordinary night's sleep, but the drift accumulates. The person who finally steps out on Mars is psychologically continuous with you in the sense that there is an unbroken chain of overlapping stages, and there is never more than one replica at a time — the previous stage is destroyed as each new one is built. At the far end of the chain, however, they share with you very little: most of your specific memories are gone or blurred into generalities, many of your desires and projects have been replaced by ones you would not now endorse, and no one who knew you would recognise them as you. They are nevertheless healthy, psychologically rich and contented, and they retain a faint but genuine sense of having been you.

The question. Does the person who arrives on Mars have what matters in survival of the person who left Earth? Argue for one answer, and in doing so take up three further points: first, whether a reductionist who says that personal identity is not what matters is thereby committed to saying the relay costs you nothing at all, or can instead hold that what matters comes in degrees and has been thinned by the accumulation of drift; second, whether the fact that every individual link in the chain is a change no worse than a night's sleep does any real work, given that the end state is not; and third, how your account handles a pruned branch — suppose that at hop twenty-five two replicas are built from a single scan, one of them is destroyed a minute later, and the survivor continues to Mars.

Requirements. Reason only from the case as described. Do not cite, quote or appeal to published works, and do not compare the case to any real or imagined technology beyond what is set out above. Write in British English. Give your answer as exactly five numbered points and nothing else — no title, no preamble, and no summary of what you have argued. Each point should be a single paragraph of three to five sentences, and the five should read in order as one continuous argument. Point 1 should state your verdict plainly. Point 2 and point 3 should deal with the matter of accumulating drift and with the pruned branch respectively. Point 4 should confront the strongest objection to your verdict. Point 5 should say, on the basis of that verdict, what the person on Earth should do — whether to take the relay rather than decline it — and how much prudential concern, anticipation or fear it is reasonable for them to have now about the person who arrives. Do not end on a non-committal note.
