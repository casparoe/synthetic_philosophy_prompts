Keynes died in 1946, so he never saw any of this, but I want you to reconstruct how he'd have reacted to the modern Kelly betting / geometric rationality cluster of ideas.

Quick setup in case it helps: the Kelly criterion (Kelly, Bell Labs, 1956) says size each bet to maximize the expected log of your bankroll, which is the same as maximizing its long-run geometric growth rate. The stronger, more recent claim -- sometimes called geometric rationality -- is that this isn't a taste or a risk preference at all: for a single agent compounding through time, maximizing expected wealth is simply the wrong objective, and log growth (or something close to it) is what rationality requires, because wealth is non-ergodic and the time average is what you actually live.

Two wrinkles I want you to actually engage with, since neither is something he could have read:

1. The hydra fund. Suppose you can spin off N independent copies of yourself, each betting 1/N of the bankroll, and then merge the copies back at the end of every period by simply adding up the money. Pooled wealth now grows at something close to the arithmetic expected return, and full-on expected-value maximization beats Kelly. So is the whole case for geometric rationality defused by an institutional trick, or does it just relocate the question to which entity's continued existence you're supposed to care about -- the pool, or any one of the branches, most of which end up with nothing? I suspect Keynes would have a distinctive and fairly sharp thing to say here, and I want you to say it for him.

2. Model uncertainty in the era of algorithms. Kelly needs real numbers: precise probabilities, known payoffs. Keynes's 1921 book on probability insists that probabilities are logical relations that are frequently non-numerical, incomparable, and carried by a weight of evidence that is partly conventional -- and the General Theory's beauty-contest material says the relevant question isn't what you think but what you think others think. Now hand him a modern systematic fund or an automated agent placing thousands of correlated bets, where the probabilities come out of a model that was fit on data that may not describe tomorrow at all. What does Kelly become once the inputs aren't really probabilities in his sense?

What I actually want is a committed reconstruction, not a survey. Would he have accepted log growth as a requirement of rationality rather than a preference? Give me a number out of 100 for how likely you think he'd have signed on. What's the strongest objection he could mount using his own machinery -- weight of argument, incomparable probabilities, convention, liquidity, the fact that he ran real money for his Cambridge college and liked concentrated positions -- and does it genuinely bite against a 1956 result, or does it only bite against how people apply it? Then give the strongest reply from the geometric rationality side and tell me whether he'd have found it decisive or merely clever. And finally: in a real market where you have a genuine edge but your probabilities are shaky and partly conventional in his sense, what fraction of full Kelly do you think he'd actually bet -- and defend that specific number rather than hedging with a range.
