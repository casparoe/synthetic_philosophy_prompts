ok so context: i've been messing around (badly) with a little program that spits out alphabets on a coarse grid — same basic idea as that old Letter Spirit project: you've got a gridfont, each glyph is a bunch of gridpoints, and the dream is that the thing produces a whole alphabet where every glyph is unmistakably *its* letter while all twenty-six of them also share one "look". What I keep getting stuck on, and what I'd like you to actually explain rather than just tell me is hard, is the asymmetry the setup seems to assume. Letter identity (a-ness, g-ness) is treated as given: fixed, top-down, the categories are just handed to the program. Style is the thing that's supposed to *emerge*, bottom-up, out of the program's own activity. But those are the same kind of sameness — a sameness holding across different concrete instances — and they get completely different treatment. Why? Is that asymmetry defensible, or is it just an artifact of the fact that whoever builds such a thing already knows the alphabet and doesn't yet know the font?

Here's the part I really want you to get at. Think about the single-story a versus the double-story a. They share almost no part-structure: one is a bowl plus a stem, the other is a bowl plus a curl with no stem at all. If a-ness is a set of relational roles that get filled in by parts, then these two instantiate different role-structures — so either a-ness is a disjunction of two unrelated skeletons, or one of them isn't really an a. But it isn't a *style* difference either, not in the ordinary sense: Futura's a and Garamond's a differ in style, fine, but the one-story/two-story choice doesn't vary inside a single typeface, it's settled once for the whole design — while it also obviously isn't forced by what an a has to be. I've started calling these skeleton choices (no idea whether there's a real term for them): too coarse-grained to be style, too specific to be essence.

So: (1) explain, intuitively, what kind of thing a skeleton choice is and why a two-axis picture — essence here, style there — has nowhere to put it; (2) say what it would take to represent a-ness in a program so that both a's come out as a's without just hardcoding two templates and OR-ing them; and (3), the bit I actually care about: once you let skeleton choices in, does "essence" just become a slower-moving style? Does the asymmetry collapse, and does the whole project loose the thing that made it interesting in the first place? And if you think it doesn't collapse, say what's still holding the line.

Keep it gut-level, maybe 700 words, and please don't cite anything or point me at papers — I want the shape of the problem, not a reading list.
