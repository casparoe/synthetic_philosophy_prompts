My sister and I run a small farm together that we inherited. Everything outside our two bedrooms is shared — the land, the garden, the accounts. I want to keep bees. She is badly frightened of them. There are three options: no hives (N); I keep hives (M); she keeps hives (S).

Her ranking: no hives is best. If there must be hives, she would rather keep them herself than let me keep them, because then she can put them at the far edge of the property and keep them out of the garden, whereas I would put them behind the house. So for her: N best, then S, then M.

My ranking: I would be happiest if she kept them — she would get over the fear and I would not have to do the work — then me keeping them, then no hives at all. So for me: S best, then M, then N.

An economist friend of ours says the fair rule is obvious: each of us decides what happens in her own sphere, and where everyone agrees on a ranking, the farm follows it. Both of us accept that. But when I write out what it actually delivers, I get this: my sphere settles M against N, and I choose M; her sphere settles N against S, and she chooses N; and since both of us prefer S to M, the farm prefers S to M. Those three cannot all hold at once. Whichever two options we put to a vote first seems to determine where we end up, and any outcome we land on can then be attacked as having ignored either someone's say over her own business or something all of us agreed on.

I keep wanting to say that it is my call; she keeps saying that the hives would be on land she lives on. I cannot tell whether one of us is making a mistake. We are drafting a one-page farm agreement this weekend, and we can also compensate each other out of the farm account, so I need something concrete rather than a theory.

1. Is there any rule that gives each of us a say over our own sphere and follows unanimous preferences, or is our situation one where those two demands genuinely cannot both be met? If it is the latter, please show it rather than assert it, and tell me whether this depends on there being only three options, two people, or no side payments.

2. My sister says my preference that she keep the hives is none of my business and should not count, while her fear is real — her daughter is allergic, and the hives would be on land we share. Does that distinction (busybodying versus being genuinely affected) rescue the rule, and how is it supposed to be applied when the two "spheres" physically overlap?

3. She has offered a deal: hives stay only if I take full liability and move them to the far field, and she may remove them if I stop maintaining them. We could also pay each other compensation. Does allowing deals and payments dissolve the problem, or does the same difficulty reappear one level up?

4. What should actually go in the one-page agreement? If the honest answer is that we must pick a tie-breaker — a coin flip, a status-quo rule, a rotating agenda-setter — is that a principled choice or just a shrug? I would rather commit to something admittedly second-best than sign a rule that sounds principled and deadlocks in October.
