So I've been going in circles on Pascal's mugging again, and instead of re-reading the usual posts I want to try the thing where I write down my actual gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and see whether anything principled falls out. Here they are, roughly in the order I thought of them.

1. Standard one. Guy in a hoodie, zero evidence, says hand over five dollars or he'll spin up 3^^^^3 simulations of a person being tortured for a year. I don't pay, and it doesn't feel close.

2. The retro version, which I haven't seen discussed: he says he's *already* started the run a minute ago, it's going right now, five dollars and he aborts it. No threat, no credibility incentive, no deterrent value in paying. I think I pay. That's embarrassing given case 1 and I don't know what to do with it.

3. The mechanism version: he shows me a rack of hardware, and a working demo — one simulated mind, in agony, verified by an expert I actually trust — and says the full run costs him about twenty bucks of electricity. Now I pay, happily, and I'd pay a lot more than five dollars.

4. The track-record version: documented history of a thousand threats, each small (ten lives), non-payers harmed maybe ten percent of the time. Now he threatens 3^^^^3. I pay, but I notice I'm implicitly assuming that a track record at the ten-lives scale licenses something about the 3^^^^3 scale, and I don't know why it would.

5. The zero-cost version: he says just say the word "glorp" out loud and he won't run 10^15 lives for a year, and my honest probability on him is like 1 in 10^20. I say glorp. Obviously. Even though the numbers are exactly as silly as case 1.

6. Many muggers: 10^9 of them, mutually inconsistent cosmologies, each wants five bucks. I pay nobody. Paying the single most probable one also feels wrong.

7. The meta mugger: pay me or I'll create 3^^^^3 muggers who will each do the 3^^^^3 thing. This is strictly astronomically worse than case 1 and it strikes me as strictly *sillier*. I don't pay and I laugh.

8. Pascal's blessing instead of the wager: pay me five dollars and I'll give you a one-in-a-billion shot at creating 3^^^^3 happy lives. I don't pay, and I notice my reason is different from case 1 — with the threat he has some incentive to follow through, with the blessing he has none and just pockets the cash.

9. The boring respectable version: five dollars buys roughly a one-in-a-million chance of averting a catastrophe that kills 10^11 people, numbers from a model someone else can check, mechanism understood. I pay, obviously. And this is the pair that actually keeps me up: 1 and 9 have the same shape and I go opposite ways.

10. Lottery ticket, one in 300 million for 300 million dollars. I don't buy one and I also don't think buying one is insane. Feels like a case where expected value is simply the wrong tool in both directions and nobody minds.

11. The one I made up and like: he says forget the threat, pay me five dollars and I'll hand over the complete design so that anyone can check whether runs of that size are possible at all. If it checks out, everyone knows; if it doesn't, everyone stops wasting money on muggers. I pay, but for reasons that have nothing to do with his threat — it's the option value of settling the question for everyone, once. Which makes me wonder whether my "no" in case 1 is secretly a claim about the value of information rather than about tiny probabilities.

12. Policy version: instead of asking for five dollars he says *adopt the general policy of paying anyone who makes a claim of this shape*, or else. Refusing feels even more obvious here than in case 1, even though naively it dominates.

My own stab, which I'm fairly sure is broken: the number I should use isn't the probability he states, it's my prior on "a stranger makes a claim of size N," and that prior has to decay at least about as fast as 1/N in log space, so the expected value of a claim stays bounded even when the payoff doesn't. But that seems to kill case 9 along with case 1, and it doesn't explain 1 vs 2 at all, and it says nothing about why 7 is funnier than 1.

What I want: two or three candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply one to a case you haven't seen, with the clause-by-clause "this clause is here because of case N." Then tell me which of my verdicts each one has to overturn, which pair it thinks I'm most confused about, and what it predicts for a couple of cases I didn't list — say, a mugger who demands your vote rather than your money, or one who offers to call off the run if you stop donating to the thing in case 9. Please don't give me a reading list, I've read the obvious stuff; I want the principle, and an honest note about where it breaks.
