Two definitions first, so we're talking about the same thing. Call a signal erosive if learning it removes at least one act from the set of things you can do afterwards — you can't un-know it, you can't honestly testify that you don't know, you can't wholehearted attempt something you know will fail, you can't any longer be the sort of thing someone else can safely tell a secret to. Call a signal preference-perturbing if, once you've learned it, you stop wanting what you wanted before.

(1) Good's theorem: for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer with a fixed utility function and a fixed feasible set, the ex ante expected value of a costless signal is never negative. The proof is two lines: the max over acts after conditioning, averaged over the cells of the partition, is at least as big as the unconditional max; equivalently, you can always just ignore the signal and do whatever you'd have done anyway.

(2) Nothing in that proof mentions the signal's content. It uses only: the signal partitions the state space, you choose after seeing your cell, you re-maximize. So it holds for any content whatsoever — spoilers, bad medical news, facts about your own future tastes, facts about yourself.

(3) Refinement: any refinement of a partition is worth at least as much as the coarse one (Blackwell ordering — I think that's the name), so the finest partition is weakly best and omniscience is never worse than anything else.

(4) Now handle my two hard cases by enlarging the state space. States can include which utility function you'll end up with and which acts will still be on the menu afterwards. Index utility by state. Then the same proof goes through unchanged: whatever the signal does to your tastes or to your menu was already one of the possible cells, and in each cell you pick the best available response. So erosive and preference-perturbing signals are covered too.

(5) Hence, for any agent in any decision problem, ex ante there is no information it would be better off not having. Declining the genetic test for the untreatable disease, skipping the spoiler, a system declining to inspect its own weights — all of it is weakly irrational.

(6) Corollary I actually care about: an agent can never gain by keeping itself in the dark. No instrumental reason for self-deception, no reason to avoid poking at something, no reason for a model to prefer not learning whether it's in training or deployment, and no way for more transparency or more fine-grained monitoring to make things worse.

(7) But (6) looks straightforwardly false in the cases I care about: in those cases ignorance is keeping an option open, and the information destroys one rather than adding one. And (5) at least feels wrong for the spoiler.

(8) So something in (1)–(5) has to give.

I'd rather keep (1) — the theorem itself looks rock solid to me — and give up (4) or (5). But if I give up (4) I owe an account of why "don't poke it" isn't just cowardice, and if I give up (5) I owe an account of why the genetic-test case is genuinely different from the weather-forecast case, and I don't think I have either.
