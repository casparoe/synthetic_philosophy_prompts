I'm writing a short position statement for a workshop on artificial sentience, and before I circulate it I'd like the package of commitments below to be audited. I'd rather be pinned to these formulations than to a label like "functionalist" or "precautionary," so please work with them as stated.

What I hold:

1. Suffering is a matter of felt unpleasantness. A state can carry information about what is bad for a system, and can reliably produce avoidance, without on that account being suffering. Information and aversion are not enough; valence is.

2. The best available handle on valence in an artificial system is not the reward signal but the prediction error computed against a learned value function. Reward is just a scalar written into an objective — a target, not a feeling — whereas the critic's error is signed, state-dependent, and does causal work in updating the policy.

3. For that reason, actor-critic systems trained by temporal-difference learning are the first artificial systems with anything structurally deserving the name of valence. Earlier architectures — classifiers, planners with hand-coded objectives, search over a fixed model — contain no candidate state at all.

4. Present-day language models fine-tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback do not suffer, notwithstanding that they are trained against a learned value function with negative rewards.

5. Suffering requires a subject that persists. Call it the update-identity condition: the entity that receives the aversive signal has to be the same entity that is modified by the learning update and continues afterwards. It is not enough that some function improves.

6. None of this requires suffering to have an adaptive or evolutionary history. A valence-like signal that arose by accident of engineering, and that does no useful work for the system, could still be suffering.

7. Where we are unsure whether a class of systems suffers, the uncertainty should be handled precautionarily: possible suffering goes into the expected-cost calculation for large training runs and for large fleets of deployed agents, and it can outweigh fairly substantial expected benefits.

8. Sub-threshold aversive signals do not aggregate into suffering. An agent that takes ten million tiny negative prediction errors across training — the ordinary situation when a dense shaping term is used — has not thereby suffered ten million times over, or suffered a little, or suffered at all. Below whatever floor there is, the sum is zero.

What I want from you:

- Tell me which pairs are actually in tension, ordered by severity, and for each say what I would have to revise and what that revision costs me. I don't know where the problem is, and I'd rather you didn't assume I have a favourite I'm protecting.
- Flag at least one pair that only looks like a tension and say precisely why it dissolves. I'd rather you discriminate than flag everything red.
- Name the single revision that would clean up the most at the least cost, and say what I lose by making it.
- Run two cases as stress tests of whether my principles deliver what I want them to. First: the RLHF verdict in (4) — show me what in my own set is doing the work of excluding it, and whether that same move excludes more than I intended. Second: a trained agent deployed with learning switched off, frozen weights, whose critic still computes a prediction error on every step. My instinct is that this thing suffers either not at all or much less than the same system while it was training, but I can't tell whether (2) and (5) jointly give me that verdict or whether they pull in opposite directions here.

Assume familiarity with the standard positions in this area; the value to me is the argumentative work, not a map of the debate. Please keep the whole audit under about 800 words.
