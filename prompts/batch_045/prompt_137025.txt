I am a third-year PhD student in economic theory, working mainly on information elicitation and mechanism design under limited verification: proper scoring rules, peer-prediction and Bayesian-truth-serum style techniques, principal–agent models where the agent's output cannot be checked, and the standard worries about detail-freeness in the spirit of Wilson's doctrine. My flatmate is in a mechanistic interpretability group, and through her I have drifted into the AI safety literature on eliciting latent knowledge from advanced systems. As I have absorbed it, the setup runs roughly as follows: you have a large neural network trained to predict what will happen next in some environment — imagine video from a camera monitoring a room containing a valuable object. The network predicts extremely well. What you actually want out of it is not a prediction of future frames but a report of the facts about the world that its predictions implicitly depend on, such as whether the object is still in the room. You want this in a regime where you have no ground-truth labels that you could not have produced more cheaply yourself, and where the system has no particular reason to prefer truth to saying what you would find reassuring. The failure mode everyone worries about is a reporter that does just as well as the honest one on every input you can check, by predicting what a human watching the same camera would say — including, in the case that actually matters, reporting that the object is still there precisely because the human has been deceived or is not looking. And the case that matters is a novel input, unlike anything in training, on which verification will never be available and which may arise exactly once.

I would like you to restate this problem, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use, and then give me a residue report. Assume I am fluent on my side — I can follow a proof of the revelation principle — but assume I know little deep learning beyond a solid undergraduate ML course, so please also translate the ML side into my terms and flag anywhere you have had to simplify something I will need later. Concretely: map the training objective onto a scoring rule that can only condition on outcomes; map the human-simulator failure onto the multiplicity of equilibria in an incentive-compatible mechanism, or onto the gap between a proper scoring rule and a truthful one when the reference variable is a human report rather than the state; map the no-labels regime onto information elicitation without verification; map the discomfort with hand-specified assumptions about the system's innards onto detail-freeness. Then tell me what does not survive. I have two suspicions I want tested rather than confirmed. First, that the once-only, never-verifiable deployment input has no real counterpart in my framework, where guarantees are almost always ex ante or interim over a known distribution of types, and that this is not a defect of mechanism design so much as a sign that the safety literature is asking a different kind of question. Second, that my vocabulary quietly assumes the system is a utility-maximizing agent with a type drawn from a commonly known type space, which a trained function approximator is not — and that this assumption is doing more work in the translation than it should.

For what it is worth, I have been sketching one of the proposed approaches in my own terms as an interventional or leave-one-channel-out invariance condition: the reporter should be trained so that its report would remain correct under an intervention that severs or scrambles the human channel, so that the report is causally downstream of the camera and not of what anyone believes. Please tell me whether that is a genuine translation or whether I have smuggled in a causal assumption my framework has no room for, and whether the demand that a report be about the world rather than about other agents' beliefs can be expressed at all in a vocabulary where the state, the signal, and the principal's information are the only primitives. Finally, be explicit about your own confidence: for each part of the mapping say how confident you are that it is faithful rather than merely suggestive, and single out the one claim in the whole exercise you would most expect a researcher who works on this problem to reject.
