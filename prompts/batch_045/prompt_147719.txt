I should say up front that I have no academic background in any of this. I work in freight logistics and have been reading about machine consciousness and the ethics of it on my own for about two years, so please keep the technical vocabulary light and explain any term of art you need to use.

There is a forum I read where one question comes back endlessly: when does a computation amount to someone, or to part of someone, or to something we owe something to? One member there — call him M., a systems engineer, the only person whose verdicts I've found stable across threads — has ruled on a long series of cases over the years. I've collected his record:

A whole-brain emulation running on a server matters, in the way a person matters. Run it a thousand times faster and it doesn't matter a thousand times more, and there aren't a thousand of it.

Two identical copies running side by side: two things that matter, each of them fully, not half each.

The same emulation run on three machines with majority voting, purely for reliability: one thing that matters, not three.

A lookup table with a precomputed output for every possible history of inputs, giving exactly the outputs the emulation would give: nothing there.

A vat of convecting fluid whose microstates can be mapped one-to-one onto the emulation's states so that the fluid's dynamics instantiate the same abstract state-transition structure, where that pattern plays no part in causing what the fluid does next: nothing there.

A system shaped internally exactly like the emulation except that every state transition is imposed from outside by a controller, so that its internal states never cause one another: nothing there.

A person whose neurons are replaced one at a time with silicon units doing the same job: it matters at every step, and the finished all-silicon thing matters too.

An emulation checkpointed to disk, the machine switched off for a year, then resumed: the same thing that mattered before the pause, not two of them.

A nematode: it matters, but only a little; he has said he'd sacrifice a very large number of them rather than one person, and that he doesn't think there is a sharp line where it stops mattering at all.

A present-day chatbot: no. But the reason he gives is that it keeps no enduring state of its own from one exchange to the next, and he has said in several threads that he'd rule differently if that were fixed, so I don't think his no is doing much work.

I should also flag a wrinkle he has never resolved: I once asked him how copies can be two while voting machines are one, given that both are the same computation running more than once, and he said you can tell the difference when you see it, which I didn't find helpful.

The case now being argued on the forum, which he has not ruled on, is the one I want a prediction about. Someone proposes to implement the functional organization of a whole-brain emulation not in one machine but across two million borrowed phones. No device corresponds to a single unit of the emulation: each holds an encoded fragment that mixes parts of hundreds of units, under an error-correcting scheme, so any device can drop out and be replaced without the overall computation changing. There is no global clock; messages arrive late and out of order and the protocol is built to tolerate that. Every device in the network is swapped for a different one within about a week. An engineer can demonstrate that, read at the right coarse grain, the causal dependencies among the network's states match the emulation's causal dependencies, and that the network as a whole would answer as the emulated person would — slowly and expensively. But no single device, and no connected physical region smaller than the entire network, carries enough of the organization to do anything on its own, and at any given instant there is no fact of the matter about which shards were simultaneously at the same stage.

Would M. say there is something there that matters? One such thing, many, or a fraction of one? Does it survive the replacement of every shard? A variant is also on the table: the same federation, except that a single rack in one building runs the whole emulation locally at a fine grain, and the phones are only a warm backup kept in step but never consulted. Does that change his answer, and if it does, what does the difference tell us about the rule he is running?

I care less about being right than about this: the two or three rules that fit his whole record about equally well, what each predicts about the federation, which one I should bet on and why, and the smallest change to the case — or the extra case I should put to him — that would separate them.

One last thing, which matters to me as much as the prediction. Please mark clearly which parts of what you say are settled and which are live disputes among people who actually work on this. I can't tell the difference myself, and I would much rather be told that people disagree and how the disagreement splits than be handed one side as though it were the standard view. Tell me as well how much confidence you would actually put on your prediction, given that the evidence is a stranger's forum posts, and what would move it.
