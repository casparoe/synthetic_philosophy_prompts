I am writing a paper on whether "all-things-considered better than" is transitive, and I have been trying to rebuild the familiar spectrum arguments of the sort used against the repugnant conclusion so that they no longer rest on intuitive verdicts about neighbouring cases. Those verdicts invite the reply that each neighbour-pair is simply borderline, so that there is no fact of the matter and no intransitivity left to explain. My version runs on structural premises instead. It now appears to prove a great deal more than intransitivity, and I cannot locate the cut.

Set-up. Outcomes are distributions of lifetime well-being across a fixed population. Write x ≻ y for "x is better than y", and say that y is not worse than x when it is not the case that x ≻ y. I assume nothing about transitivity.

(1) Pareto. If x comes from y by raising someone's well-being and lowering no one's, then x ≻ y.

(2) Local margins. For every outcome x there is a number ε_x > 0 such that any outcome y obtained from x by changing everyone's well-being by less than ε_x, with some people raised and some lowered, is not worse than x. The idea is that comparative betterness is insensitive to sufficiently small non-uniform changes; this is what is supposed to make it impossible to locate a cutoff anywhere along a spectrum.

(3) Reachability. For any two outcomes x and z there is a finite sequence x = x_0, …, x_k = z in which every step is a non-uniform change of less than ε_{x_i}, the margin at x_i. The idea is that the distance between any two outcomes is finite, and that for a population of three or more one can always route between them in small steps, rotating a small bonus from person to person so that no single step moves everyone in the same direction.

(4) No unwitnessed betterness. If x ≻ z, then on any finite sequence of small steps leading from x to z there is some step i at which x_i ≻ x_{i+1}. The idea is that if the endpoints really differ in betterness, the difference has to show up at some step: betterness cannot hold between the ends of a path without being visible somewhere along it. I use (4) rather than simply chaining "not worse than" from step to step, because that chaining would presuppose the very transitivity I am calling into question.

The argument. Take any outcome A and let A⁺ be A with everyone raised by one unit. (1) gives A⁺ ≻ A. By (3) there is a finite small-step path from A⁺ to A; by (2), at no step is x_i ≻ x_{i+1}; by (4), contraposed, it is not the case that A⁺ ≻ A. So (1) to (4) are jointly inconsistent. Worse, nothing in the reasoning turns on A⁺ dominating A: run against an arbitrary pair A, Z it yields the negation of A ≻ Z for every pair, i.e. betterness is empty. I set out to make trouble for transitivity and seem to have shown that nothing is better than anything.

I want to keep (1), and I want to keep something in the neighbourhood of (2), since margins are what I need to explain why spectrum reasoning looks compelling in the first place. So I would rather cut (3) or (4) — but I want to know exactly what I am refusing.

Please tell me which step you reject, and why that step looks innocent. Two points in particular: does (3) actually follow from (2), or am I sliding from "every outcome has some positive margin" to "the margins along a path are bounded away from zero"? And is (4) a genuine constraint on betterness or only the shadow of assuming that betterness is representable by something quantity-like — and is its quantifier over paths doing work it has no right to do? Then price the options: for each of (2), (3) and (4), what does rejecting it commit me to about sharp cutoffs and vagueness in spectrum cases, about lexical or otherwise discontinuous structure in value, and about rational choice and money pumps? Which of these commitments is cheapest for someone whose real aim is to resist the repugnant conclusion without surrendering Pareto? Please answer in British English.
