I run grantmaking for a foundation that gives away roughly $40 million a year across global health and economic opportunity: about sixty grants annually, six program staff, no in-house economist, and a board that must approve anything above $250,000. The board has asked us to rank proposals by cost-effectiveness in the style associated with effective altruism. I want to do that while taking the movement's critics seriously: that quantification quietly privileges whatever is easy to count, and that treating a problem as a series of individually purchasable fixes displaces the structural work that would end it.

What I need is a procedure, not a position paper: the gates a proposal passes through in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each gate, where the procedure returns a no, and where it stops and hands the decision to the board because the question is one of values rather than evidence. Constraints: runnable in about two hours per proposal by a program officer with no economics training, with a part-time consultant available for the numbers, and only what survives the gates reaches the board. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed as procedure, and about which distinctions from the critique you had to drop to make the thing usable.

One device I want built in rather than argued about: each proposal is scored on two ledgers, one for effects we can count and price with a stated uncertainty range, one for effects we believe in but cannot measure, such as shifted local power, deterrence, or dignity; and the board sets, once a year and before seeing any proposals, the rate at which it is willing to trade one ledger against the other, so that unmeasured effects are neither scored at zero nor quietly rescued case by case.

Apply the procedure to three live proposals: a $2M renewal of an unconditional cash-transfer program with randomized evidence and a cost-per-outcome estimate that survives a fragility check but does nothing about why recipients are poor; a $600k grant to a land-rights legal-empowerment group whose theory of change runs through changing how district officials behave and which has no credible comparison group; and a $900k fellowship that pays graduates to take high-earning finance jobs and donate a share of their salaries, which two board members already champion. If the procedure says no to something we currently fund and are attached to, say so plainly, and say what would have to change for it to pass. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do: which decisions it will systematically get wrong, and where an officer should overrule it.
