Hi — I'm a TA for a first-year course with a name like "Chance, Evidence and Reason" (philosophy course with a bit of formal content, roughly 24 students, mostly philosophers plus a handful of maths people who are quietly bored), and I've got a 75-minute slot next week in a room with a projector, a bit of open floor, and no other kit. I want to do the Bertrand chord business with them — pick a chord of a circle at random, what's the probability it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and depending on how you cash out "at random" you get 1/3, 1/2 or 1/4 — but I absolutely don't want it to be me at the board writing three slightly different integrals and then announcing "isn't that odd". I want them to produce the three numbers with their own hands, be genuinely surprised that the room disagrees with itself, and only then hear that this has a name.

The shape I'm imagining: split them into four stations, and give each station a physical procedure with no label and no hint that the procedures differ in any important way — just "here is a way of getting a chord; do it 40 times; each time just record whether the chord is longer than the side of the triangle; someone from your group comes and writes your fraction on the board." Station 1 picks two points on the circumference (spinning something, or a random angle from their phone). Station 2 picks a radius, then a point along it, and draws the perpendicular chord. Station 3 picks a point in the disc — darts, or dropping a grain of rice on a printed circle — and draws the chord that has that point as its midpoint. Station 4 does something where nobody picks anything: they throw drinking straws onto a big circle taped out on the floor and extend each straw to a chord. Then we look at the four numbers. I need help making each of these actually workable and fast — what exact materials, what the recording sheet looks like, how to stop a station from finishing in four minutes and then wandering over to see what the other stations are doing, and how to keep the "longer than the side of the triangle?" judgement from being a fiddly eyeball call that eats the whole session. Also tell me if 40 is the wrong number of trials for the effect to show up.

Two things I want the debrief to get to and am not sure how to get to. First, a friend who does integral geometry told me that station 4 isn't just a fourth arbitrary procedure: if you generate a straight line in a way that doesn't care about where the circle sits or how big it is — invariant under shifting and rescaling the whole setup, which throwing straws roughly is — the distance from the centre to the line comes out uniformly distributed, and that lands you on one specific answer (I think 1/2, but correct me if I've got that backwards, and tell me if the straw version really approximates it or if the finite straw length and the misses mess it up). I'd like that to arrive as a fourth position someone argues for, not as a fact I state. Second, and more important: I want them to see that the puzzle isn't really about circles at all, it's that the principle of indifference is incomplete — "no reason to prefer one alternative" doesn't tell you what to be indifferent *between* until somebody has already handed you a space of alternatives, and each station quietly spread its indifference over a different space (pairs of endpoints, positions along a radius, points in the disc, lines in the plane). And I want the fight about whether one of those spaces is the "really random" one to happen out loud between them, not between me and them.

So: give me the runnable thing — materials and exact procedures per station, number of trials, what goes on the board and in what order, a minute-by-minute for the 75 minutes with nothing lectured up front, and the debrief with the actual moves from "we got different numbers" to "indifference needs a space of alternatives given in advance", including the lines I can use to get the invariance point out of them rather than telling them. Then be honest about the failure modes and the limits: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I'm guessing straws that miss the circle entirely, someone in station 1 pointing out their angle isn't really uniform, groups peeking, the trials taking forever, and one smug person who's read about the paradox and shouts the answer in the first five minutes) and how to handle each; and what the activity just can't deliver. My own suspicion is that it can show them "different procedures, different numbers" really vividly but can't by itself deliver the "so the question was ill-posed" moral, and that bit has to come from me — tell me if you think that's right or if there's a way to stage it.
