ok so I'm a junior and I got sucked into an argument in a philosophy group chat and its been rattling around my head for about a week. This guy in there (first year of uni, doing philosphy) claims he can prove that nobody has ever meant anything by any word. Here's his argument, more or less as he wrote it:

(1) Take any rule and any finite batch of examples of me using it — every sum I've ever actually done in my life, say. There are infinitely many different rules that match all of those examples but disagree about the next case. 'Add' fits my whole history, but so does this thing he calls quus: add the two numbers, except when both of them are bigger than 57, in which case the answer is 5. I've never actually done a sum where both numbers were over 57, so both rules fit everything I've ever said perfectly.
(2) So the examples I've produced so far can't single out which rule I'm following.
(3) The only other things that could make it true that I mean add are my conscious intentions back when I learned it, or my dispositions to answer.
(4) Intentions can't do it. An intention has content, so the same question just comes back one level up: what makes it an intention to add rather than an intention to quadd? And then one level above that. Regress.
(5) Dispositions can't do it either. They've only ever been exercised finitely many times, so (1) applies to them again anyway; and besides, I'm sometimes disposed to screw up, and 'plus' can't just mean 'whatever I happen to be disposed to say'.
(6) So nothing makes it true that I mean add rather than quus. There is no fact of the matter about what I mean.
(7) Meaning something requires there to be a fact about what you mean. So nobody means anything by anything, and nothing ever follows any rule.
(8) And nothing in (1)–(7) uses anything special about arithmetic or about being human: my calculator has also only ever been fed finitely many sums, so by the same reasoning there's no fact about what function it computes either.
(9) Which means there's no fact about what (1)–(8) themselves mean, so he isn't really managing to tell me anything true.

I obviously don't accept (7). But every step looks fine to me, and the two places I keep squinting at — the move from 'no fact about the cases I haven't done yet' to 'no fact about what I meant back in third grade', and the way he killed my one escape attempt — I can't turn into an actual objection.

The escape attempt: I invented a board game with exactly 40 legal positions, and instead of a rule I wrote down a complete list in a notebook — for each position, the legal moves. There's no 'next case' left over, so the infinity trick in (1) can't even get started, and it really seems to me like there IS a fact about what the correct move is, because it's literally written down. He says the dodge fails, because reading the notebook is itself following a rule, and whatever tells me which squiggle goes with which column is another rule, and so on. I think that's way too fast but I can't say why.

So: which step would you reject, why does it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost you? I'd rather keep (1) than anything else in there — that one feels like just maths — and I'd really like to keep the claim that my notebook case has a right answer in it. If what I'd have to buy is one of the labelled positions (dispositionalism, the sceptical solution, non-factualism about meaning, whatever the options actually are), say which one and what it commits me to.
