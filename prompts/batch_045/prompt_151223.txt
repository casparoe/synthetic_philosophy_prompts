my flatmate does survey work in the Aegean and we ended up shouting at each other about cemeteries again last night, so I want a third opinion.

The fight is whether you can read a property institution — heritable land, a heritable office, that sort of thing — off a burial assemblage. His position: if a small minority of graves consistently get a distinctive package (copper axes, cattle skulls, their own sector of the cemetery), and the package shows up in kids too young to have earned anything, then you have inherited rank and you are entitled to say so. My position: everything in that grave was chosen by the people who were still alive, at a moment of crisis, for an audience, so what you actually have is evidence about a display convention, and the institution is one inference too far. Then theres the third position people retreat to when the argument gets boring: nothing follows, the record is silent, go home.

It has been going round in circles for fifty-odd years and everyone knows every objection — equifinality, mourners rather than deceased, formation processes, the fact that rich/poor is our axis and may not be theirs. Nobody moves. Here is a stylised version of the case we were actually arguing over: 200 graves across 300 years, about 12% carrying the axe-and-cattle package, clustered in one sector; the package correlates with house size but not with skeletal markers of workload; subadults get two of them; and the package first appears roughly 200 years after the big houses do and carries on about 150 years after the big houses are abandoned. He keeps restating this rule of his — presence, frequency, covariance — which I suspect is doing all the work in his argument without either of us noticing.

My hypothesis is that this isnt a factual dispute at all. I think three target variables are sliding into each other: labour invested in the funeral, what the mourners wanted to claim in public, and the actual standing of the dead person while alive. The real question is how tightly those are coupled, and that is an empirical question about a particular society that we are arguing as though it were a methodological one. I also think we have different priors about lag: he reads the 200-year mismatch as noise, I read it as an ideology outliving the institution it was shoring up.

So: is that right? Verbal, substantive-but-hidden, or bedrock priors about how much of the past is recoverable? What does each side actually rely on? And what would count as progress — I suggested calibration cases where you have a cemetery and independent documentary evidence of legal status for the same community, but he says those all come from periods where the rite is already church-run or state-run, so they tell us nothing about the cases that matter. Is he right about that, is there some other thing that would settle it, or should we just expect to be having this exact argument in ten years?
