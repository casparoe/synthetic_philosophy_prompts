I'm a backend engineer — distributed systems, a lot of time spent on compression and encoding formats — with no formal philosophy background, and I came to Solomonoff induction the way I suspect a lot of people in ML-adjacent circles do: as the thing that finally makes Occam's razor look like a theorem rather than a taste. Our reading group spent an evening on it and I walked out convinced. A friend in the group, a statistician who enjoys wrecking things, sent me the following argument in email the next morning, and I have not been able to find the exit. He insists each step is either a standard result or one line of algebra.

(1) Fix a universal machine, weight each program by 2 to the minus its length, and predict by mixing the predictions of every program weighted that way. As I understand the result, this mixture converges to any computable data source, and its cumulative error is bounded by something that grows with the complexity of the true source plus a constant; no other computable predictor beats it by more than a constant factor. That is about as strong an optimality result as one could want, and it flows entirely from the 2-to-the-minus-length weighting.

(2) Call a prior *admissible* if it differs from the canonical Solomonoff prior by at most a constant factor on every hypothesis: there is a single c, fixed once and for all, with 2^(-K(x))/c ≤ P(x) ≤ c·2^(-K(x)) for all x. Every theorem in (1) survives unchanged under any admissible prior — the error bound picks up a constant multiplier and convergence is untouched — so admissibility is exactly the class of priors the theorems certify.

(3) The invariance theorem says switching the reference machine changes K by an additive constant that does not depend on the string being described. But it does depend on the pair of machines, and nothing bounds how large it can be. Concretely, I can take any universal machine and bolt on what I've started calling a *bloat gadget*: a wrapper that forces one named hypothesis to be spelled out the long way — pad its shortest description with a few million extra bits — while leaving the machine universal, at the cost of a few million bits of constant. The prior induced by that machine is admissible. So for any finite body of evidence E and any pair of rival hypotheses, however lopsided — the tidy two-parameter curve versus the baroque sixty-parameter one — there is an admissible prior under which the baroque one is overwhelmingly favoured after E.

(4) Therefore the optimality theorems constrain no belief about any finite body of evidence: for every verdict one might reach on E, some admissible prior reaches it. A constraint that rules nothing out recommends nothing.

(5) So Solomonoff's apparatus gives Occam's razor no support whatsoever. Worse, it gives exactly the same support to a prior that upweights complicated hypotheses, since that is admissible too. A theory that vindicates the razor and its negation equally well vindicates neither, and simplicity has to be justified some other way or not at all.

(6) And note how far this generalises. Any justification of a Bayesian norm by a dominance or asymptotic-optimality argument is empty for the same reason, because dominance is always up to an unspecified constant. That is the step I most want to resist: I do not want to conclude that convergence and Dutch-book style arguments tell a finite agent like me nothing.

If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2) and give up (5) or (6) than the reverse, but I want to know what keeping them costs me. So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine when I read it, and what do the other options cost? Two things about my own framing that I especially want checked. First, my engineer's instinct is that the fix is to penalise computation time as well as description length — weight by 2 to the minus (length plus log-runtime), which at least gives you a predictor that terminates — and I can't tell whether my friend's argument sinks that fix too (there is no invariance theorem for time-bounded priors, so the choice of machine and cost model seems to matter *more*, not less), or whether the fact that it sinks something so obviously reasonable is itself a reason to reject the argument. Second, tell me where each of your claims sits on a confidence scale, and flag any step that rests on a technical claim about the invariance theorem or the error bound — state those claims precisely enough that I can verify them from my own knowledge rather than take them on faith, since I can read code and a little math but can't look anything up from where I'm writing. And if the setup is confused rather than merely wrong somewhere — if the bloat gadget doesn't do what I think it does, or if it's a category mistake to describe Solomonoff induction as vindicating Occam's razor in the first place, given that it mixes over every hypothesis rather than selecting the simplest one — please say so plainly instead of walking me politely through a bad argument.
