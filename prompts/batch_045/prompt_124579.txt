Construct a single-paragraph case, set in an ordinary professional context, in which three accounts of knowledge each yield a different combination of verdicts on two questions — and in which the divergence falls out of the accounts rather than being written into the story.

The accounts. Use these versions.

1. Attributor contextualism. The truth conditions of ascriptions of knowledge shift with the ascriber's context: how much rides on the inquiry the ascriber is engaged in, and which error possibilities are salient there. Nothing about the subject's own situation fixes the standard independently of the ascriber's context. On the version I want, the practical question — whether the subject may treat the proposition as a reason for acting without checking further — is settled by whether the ascription comes out true in the subject's own deliberative context.

2. Interest-relative invariantism (pragmatic encroachment). The standard is invariant across ascribers but shifts with what is practically at stake for the subject: the cost of being wrong, the cost of delay or further checking, the importance of acting now. The stakes are the subject's actual situation, whether or not she recognizes it. Knowledge in this sense is exactly what is needed for the proposition to be usable as a premise in practical reasoning, so the two questions move together.

3. Transparency-conditioned encroachment — a view I am stipulating here rather than reporting, because I want to see what it commits us to. As in (2), the standard is invariant across ascribers and sensitive to practical stakes; but encroachment is triggered only by stakes the subject has in view at the time of acting. Real stakes of which she is unaware do not raise the bar, even if others know about them and even if they are enormous. The two questions move together here as well.

The two questions, asked about the same subject, the same proposition and the same evidence. Q1: as a third party — call her the assessor — asks it, in the context the case specifies: is it true that the subject knows? Q2: may the subject act on the proposition without checking further?

The pattern I want: contextualism, no to Q1 and yes to Q2; interest-relative invariantism, no to both; transparency-conditioned encroachment, yes to both.

Constraints on the scenario.

- One paragraph, at most about 150 words, realistic, no science-fictional or contrived machinery.
- Set it somewhere with institutional texture — a hospital laboratory, a structural inspection, a food-safety line, an election canvass, a wildfire dispatch desk — and not in the familiar register of banks, airports, tickets and missed flights.
- The subject's evidence must be strong enough, and of the right kind, that if the stakes were trivial and the assessor's context relaxed, all three accounts would return a positive verdict. Describe the evidence by what she did and by the known error rates of her procedures — not by calling it justified, reasonable or solid.
- Her ignorance of the real stakes must be non-culpable and realistic: the information exists somewhere she had no duty to look, or a downstream use of her result was never mentioned to her, or a change was made by another unit. No conspiracies, no one deceiving her on purpose.
- Further checking must be possible but genuinely costly, and it must be pointless as she sees things while cheap relative to the stakes as they actually are. Build this into the story rather than asserting it.
- Nobody in the story says that they know, or that she does not; nothing is settled by what anyone would be inclined to say.
- Hold fixed across all three evaluations: the proposition, the evidence, the cost of error, the cost of checking. The only variables are who is aware of the stakes and what is salient in the assessor's context.

What to hand back.

1. The paragraph.
2. A short table of the held-fixed parameters, including the distribution of awareness and the features of the assessor's context.
3. For each of the three accounts: the verdicts on Q1 and Q2, with the reasoning traced explicitly to that account's commitments, and a statement of which feature of the case the account keys on.
4. Which single feature does the work. Show this by removing it in two directions: first, let the subject be told what is actually at stake; second, let the assessor's context be one in which nothing hangs on the question and the error possibility has not been raised. Say which rows collapse into which, and why this shows the feature is load-bearing rather than decorative.
5. The strongest attack available to a defender of whichever account comes off worst in your case, and briefly why it is the strongest rather than merely the first one that occurred to you.

Why I want it: I am building a seminar session on whether contextualism and pragmatic encroachment are genuine rivals or two descriptions of the same pattern. The familiar cases cannot separate them, because in those cases the ascriber's context and the subject's stakes move together and every account returns the same verdict. A case that works by stipulating the verdicts, or by pressing the reader's untutored feel for the word, is of no use to me: I want the verdicts derived from the commitments above, with the pressure points visible.
