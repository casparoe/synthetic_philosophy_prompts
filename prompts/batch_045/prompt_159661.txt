I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I am working on a long piece about how the question of responsibility for the First World War gets re-litigated every time a great-power crisis comes along — not as history for its own sake, but as a template people reach for when they want to say whether a rival power is, or is not, the one that wants this. What has struck me while reading across the historiography is that the argument itself is frozen. Fischer's thesis in the early 1960s, the reaction to it, the more recent "sleepwalkers" reading, the rejoinders to that, and the perpetually renewed claim that all the powers share the guilt: the same documents, the same quotations from the same memoranda, the same conclusions. New monographs get absorbed into one camp or the other without denting either.

The positions, as I understand them, are these. The primacy camp argues that Berlin gave Vienna a blank cheque and used the July crisis to force a war it had already concluded was necessary — a preventive war justified by a closing window of military power, made attractive by domestic pressures that made a flight forward the path of least resistance — and reads the notes of the Kaiser's December 1912 meeting with his service chiefs and the September 1914 catalogue of war aims as continuous evidence of that disposition. The diffusion camp argues that nobody in authority wanted a continental war; that each capital made a locally defensible decision under severe uncertainty, misread the others' signals, and took risks it did not fully understand; and that the outcome was an emergent product of an interacting system rather than the execution of a plan. Then there is a methodological skepticism layered over both: the counterfactual in which Berlin restrains Vienna is unfalsifiable, so the dispute is really about how much weight to give to documents of intent, whether a memorandum is evidence of intention or of self-dramatizing talk, and whether the structure of a political system or the beliefs of its leaders do the explanatory work.

My own hypothesis is that "responsibility" is doing three jobs at once and that the sides are answering three different questions under one name: causal pivotalness — whose action, if deleted, would have prevented the war, complicated by the fact that several actions look individually sufficient and none looks strictly necessary; wrongful initiation — who did the thing that constitutes starting it, and whether mobilizing or declaring first is a normative marker or merely a conventional one; and blameworthiness — who acted with foresight, with live alternatives, in an official capacity, and so is open to moral criticism rather than to causal explanation alone. Behind all three sits a fourth difficulty I have never seen handled well: whether "Germany," "Russia," or "France" is the kind of thing that can be responsible at all, when the decisions were taken by a handful of men who disagreed with one another and whose interaction produced something none of them quite chose. If that is right, the debate is stalled because participants score points on one question and claim victory on another. If it is wrong, I want to know what the actual structure is: a difference about evidentiary standards, about what counts as a historical cause, about priors concerning how states behave, or about the present-day policies each answer is heard as licensing. I would also like to know whether an asymmetry I keep noticing belongs in the diagnosis — readers find it easy to imagine Berlin restraining Vienna and strangely hard to imagine St Petersburg or Paris declining, which suggests that the availability of counterfactuals, rather than the evidence, may be carrying a good deal of the weight.

I do not need a narrative of the July crisis and I do not need a reading list; I have those. I want the diagnosis with reasons, and then, given that diagnosis, an honest account of what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the dispute, a way of formalizing the causal question so that attribution of an overdetermined outcome becomes tractable, a re-description that converts the unresolvable part into an empirical one, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
