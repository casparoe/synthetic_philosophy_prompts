I work in digital preservation at a university library — me and three others, and we triage incoming collections in a weekly meeting, 30 to 40 items a session, so realistically each item gets about two minutes and a one-line justification that goes in the record. Our options per item are always the same three: keep a bit-exact image of the original (expensive, and storage is what we're always fighting about), normalise it to something like PDF/A or CSV and drop the original, or discard. We can't do everything, depositors are usually not contactable in ten years, and there's a data-protection clock on some of it, so discard is sometimes right even when we'd rather it wasn't. We also can't go look up format specs for every weird file — the checklist has to work at the level of "what does the outside of this thing tell you".

I want to steal the frame / outer / inner message distinction from Hofstadter's chapter on the location of meaning — frame message being whatever says "this is a message, decode me", outer message being the instructions or key for how to decode it, inner message being the content once decoded, with the wrinkle that the outer can sometimes be reconstructed from the inner by a clever enough receiver — and turn it into a triage procedure we can run in two minutes on a folder we mostly can't open.

Give me: the questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each when we can't read the bytes; where it stops with "image everything", where with "normalise and discard the original", where with "discard outright"; and where it hands the decision back to a human because it's a values call about significance rather than a question about recoverability. Three things my team already argues about and that I want it to settle or at least name properly:

- borrowed frames: cases where the thing that says "this is a message" isn't in the object at all but in its container — the labelled box, the folder name, the file extension, the depositor's email, the disk image it arrived on. Those don't survive the object being seperated from that context, and I think we systematically overestimate how much of our stuff is self-describing.
- reconstruction bets: when we can't preserve the outer message (dead format, missing key, no documentation) we're really betting that some future receiver can rebuild it from the inner message plus conventions that outlive the carrier. I want the procedure to say when that bet is reasonable and when it's wishful, instead of us deciding by vibes every time.
- phantom frames: files that look like they carry structure but are noise, padding, or bit-rot. We waste real hours on these and I'd like a cheap test that kills them early.

Then run it on three things actually on our list: (1) a thousand-odd TIFF scans of handwriten nineteenth-century letters, no transcripts, filenames like IMG_0001.tif; (2) a deceased academic's laptop with a folder of PGP-encrypted files and no key anywhere we can find; (3) about 20,000 Word and Excel files from a government department that convert cleanly to PDF/A and CSV. I expect it to tell me to image (1) and (2) — I most want to see what it does with (3), because my instinct is definately to keep the originals and I suspect the procedure won't let me.

Be honest about what the procedure can't do. In particular I can already see that it's about whether a message stays recoverable, not about whether it's worth keeping, and that the receiver's future cleverness is the one variable it can't pin down — say how that should show up in our one-line justifications rather than being silently assumed.
