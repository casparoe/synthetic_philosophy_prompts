I'm writing my undergrad thesis chapter on whether the lying/misleading distinction actually does any moral work, and I need a case built to order. My supervisor's line is that every example where the truthful-but-misleading option looks worse is really a case where something else is going on — more harm, more planning, a second wrong stacked on top of the deception — and that once you strip that away, misleading is at worst equal to lying and usually milder (you let the hearer draw the inference themselves, you don't put your word behind the falsehood, you leave them their agency). I want a case that survives her.

Specifically: a case where the misleading version is clearly morally worse than the outright lie, and where the extra badness falls out of the very feature that's supposed to make misleading milder — that the hearer supplies the false inference themselves instead of having it handed to them by an assertion.

Constraints:

- One short paragraph, ordinary setting. Workplace, doctor's office, family, whatever. No courtrooms or oaths, no espionage, no one dying.
- Same speaker, same hearer, same false belief produced, same stakes, same downstream consequences across variants. So the difference can't be consequences. And it can't be that the misleader worked harder, planned more, or knew the hearer better.
- The hearer's inference in the misleading variant has to be a reasonable one given what they know — not a blunder, not wishful thinking. Otherwise the reply is just that the hearer deceived themselves.
- It should resist the collapse objection: that the misleader has really asserted the falsehood (or straightforwardly implicated it), so it's just lying with extra steps and the distinction never got off the ground. I'd like the sentence to be plainly true and unambiguous, with all the misleading work done by context and by what the hearer brings.
- It should resist my supervisor's objection: I need to be able to say why the extra badness isn't a separate wrong (manipulation, free-riding on the truth-telling norm, keeping your hands clean) bolted onto an equally-bad-or-milder deception.

What I've already tried and couldn't make work, so you don't redo it: building it around withholding a fact (that just became a case about omission, and she says omission is its own topic); and the angle where the hearer ends up more confident because they worked it out themselves (I couldn't get the stakes identical once I added that in).

Deliverable: the scenario, then what each of these says about it — (i) the view that lying is specially bad because of the wrong of asserting what you believe false; (ii) the view that there's no moral difference, it's all intention to deceive; (iii) whatever view would actually predict the misleading version is worse. Where (i) and (ii) go flat. Then which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the verdict if I remove that feature, and how my supervisor would attack the case. If it can't be built to spec, tell me that instead and describe the nearest case that can be.
