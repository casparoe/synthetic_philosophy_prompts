I write a column on science and ideas, and I've got a piece in the pipeline about how the maths of machine learning keeps re-deriving bits of philosophy that everyone had filed away as unformaliseable. The bit I keep coming back to is the no-free-lunch theorems from the mid-90s — the ones for search and the ones for supervised learning — and I've drifted into holding a fairly strong position about them: that they are, in substance, Hume's problem of induction rendered in the notation of learning theory. The thought is that they're the closest thing we have to a formal vindication of Humean scepticism: absent an inductive bias of some sort, no method beats any other off the training data, including the deliberate anti-inductor that predicts the opposite of whatever the data suggests, and the average over target functions washes everything out to the same number regardless of how much data you have.

So: what would count against that? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate the ones a defender in my position should honestly concede from the ones we'd be entitled to wave off as missing the point. A few things I've been chewing on, in case they're useful starting points rather than distractions. One: the uniform average over target functions isn't the assumption-free baseline it looks like — it's a whopping commitment, and it's dominated by functions that are algorithmically random noise, so maybe the result only says that assuming nothing gets you nothing, which is trivial and was available to Hume without any theorem. Two: there seem to be genuine free lunches once you restrict attention to the computable, compressible, or physically realisable problems — Solomonoff-flavoured guarantees and so on — and my instinct is that this just relocates the bias rather than removing it, but I'd like that instinct tested. Three, and this is my own pet idea that I'd most like punched: it looks to me as if these setups are all archival, fixed sample, learner has no say in which points get sampled next, whereas science is interventional — we design experiments and go poke the world where it will tell us most. Does the averaging argument survive once the learner gets to choose its own data, and if it doesn't, does the analogy to induction die with it? Four: the result can be read as just the observation that for every method there's a mirror method that wins exactly where the first one loses — in which case the question is whether Hume's worry was ever only that mirror worlds exist, or whether it was that nothing non-circular rules them out, and whether the theorem says anything about that second part at all.

The thing I'd personally find most persuasive is a case where the formal result and the philosophical claim actually diverge — something the maths forces that a Humean shouldn't want, or something Hume is committed to that the theorem doesn't deliver. And I should flag where I think I'm most exposed: when pressed, I tend to retreat to the claim that all this really shows is that you need *some* inductive bias, which is something nobody has ever denied — at which point I worry I've qualified the position into unfalsifiability. Tell me whether that retreat is forced, whether it's fatal for a view of this kind, or whether it's fine because the view was only ever meant to be a translation exercise. Oh, and if the optimisation version and the supervised-learning version pull in different directions here — I've been treating them as more or less the same result in my notes, which may be sloppy — say which one the Humean reading ought to hang its hat on.
