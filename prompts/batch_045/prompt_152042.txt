After forty years as a research chemist I have been reading my way into decision theory, starting with the St Petersburg paradox, and I have built myself a variant that seems to go wrong in a different way. I cannot tell whether I have stumbled on something with a literature or merely reinvented a wheel, and I would rather be told the former.

A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time. If the first heads falls on the nth toss the player is paid minus one to the power n minus one, times two to the n, divided by n: plus two on the first toss, minus two on the second, plus eight thirds on the third, minus four on the fourth, and so on, with the sign alternating and the magnitude growing. Summed in the natural order the expectation is one minus a half plus a third minus a quarter and so on, that is, the natural logarithm of two. But the terms are not absolutely summable, and by the rearrangement theorem the very same game can be summed to any real number one pleases, including large negative ones, simply by taking the outcomes in a different order. Since the outcomes form only an unordered countable partition of the sample space, nothing in the description of the game appears to privilege one ordering over another.

Here is what makes me hesitate. If I cap the game at N tosses, paying nothing when no heads has appeared, the expectation is the Nth partial sum, and these tend to the logarithm of two — and they do so under every ordering, because a finite sum does not care about order. So an ordinary limiting procedure seems to pick out that value after all, and makes the rearrangement worry look like an artefact of bookkeeping. Yet I also have the feeling that a gamble whose expected absolute payoff is infinite is exactly the case where limiting procedures of this kind cannot be trusted.

So my question is whether this configuration has an established name and literature, or is only a footnote to St Petersburg. A name alone would not settle anything for me; what I want is the carving. What is the distinction I am groping at — an expectation that is infinite but well defined, versus one that does not exist at all, versus a limit of partial sums that exists relative to a chosen enumeration while the corresponding integral fails to exist? Does the truncation argument I sketched actually rescue the logarithm of two, or does it assume what the rearrangement argument denies? And where does the real moral land: on the definition of expectation, on countable additivity, on the admissibility of unbounded utility, or on the assumption that every gamble has a fair price? I would also like to know how near this sits to the two-envelope puzzle, where infinities of a similar shape turn up but, as far as I can tell, for a different reason.
