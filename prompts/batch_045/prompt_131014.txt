Hi — hoping for a second opinion on something at work. It's an engineering problem but it keeps turning into an argument about a word, so I figure that's your department.

I'm on the reliability team at a company that operates about 3,000 air-source heat pumps in apartment buildings. We monitor them remotely and we have an anomaly detector (basically an isolation-forest-type thing on the telemetry) that clusters units that are behaving oddly.

On our old fleet — vendor A, installed around 2018 — there's a failure everybody knows as F-07. In a cold snap, refrigerant migrates into the compressor crankcase overnight and dilutes the oil; when the unit cold-starts, the oil film in the scroll bearing doesn't hold, you get metal contact, and the compressor dies within a few hundred hours. The telemetry signature is fairly distinctive: a dip in discharge temperature plus a particular sawtooth in compressor current over roughly 90 seconds after start. We fixed it with a crankcase heater plus a firmware change that delays startup by four minutes. Retrofit worked, failures dropped about 90%.

Now we have 800 units from vendor B (2022, inverter-driven compressor, different oil, different control board). The detector flagged 60 of them in the same cluster as F-07. We tore down four. All four had bearing scoring consistent with oil starvation, but the oil analysis came back essentially clean — no meaningful refrigerant dilution. Two of the four also showed something else: the B units run a much longer defrost cycle, the reversing valve slams over, and one of our techs thinks the real story is a pressure transient at the restart after defrost, with the oil getting physically pushed out of the bearing rather than thinned. Nobody's sure.

The argument on the team is whether this is "the same failure mechanism."

- One engineer says obviously yes: same part fails, same way, same signature, and the crankcase heater retrofit cut the flag rate on the B fleet too (60 down to 22). So: same mechanism, retrofit everything, bill the vendor.
- Another says no: different parts, different activities, different order of operations. The chain of events isn't the same chain, it just ends in the same place.
- I pointed out that the heater also helps on our vendor C units, where we know the mechanism is something else entirely — there it's basically just raising temperatures and masking everything. Someone replied, "then define the mechanism by what the fix targets, not by the parts."
- A fourth person says the whole question is ill posed until you say what level you're describing it at: at the level of "the bearing loses its oil film and dies" it's one mechanism, at the level of the actual sequence of events it's two, and both descriptions are true.

Two more wrinkles. First, in three of the 60 flagged B units we found nothing wrong with the machine at all — the "anomaly" was a telemetry artifact from a gateway firmware update. So part of what the detector calls the same anomaly isn't in the machine at all. Second, there's a side argument about whether the control firmware is part of the mechanism or just the thing that sets it off, which apparently matters for whether we call the B failure a hardware problem or a controls problem.

What I actually have to decide: whether to spend roughly $400k retrofitting all 800 B units; whether we can honestly write to the vendor that "this is the known failure mode from the A fleet" in a warranty claim; and whether to keep running one detector across both fleets or train separate ones. We can instrument maybe ten units with extra sensors for a few weeks and we can run whatever tests we want on them.

So: how should I be thinking about whether these are the same? Is there a fact of the matter here, or does it depend on what I need the sameness claim for? What evidence would actually discriminate the two stories rather than merely being consistent with both? And is there a version of this where both of my colleagues are right and I still have to pick one for the warranty letter — and if so, how do I pick?
