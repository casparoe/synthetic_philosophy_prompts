I keep going back and forth on which sentences containing 'true' actually get truth values, so let me just write down what my gut says case by case and see if anything tidy covers it.

1. 'This sentence is not true.' — neither true nor false, and I want to be able to say that out loud.
2. 'This sentence is true.' — also neither, even though nothing blows up if I call it true; it just feels unmoored rather than paradoxical.
3. 'Either this sentence is not true, or snow is white.' — true. This one surprised me: it refers to itself and comes out fine, because the second half settles it.
4. Alice, who has said only one thing in her life, says 'Everything Bob says is not true'; Bob, who has said only one thing in his life, says 'Everything Alice says is not true'. — both neither, and note there's no self-reference here at all.
5. 'The sentence written on the card in my pocket is not true', where the card says 'Snow is white'. — straightforwardly false; swap the card for one saying 'Snow is black' and it's straightforwardly true. So whatever goes wrong in 1, it isn't self-reference or containing 'true', and it can depend on how the world happens to be.
6. 'This sentence is either false or neither true nor false.' — I want to say neither here too, but I can't say that without contradicting myself, and it's the same verdict I wanted in 1. This is the one I keep coming back to.
7. 'It is more likely than not that this sentence is not true.' — gut says true, but then my confidence in it should be high, which makes it false. And unlike 1 this one seems to say something about the world.

My working principle: a sentence gets a truth value iff evaluating it bottoms out in non-semantic facts, otherwise it's gappy, and 'not true' said of a gappy sentence isn't true either. Test that against the list and repair it where it breaks. Specifically: does it really deliver true for 3 and false for 5? Is there any way to hold onto 1 and 6 together without climbing a hierarchy of truth predicates? And what does whatever account you end up with say about two cases I haven't listed — a sentence that says of itself that it has no truth value, and one that says of itself that it never gets a value at any stage of the evaluation? Be honest about which of my verdicts I have to drop, and which clause of your principle each case is paying for.
