I'm writing a seminar paper on how a Bayesian should revise when experience doesn't deliver anything for certain, and I've worked myself into an argument whose conclusion I can't accept but whose steps I can't break. I'd like you to find the break, and then tell me what it costs to repair.

Setup. Let P be my credence function over a finite algebra, so that there is a finest partition: the one whose cells are the individual atoms (maximally specific ways things might be). The rule under discussion: when an experience shifts my credences across a partition E₁, …, Eₙ from P(Eᵢ) to qᵢ, and rigidity holds for that partition — my conditional credences P(A | Eᵢ) are undisturbed, for every proposition A and every cell — then my new credence in A should be Q(A) = Σᵢ qᵢ · P(A | Eᵢ). The paradigm it is supposed to get right is the dim-lighting case: I glimpse a piece of cloth in bad light, my credence that it is red moves to 0.7, nothing else in my belief state is directly touched, and the redistribution propagates outward exactly as the rule says.

The argument:

(1) The norm for revising on uncertain evidence is that rule: fix your credences conditional on the cells of a partition and let the experience redistribute credence across the cells.

(2) Take any prior P and any posterior Q whatsoever, subject only to Q's assigning positive credence to nothing that P assigned zero. Now let the partition be the finest one, whose cells are the atoms. For any atom w that retains positive credence, P(A | w) and Q(A | w) are both 1 or both 0, so rigidity holds trivially; and if the experience is credited with moving my credence in each atom from P(w) to Q(w), the rule returns Q exactly.

(3) So every possible belief change is a correct instance of the rule: becoming convinced of a contradiction on a whim, becoming more confident in a hypothesis precisely because you have just been shown evidence against it, dropping a well-confirmed theory for no reason, and — since the construction doesn't care — every case where everyone agrees the rule is supposed to fail, such as an experience that teaches you a new connection between how things look and what is true.

(4) But a rule that licenses every possible change licenses nothing. It is not a norm; it can underwrite no verdict about any actual case, including the ones it was introduced to handle.

(5) The same construction applies when the evidence is certain: learning E outright is also a redistribution over the atomic partition. So the distinction between conditioning and kinematics — the distinction the apparatus exists to draw — evaporates as well, and the much-discussed failure of commutativity stops being a fact about the rule and becomes a fact about which representation you happen to write down.

(6) Therefore either (1) is not yet a complete statement of the norm, or there is no distinctive norm for uncertain evidence, and the sense that the rule solves a problem is an artifact of fixing the partition by hand and then not noticing that the choice did all the work.

What I've tried. The obvious repair is that the partition isn't up for grabs: the experience is directly about some particular partition, and rigidity is required with respect to that one. But I can't see what settles which partition that is without either an appeal to the phenomenology of the experience — which looks psychologistic and, in the dim-lighting case, looks too coarse to capture what the light does to me — or an appeal to the propositions the experience bears on, which looks circular, since which propositions it bears on just is which conditional credences it leaves alone. I also tried the minimal-change gloss: the rule is what you get by minimizing divergence from your prior among all credence functions that match the new marginals over the partition. That makes the partition do even more visible work. Stated over the atoms, the constraints pin down Q outright and minimization says nothing; stated over a coarse partition, minimality is what generates rigidity. Either way the partition constraint is the entire content of the norm.

What I want. Name the step where this goes wrong and say why that step looked compelling. Then price the exits I haven't taken. If the normative content really has to be carried by a constraint on which partitions are admissible, what could such a constraint be, and what does accepting it cost — does revision become relative to a language, to a conceptual scheme, to the agent's psychology, or to something else? Is there any version of the norm that survives without it, for instance by treating the experience as a proposition in its own right with a likelihood attached and conditioning on that in the ordinary way? I would most like to keep two claims: that some revisions are flatly irrational, and that there is something distinctive about learning from an experience that leaves you unsure what you saw. I am happy to give up the claim that the rule as usually stated is complete. If you think the argument is sound and the conclusion should be embraced, say so, and say what a positive account of rational revision looks like once kinematics is set aside. Please argue in your own terms rather than by appeal to what the literature has settled; I'll do the citations myself.
