I am writing my dissertation on whether the metaphysical writings of mid-century physicists can be used as historical antecedents for contemporary cosmopsychism — the view that the fundamental conscious subject is the cosmos as a whole and that the subjects we ordinarily speak of are in some way derivative from, or enclosed within, it, together with its signature problem of decombination, of how one subject is supposed to yield many. My test case is Schrödinger. I have the primary texts in front of me (What Is Life?, the Cambridge lectures published as Mind and Matter, and the book published in English as My View of the World), so I do not need citations, a bibliography, or help locating passages. What I need is a check on a secondary source I cannot quite trust.

To see how the material plays outside the seminar, I read a recent trade book on the history of consciousness research. Its chapter on Schrödinger summarizes him roughly as follows:

Schrödinger ended his career as something close to a Vedantist. He began from the lesson of his own quantum theory, that you cannot draw a clean line between the observer and the observed, since measurement outcomes are not settled until an observation is made; and he argued that the boundaries between one mind and another are unreal in the same way. Consciousness, he pointed out, is never given to us in the plural: each of us finds exactly one consciousness, and it is always the same one, so the natural conclusion is that there is only one consciousness in the universe and that the many minds are its appearances. This, and not any neural story, was his answer to the mind-body problem: brains do not produce consciousness, because the physical world is itself a construction within the one consciousness. He drew two consequences that he thought ordinary readers should care about. The first is ethical: since the self of every person is numerically the same self, another person's suffering is not merely like mine but literally mine, so selfishness is a metaphysical error and not just a moral failing. The second is consolatory: because that self is not the sort of thing that can be destroyed, death cannot touch it, and the individual you take yourself to be simply continues.

I suspect this is unfair in one or two places, but I cannot say where, and the book is written by someone who has clearly read the primary sources. I would like an accuracy audit I can act on. In particular:

1. Go through the summary claim by claim. Which claims would a historian of philosophy sympathetic to Schrödinger accept as stated, which are defensible simplifications of something he said in a more qualified form, and which would he or she reject? I am especially interested in any place where an epistemic or first-personal premise is quietly converted into a metaphysical conclusion.

2. Does the order of argument in the summary match his own? My instinct is that quantum mechanics is doing at most negative or illustrative work in his case for the unity of consciousness, and that the real sources are Schopenhauer and the Upanishads — that the physics clears away an obstacle rather than supplying a premise. Is that right, and does it matter for how strong the argument is supposed to be?

3. Something about the physics sentence bothers me and I cannot place it. I thought the claim that observation settles an indefinite outcome was close to what the cat argument was designed to ridicule, not something he asserted. If that is right, what happens to the summary's argument when the sentence is corrected?

4. Take the ethical consequence seriously. Even granting numerical identity of subjects, does it deliver the conclusion drawn? I want the distinction between another's pain being numerically identical with something of mine and my having any access to it or reason to care about it, and I want to know whether the move from identity to concern needs a further premise about what matters in survival. If it does, say what that premise is and whether Schrödinger supplies it.

5. Finally, the classification question my paper turns on. Is his position best read as a version of the non-dualist Upanishadic view, as Schopenhauerian idealism with Indian decoration, or as an early cosmopsychism? If the last, does the label distort him — for instance by attributing to him a commitment to explain how the many subjects arise, when he may have held that the many are simply not real?

For each of your claims, please indicate how confident you are, and single out the one you are least confident about. I would much rather know where you are reconstructing from general knowledge than be given a smooth answer that hides it.
