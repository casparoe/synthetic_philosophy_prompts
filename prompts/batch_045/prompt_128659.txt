I am a professor of philosophy, and next month I am presenting the third chapter of a book manuscript on social aggregation under uncertainty at a works-in-progress workshop hosted jointly by a political theory group and a public economics group. I would like you to work as a formal welfare economist and decision theorist who has published on both Harsanyi-style aggregation and non-expected-utility models, and to help me prepare for the question period. Reason the mathematics through carefully before you write anything down.

My thesis in the chapter is that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem does not really depend on the independence axiom. Suppose each individual's preferences over objective lotteries on social outcomes are represented in the style of Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility: a utility index u_i over outcomes together with a strictly increasing risk function r_i applied to cumulative probabilities, so that the weight an outcome receives depends on the probability of doing at least as well as that outcome and not merely on its own probability. Add what I call the Common Risk condition: the individuals share one risk function r. Suppose the social ordering has the same sort of representation, with some u_0 and r_0. My claim is that Pareto indifference still does its old work and forces u_0 to be a positive weighted sum of the individual u_i, and that it also forces r_0 = r; I call the second half the Inheritance Lemma. I then use the lemma to argue that no aggregation theorem of this shape can underwrite a planner who is more risk-tolerant than the people she aggregates: the familiar thought that the state may be nearly risk-neutral because it pools risks has to be earned somewhere else, by pointing to idiosyncratic rather than aggregate risk, and cannot be read off the theorem. I also claim that the generalization is in one respect better placed than the original, since the risk function ranges over objective probabilities and is therefore interpersonally comparable without any convention for normalizing utility levels.

The room will be roughly this. Six or so welfare economists for whom Harsanyi's theorem is routine background: they think in terms of Pareto conditions, mixture spaces, and invariance under independent positive affine rescaling of the individual utilities, and they will want to know whether my weights are as underdetermined as his are, and whether Common Risk is really an interpersonal comparability assumption smuggled in under a technical name. Two formal decision theorists, one of whom has read the risk-weighted utility literature closely and is unsure whether it is a genuine alternative to expected utility or only a redescription of it, and who will care about how r is identified from preferences and about what happens to the representation once probabilities stop being objective. Three normative theorists of a prioritarian and sufficientarian temper who think ex ante aggregation is precisely where Harsanyi goes wrong and who will ask what is owed to people after the lottery is resolved. Nobody in the room works on my topic.

What I want from you is this. Work privately through a long list of candidate objections first, then discard the ones this particular room would not actually raise, and return only the survivors. Give me exactly five numbered items, no introduction and no conclusion, ordered by how much damage each would do me if I had no answer ready. Each item must be exactly two sentences: the first states the objection in the voice and vocabulary of the person most likely to raise it; the second says what I should concede up front, gives the reply I should then make in about ninety seconds, and closes by naming the point at which that reply is soft. Make the fifth item the question I am hoping nobody asks.
