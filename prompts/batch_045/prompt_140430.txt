I'm giving a department colloquium in four weeks — forty minutes plus thirty of questions — and I want a Q&A prep sheet, not feedback on the paper. Below is my thesis, a compressed sketch of the argument, and a description of the room.

**Thesis.** The dispute between dialetheists and their critics has been conducted at the wrong level. Both sides argue about whether some contradictions are true, but the only thing that has ever done real argumentative work is the question of what follows from a contradiction once you have one. My target is the standard dialetheist package: a naive truth theory, an LP-style semantics in which the both-true value is designated, and "classical recapture" for the fragment in which nothing is a glut. I claim this package cannot deliver recapture without an isolationist premise — it has to know in advance which sentences are gluts before it can license classical reasoning about them — and that glut-hood is not something the theory can circumscribe from inside. So the recapture the dialetheist offers is true but unusable.

**Positive proposal (compressed).** Instead of a binary designated/undesignated split, read the Kripkean jump as assigning each sentence a *grade*: the least stage of the construction at which that sentence's value stabilizes, or no grade at all if it never stabilizes. Inference rules are then licensed relative to grades, not absolutely — modus ponens, say, is licensed when the grade of the antecedent is strictly below the grade of the conditional to which it is applied. Sentences that stabilize early (which is all of ordinary empirical and mathematical practice) get fully classical reasoning; the liar and the Curry sentence get no grade, and take no rules with them. The selling point is that no sentence has to be flagged as paradoxical in advance: the grades fall out of the same construction that gives the semantics, so there is no isolationist premise, and Curry is blocked without giving up transitivity or contraction.

**Where I'll concede up front.** The grading is not in general computable, and the resulting consequence relation is almost certainly not recursively axiomatizable; I plan to concede both and argue that every fragment anyone reasons in is bounded and effectively graded.

**The room.**
1. Two senior philosophers of logic with fixed-point and contextualist sympathies: they think the right response to the liar is to restrict the T-scheme or the truth predicate, not to admit true contradictions, and they are fluent in groundedness, expressive strength, and revenge paradoxes.
2. One relevance-logic sympathizer: happily paraconsistent, firmly anti-dialetheist. She thinks non-explosiveness without gluts is the whole point, that the conditional must be relevant rather than extensional, and that Curry's real lesson is substructural.
3. A formal epistemologist who models rational belief with imprecise probabilities and non-Bayesian updating. He will hear "grade" as "degree of confidence" and will want to know what an agent is supposed to *do* with graded inconsistent inputs.
4. Two visitors from computer science who work on inconsistent specifications, database repair, and belief merging. They care about axiomatizability, decidability, complexity, and which structural rules survive.

**What I want.** Eight or nine objections, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by how likely they are, each labelled with who raises it and phrased the way that person would phrase it — their vocabulary, their examples, their standard moves. For each: a one-line note on whether I should concede it in the talk itself or wait for the question, a reply I could actually deliver in about two minutes, and a frank note on where that reply is soft, circular, or just changes the subject. I do not need the generic objections ("surely contradictions cannot be true", "this violates the law of non-contradiction"); I have fielded those for years.

There is one objection I am hoping nobody asks, and I have a specific dread that self-reference can be run again one level up, against the grades themselves rather than against truth. If you can see how that question goes, write it in the idiom of whoever in the room would be most likely to think of it, tell me how much of my proposal it takes down, and tell me honestly whether I have an answer.

Two constraints: no bibliography or citations of any kind, and keep notation minimal — explain any symbol you introduce.

Finally, close with a short note of your own, no more than a couple of paragraphs: which of your predictions about this audience you are least confident in; the single objection which, if it landed and I had no reply, would most change your assessment of whether the view survives the session; where you think my argument is weakest even if nobody in the room finds it; and any place where my sketch of LP, of classical recapture, or of the substructural responses to Curry is stated loosely enough that this particular room would call me on it.
