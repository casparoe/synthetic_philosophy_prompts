I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophical logic, and I need one essay question for the final that separates students who genuinely understand the problem of justifying deduction from students who have memorised the slogans that circulate about it. The topic is the regress in Lewis Carroll's brief dialogue about Achilles and the Tortoise.

What I want from you: a single self-contained essay question, plus a model answer of roughly 900 words, plus a marking scheme out of 25. Assume the exam is closed book, sixty minutes, with a target length of about a thousand words.

Constraints on the question itself.

It must state the regress in the question, in two or three sentences, so that a candidate who has never read the dialogue can answer it: the Tortoise grants Achilles every premise and every conditional premise Achilles adds, yet declines to accept the conclusion, apparently without contradicting himself, and the sequence of added conditionals never terminates. Do not presuppose knowledge of any particular secondary author. If the question invokes a position — for instance, the view that the moral is simply that a conditional asserted as a premise is not a licence to infer, or the view that a justification of a rule which itself employs that rule is circular only in a benign way — characterise the position in the question rather than naming its defenders.

It must be unanswerable by reciting that moral or by announcing that deduction cannot be justified without already relying on deduction. My students can all produce those two sentences; I want a question in which each of them earns at most partial credit.

The crux I want candidates to confront is a pair of proposed escapes from the regress. Both look decisive, and I do not think either succeeds; the question should force candidates to locate exactly where each fails and to say what that failure shows about what a justification of deduction would have to be.

The first escape, which I will call the closure escape. The Tortoise's commitments — say A, and if A then Z — are already jointly inconsistent with the denial of Z. So, it might be said, the Tortoise never needs to perform any act of inference at all: if he is committed to the closure of his commitments under logical consequence, he is committed to Z already, and the regress is an artefact of thinking of reasoning as a sequence of steps. The question should press the following: to extract Z from closure, the Tortoise must first establish the entailment fact, that A together with if A then Z entails Z; and establishing that is itself an exercise of deductive reasoning. Does the escape therefore merely relocate the regress one level up, into the Tortoise's(title) warrant for his knowledge of entailment facts? And is the relocated regress the same regress or a different one — does it matter that the reasoning moved to is semantic or metalinguistic rather than an object-level act of detachment?

The second escape, the mechanism escape. Suppose we simply install in the Tortoise a reliable disposition: whenever a sentence of the form if p then q and the sentence p are both before his mind, he comes to accept q. He now reaches Z in one step and the dialogue never begins. The question should ask whether this answers Carroll's problem or changes the subject. I want candidates to distinguish two readings of the Tortoise: on one he is a creature with a performance deficit, who cannot get himself to infer; on the other he is a creature asking a normative question, who grants every truth and every entailment and asks what reason he has to move. Supplying a mechanism cures the first and says nothing to the second. Candidates should be made to say which reading makes the dialogue a philosophical problem rather than a clinical one, and what follows about the kind of thing that could possibly count as an answer.

Finally, a strong answer should have a place for the rule-circular response: the suggestion that an argument for the reliability of modus ponens which uses modus ponens is circular only in a way that still discriminates between trustworthy and untrustworthy rules, since the same style of argument cannot be run, or cannot be run to the same effect, for a rule that licenses inferring the antecedent from a conditional together with its consequent. The question should ask whether a justification of that shape could satisfy the Tortoise on the normative reading, and whether the Tortoise is entitled to treat rule-circularity as a defect at all, given that what he is asking for is a reason rather than a cause.

What I want in the accompanying material.

A model answer of about 900 words that reaches an actual conclusion: does either escape work, and what shape would a genuine response to the normative reading have to take — a demonstration that deduction needs no justification, a demonstration that no justification is possible in principle, or an account of a non-inferential entitlement to infer? The model answer should commit to one of these and defend it; it should not close by saying that the matter is delicate and that more work is needed.

A marking scheme out of 25, organised around five or six must-hit points, each with its mark allocation and a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. The notes should name real errors, for example treating the closure escape as a restatement of the distinction between conditionals and rules of inference, or responding to the mechanism escape by saying only that a mechanism could in principle misfire.

A short closing note, no more than a paragraph, on what separates an answer in the 20–25 band from one in the 15–19 band: identify the single move that a first-class answer makes and that a competent but merely good answer omits.
