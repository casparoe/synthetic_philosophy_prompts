I wrote the following for my reading group and now I think it proves way to much, but I can't find the bad step.

(1) Definitions first. Say F is relevant to B, for an agent with background corpus T and credence function P, iff P(B | T and F) ≠ P(B | T) — i.e. if the agent took in F, its confidence in B would have to move. Say an agent's update procedure is update-complete iff: whenever the agent learns an F that is relevant to one of its standing beliefs B, the procedure at least consults B.

(2) Rationality requires update-completeness. A procedure that leaves B alone while the agent takes in something that bears on B is ignoring evidence, and ignoring evidence is paradigmatically irrational.

(3) Now take any F and any B you like. There is always some extension T' of the agent's corpus in which F is relevant to B: add one connecting auxiliary hypothesis and you are done (the book on the table is rigged to a switch that repaints the wall; the phone call turns out to be about the very thing you believed for unrelated reasons; whatever). And the agent can never rule out all such hypotheses.

(4) So no F is ever safely screened off from any B, and update-completeness requires that on learning anything at all, the agent consult every belief it holds.

(5) No finite agent can do that, and the reason it can't is just the frame problem in its inferential form.

(6) So either rationality is unachievable for any finite agent, or (2) is false.

(7) Finite agents are plainly rational some of the time. People cross streets, take phone calls, and revise their plans without freezing up. So (2) is false: rationality does not require update-completeness.

(8) And then there's no frame problem to solve. It's an artifact of a demand that rationality never actually made, and decades of work went into a pseudo-problem.

I'd really like to keep (2) and (7) — ignoring evidence that bears on your beliefs does look like a rational failing to me — so I'd much rather give something up around (3) or (4). But (3) looks like nothing more than a fact about how expressive our language is: you can always write down some hypothesis that links two things. Which step do you reject, and why did it look fine? What does rejecting it cost you — in particular, what are you then committed to saying about (2)? And does the frame problem survive in any form, or does it turn into an empirical question about the environments agents actually live in rather than an a priori one?
