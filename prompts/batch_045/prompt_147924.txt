I sat on a university hiring committee this spring, and the experience left me puzzled about something I can't quite formulate. In the same week, all of us were required to complete the campus bias-training module — which argued, at length, that unstructured holistic judgments of a person's character are noisy, biased, and barely predictive of anything — and we were also asked to write third-year review letters assessing colleagues on "collegiality," and to sign a form certifying that a candidate is of "good moral character." Since then I keep seeing the same shape elsewhere: bar admission character-and-fitness questionnaires, security-clearance adjudications, reference letters, the way one old act of dishonesty is treated as revelatory of a person while an equally old act of violence gets written off as youthful indiscretion, and the way nearly all of these institutions also maintain some kind of rehabilitation window after which past conduct stops counting. It looks like every institution that judges character also trains its own members not to.

I've been reading about the situationist critique of virtue ethics — the argument, drawn from social psychology, that behavior is driven mostly by features of situations rather than by stable global traits (the obedience experiments, the seminary students who hurried past a stranger slumped in a doorway when they were late), so that attributing robust character traits to people is a systematic error. Taken at face value, it makes my committee's practice look incoherent: we are trained not to make exactly the judgment we are then asked to certify. But I don't trust that reaction, and I'd rather have the implicit theory reconstructed properly than a verdict on whether the critique is true.

So: what view of character do these practices actually presuppose? Do they strictly require global-trait realism of the sort the situationist denies, or would something much thinner do the work — only that conduct in one situation predicts conduct in relevantly similar ones, or that a person's history is evidence about how they will respond to the specific situations a role puts them in? Which rival views do they quietly rule out: thoroughgoing situationism, a local-traits-only view, a view on which character is a normative ideal or a regulative fiction rather than a psychological posit? Are their parts even consistent with one another — can one institution coherently treat single acts as revelatory, discount old ones, and honor a rehabilitation window, when each of those seems to assume something different about how stable character is and how diagnostic any given act is? Where, if anywhere, is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several quite different theories of character would license it equally well? And the case I actually care about: suppose a licensing board took the critique seriously and replaced its global "good moral character" finding with something situation-indexed — referees rate candidates only on specified domains ("admits their own mistakes when they'd gain from hiding them," "reliable under deadline pressure," "behaves similarly toward people with less power"), are explicitly told not to aggregate those ratings into any overall verdict, and must ignore conduct more than seven years old. Would that be a practice a situationist could endorse, or has the trait attribution merely moved one level down, into the domains? What would have to change institutionally for the reform to be real rather than cosmetic, and what would we give up?
