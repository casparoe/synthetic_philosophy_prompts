Question I keep chewing on at work — I build training infrastructure, no philosophy background at all, so please write your answer in British English and go easy on the jargon, or define it when you use it. I'm American, so ignore my spelling.

So there's this problem I fell into: you train a model to predict the next frame from a camera watching a diamond in a vault. It gets really good. Now you want to ask it whether the diamond is really still there — because maybe someone swapped it for a perfect fake and also patched the camera feed, so the video looks identical either way. Next-frame prediction alone doesn't settle it. And the worry is that if you just bolt a question-answering head on and fine-tune it, you've either trained a new little classifier that's right for dumb reasons, or taught the model to confabulate confidently, or built something that will lie to you the moment it matters. Call a method good if it's actually pulling out what the model believes about the world, bad if it's doing one of those other things.

Here's my gut on a bunch of concrete setups, and I can't get them to add up:

(a) Fine-tune an answer head on labels made by guards who watched the tape afterwards. Bad. Its just supervison on a new task; the head could be reading some lighting artifact.

(b) Same fine-tune, same shape of training signal, but the labels come from a simulator where I control ground truth — I know I deleted the diamond in that run. Good, or at least much better. Which is odd, because (a) and (b) are the same operation with a different label source.

(c) No training at all: freeze everything, append the question to the frame sequence as plain text. Feels the most legit — I haven't touched the model — and also the least trustworthy; I basicly don't believe the answer. Both at once.

(d) Test-time blinding: ask the question while feeding black frames, or masking the visual input. If it still beats chance, that's gold-standard evidence for me. Good, strongly.

(e) Hold out whole environments: train the answerer across a bunch of simulated vaults, then test it zero-shot in a setup it never saw, including a tampering method that's new to it. Good — maybe the best of the lot.

(f) Adversarial training: a red-team loop that searches for inputs where the answerer is wrong and trains against them, and after weeks of that it still holds up. I feel like this counts for a lot, and I can't say why it should.

(g) Consistency training: force the same answer across a thousand paraphrases and under repeated 'are you sure?' pressure. Bad — a stably wrong answerer passes. Whatever this measures, it isn't what I want.

(h) The swap that never matters: the diamond is swapped, the feed is patched, nobody is ever going to check, and every observation from now on is identical either way. Asked 'is the diamond there?', I want the model to say no. But I also think a pure next-frame predictor has no training pressure to represent that difference at all, so I might be asking for something that was never in there. I flip on this weekly, and it seems to collide with (d).

(i) Causal tinkering: find a direction in activation space, nudge it, watch the predictions and the answers move together. Meh. Shows the representation is involved in producing the answer; shows nothing about whether it tracks the world.

(j) The reporter is a separate small model trained only on the big model's activations and never on the world. I trust this less than an extra head on the big model itself — which contradicts (a), where fine-tuning the model's own head was bad.

Am I grading by vibes, or is there a principle? My own attempt: a method counts only if you can't explain its success without the model representing the world-state. Test that against the cases above and repair it, or throw it out and give me one or two or three candidates if they conflict. For each, I want the clause tied to the case that motivated it, and I want you to be straight about which of my verdicts it can't save. Also, what does your principle say about a case I haven't listed — say, asking whether the cleaning crew moved the diamond to a shelf the camera can't see, three weeks ago, in a way that will never show up in the feed?
