I am writing a short position statement on the special composition question and, before I commit myself to it in print, I would like an audit of whether the package I have assembled actually hangs together. Here is where I stand:

1. Against nihilism: there are composite material objects — organisms, but also artifacts such as bicycles and carburettors — and ordinary claims about them are literally true. I also want to say, in the standard statue-and-clay sort of case, that there is exactly one thing there rather than two coincident objects.

2. Against universalism: composition is restricted. There is no object composed of my bicycle and the far side of the Moon, and I am unmoved by the argument that unrestricted composition is worth buying because it lets a theory get by with a more economical ideology, paying for that economy only in the number of objects it admits.

3. Against brutalism: the correct answer must state an informative, non-arbitrary condition on when some things compose something. I reject the view that the facts about composition are simply brute and unstructured, and I equally reject as uninformative any condition of the form: the things compose something just in case they are arranged bicycle-wise.

4. Against deflationism: the disagreement between the nihilist, the universalist and the moderate is a first-order dispute about what there is. It is not a dispute about our concepts, our words, or about what our quantifiers mean, and I do not think the easy or conventionalist treatments of existence questions settle the matter.

5. Causal idleness: composing something adds no causal powers. The causal profile of a composite is fixed by its proper parts and their arrangement; there is no downward causation and no novel causal power that appears only at the level of the composite.

6. The restriction, whatever exactly it is, has to be grounded in something like causal or dynamic integration among the parts. That is the only place I can see to draw a non-arbitrary line, and I am attracted to a generalization of the life-based criterion, extended far enough that artifacts come back in. I cannot accept that criterion in the demanding form its best-known defender gives it, since on that form there are no bicycles and no buildings.

7. Against groups: there are no corporations, universities or states over and above the people who make them up and what those people do. No group object, no group agent.

8. I accept the argument from vagueness: it can be genuinely indeterminate whether some things compose something, and I want the view to accommodate that rather than explain it away as mere indecision on our part about how to describe a perfectly determinate reality.

What I want from you: work through these and tell me which of them pull against each other, ordered by severity, and which are only apparently in tension. The pair I keep circling back to is 1 and 2 — the familiar complaint that nihilism and universalism are the only stable resting places — but I am not confident that this is where the real trouble lies. For each genuine conflict, say which commitment would have to be given up or qualified, and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set; then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please discriminate rather than flag everything: I would rather be told that two or three of these pairings are fine as they stand than be handed a list in which all eight items are equally suspect.
