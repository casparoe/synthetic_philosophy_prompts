I sit on the methods committee of a psychology journal that is rewriting its editorial policy, and I keep coming back to a norm that nearly everyone in my field accepts but that nobody on the committee can state precisely. The norm is that a hypothesis earns much less confirmatory credit if it was formulated after the investigators saw the data. Concretely: we run "registered reports," in which the design and analysis plan are peer-reviewed and time-stamped before data collection and the paper is accepted in principle whatever comes out; we treat hypotheses reverse-engineered out of a finished dataset — the thing the methodological literature calls HARKing — as nearly worthless as evidence, even when the fit the authors report is excellent and would have impressed us if it had been predicted in advance; and we are now toying with making this quantitative, by attaching to every piece of evidence a "novelty weight" between 0 and 1, set by how much of the hypothesis was fixed in a sealed archive before the data existed, to be multiplied into whatever evidential score the study would otherwise receive. What puzzles me is that the same community, in the same breath, treats explaining facts that were already on the books as the highest form of scientific achievement. Biologists still cite it as the strength of a unifying theory that it accounts for a pile of previously unrelated, long-known findings — anatomical homologies, island distributions, the sequence of forms in the fossil record — none of which anyone predicted. Physicists treat the recovery of Mercury's anomalous perihelion precession, a discrepancy of a few dozen arcseconds per century that had been on the books since 1859, as a triumph of general relativity rather than a discount. And in astronomy and genomics, purely exploratory work — dredge the survey, spot the pattern, then think of the mechanism — is normal, prestigious practice rather than a scandal. We seem to be doing X on Tuesdays and Y on Thursdays.

A colleague says this is all one worry in disguise, and I cannot tell whether she is right. As I see it there are three candidate worries the norm might be tracking. First, that the fact was already known before the theory existed — the sheer "oldness" of the evidence, which is what people are pointing at when they note that on a straightforward Bayesian picture the evidence already has probability 1 for you, so conditioning on it cannot move you at all, the familiar complaint that probabilistic confirmation theory has trouble with old evidence. Second, that the data did double duty, being used to construct the hypothesis and then again to test it — the worry you get from a large hypothesis space plus an unblinded search, where with enough candidate hypotheses some of them will fit any dataset you like. Third, that the hypothesis could have been read off the old data by anyone who had the data and some ingenuity, whether or not it actually was; I have been calling this the "derivability audit," and my hunch is that it, rather than oldness or double-duty, is the thing our novelty weight ought really to be estimating. Are these three the same worry in different clothes, or genuinely different ones, and which of them does our actual practice track? More to the point: what would my committee be committing itself to by adopting the novelty weight? Would we have to hold that confirmation is not a two-place relation between a hypothesis and a body of evidence but a three-place one that also takes in the order of inquiry and who knew what when? Would we have to hold something irreducibly non-probabilistic — for instance, that a theory's explanatory unification of already-known facts is evidence in its own right, and not merely a symptom of a favourable likelihood ratio? Or is there a reading on which the whole policy is consistent with an austere probabilism: pre-registration as a mere instrument for keeping the effective hypothesis space and the reported likelihood honest, and old evidence handled by conditioning on the discovery that the theory entails the fact, or by reinstating the probability one would have assigned the fact before one learned it?

Please write your answer in the following six sections, with roughly these lengths: "The implicit theory" (about 300 words) — reconstruct as charitably as you can the view of evidence and confirmation that this editorial practice only makes sense under; "Strict presuppositions vs. convenient fits" (about 150 words) — separate what the practice cannot survive without from what it merely sits well with; "Oldness, double-duty, derivability" (about 250 words) — say whether the three worries coincide and which one the practice is actually tracking, with the case that discriminates between them; "The novelty weight" (about 200 words) — whether a single scalar is even coherent here, what a derivability audit would have to measure, and whether that is measurable in practice; "Under a strict probabilist picture" (about 200 words) — what would concretely have to change in editorial practice if we went Bayesian through and through, including anything we would have to stop doing; and "Bottom line" (about 80 words) — one recommendation to the committee, and the single sub-question on which it turns.
