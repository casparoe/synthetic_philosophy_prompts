I am putting together a classification instrument for a half-day workshop I run for a mixed group — animal-welfare scientists, veterinary regulators, and academic philosophers — who are asked to go on record, taxon by taxon, about which non-human animals they take to be conscious and on what basis. What I want from you is a decision tree, not a scored quiz: something we can walk through together in about twenty minutes, with no more than eight branch nodes and between five and seven leaves.

The point of the instrument is to sort participants by the reasoning they actually apply to evidence rather than by the theory label they would give themselves. So no node may ask which theory of consciousness a respondent accepts, and no node may ask them to name or recognise a position. Every node should turn on a concrete case or a concrete evidential situation, phrased so that a veterinarian with no philosophy training could answer it, and so that a philosopher cannot tell from the wording which animal the answer is supposed to commit them to.

I would like the tree built out of the logical space first, with the names attached only afterwards. In particular, the first three cuts should all concern what would settle the question for the respondent: what kind of evidence they treat as diagnostic, what they do when two such markers come apart, and whether they treat consciousness as something an animal can have to a degree or as something that is either present or absent. Only below that should the tree start to separate views about which animals are conscious. Where your branching permits a combination of answers that no position anyone actually holds occupies, say so, and say whether that is because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

The leaves must include at least: a view restricted to mammals and birds and keyed to a particular neural structure or its functional homologue; a view on which flexible aversive learning is sufficient and neural homology beside the point; a view on which the question is in principle undecidable for any non-human subject; and a genuinely gradualist, multi-dimensional profile view. Add whatever else you think belongs, including leaves nobody defends. Each leaf should carry the name the position goes by in the literature, or unnamed if it has none, one sentence of characterisation, and one sentence on its single most awkward commitment — the thing its holders most often decline to say out loud.

Three cases must do work at branch nodes. First, a marker dissociation: an animal with none of the neural apparatus usually taken to be required, whose avoidance learning is nonetheless reversed by analgesics and which trades off a noxious stimulus against access to a preferred resource. Second, a candidate subject that is a collective rather than an individual organism — a eusocial colony, or a siphonophore — where integration looks organism-grade at the level of the group and thin at the level of the individual worker or zooid. Third, a case in which the behaviour is outwardly indistinguishable from pain behaviour but the animal cannot learn from it: a fixed response that never updates.

Finally, the output of the tree for a given participant should not be a label alone. It should be a verdict profile across a fixed list: honeybee, octopus, decapod crab, teleost fish, reptile, bird, holometabolous insect larva, bivalve, nematode. For each leaf, tell me what profile it yields, and flag any two leaves whose profiles come out indistinguishable. I would much rather know which distinctions my instrument cannot make than be given a tree that appears to make them all.
