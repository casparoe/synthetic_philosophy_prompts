I came to philosophy late — most of my working life was in experimental physics, and now I go to a pub philosophy group on Tuesdays. One of the regulars, Doug, has kept an informal ledger for years of which things "count as thinking". His verdicts so far:

- a chess program that beats grandmasters by brute-force search, no learning, does nothing else: no
- one of those 1960s pattern-matching "therapist" programs that fooled a few people for about ten minutes: no
- a current large language model chatbot — text in, text out, no body, no senses: yes, a mind or near enough as makes no difference
- a honeybee swarm choosing a new nest site, where the collective does something no single bee could manage: no for the swarm (he allows the individual bees "maybe a bit")
- the control system of a self-driving car handling city traffic: no
- a human newborn: yes, and he isn't being cute about it
- a patient in a deep coma, no response to anything: yes, still a mind — this is the case he always reaches for to insist he's not a behaviourist

The case coming up on Tuesday is this: a system that learned over years by fiddling with objects in a simulated physical world, and can also drive a real robot arm. It plans ahead, remembers which puzzle boxes it has already solved, gets stuck and then abandons that approach for a completely different one, and builds jigs and tools to get at things it can't reach. It has no language at all — nothing you could hold a conversation with — and it's problem solving looks nothing like human problem solving: no steps you could put into words, just physical trial and error.

Take it seriously and work it through before you commit to anything: you're a philosopher of mind who also knows how systems like these are actually built. Start by listing every rule you can think of that fits all seven of his earlier verdicts at once, including the ones he himself probably wouldn't put that way, then work out what each one says about the new case.

Then give me a table, one row per candidate rule (I'd guess two or three genuine ones — don't pad it out to a number), with these columns:

- rule: one-sentence statement of it
- verdicts_explained: which of the seven it handles easily, and which one it has to work to accommodate
- prediction: does Doug say yes, no, or does he squirm — plus one clause saying why
- discriminating_case: the one further case I could put to him on Tuesday that would separate this rule from the others

Under the table, a short block with: your own best guess, how confident you are, and the single sentence you'd say to him if you had thirty seconds. Skip citations and reading lists — I'm not going to look any of it up.
