I am a retired physical chemist — some thirty years in statistical thermodynamics and spectroscopy — and for the past two years I have been reading philosophy of mind and ethics seriously for the first time. I have become stuck on a problem that, from where I stand, looks like a problem I already know how to handle. I want you to attempt the translation honestly and then tell me exactly where it breaks.

The problem is the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. As I understand it: the hedonist says that what makes something good for a person is that it is pleasant, and the objector replies that pleasant experiences have no single qualitative feature in common — the pleasantness of a cold drink on a hot afternoon, of solving a hard problem, of hearing a cadence resolve, and of scratching an itch do not seem to share a phenomenally identifiable tone, the way they all share, say, the property of being had by me. As I have the map drawn, there are roughly three families of reply: the feeling theorist, who holds that there is such a common quality, a hedonic tone varying only in intensity and duration, which we overlook because attention goes to the object and not to the tone; the attitudinal theorist, who holds that what pleasant experiences share is not a quality but an attitude toward their object — one and the same enjoyment across wildly different contents; and the adverbial view, which holds that pleasantness is not a separable component or extra feeling at all but a way in which an experience is had, so that the demand for a common ingredient was misposed from the start. Correct me in a sentence or two if that taxonomy is wrong, but do not spend the answer on it; it is only the input.

Here is what I want. Restate those three positions — and the objection they answer — in the vocabulary I actually think in, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. The vocabulary is that of a physical chemist and amateur measurement theorist: macrovariables and microstates; state functions and their multiple realizability; reduction, of the kind where temperature is reduced to mean molecular kinetic energy and yet remains the same quantity in a gas, a crystal and a plasma; intensive versus extensive variables; operational definitions; latent variables inferred from several imperfect indicators; whether a battery of observables is best modelled by one factor or by several; natural kinds versus merely serviceable classifications. Take temperature as your worked example where it helps, since it is my home ground: a quantity that admits of a sharp operational definition, whose realizers have almost nothing in common microstructurally, and which nobody would think of denying merely because its instances differ in what the system is doing.

Then the residue report, which is the part I care about. For each of the three positions, tell me what translated cleanly, what you had to distort or fudge to get it into my vocabulary, and what could not be expressed in it at all. In particular, press on three points that I suspect are the crux — though say so if I have picked the wrong three.

First: whether the heterogeneity objection is one objection or two, a metaphysical one about whether there is a single property there to be found and an epistemological one about whether one scalar, or one latent factor, suffices to predict and explain the data — and whether the temperature analogy quietly discharges the first by changing the subject to the second.

Second: whether the attitudinal reply’s unity-by-relation rather than unity-by-quality is, or is not, the same manoeuvre as defining temperature functionally by its role in the causal network of thermodynamics — by what it does, equilibrating and driving heat flow and appearing in the equations — rather than by what it is intrinsically. If it is the same manoeuvre, what does the analogy buy the attitudinal theorist and what does it cost them? Does it cost them the connection to how pleasantness seems from the inside, which is precisely what the feeling theorist was trying to preserve?

Third: whether the adverbial view — pleasantness as a mode of experience rather than a component of it — has any counterpart whatsoever in a vocabulary which, like mine, has no slot for what-it-is-like-ness and treats every property as either structural and relational or as nothing.

And then the thing I most want your judgment on. Where the residue appears, is it evidence that my vocabulary is missing something real, or evidence that the philosophers’ question is less unified than it looks — that what do pleasures have in common is being asked with two different downstream purposes, one about the metaphysics of experience and one about what we should aim at in a life, which happen to share a word?

Be blunt where I have drawn the analogy too smoothly. I would rather be told that my temperature example is a rhetorical trick than have it passed off as a translation. Please give me no bibliographies, reading lists or citations: I have no library access, and I want the argument rather than the literature.
