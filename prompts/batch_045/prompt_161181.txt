hey — I run a tiny reading group and two of the regulars have been circling each other on Benacerraf's dilemma for about a month, so I want to force them to actually produce something. You know the shape of the thing: give mathematical sentences uniform, Tarski-ish truth conditions — "there are infinitely many twin primes" is true iff there really are infinitely many of them, same as "there are infinitely many stars" — and you owe an account of how anyone could ever *know* such things, given that our best epistemologies want some causal or causal-ish link between knower and known and abstracta don't push on anything. Keep the causal-ish requirement and you have to do something clever to the semantics instead. Party A is the plenitudinous Platonist: every consistent mathematical theory is true of some part of a vast platonic realm, so having a consistent theory already secures truth and there's no deep access problem left over. Party B is the fictionalist: mathematical claims aren't literally true, they're claims made inside a useful fiction, and what we can really know is what follows from the fiction plus that it's conservative over the nominalistic stuff.

Write the joint statement they'd both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section — what both can sign about the semantics, about what a theory of mathematical knowledge has to deliver, about what Benacerraf-style reasoning actually rules out vs. what it just makes awkward, and about empirical applicability (I suspect both can agree the "no miracles" argument is a seperate argument that doesn't settle this one, but say so if you think that's wrong).
2. A disagreements table: the issue | the formulation Party A would sign | the formulation Party B would sign | genuine or merely verbal. The wording is the whole game here — each side should be able to put its name under its own cell without wincing, and nothing in either cell should be so weak the other side could sign it too.
3. The cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. For each crux give a case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance.

Please use at least these two cruxes of mine, and add any of your own that cuts deeper:

- The oracle: a system trained on the mathematical literature and on computation traces spits out verdicts on Pi-1 arithmetical statements, the Goldbach-ish kind, "for all n, P(n)" with P mechanically checkable. On ten thousand held-out statements whose truth values were only settled years later by brute computation or very long proofs it was right 99.7% of the time, and it keeps being right. No proofs come out of it and nobody has a mechanism story. Does a mathematician who consults it and forms a true belief thereby *know* the statement? A says yes, because counterfactual sensitivity to the truth was always the real requirement and causation was a red herring; B says no, because perfect correlation with mathematical truth is itself just another mathematical fact you can grant without granting any objects. What would each count as settled by further probing of the oracle?
- The deviant community: a research community whose practices are exactly as disciplined as ours but which works in a consistent yet omega-inconsistent theory, so it cheerfully proves "there is an n with P(n)" while proving not-P of every numeral. Plenitude makes its theory true of something. Is that a feature — the reliability of mathematics explained without mystery — or is it the objection that plenitude makes truth so cheap that no belief could ever fail to latch on, so that "knowledge" has been bought at the price of not being able to be wrong?

Also tell me where exactly this is verbal and why, and whether anything either side could actually do would move the debate or whether it just sits there. Dont pad it with citations or a reading list, nobody needs to look anything up, and no hedging mush like "both views raise important questions" — if a formulation would be vacuous, say so and give the strongest non-vacuous one you can.
