I'm putting together a problem set for an upper-level seminar on confirmation theory, and I keep running into the same frustration: every example I try for the problem of old evidence either collapses back into the usual chestnut about Mercury's perihelion and general relativity — which half the class has already read about somewhere and can recite — or else it only seems to work because I've quietly smuggled in some fact the agents in the story didn't already have. So I want to build a clean case from scratch and run it against the standard machinery, and I'd like you to design it with me.

Here is the specification. Construct a short episode — one paragraph, no more — set in some concrete field I can picture (epidemiology, structural engineering, historical linguistics, agricultural science, whatever you like), in which a piece of evidence E is genuinely old: known for decades, in the handbooks, assigned probability 1 by every competent researcher in the field long before the theory H ever appears. Yet E must genuinely and strongly confirm H. Three blocking conditions, because each one is there to kill off a fix my students will propose within thirty seconds: (1) nobody in the story learns any new logical, mathematical, or computational fact — the entailment from H to E is obvious to everyone the moment H is stated, so no confirmation can be squeezed out of conditionalizing on "H implies E"; (2) E is not the sort of thing one can suppose away — under every live rival theory on the table at the time, E was antecedently very probable anyway, so the maneuver of asking how surprised you would have been by E had H been false comes out low-surprise across the board and discriminates between nothing; (3) H is not reverse-engineered to fit E — it is proposed for independent reasons, and accommodating E is a bonus rather than its motivation. I want the pull of the intuition to be strong and unembarrassed, the sort of thing a working researcher would say out loud at a conference, not a contrivance only a philosopher would defend. No omniscient agents, no ideal reasoners, no science fiction: a lab, a field site, an archive, or a data set.

Then do the work on the case. Assign rough credences yourself and flag them as your own stipulations, and show what each of three approaches actually delivers and why: first, straight Bayesian conditionalization on E; second, the fix that says what you really learn is not the datum but the logical fact that H entails it; third, the counterfactual fix, where you ask what your credence in E would have been had you not known it and had H been false. My expectation is that all three deliver approximately nothing, or the wrong verdict, given the blocking conditions — but demonstrate that rather than asserting it. After that, isolate the single feature of the case that is really carrying the confirmatory weight, show that the confirmation evaporates if you remove that feature while holding everything else fixed, and say which of the three approaches, if any, can be patched to capture it and what the patch costs. Finally, since I will get pushback in seminar: give me the strongest version of the objection that there is no genuine problem here — that once the agents' total evidence is described properly, or the space of hypotheses is re-cut in the right way, ordinary Bayesian updating returns the right answer and my case only looks like a counterexample because I set up the partition badly — and then tell me honestly whether that objection defeats the case or merely relocates the difficulty. If you think the three blocking conditions cannot be jointly satisfied by any case in which confirmation is real, say so explicitly, tell me which condition you would relax and why, and give the best example you can under the relaxed version. Please write your answer in American English.
