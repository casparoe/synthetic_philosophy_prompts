I help run a small rationality-meetup thing in the back room of a pub in Bristol, mostly software people plus a couple of friends of friends from the university, and I've stupidly agreed to give the talk next month — twenty-five minutes and then questions. I'm not an academic at all, I work in IT procurement and got into all this a few years ago after falling down a rabbithole about the prisoner's dilemma, so I know enough to get myself into trouble but not enough to get out of it. It's the questions bit I'm dreading.

The talk is my own attempt to fix a thing that's always bothered me about Hofstadter's superrationality idea — you know, the thought that if you're in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with someone who reasons the way you do, your two decisions aren't really independent, so you should just pick the move you'd want the whole class of similar reasoners to make, and cooperate. My complaint is that nobody in real life is ever an exact copy of anyone, so I've built a version where the mirroring is a matter of degree. I call it the mirror coefficient: a number m between 0 and 1 for how likely it is that whatever you decide, the other person's reasoning lands on the same decision. Then you just do the sums — if I defect I get the mutual-defection payoff whatever happens (they either mirror me into defecting, or they're an independent stranger who defects anyway), so that's 1; if I cooperate I get 3 if they mirror me and 0 if they don't, so 3m. Which means cooperation only pays once m is above about a third, and below that you should defect like a normal person. I like that it turns superrationality into a threshold instead of a club you're either in or not, but I've noticed that the temptation payoff of 5 drops out of the maths entirely, which feels like it shouldn't happen, and the last five minutes where I try to say how you'd actually *estimate* m in practice — shared culture, shared training, whether you'd both read the same blogs — is where I think the whole arguement is most exposed.

The room'll be maybe thirty people, and it's a mixed bunch: a good few software engineers who've read the rationalist blogs and are very into functional decision theory (the "decide as if you're choosing the output of the algorithm you're running" line) and will want to know what my rule says when the other person's algorithm is only partly related to mine; two or three economics/game-theory PhD students who think all of this was settled decades ago and that defecting in a one-shot game is just what rationality requires, and who will want it in their vocabulary — equilibrium, dominance, what the correlating device is supposed to be; and one older moral philosopher who is broadly sympathetic to the thought that rationality might permit you to dispose yourself to cooperate with other cooperators, but who thinks no amount of decision theory will get you there on its own. Plus a handful of people who'll ask friendly and completely unanswerable questions about climate change.

For each: a reply I could give in about a minute, an honest note on where my reply is weakest, and a flag for what I should just concede up front in the talk itself rather than get caught out on, versus what I should dig my heels in on.
