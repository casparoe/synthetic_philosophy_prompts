I write features on science and ideas, and I'm researching a long piece on where the units-of-selection debate now stands and where the effort in it should go next. Suppose a new private foundation asked you to advise it. It has money for about ten postdoctoral fellowships over three years, conceptual and theoretical work only, no new data collection. Its interest is narrow: it wants to know which evolutionary explanations should count as respectable when non-biologists invoke them — psychologists, anthropologists, economists, epidemiologists, anyone writing about human social behaviour. Call a question export-grade if a settled answer would change how someone outside evolutionary biology ought to phrase or defend such an explanation, and in-house if it mainly rearranges the terms of the internal dispute.

Which open questions in this area are genuinely export-grade, and how would you order them by urgency for this funder? Some candidates, which you should treat as provisional rather than binding: whether the gene's-eye view and multi-level selection differ causally or only as alternative ways of partitioning variance, and what causal would have to mean for that to be decidable without new data; what I think of as the grain problem, where the level at which heritable variation is transmitted does not match the level at which the selective interaction occurs, which looks like the live version of the question for cultural inheritance, epigenetic inheritance, and microbial systems with horizontal transfer; whether a higher-level description is ever more than bookkeeping, in the sense that it screens off the lower-level dynamics, generalizes to novel cases, or licenses interventions the lower-level description does not; whether any account of what is selected survives the fact that many candidate units — soma, demes, firms, languages — are not things with clean lineages; and whether the debate has testable content at all once cross-level byproducts and soft selection are on the table.

Defend the ordering rather than just asserting it: say how much hangs downstream on each question, how tractable it is for theorists with no new data, how neglected it is relative to the attention it gets, and how you weigh those criteria against each other. Say plainly which of these you take to be verbal or overrated and why. And for each of your top four, say what result or argument in the next three years would move it up or down the list. I don't want a literature review, a reading list, or citations — this is for a general audience and I need the arguments, not the sources — and I'd rather have four questions argued hard than ten gestured at.
