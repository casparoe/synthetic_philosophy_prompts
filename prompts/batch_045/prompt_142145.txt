I am a high school student writing an extended research paper on why psychiatric categories seem to change over time, and I would like help with a distinction I have been trying to draw and cannot quite make precise.

As I understand it, Ian Hacking's idea of looping effects is that a classification of people can be taken up by the people it classifies: they come to understand themselves and to behave in light of the description applied to them, experts then have to revise the classification to fit what these people are now like, and the revised description is in turn available for the classified to react to. On this picture the instability of a psychiatric kind comes from the fact that the people classified can know about the classification and respond to it, which is what makes such kinds, in Hacking's vocabulary, interactive, in contrast to the indifferent kinds of natural science, which are unaffected by how we describe them.

The mechanism I keep encountering in my reading seems to be a different one, and I have been provisionally calling it the accommodation ratchet. Once a diagnostic category exists, an apparatus grows around it: screening instruments used in schools and in primary care, referral pathways, insurance and educational accommodation codes, patient advocacy organizations, clinics whose staffing and funding track how many patients they see, and commercial markets for treatment. That apparatus then exerts pressure on both sides of the consulting room, giving people reasons to seek the classification and clinicians reasons to apply it, while the instruments that detect it get recalibrated against the wider and milder population now being screened. Measured prevalence rises; the rise is then reported as evidence that the condition had been under-recognized, which justifies further screening; and a category that has become a route to support, to identity, or to funding turns out to be very hard to shrink. Crucially, none of this seems to require the classified person to change, or even to know that the category exists.

So my question is a comparative one: how exactly does this accommodation ratchet differ from Hacking's looping effect, and is it genuinely a separate mechanism or only a special case of looping?

In answering, I would be grateful if you addressed three things in particular.

First, whether looping properly requires the classified people to be aware of the classification and to revise their self-understanding in its light. If it does, then something like the ratchet could presumably operate on an indifferent kind as well — an infectious or endocrine disease, say — whose measured prevalence also climbs as testing is extended down the severity gradient, without the disease changing at all. If that is right, what does it show about Hacking's interactive/indifferent distinction: is it incomplete as an account of why our classifications are unstable, or is it simply not trying to explain the same thing?

Second, how the two mechanisms differ in what they lead one to expect. My tentative suggestion is that looping should change the character of the condition itself — how it is experienced, narrated, and presented — whereas the ratchet should mainly change who is counted, pulling in milder and previously subthreshold cases at the boundary while the severe cases look much as they always did. Is that the right axis on which to distinguish them, and what sort of observation would actually discriminate between the two explanations of a rising prevalence?

Third, how far the two can be pulled apart at all. Might the ratchet be among the things that make looping possible in the first place, by building the institutions through which a diagnosis becomes publicly available as something to identify with? And if they are entangled in that way, should I treat the instability of psychiatric kinds as one phenomenon or as two?
