I convene a fortnightly seminar on moral responsibility that has now run for several years, and one argument in it recurs in nearly the same shape every time. Nearly everyone in the room now grants two things: that the bare truth of determinism cannot by itself settle whether anyone is responsible, and that some account of the reactive attitudes has to appear in any adequate theory. Past that point nothing moves. The Strawsonians — roughly, those who follow Wallace in treating holding responsible as a matter of judging that a moral obligation has been breached and of the warrant for the associated attitudes being supplied by that obligation rather than by the attitudes themselves — repeat that the rationalists have psychologised what is a normative relation. The reasons-responsiveness theorists — those who follow Fischer and Ravizza in locating responsible agency in the sensitivity to reasons of the mechanism that issues in action, with the reactive attitudes demoted to evidence or to downstream effects — repeat that the Strawsonians have described our practices without saying which agents qualify as their proper objects. A third group says Strawson quietly changed the subject; a fourth says he dissolved it. Every objection is one the other side has already absorbed.

What makes me think this is not a single dispute is that the two sides seem to me to be picking different *explananda*. One is asking what we are doing when we hold someone responsible — whether it is an act of address, a set of attitudes, a demand that the other party register. Another is asking when it is fitting or fair to do it. A third is asking what an agent must be like in order to be a possible object of it. Each camp then treats the other camps' answers as evasions rather than as answers to a different question. I want that hypothesis tested against three cases I have found useful for separating the questions.

First, the one-sided community. Imagine a population with intact moral judgment and intact self-directed reactive attitudes — guilt, remorse, a sense of owing repair — but no other-directed ones: no resentment, no indignation, no gratitude. They register injuries to themselves as damage to be repaired and as reasons to revise their expectations of the injurer, and they never resent anyone. Do they hold one another responsible? The theories appear to diverge here, and it is unclear to me whether the divergence is descriptive (could such a psychology exist, and would it be stable, given that resentment may do work in enforcing norms that repair alone cannot do) or normative (would the practice be missing something, or merely be less clamorous than ours).

Second, address without an audience: blame directed at the dead, at a permanently unconscious wrongdoer, at a corporation, at a system incapable of registering the address. If holding responsible is essentially transactional, these look degenerate; if it is not, the participant stance is not doing the constitutive work it is credited with. Our practice seems to split — we blame the dead but hesitate to punish them, and we demand apologies we know cannot arrive.

Third, a calibration test. Suppose it turned out empirically that resentment is keyed tightly to the cost of an injury and the closeness of the injurer and only loosely to the injurer's reasons-sensitivity, so that our attitudes are badly calibrated to the property the rationalists care about. Is that a debunking discovery or an irrelevant one? Both sides can claim it, which suggests to me that the live issue is the direction of explanation, not any fact awaiting discovery.

My hypothesis, then, is that the standoff is several disputes sharing a name: a conceptual question about which phenomenon is to be explained; a first-order normative question about the conditions under which blame is fair; a hidden empirical question about the actual structure and plasticity of the reactive attitudes; and possibly a bedrock disagreement about whether a moral life without blame would be impoverished or merely quieter.

I do not want a verdict on which theory is true, and I do not want a survey of the literature or a reading list. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, grounded in what each position actually claims, and an argument for the diagnosis rather than an assertion of it. Tell me which of these disagreements — or which other one — is really doing the work, and whether the conceptual, the normative and the empirical strands can be pulled apart at all. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress for the seminar: a distinction that dissolves the appearance of conflict (the fittingness of an attitude versus the fairness of a practice, say, or the split between attributability, answerability and accountability), an empirical result, a formal result, or a change of question. If your diagnosis implies that no progress should be expected, say so and say what the seminar should do with the hour instead. Argue for your answer in about 1,200 words, and say plainly where your diagnosis is itself contested.
