I write about the social sciences for a national newspaper, and for about a decade I've been the person the desk sends to one particular story: the cross-national finding that the more gender-egalitarian a society is by the standard measures — income, education, legal standing, physical security — the less similar men's and women's career choices tend to be. I have written that sentence, or something close to it, perhaps a dozen times, and every time I have to add a clause saying what the finding actually shows. I have never written a clause I believed, and I've concluded that the problem is mine rather than the copy desk's: my reactions to the concrete cases don't add up to anything I can state.

So I'd like to do this properly. Below are the cases and the verdicts I find myself holding, including the ones I can't reconcile. I don't need the underlying figures checked — the shape of each case is what matters — and I'm not looking for citations. I'm looking for the principle.

1. In a country well below my own on every equality index, women are roughly 40 per cent of engineering graduates; in mine, under 20. I do not read this as showing that women there are freer, or that women here are being steered away from engineering by anyone in particular. I read it as showing that where the alternatives are poor, a technical degree is a rational route to independence — which means the number tells me almost nothing about what women in either country would choose under better conditions.

2. In the Nordic countries, where the material penalties for any choice are lowest, occupational segregation by gender is not weaker but starker. Here I do treat the pattern as evidence about what people on average want — I'm willing to say that when you remove the constraints, people sort somewhat differently, and that this sorting is partly informative rather than merely residual.

3. In the same egalitarian country, medicine went from heavily male-dominated to female-dominated within about two generations, while engineering did not move comparably. I don't think either fact reveals a fixed trait, but I think the flip is the most important item on this list: it shows the magnitude of these gaps is not fixed by anything that moves on a biological timescale.

4. Stipulate a finding I haven't seen reported but which would not surprise me: in the most gender-equal country, women are four-fifths of the veterinarians who treat companion animals and a fifth of those who treat livestock, where the work pays better, is more mechanical, and involves far less client contact. Someone will say this is the familiar people-versus-things contrast. I don't think that contrast explains it, and more to the point I don't think it predicts it — it can be made to fit a result and its opposite, which is why I distrust it.

5. A multinational headquartered in an egalitarian country adopts a 40-per-cent target for women in its engineering hires and meets it by recruiting in countries where engineering is more female, at wages that draw those women out of their home labour markets. I think this is objectionable, and not merely because I have views about targets: it looks to me like converting one society's constrained choices into another society's statistics.

6. Men are a small and shrinking share of entrants to teaching, psychology and veterinary medicine, and a large majority of both workplace fatalities and the prison population. If the first list is free choice and therefore not a justice matter, I cannot see how the workplace deaths are a justice matter — yet I want to say they are. About the prison population I want to say nothing at all in this connection, and I'd like to know whether that is evasion or a distinction I haven't articulated.

7. The case I cannot settle. Suppose we could raise a generation under full material equality with no gendered socialization of any kind, and the engineering intake came out 70–30. I do not know whether I would call that just, or a problem, or none of my business. My reaction oscillates between "that is what freedom looks like" and "no distribution of a society's money and standing that tracks a trait you were born with is just by default."

The rule I've been using in print is: the freer the choice, the more the outcome is evidence about preference and the less it is evidence of injustice. I now think it is too crude — it seems to license the inference I refuse in case 1, to make the flip in case 3 invisible, and to tell me nothing I can use in case 5 — and I would rather have it repaired than replaced with something I can only say while holding my nose.

What I need from you: one principle, or at most a small set of rival candidates, stated precisely enough that I could print it in a single sentence and then defend it for the rest of the paragraph; for each clause, the case that motivates it; an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would require me to give up (I'd rather be told which of them is the weak one than have all seven flattered); and its verdicts on the cases I haven't listed — case 7 above, and the case of a field that is entered mostly by women and then suffers a well-documented decline in pay and prestige, where I want to know whether the decline should change my view of how free the original sorting was. Finally, tell me where the principle commits me to something I would be uncomfortable saying on the radio, since that is usually where I find out what I actually think.
