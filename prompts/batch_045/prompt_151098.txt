I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and I am working on a piece about mid-century philosophers who intervened in public controversy. In a recently published encyclopedia of philosophy—the sort of reference work that compresses a whole episode into a single paragraph—I came across the following account of G. E. M. Anscombe's protest against Oxford's proposal to give Harry Truman an honorary degree after the atomic bombings:

"Anscombe's objection was not a pacifist objection to war, or even to aerial bombing as such. It was a point about the concept of murder. Murder, for her, is not defined by the horror or scale of a killing but by the intentional killing of the innocent. The inhabitants of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were innocent in the relevant sense: they were not combatants and were not supplying Japan's war effort. The United States killed them deliberately, as a means of forcing Japan to surrender. That makes Truman a murderer, and for a university to honor him would be to honor a murderer. The standard excuses collapse under scrutiny. The defense that Truman only signed a piece of paper is like saying the man who hires an assassin has not killed anyone. The defense that, if killing harmless people is murder, then killing enemy soldiers in their sleep must also be murder, fails because Anscombe's distinction is between combatants and noncombatants, not between people who are dangerous at a given moment and people who are not. Her deepest objection, however, was to the doctrine of double effect. Catholic moralists, she thought, had turned double effect into a loophole: provided you describe your target as a military installation and describe the deaths of nearby civilians as merely foreseen rather than intended, you may annihilate a city. Anscombe regarded this as sophistry. If the deaths are foreseen, she held, they are intended; intention just is what you accept will happen. So the bombings were murder even if the crews were aiming at military objectives and merely foreseeing civilian deaths. Once this is understood, her pamphlet looks like an early statement of a total rejection of nuclear weapons: it implies that any use of a nuclear weapon, any policy of nuclear deterrence, and any willingness to keep a nuclear arsenal as a threat is murder in exactly the same sense, because in every case the deaths of innocents are either intended or cynically foreseen."

I am inclined to accept the first half: the bit about combatants and noncombatants, and about Truman's responsibility for what he ordered, matches what I have read elsewhere. But the paragraph on double effect and the closing claim about nuclear deterrence feel like a stretch to me, and I do not know enough about Anscombe's actual argument to say where the stretch begins. Could you give me an accuracy audit? Please go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a careful defender of Anscombe would accept as a fair statement of her view, what they would describe as a simplification or a subtle misrepresentation, and what they would say is simply wrong. I am especially interested in three things: whether she really equated foresight with intention, or whether she kept a distinction between intended and merely foreseen death that both supporters and opponents like to collapse; whether her distinction between combatants and non-combatants is correctly described as a status distinction rather than a moment-by-moment judgment about dangerousness; and whether the passage's final implication—that her argument generalizes to all nuclear weapons, to nuclear deterrence, and to merely maintaining an arsenal—actually follows once the earlier misrepresentations are corrected. If the summary is unfair, tell me what principle or distinction the summary has lost, and what a corrected version of that closing claim would look like. In particular, say what her view implies about a threat to use a nuclear weapon that is never carried out: is that murder, as the summary's closing sentence suggests, or does her account of intention and action have resources to treat the threat as some other kind of wrong?
