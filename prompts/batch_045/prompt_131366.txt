I am a novelist, and the book I have just finished has got itself entangled with a problem in the theory of translation that I never intended to write about. My protagonist — a restorer of frescoes, not a scholar — spends the last third of the novel trying to render into English the short Renaissance French sickroom lyric that Hofstadter built his 1997 book around, the one he nicknames Ma Mignonne; the versions she produces are printed in the text as she produces them, and they carry a good deal of the book's argument. Since I could not write her convincingly without doing the work myself, I have spent several years on my own suite of English versions, made under three self-imposed ledgers: a syllable ledger (matched syllable count, line for line), a consonant ledger (my name for the practice of reproducing, in sequence, the line-initial phoneme of each line across the whole poem), and a register ratio (matching the proportion of learned, Latinate vocabulary to plain vernacular words in my text to the proportion in the French, on the theory that what a reader actually hears is the distance between the poem's diction and the speech of the street). I have also written a short afterword in which I try to say, as plainly as I can, what I take myself to be doing when I do this. My editor wants the afterword hardened before we go to copy-edit, and a translation scholar I admire has agreed to discuss it with me at a public event in the spring, so I would like to know whether what I have written actually hangs together before someone else points out that it does not.

Roughly, the commitments in the afterword are these. (a) The formal pattern — syllable counts, stanza shape, rhyme scheme — belongs to the identity of the poem, not to its packaging; a rendering that keeps the sense and abandons the pattern is a crib, useful but not a translation of the poem. (b) The actual sounds are not transferable and I do not pretend otherwise: I substitute an analogous music in English, and I treat the consonant ledger purely as a compositional device that hands me lines I would not otherwise have found, not as something that makes a version more faithful. (c) I always work from a literal prose crib, and I treat that crib as the authority on what the poem says; where a finished version and the crib conflict, the version is wrong. (d) Constraint is a discovery procedure: when the form hands me a word I would never have chosen, I take that as a reason to trust the form over my own intentions, and the lines I like best in my own suite are ones the form found rather than ones I found. (e) Even so, I require of myself that every departure from the crib be defensible by appeal to something in the original; I am not allowed to say only that the rhyme made me do it. (f) Register parity matters more than lexical literalness: I would rather move a concrete reference — this flower for that flower, this room for that room — than lose the distance between the poem's diction and ordinary speech of its moment. (g) I publish three or four variants side by side and deny that there is a single correct translation, while also having publicly called a couple of other people's published versions flatly wrong, which I stand by. (h) I think of the translator as a co-author of the English poem, and yet I insist on the credit line translated by and have refused the versions after formulation my publisher prefers. And there is one case I am genuinely unsure about: in the suite, one variant moves the poem's situation from a sixteenth-century sickroom to a contemporary hospital, on register-parity grounds, and I have printed it as a translation rather than as an imitation.

What I want from you is an audit rather than an introduction to the subject. Go through that list, tell me which pairs are really in tension and which only look like it, and rank the real ones by severity rather than listing them as equivalent; for each, say what I would have to give up and how costly that would be for someone with my practice — which is to say, what sentence in the afterword I would have to delete or rewrite. In particular I would like to know whether the crib's authority in (c) is even compatible with (a), whether (d) and (e) can both be true at once or whether one of them is doing work I have misdescribed, whether my insistence on the translated by credit is better supported by commitments I do not actually hold, and whether anything in my stated principles settles the hospital variant one way or the other or whether it simply falls through a gap. Finally, if there is one revision that would clean up more than the rest — one thing I could stop claiming that would leave the other claims intact — I would like that named, together with what it costs me.
