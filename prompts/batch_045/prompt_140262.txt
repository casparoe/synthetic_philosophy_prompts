I'm helping put together a small closed-door workshop in the spring — maybe fifteen people, mostly stratigraphers and Earth-system modellers plus two historians and a science-studies person — on the question of when the Anthropocene starts and who actually gets to decide. The two camps have been shouting past each other for years and the organiser's idea is to force them into an adversarial-collaboration document, so I need a draft of that document that neither side would immediately throw across the room.

The two positions:

- The mid-century camp: the boundary belongs in the 1950s, at the Great Acceleration — radionuclide fallout from atmospheric weapons testing, plastics, concrete, the sharp inflections in essentially every global curve. Their line is that a chronostratigraphic boundary exists for global time-correlation, so what matters is a signal that is essentially synchronous worldwide and durable in the rock record, and they think the earlier candidates fail on both counts.
- The long-Anthropocene camp: the boundary should mark the onset of large-scale human domination of Earth systems, which puts it somewhere between the Columbian exchange (the CO2 dip around 1610 and the biological homogenisation that followed) and the early-agriculture greenhouse hypothesis, with megafauna extinctions and anthropogenic fire as the deep option. Their line is that a mid-century GSSP tells a story about detectability, not about causation, and that treating detectability as the criterion quietly makes the unit a monument to our instruments.

Because I want the document to be more than a rehash of positions everyone has published, I want you to work with four stipulations I'm feeding in. Treat them as given — don't fact-check them, and don't worry that they're hypothetical:

1. A paper comes out with an annually laminated marine varve sequence off Namibia in which the first unambiguous plastic-particle horizon is dated to 1911, and it lags a comparable Northern Hemisphere section by twenty to forty years. Both camps accept the dating.
2. Someone proposes a "synchrony quotient": out of a reference network of forty globally distributed sections, the fraction in which a candidate marker's first appearance falls within plus or minus twenty-five years of the proposed boundary. A provisional rule is floated that a GSSP-worthy marker needs a quotient of at least 0.8.
3. Diagenesis modelling suggests the plastic and radionuclide signals degrade on very different timescales under burial, and that the 1610-era signal, while weaker, is more likely to survive a hundred thousand years of burial as a chemically distinct horizon than the plastic one is.
4. A draft "Cape Town Protocol" circulates, proposing to split the term in two: "Anthropocene" stays a formally defined chronostratigraphic unit decided by stratigraphic procedure, while a separate, non-stratigraphic period term for human history gets set by a mixed panel of historians, archaeologists, modellers and Indigenous representatives, with no requirement that the two boundaries coincide.

What I want from you:

A numbered agreed-claims section first — the substantive things both camps can sign. Not vacuities. I mean things like what a GSSP is for, what the current rules require, whether the choice of boundary has any bearing on the size of past human forcing, whether "first detectable human impact" is a coherent candidate boundary at all, whether the term is already doing political and pedagogical work that the formal decision can't undo, and what each side concedes about its own marker's weak point. If a claim is only signable because it's empty, leave it out and say why you left it out.

Then a disagreements table with these columns: the crux | the mid-century camp's position, in wording they'd accept for themselves | the long-Anthropocene camp's position, in wording they'd accept for themselves | a concrete case, argument, measurement or rule both would accept as genuinely bearing on that crux | the mid-century camp's predicted outcome for that case | the long camp's predicted outcome. Predictions should be written down in advance and be falsifiable — "this would be awkward for us" is not a prediction.

The cruxes I'd want covered at minimum: whether a chronostratigraphic boundary is a correlation tool or a causal marker (or whether the unit has to do both at once); how much diachroneity a boundary can tolerate before it stops being a boundary — use the synchrony quotient and the Namibian varves here; whether durability in the rock record should count for more than the magnitude of forcing, given the diagenesis result; who legitimately decides, given that the word is now load-bearing in history departments, museums and policy documents as well as in stratigraphy — run the Cape Town Protocol through this one, along with the question of whether splitting the term dissolves the dispute or just relocates it somewhere with worse referees; and whether any of the disagreement survives once you separate "onset of human forcing," "onset of a globally synchronous signal," and "onset of a distinct Earth-system state."

Finally, a short closing section: which parts of this dispute you think are genuinely verbal — about words or about the definition of a term rather than about the world — and which are about something real. And flag, for each camp, the one formulation of the question they'd refuse to sign even if it were neutral-sounding, because accepting the formulation already concedes the argument. Keep it in draft-document register, no citations, nothing that needs a library. Two typos are fine, I'll clean it up before it goes out.
