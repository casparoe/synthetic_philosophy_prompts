I'm in a graduate seminar on the economics and governance of AI, and I've built the following argument for a response paper. The conclusion is not one I believe, but I can't find the step to reject, and I'd be grateful for a diagnosis.

(1) Call the *alignment tax* whatever a developer gives up — capability, time-to-market, or margin — in order to ship an aligned system rather than the most capable system it could have shipped.

(2) A firm that bears a cost its rivals decline to bear loses margin and share to them; a firm that does this systematically exits the market.

(3) So in an unregulated competitive market, no firm will voluntarily bear an avoidable safety cost. Call this the competitiveness constraint.

(4) The alignment tax is an avoidable safety cost. So absent regulation, competition selects for unaligned systems, and the *race to the bottom* dynamic people worry about is real: the tax will be bid down toward zero, not because anyone wants unsafe systems, but because no one can afford to pay it alone.

(5) But (2)–(3) generalize. Crash-testing, seat belts, sterile manufacturing, load-bearing margins in bridges, cold chains in food — each is an avoidable cost that a rival could skip. If (2)–(3) were sound, no competitive firm would ever have adopted any of them voluntarily, and every market with more than one seller would collapse into a race to the bottom.

(6) That plainly isn't what happens. The standard explanation is that buyers can observe the safety-relevant property, or trusted third parties can certify it, and they value it at more than it costs; so the firm recovers the cost in the price. Competition doesn't select against costs as such. It selects against costs whose benefits the firm cannot recover — because the attribute is unobservable to the buyer, or because the benefits accrue to people who aren't the buyer.

(7) Apply (6) to AI. If alignment is observable — to the buyer, or to an auditor the buyer trusts — then firms that pay the tax are rewarded and firms that skip it are punished, and the market supplies alignment at the efficient level. If it isn't observable, the remedy is evals, audits, certification, disclosure: make it observable. There is no third case.

(8) Either way there is no distinctively competitiveness-shaped obstacle. The racing literature is studying an artefact. What looks like firms being forced to skip safety work is really the familiar difficulty of measuring a quality attribute of a credence good, and it should be theorized as such.

I would much rather keep (4) than (8); (8) has the smell of an argument that works too well. But (5) looks like a fair generalization of (2)–(3), and I can't see why the AI case should be an exception to (6).

One thought I can't make precise. Someone in the seminar said the market for frontier AI isn't like the market for bridges: there are only a handful of sellers, the prize is being *first* rather than being *better*, and where payoffs are positional an expenditure that improves absolute quality can still be self-defeating, because what is being bought is relative standing. If that is right it might block the move from (6) to (7). But I don't know how to state the point so that it doesn't also predict, absurdly, that firms in any concentrated industry never invest in quality; and I don't know whether it rescues (4) or merely relocates the problem.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you reject (6)'s account of when a cost gets competed away, what is the correct statement of it, and does AI safety fall on the safe or the unsafe side of the corrected version? And is there a formulation of the positional-race point that does real work here, or is it a red herring?
