I've been going down a bit of a rabbit hole on escalation of commitment on my own — no philosophy or economics background, just reading whatever I can find at odd hours — and I've got a standing argument with my brother-in-law, who's a project manager and calls everything a sunk cost fallacy. He says the American escalation in Vietnam is a textbook case: they'd already spent the money and the lives, so they kept spending more rather than admit it had all been for nothing. I think that's too glib, and about a fortnight ago I ran into the dollar auction, which I'd never heard of, and now I want somebody to actually do the mapping rather than hand-wave at it.

So: take the US involvement in Vietnam — the advisory years under Kennedy, the big build-up from a few thousand advisers to over half a million troops, Tet, the withdrawal in the early seventies and the fall of Saigon — and analyse it as a dollar auction. As I understand the game, an auctioneer sells a quid on the rule that the top bidder pays their bid and gets the quid while the runner-up pays theirs and gets nothing, so once you're in second place you're better off bidding a bit more to turn a certain loss into a smaller one, and the bidding sails straight past the value of the prize with everybody acting sensibly at every step. (Someone said it's really a war of attrition in disguise, which I half believe.) I want the careful version of that: who exactly are the two bidders; what counts as a bid — troops, bombing tonnage, aid to the South; what the prize actually was and whether it had a fixed, known value to either side at the time, given the domino-theory way of framing it; and the bit I can't get straight, who or what plays the auctioneer. In the game there's somebody standing there collecting the bids and profiting from the escalation, and I dont see who that is in a war — say plainly if there isn't one, and what that does to the model.

Then the parts I actually care about. First, there look to be three different engines that can keep people going and I want them kept apart: the loss-cutting one where continuing really is rational given the rule, which is the auction case; the face-saving one where quitting has honest forward-looking costs because allies and voters and the other side are all watching; and the informational one where the money already spent is evidence that the thing was worth doing in the first place. Which of them is doing the work at which stage, and is the sunk-cost story needed at all? The thing that broke my brother-in-law's line of argument for me is that each new administration inherited somebody else's bid, so on his own logic those people should have been the readiest to walk away, having nothing of their own to justify — and yet they went deeper. What does that do to the explanation? Second, I've been playing with two tweaks to the game to find out what's load-bearing: one where the runner-up gets their bid back, where my guess is the escalation stops near the value of the prize but I'm not sure what happens to whoever would have been left holding nothing; and one where the quid is only handed over if the winning bid reaches some floor, so bidding at all is pointless unless somebody goes far enough — which feels much closer to how real projects and real wars work, where half a railway or half a war is worth nothing. Does either change turn escalation into something rational rather than a trap, or do they just move the threshold? Third, it did stop. What, in the terms of the model, broke the logic — one bidder finally dropping out, the prize turning out to be worth less than thought, or something the model has no vocabulary for?

I'm not asking you to re-litigate whether the war was justified, and I don't need the narrative retold, I know roughly what happened. Please don't give me references or a reading list either; I'm doing this on my phone and can't look anything up, and it's the reasoning I'm after. What I most want is the other half of the exercise: where the dollar auction misdescribes this sort of situation. My suspicion is that the game is really a picture of a rule that manufactures escalation rather than of escalation itself, and that Vietnam was escalation of a different kind which the game only resembles — but I'd rather you argued me out of that than agreed with it.
