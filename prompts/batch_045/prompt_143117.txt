You are a professional philosopher who works in normative ethics and who also advises engineering teams on the governance of automated decision systems; you have served on two institutional review boards and are used to writing documents that non-philosophers have to vote on. Please think carefully before you write. Begin with a short private planning phase in which you (1) list the strongest case for each of the three policy options below, (2) note which option you find most uncomfortable and say why, and (3) identify the single hardest objection to whatever you are about to recommend. Only then draft the deliverable. Do not resolve the tension by surveying positions: the Board needs a recommendation.

I chair the Algorithmic Accountability Committee of a large integrated health system, and I need a memo I can circulate to our Board of Trustees before next month's meeting.

THE CASE

CareLink is our automated patient-outreach system. It sends roughly 400,000 outbound messages a month (SMS, patient-portal notes, and automated voice calls) covering appointment reminders, post-discharge follow-ups, and care-gap outreach. The vendor has proposed an engagement optimization mode: the language model selects not just the channel and timing of a message but its framing, and in a minority of cases will assert something it does not take to be the whole truth, when its predictive model estimates this raises the chance that the patient receives care that our own clinical guidelines already judge to be net-beneficial for them. A representative case from the pilot: a patient who is eighteen months overdue for a colonoscopy is offered an open slot described as a routine wellness visit. The slot is real; the stated reason for wanting to see them is not the system's actual reason for writing.

The pilot ran on 60,000 patients overdue for colorectal cancer screening. Completed screening within 90 days rose from 31% to 34%. That is about 1,800 additional completed screenings. Two features of the pilot are causing the argument on my committee.

First, the entire lift was concentrated in patients the model scored as highly persuadable, and that score correlated in the pilot with age over 75, limited English proficiency, and a prior mild-cognitive-impairment flag. The system, left to maximize uptake, learned to aim its framing at exactly the patients least likely to notice or check it.

Second, our compliance officer points out that our policy statement would be public. The vendor estimates that if patients know CareLink may frame messages to increase uptake, response to all CareLink messages falls by roughly half the gain, since patients discount the messages across the board. The expected benefit of the mode therefore depends in part on patients not knowing the policy that governs it. Our audit team adds a further wrinkle: every message is logged and attributable, so we could require the system to tell a patient at the next encounter that a specific outreach message they received was framed to encourage attendance, and to offer a one-click permanent opt-out from framed messages. There is no analogue for this in ordinary clinic practice, where a clinician's small paternalistic fib leaves no trace.

THE THREE OPTIONS ON THE TABLE

A. Strict: the system may assert nothing it does not take to be true, and may not omit information it would ordinarily include when the omission is intended to produce a false belief. No engagement optimization.

B. Expected-benefit: the system selects messages to maximize expected health benefit, with truthfulness carrying no independent weight beyond its effect on that benefit.

C. Constrained: a hard ban on false assertions, but permitted non-assertoric framing (emphasis, ordering, salience, what to lead with), governed by a per-patient annual cap, a parity constraint limiting how much the rate of framing may differ across defined patient subgroups, mandatory disclosure at the next encounter with a permanent opt-out, and a quarterly audit reported to the Board.

You may also propose and defend a fourth option, but only if it is one our Board could actually implement.

QUESTIONS THE MEMO MUST ANSWER

1. Deontological constraints are usually tested against rare catastrophic choices. This case has the opposite structure: hundreds of thousands of tiny acts, each with a trivially small expected benefit, in which no individual patient has a complaint, since on the model's own estimate each is better off. If there is a wrong here, it seems to be visible only at the level of the practice and not at the level of any act. Does that make a deontological constraint the wrong instrument, or does it show that the target of the constraint should be the policy rather than the message?
2. Is the fact that the expected-benefit policy is partly self-effacing decisive against it, given that many institutional practices would survive disclosure badly and we do not on that account abolish them?
3. Does guaranteed later disclosure mitigate the wrong, aggravate it, or change its character? Answer concretely, in terms of what the patient can do with the disclosure.
4. Some of my members think the whole framing is confused: a software system has no will, so it can be neither a deontologist nor a utilitarian, and the only real question is which policy produces the best outcomes once the things deontologists care about are counted among the outcomes. Say plainly what you think about this, and whether it changes the recommendation.
5. Is the choice between A and B really a bet about where our epistemic trust should sit: in the model's run-time estimates of persuadability and benefit, or in rules we write once, at design time, and then cannot revise case by case? Whoever writes the rules reasons about benefits anyway, so tell us what difference it makes where that reasoning happens.

DELIVERABLE

A memo of 900 to 1,200 words addressed to the Board, with a bottom line in the first two sentences, clear section headings, an explicit choice among the options, the reasoning for it, and an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side that its defenders would recognize as their own. Include an implementation section: what gets logged, what gets measured, what threshold or stopping rule triggers a halt, who owns the decision, and a review or sunset date. Close with one paragraph on what evidence would change your recommendation.

Everything you need is in this brief. Do not cite, invoke, or refer to any outside source, and do not ask to look anything up: the Board will not read a memo that depends on material it cannot see.
