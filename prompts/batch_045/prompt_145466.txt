**Question.** Strawson treats the reactive attitudes as partly constitutive of what it is to hold someone morally responsible, and he argues that the practice as a whole is not the kind of thing that could be given up or assessed from outside: its justification is not something we can step beyond the participant stance to evaluate. Call this the *internal adequacy* condition on an account of that shape: it must distinguish, using only resources internal to the practice itself, blame that is apt (fitting, warranted) from blame that is inapt — misdirected, excessive, or aimed at someone who is not a proper object of it at all, such as a small child, a person who acted by accident, or a person who was coerced. Does Strawson's account satisfy the internal adequacy condition? If it does, does the way it satisfies it collapse the view into a rival one on which blame is warranted by the agent's reasons-responsiveness or by the quality of will her action manifests, rather than by the practice itself? If it does not, is that a defect in the account, or something a Strawsonian should simply accept? Discuss at least one case in which resentment seems inapt, and say what Strawson's account is committed to saying about it.

**Candidate's answer.** Strawson's strategy in *Freedom and Resentment* is to change the subject from metaphysics to our actual practices. The reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, guilt, indignation — are natural human responses to the good or ill will that others display toward us in their conduct; they are not adopted as a policy, and we do not decide to have them. Holding someone responsible just is being liable to these attitudes: on Strawson's view an agent is morally responsible when and because resentment toward her would be warranted, which is why he can hold that the truth of determinism makes no difference to responsibility. Determinism is irrelevant because the practice grounds the attitudes in natural facts about human nature and about how much we care about being treated with some degree of goodwill, not in any claim about the agent's ability to have done otherwise.

The objective attitude is the contrast case. We can adopt it toward particular people or in particular contexts — the child, the psychopath, the person acting under coercion, the official acting in a role — but we could not abandon the participant stance altogether, since that stance belongs to the general framework of human life rather than being something that comes up for review as particular cases do.

The internal adequacy condition is where Strawson fails. Because the practice is not answerable to any standard outside itself, he cannot say of any particular episode of resentment that it is mistaken: what we actually feel is the standard. Cases of apparently inapt blame are handled only by observing that in them we simply do not feel resentment, or by reclassifying the target as someone who is not a participant at all. So the account has no resources of its own for separating apt from inapt blame.

That is why later writers had to supplement him. Wallace fills the gap by interpreting the reactive attitudes as responses to an agent's reasons-responsiveness: blame is warranted when the agent could have recognised and responded to moral reasons, and this independent standard is what separates warranted from unwarranted blame. Watson's objection from the two faces of responsibility makes the same point from another direction: he argues that Strawson conflates accountability and attributability, and that once they are prised apart we should conclude that only the aretaic notion is really constitutive of responsibility, the reactive attitudes being at best a useful heuristic for social regulation.
