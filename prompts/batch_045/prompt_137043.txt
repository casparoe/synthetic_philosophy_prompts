I am a retired sensory physiologist — some thirty years of measuring how intensity, adaptation, and discrimination behave in the somatosensory system — and for the past two years I have been sitting in with a philosophy reading group that works on axiology and population ethics. I want to set out an argument I assembled out of what I took to be commitments several of us share, because it takes me somewhere I cannot accept, and I cannot locate the fault.

The target is the package the group has been calling lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. As I understand it: unpleasant experiences above a certain intensity are not merely weightier than pleasant ones, they are weightier in such a way that no finite quantity of pleasure can offset them, while experiences below that intensity are traded off against pleasures in something like the ordinary way. The threshold is, on my reading of its motivation, the brake. It is what is supposed to keep the view from collapsing into the plain negative utilitarianism on which we should extinguish sentient life, since ordinary lives, however good, contain at least some unpleasant experience, and ordinary unpleasant experience sits below the threshold. My argument is that the brake is not connected to anything. I suspect the fault is mine rather than the view's, but I cannot find it.

Set up the protocol first. Take an ordering of unpleasant experiences by intensity, and for each rung ask how many hours of the best pleasure available to the subject would exactly compensate one minute at that rung — exactly, in the sense that a rational subject would be indifferent between the two. Below a certain rung people will name numbers. At and above a certain rung they stop naming them, and not because the numbers become very large: they refuse the question, or name a figure and retract it on reflection, or say that no amount would do. Call that rung T. The natural reading is that above T there is no exchange rate between the two kinds of state — that they are not two quantities laid on one line.

(1) Above T there is no exchange rate: for no finite n is n hours of the best available pleasure such that a subject could rationally be indifferent between that and one minute at T. And this is not mere ignorance or squeamishness on the subjects' part; the refusal is stable under reflection, and, from what I used to measure, the two kinds of state were never two directions on one dimension.

(2) If there is no exchange rate between B and A — no amount of A that exactly compensates a unit of B — then B is lexically prior to A: in any comparison between them, any amount of B outweighs any finite amount of A.

(3) So unpleasant experience at or above T is lexically prior to every good of the pleasure kind. That is lexical threshold negative utilitarianism.

(4) Lexical priority is a relation between outcomes, and it is complete and transitive: any two outcomes whatever can be ranked, and the rankings compose. An ordering that held only between hand-picked pairs could not do the work of an axiology.

(5) It must also be usable under uncertainty. If a unit of B lexically dominates any finite amount of A when both are certain, then any nonzero probability of B lexically dominates a certainty of any finite amount of A. An ordering that ranked only certain outcomes would tell nobody what to do, and I do not think the group wants that.

(6) Every creature that exists at all faces some nonzero probability, over the whole of its life, of an episode at or above T; and existing is a necessary condition of undergoing one.

(7) The state of affairs in which a given creature never exists contains no episode at or above T, and no probability of one.

(8) Therefore creating any sentient creature is, other things being equal, worse than not creating it — even a creature whose life contains far more happiness than suffering, and in which no episode at or above T in fact occurs.

(9) The ratchet. T is not fixed by the intensity of the stimulus alone. In the work I used to do, every threshold we measured — detection, discrimination, the point at which a stimulus becomes painful — was a function of the adapting background, not of the stimulus considered in isolation: hold the stimulus constant, shift the background, and the threshold moves. If uncompensability is a relational property, holding between an item and the range of alternatives it is compared against rather than between an item and nothing, then the same should hold of T. But then (1) through (8) do not stop where I left them. Once everything at or above T is eliminated, the worst remaining unpleasant experience stands in exactly the structural position, relative to the alternatives now available, that the T-level case stood in before; so the argument re-applies to it; and so on down. T drifts to zero. At the limit any episode with negative valence, however slight, is lexically prior to any finite amount of pleasure, and with (6) and (7) every possible sentient life is on balance bad. The threshold has bought nothing.

So the brake is not connected: the device meant to separate this view from plain negative utilitarianism does not separate it, and I end up required to prefer that no creature ever exist. I do not accept that.

What I would least like to give up is (1), read as a report about the protocol, together with the thought that the refusal above T is tracking something real about the two kinds of state. What I would give up cheerfully is (8). What I cannot wave away is (9), because the background-dependence of thresholds is the one claim in the argument I have direct professional experience of, and I can see no reason it should fail here.

So, four questions. First: which step fails, and why does it look as though it follows? I want a diagnosis of the chain, not a survey of the positions. Second: price the alternatives. If the failure is at (2), then what exactly must I say about the commensurability of pleasure and pain in order to reject it, and does rejecting it commit me to holding that enough mild pleasure can outweigh an episode of agony? If the failure is at (4) or (7), what has to be true of an axiology for an empty outcome not to be on the scale at all — is that a person-affecting restriction, or something weaker that still lets the ordering do its work? Third: is (9) sound? Is uncompensability a property of intensity or a property of an item's position within a comparison class, and can that be settled by the protocol itself, or must the answer come from somewhere else? Fourth, separately: indifference is symmetric, so if no amount of the best pleasure is indifferent to one minute at T, then equally no amount at T is indifferent to an hour of the best pleasure. The protocol therefore seems unable by itself to say which way the priority runs, and run in the other direction it seems to establish that the highest ecstasy is lexically prior to any amount of pain — which I cannot have alongside (3). Is that a genuine symmetry the argument has to answer, or am I confusing two different things there?
