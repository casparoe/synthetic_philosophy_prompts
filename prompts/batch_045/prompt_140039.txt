I'm in the fourth year of a PhD in philosophy and I'm applying for a nine-month dissertation-completion fellowship; the application wants a two-page proposal for the chapter I'd write during it. The chapter is the third of four, and the dissertation as a whole is about what we owe each other when we disagree about what we owe. My half-formed idea is that the case for moral trade has been built on a quietly restrictive model of the values that get traded, and that once you notice the restriction the practice both shrinks and changes shape in ways the existing discussion doesn't handle.

Here is where I've got to. The standard examples pair two people who care about different things — one about global health, one about restoring a building or keeping a local institution alive — and have each give to the other's cause, so that both end up better off by their own lights. What makes that work, I think, is that the goods on both sides are fungible: neither party cares who brings the outcome about, so each can buy the other's outcome at a price both prefer to the status quo. But a great deal of what people actually treat as morally important is not like that. It matters to some that they, or their congregation, or their union, or their country, are the ones who do it; it matters to others that they themselves not have dirty hands; it matters to others that help arrive as a gift rather than as the delivery leg of a bargain. Call these indexical or non-fungible values. They seem to block trade in a particular way, and not merely by narrowing the menu: the person who cares that her own community feeds the poor cannot be compensated by an outsider feeding the poor, and the person who cares that he not lie cannot be paid in someone else's truthfulness. So, first thought: perhaps the trades that are actually available are only those between agents whose values are agent-neutral in the traded respect, which would mean moral trade quietly selects for, or nudges its participants toward, one shape of value — and I genuinely cannot tell whether that is a debunking observation about the practice or a harmless scope limitation. Second thought, which may or may not connect: I keep coming back to the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade result in economics (common priors, purely informational differences, rational agents shouldn't expect to gain from trade). The analogue would be that if two people share an ultimate value and differ only about which intervention produces more of it, then a moral trade between them is really a bet, and is suspect in roughly the way betting is suspect; if they differ in final values, no common prior is in play and the result has no grip. Third, and I think this is where the real action is: consider traders who are each uncertain about their own values, and uncertain in different directions — I am torn between two views about what matters and you are confident about one of them, while you are torn and I am confident. Then a trade looks less like a bet than like mutual hedging under normative uncertainty, and it may generate gains in a case where neither pure diversity of taste nor pure diversity of belief does. My supervisor's objection is that this third case collapses into the pre-existing debate about whether one should hedge under normative uncertainty at all; I don't think it does, because the trade structure changes what each side has to offer, but I would rather the proposal be honest if it does collapse.

What I need from you is the proposal itself, in a form I can send to a committee that is half philosophers and half social scientists. Please give me: a precise statement of the question the chapter answers, stated so that it could come out either way; why the positions one might expect to settle it don't, without turning into a literature survey; the method, month by month, and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives (just building a small formal exchange model, or just canvassing intuitions about cases, are the two I keep being tempted by); what the deliverable would actually consist of, including what someone who had to decide whether to set up or join a moral trade arrangement could take away from it; and an honest statement of what would count as the chapter failing, and of where it is likely to outgrow nine months. Along the way, tell me which of my three thoughts is the chapter and which are footnotes to it — I suspect I am trying to write two chapters. Don't put in any references or citations; I'll add those myself.
