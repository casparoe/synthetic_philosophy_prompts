I have been reading, on and off for years, the usual sequence-side material on miscoordination, commitment races, and programs that can read one another's source, and before I go further into individual proposals I want the specification. Here is the setting I care about. A group is going to play a game G. I, a designer, may hand them a modified game G-prime instead: adding cheap-talk rounds, letting them commit to respond to what others do, letting them delegate their moves to programs that can condition on each other, and so on. What I do not get to do is assume a probability distribution over how G would have been played: I do not know which equilibrium they would have landed on, and in the delegation case a player may not know what her own delegate would have done by default. Call G-prime a safe Pareto improvement on G when, for every way of playing the pair that I am willing to count as possible, every player does strictly better in G-prime than in G.

What I want from you is the desiderata list: what any adequate explication of that notion has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each item. To get you started, here are the demands I keep seeing made of it, some of which I suspect are prejudices of one camp rather than requirements:

- improvement with certainty, ex post, in every admissible outcome, versus improvement guaranteed only in expectation under each player's own (possibly mistaken) beliefs;
- belief-freeness about equilibrium selection, and the price of it: the only leverage left is qualitative assumptions about play, of the kind people use when they assume isomorphic games get played isomorphically, or that strictly dominated strategies get iteratively removed. Are those part of the definition, or empirical premises that could be false of the population I am designing for?
- non-vacuity: the notion has to license something non-trivial in the games that motivate it, and not only relabelings;
- a determinate baseline, given that the counterfactual is how the players would have played a game they are no longer playing, and that in the delegation case the principal cannot answer the question "what would I have done?" from the inside;
- robustness to partial adoption, and invariance to whether adoption is mandatory or optional. This is the case I care most about: if taking up the modification is voluntary, the option to take it up is itself a move in a larger game, declining can reveal something about who declined, and a scheme that helps everyone given universal take-up may hurt the one player who opts in while others do not;
- no interpersonal comparisons of utility, and invariance to rescaling each player's utility independently, which becomes delicate the moment the mechanism needs side payments;
- robustness to small misspecification of the payoffs, since a guarantee that holds "with certainty" is knife-edge if the payoffs are merely estimated;
- composition: whether successive safe improvements can be stacked, and whether two designers applying different improvements to the same game interfere;
- implementability without a trusted enforcer, using only cheap talk, self-enforcing commitments, and mutual inspection of source code;
- scope beyond two players, two actions, and complete information.

For each requirement say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp, and say which distinctions the definition must not collapse: better-for-each versus better-on-average; improving the outcome versus improving the game; belief-free safety versus robustness across a set of beliefs versus what it is subjectively rational for a player to accept. Then flag any subset of the requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since if the list itself is unsatisfiable that is the result I most want, and tell me which single requirement excludes the largest share of candidate definitions.

A short scorecard, a few lines each, against the four notions I have encountered — belief-free improvements proved from qualitative assumptions about play; cheap-talk "token" rounds combined with advance commitments to respond; renegotiation between submitted programs run by expected-utility maximizers who do have beliefs; and the older welfare-economics idea of a compensation test with hypothetical side payments — would be useful, but keep the weight on the criteria.
