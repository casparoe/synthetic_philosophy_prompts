I advise a state professional licensing board that is rewriting its adjudication manual. Most of the criteria the board applies are vague — applicants must show "substantial experience," "good moral character," "adequate supervision" — and the board has never settled how to handle cases that fall in the penumbra. Two rival drafts are on the table.

Draft A instructs examiners to proceed as though every case has a single correct verdict that the board is merely failing to discern: disagreement between examiners counts as error, resources should go into calibration and training, and a reversal on appeal means the first examiner got the case wrong.

Draft B instructs examiners to sort cases into three bins — clear pass, clear fail, and genuinely indeterminate — and to treat disagreement within the third bin as something other than error: two examiners may permissibly return "indeterminate" and "pass" on the same file without either having made a mistake.

Two further facts constrain the choice. First, the board's enabling statute contains an equality clause: no two applicants whose files are materially indistinguishable may receive different verdicts. Second, an internal audit has shown that examiners' effective thresholds are not fixed — they drift slowly and continuously over the years, so that the board's own precedents from a decade ago no longer match its current practice.

Please set out the most important implications of the philosophical debate between epistemicist treatments of vagueness (vague predicates have sharp boundaries that we cannot, even in principle, come to know) and supervaluationist treatments (borderline statements are neither true nor false, while a statement can still count as true by holding under every admissible way of making the predicate precise) for how the board should draft this manual. In your answer, address at least the following: whether disagreement in the penumbra is a defect to be engineered away; what standard of review an appellate panel should apply to penumbral verdicts; how the manual should handle cases that are themselves borderline between clear and indeterminate; and what each view implies about the equality clause and about whether the drift documented in the audit is a problem to be corrected or a feature to be accommodated.

Lead with your bottom line — which draft the board should adopt and why — in the first sentence, then give your reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate the question. Aim for roughly 600 words.
