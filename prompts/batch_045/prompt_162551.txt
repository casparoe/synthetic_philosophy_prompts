I am assembling the centrepiece handout for a second-year epistemology seminar on the epistemology of testimony, and I want it to be a classification instrument rather than a prose survey: something that starts a student from the near-universal commitment that we sometimes come to know that p just by being told that p, and sorts them, by their answers about cases, into one of the substantive positions on whether testimonial justification is basic or derived.

The constraints, in order of importance.

First, no branch node may name a position, a thesis, or a theorist, and no node may be phrased so that only someone who already knows the literature can answer it. Every node should be a concrete case or a plain claim about what has to be present for a hearer to be justified. Write the questions first, from the logical space of the issue, and only afterwards locate the actual views among the answer-patterns.

Second, the following cases must each drive at least one node, because they are where the views actually come apart rather than where the textbook says they do:

- a stranger at a railway station who gives you a direction, and whom you will never see again;
- a four-year-old told that the stove is hot, before she has any track record for reliability or any conception of deception;
- an expert assertion in a field where you cannot evaluate the evidence even in principle;
- a speaker who is entirely sincere and confident but simply wrong;
- a neighbour who means well and is honest but whose reports have been wrong most of the time you have checked them;
- one anonymous report versus many independent reports of the same thing;
- content nobody else could in principle check, such as what the speaker dreamed last night or what she had for breakfast;
- an assertion produced by a large language model, which is correct, but where there is no speaker with beliefs, intentions, or any stake in being believed.

That last case is the one I most want used as a separator, since views that otherwise agree on ordinary cases disagree sharply about whether there needs to be an agent who asserts, a source who knows, or merely a reliable signal.

Third, the leaves. Give me eight to ten, including: a global reductionism on which every instance of testimonial justification is derived from other sources plus a general empirical premise about testimony as a class; a local reductionism on which the hearer needs reasons specific to this speaker and occasion; a reliabilist reductionism on which no reasons are needed in the hearer but justification is still constituted by something other than testimony itself; a default-and-defeater view on which the hearer is entitled to accept absent defeaters but testimony is not thereby a distinct source; an anti-reductionism resting on an a priori principle about acceptable assertion; an anti-reductionism resting on the nature of communication or assurance rather than on a principle; a transmission-only view on which testimony can pass on knowledge but never generate more warrant than the speaker had; and a sceptical leaf. If a combination of answers yields no view anyone holds, keep it as a leaf marked unnamed or empty and say in one line whether it is empty because incoherent, because unattractive, or because merely unoccupied. Flag explicitly any two leaves your questions cannot separate, and say whether the difference between them is verbal, shows up only in the odd cases above, or is one you would need a further question to detect.

Please structure the response as follows.

1. Using the instrument — about 80 words: how a student works through it and scores herself.
2. The questions — about 400 words: the eight to ten diagnostic questions with their answer options, each phrased for someone with no prior exposure to the debate.
3. The space they generate — about 120 words: how many combinations the answers permit, how many the literature actually occupies.
4. The tree — roughly one page: an ASCII flowchart from the starting commitment to the leaves, nodes as questions, branches as answers.
5. The leaves — about 300 words: each leaf's standard label where there is one, a one-line gloss of the jargon, and one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment.
6. Empty and crowded cells — about 200 words: the unoccupied combinations with your diagnosis of why they are empty, and the pairs the instrument cannot separate.
7. What would change my mind about this instrument — about 200 words: where you think your own ordering of the questions is most likely to be wrong, which single question you would drop or add if you had to, and what consideration would convince you that the split you have made the trunk of the tree is not the one that really divides the positions.
