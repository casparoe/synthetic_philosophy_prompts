I'm a journalist — I write a column on science and ideas for a magazine you've probably seen in an airport — and I'm in the middle of a short series on who gets to say what happened. Here's the case that got me into it. A river town called Ash Ferry has built its entire civic identity on the story that its settlers held the ferry crossing against a militia in 1781; there's an annual reenactment, a monument, the works. A county historian recently pointed out that there are no muster rolls, no casualty lists, no militia within forty miles that year, and that the ferry wasn't licensed until 1812. The town's answer was basically: the record is just what the people who controlled the courthouse happened to write down, and the memory is ours. I keep going back and forth — I'm sympathetic to the town and I'm also scared of where that sympathy leads, and I can't tell if im being reasonable.

So I want a satirical op-ed, around 800 words including the headline, written in the voice of a true believer in what she calls mnemonic sovereignty: the view that a community's lived memory has more authority than the documentary record, because archives were produced by whoever held the pen and the filing cabinet. Invent her name, invent the institute she directs; no real people in it anywhere, and no footnotes or suggestions for further reading, since my readers can't follow a link off a printed page. Three ideas I'd like her to actually use, and I'll define them so we're on the same page. Mnemonic amortization: a memory gets more authoritative the longer it has been held, because the duration of the holding is itself a fact about the community rather than about the event. The notary's regress: anyone who certifies which memories are authentic has to keep a record of the certifications, so the cure produces a bigger and more invasive archive than the disease. The substitution rule: once you correct a record, the corrected version is what later memory forms around, so every reformer is really deciding what her grandchildren will misremember.

The comedy has to come from getting the position right — I want somebody who genuinely half-holds this view to feel gotten rather than jeered at, and I'd rather it be a fair version of the argument than a scarecrow. So land the standard objections without ever naming them: let her quietly gerrymander who counts as the community (renters, people who arrived after the mill shut down, descendants of whoever the militia story was about), let her certify a memory that a 1950s tourism board demonstrably invented and treat sincere-plus-durable as sufficient, and let the careful reader be the one who notices that her bureau is now the largest documentary archive in the state. Keep it deadpan the whole way — she is entirely in earnest, she never winks at the reader, and the piece doesn't break character at the end to reassure anyone or to shrug that both sides have a point.

Give me, in this order: a section called "The column," with the headline and the roughly 800-word piece, and feel free to drop in one short inset sidebar in a different voice — a skeptical county clerk's margin notes, or something like that; then a section called "Where I want the irony to land," about 200 words, telling me which real features of the position you exaggerated, which clause of hers is the one a reader should recoil at, and where the recoil is meant to happen.
