I'm serving on a university ethics advisory panel that has been asked to write a short public statement about a neurotechnology, and I keep failing to draft it with a colleague because we turn out to disagree about something more basic than the device. The product — call it Second Thought — is a headset that tracks the gradual build-up of evidence toward a decision and, when it predicts that a choice is about to cross the threshold, delivers a faint haptic cue and holds the decision open for roughly three quarters of a second. In the trials the company cites, it works: people act less on craving, anger and fatigue, and more in line with what they themselves endorse on reflection; the rate at which they carry out their own stated long-term plans goes up. The marketing copy says the device "gives you back the power to choose," and my colleague and I cannot agree whether that is a description, a lie, or a category mistake.

Please write the dialogue, about 900 to 1,100 words, between the two of us on the panel — call us Marisol Reyes and Tobias Lindqvist, or give them other names — as we try, and fail, to produce one joint paragraph. Marisol holds that what we want from free will is a cluster of practical capacities: responsiveness to reasons, self-control, the ability to act on one's own considered judgment rather than on impulse or compulsion, and the ordinary availability of alternatives at the moment of action. On that view Second Thought plainly increases free will: it reopens alternatives that were about to close, it extends the reach of the agent's own reflective preferences, and it damps the influence of transient states we do not treat as expressive of the agent. Tobias holds that what we want is authorship: that the agent be the source of the action in a way that can ground praise, blame, resentment and punishment. On that view the device is irrelevant at best and corrosive at worst — the cue's timing, its threshold and the population-trained model behind it are set by engineers, employers and insurers; the improvement in behaviour is produced by factors outside the agent; and the case has the shape of a manipulation argument even though no one is manipulating anyone.

Some requirements, because I want this to be a real exchange rather than two monologues. Neither speaker should be a caricature: each should be able to state the other's position well enough to hear "yes, that's exactly it," and each should make at least one concession that is genuinely costly without conceding the dispute. Marisol should face, and answer, the charge that her view just counts any well-functioning causal machinery as freedom — including machinery someone else installed — and her answer should not rest on the bare fact that the user consented; give Tobias the variant where the headset is subsidised and switched on by default by an insurer, so that the consent reply visibly does the work. Tobias should face the parity problem: if the same improvements arrived through upbringing, therapy or years of practice, he would presumably treat them as enhancements of agency, so what exactly is doing the work in his objection to the device? Both should also reckon with the fact that how much the device helps depends on unchosen features of the person — impulsivity, cue sensitivity, sleep, history — and say why that matters or does not. Avoid wheeling in the standard stock thought experiments: no Frankfurt-style counterexamples, no neuroscientists or gods pressing buttons, no appeal to quantum indeterminacy. Build the argument out of the headset itself. End not with agreement but with the two of them locating where the disagreement bottoms out — in a normative question about what free will is for, shaping and predicting behaviour on the one hand and grounding desert on the other — and whether a notion of agency that survives ordinary empirical luck is worth having.
