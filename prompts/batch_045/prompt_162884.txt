I'm finishing the providence chapter of my dissertation in philosophical theology, and my advisor wants a one-page statement of where I've landed, to circulate to the committee before the defence — something they can audit rather than a survey of positions in the literature. Here, roughly, is the package I'm inclined to defend. I hold that God has exhaustive and infallible knowledge of every future creaturely choice, including free ones, so open theism is false. I hold that at least some creaturely choices are free in a libertarian sense: the agent could have done otherwise in the very same circumstances, and nothing outside the agent — not God's decree, not prior causal history — determines the choice. I hold that there are true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, that they are true logically prior to any decree of the divine will, and that God knows them and uses them in planning a creation, with the consequence that some possible worlds are infeasible for God and the menu of creative options is constrained independently of what God wills. I also hold that these counterfactuals are not made true by God's decree, nor by anything about the creature's nature, character, or circumstances: they are, as far as explanation goes, brute — true, but not true in virtue of anything. Alongside this I hold a strong doctrine of divine aseity: God depends on nothing outside himself for what he is or for what he knows, everything other than God is created by him, and God can infallibly bring about each of the particular purposes he has for the course of history. I hold that God is timeless, with no before and after in the divine life and no discursive sequence in God's knowing. I hold that petitionary prayer makes a real difference to what God does. And I've been toying with an extension that isn't in the standard presentations: that God also has middle knowledge of counterfactuals about what God himself would freely do in circumstances he never in fact faces, and I lean on this to make the prayer commitment work. Finally, two things I hold but that I can't tell are load-bearing: that God permits an instance of evil only if permitting it is necessary for a greater good or for the prevention of an equal evil; and that for many actual evils I can see no such good, which I answer with skeptical theism — given our cognitive limits we should not expect to be able to identify God's reasons.

What I want is an audit, not a verdict on Molinism as such, and not a reading list. Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look that way. My advisor thinks the strain is all in one place: she says timelessness and logically prior middle knowledge can't both be true, because middle knowledge needs a stage at which God surveys the counterfactuals before decreeing, and a timeless God has no stages. I don't find that convincing, but I may just be attached to the package — so please say whether she's right, and whether there are other apparent conflicts that dissolve on inspection for the same kind of reason. For the tensions that are real, rank them by severity rather than listing them as equally bad; for each, say what exactly would have to give and what giving it would cost me elsewhere in the set — which other commitments I'd be quietly sacrificing, and whether the resulting view still does the work I wanted middle knowledge to do. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, if I want to keep the core Molinist machinery. Two further things: flag any item on the list that isn't actually doing any work, one I could drop without loss; and tell me whether the final two commitments — the necessity condition on permitting evil, and the skeptical theist answer to evils I can't see the point of — are really compatible, or whether the second quietly makes the first, and perhaps the whole Molinist apparatus, redundant.
