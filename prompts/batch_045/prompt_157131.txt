I am assembling the diagnostic core of a graduate seminar module on the boundaries of mind, and I need a classification instrument rather than a survey of the literature: a decision tree that sorts a person into a position in the extended-mind debate on the basis of what they say about cases, not on the basis of which label they would apply to themselves. Please build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only afterwards locate the actual views in that space, marking as "unnamed" any combination that nobody appears to defend.

Form and size. Six or seven branch nodes, each a yes/no or two-to-three-way question, terminating in eight to ten leaves. Every branch must be labeled with the concrete case or claim that does the separating, never with the name of a doctrine: write "if the notebook's contents were silently replaced overnight by subtly different contents and you never noticed, did your belief change?", not "the constitution question" and not "do you accept the parity principle?" No node may ask whether the respondent accepts the extended mind thesis, functionalism, any parity argument, or any named view; no node may ask which author or school they find persuasive. Assume the reader has no web access: no dates, no citations, no bibliographies, and no case that turns on a fact somebody would have to look up. Where a leaf corresponds to a position in the literature, a short parenthetical tag is welcome, but the instrument must remain usable for a reader who has never heard of it.

Cases. At least five of the following must appear as nodes, and you may substitute or add at most two of your own:

1. A notes app whose contents are altered by a sync error; you consult it, act on the altered entry without hesitation, and are never corrected. Did your belief change, or were you merely misinformed by an external source?
2. A memory-buffering neural interface fitted while you were under anaesthetic, as part of a treatment you did not request and do not understand, but on which you have come to rely.
3. A shared knowledge base maintained by two people, where neither can tell who entered a given item and each treats every item as something they know.
4. An assistant that periodically rewrites and reorganizes your notes and surfaces connections you never drew, by a mechanism you could not reconstruct.
5. Notes readable only while a device is powered and online; when it is off, you recall nothing at all.
6. An implanted controller that represents a bodily variable and acts on it homeostatically, without your ever representing it.
7. A device purpose-built as a memory aid versus an ordinary object you have trained yourself to use as one.
8. A practitioner who, after years with a navigation or notation aid, no longer uses it, but whose unaided reasoning has been permanently reshaped by it.
9. A second person who has simply memorized the same facts; the two of you are indistinguishable in behaviour and in how it seems to you.

Do not build nodes around the canonical museum-and-notebook pair; you may use them once, in the scoring key, purely to calibrate the instrument.

The angle. The practical stake is commercially available AI memory aids: persistent-memory assistants, personal knowledge bases with semantic search, smart glasses with recall features. Each leaf should therefore carry one sentence on what it implies about whether a person relying on such a device thereby remembers or believes. Relatedly, I want the instrument to keep apart four questions that the debate routinely conflates: where the mind stops, who the agent is, what the proper unit of explanation in cognitive science is, and who is answerable for an outcome. Note, for each node, which of these four it actually probes, and flag any place where two positions look opposed only because they are answering different ones.

Leaves. The following must be represented, under these descriptions or your own: full-blooded parity-style extension; extended cognitive system but not extended mind, with personal-level states staying in the head; embedded or scaffolded but not extended, with causal dependence without constitution; enculturation or cognitive-integration views on which the transformation of the inner system is what matters; distributed-cognition views on which the individual's mind is the wrong unit; conservative rejections resting on a mark of the cognitive together with a bloat or boundary objection; and a deflationary treatment on which the boundary question is interest-relative or merely verbal. Add leaves for unoccupied combinations as needed, and give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Apparatus. Alongside the tree, supply: a scoring key giving the answer patterns that are diagnostic, and noting where the order in which nodes are taken changes the result; a table of unoccupied combinations with a one-line diagnosis of each — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and a short section flagging any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, stating what further question would separate them and whether that question is substantive or verbal. Flag as well any node where the same answer is likely to be given for two quite different reasons.
