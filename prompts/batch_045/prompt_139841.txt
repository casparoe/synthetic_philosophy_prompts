After thirty-five years in experimental physical chemistry I now audit a philosophy seminar, and for the past year I have served as rapporteur for a small interdisciplinary colloquium on whether history can be modeled and predicted. Its two poles are a structural-demographic modeler working in Peter Turchin's tradition — secular cycles of roughly two to three centuries driven by population pressure, elite overproduction, and state fiscal strain, fitted to databases such as Seshat, and associated with a 2010 letter to Nature forecasting growing instability in the United States and western Europe in the decade that followed — and a humanistically trained historian who holds that what such models track is not the cause of events but their residue: that the units are constituted retrospectively, that the same evidence would be coded differently by different scholars, and that the historical question worth asking is never which phase of a cycle we are in but what the participants took themselves to be doing. They have talked past each other for a year and have now agreed, at my suggestion, to an adversarial collaboration whose product is a joint statement. I have been asked to draft it.

Please write that document in American English: a numbered section of claims both could sign, followed by a table of their residual disagreements and cruxes. For each crux give the disagreement in a formulation each side would accept for its own view, the test, case, or body of evidence both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. I want a document, not a verdict: do not decide who is right, but do say plainly which parts of their dispute are verbal, which are empirical and decidable, and which are philosophical and would survive any amount of data.

Four cruxes I suspect belong in the table, though I would rather you kept, dropped, or recast them as you see fit. First, what I would call the single-realization problem: history supplies one trajectory and no ensemble, so I want to know whether an apparent two-to-three-century periodicity is distinguishable from red noise plus a trend in a short, unevenly sampled, strongly autocorrelated series, and what pre-registered spectral or surrogate-data test would settle it. Second, degrees of freedom versus out-of-sample success: fit on pre-1800 (or, in the European cases, pre-1500) data only and forecast the following two centuries — together with the non-stationarity worry that industrialization and fossil-fuel growth altered the system's parameters, so that a failed forecast may indict the inputs rather than the mechanism. Third, whether elite overproduction can be measured independently of the instability it is invoked to explain, or whether an excess of elite aspirants is only ever diagnosed after the breakdown has already occurred. Fourth, how much of the quarrel is terminological: 'predict', 'mechanism', 'cause', 'law', and 'data' each carry a different sense for the two of them, and I suspect the modeler is importing standards of evidence from population ecology without saying so.

Where my own framing of a crux begs a question that one of them would reject, say so in a note rather than quietly correcting it.
