My wife got her citizenship in March — fourteen years, two lawyers, one interview that got rescheduled twice because of a snowstorm — and now the county clerk's office has asked me to give the little speech to the new citizens at the ceremony in September. Apparently there's a slot for it, between the high school choir and the oath.

The room is going to be about 180 new citizens from somewhere around fifty countries, packed into a community college auditorium with their kids and grandparents, plus a federal judge, the mayor, the clerk, and — this is the part I can't stop thinking about — at least thirty people in it who are years away from any status themselves, and maybe two or three who have no status at all. One of them is my mother in law, who came on a tourist visa in 2017 and never went back and can't adjust and is coming anyway because its her daughter. My wife's brother is coming too, and he's spent eleven years in Border Patrol. Her other sister is an immigration lawyer who thinks the whole system is a crime scene. All three of them will be in the same row.

I've been reading about this stuff since my wife's letter came, mostly because I didn't want to stand up there and say something stupid. The thing I keep coming back to isn't really the open borders argument itself but a distinction underneath it: there's letting someone be here — hospitality, a guest in your house, temporary, conditional, you set the terms and the door stays yours — and there's membership, which seems different in kind, because a member doesn't only get to be in the room, they get to say how the room should be run, including saying that the door should have been open the whole time. And what I noticed is that basically every one of these speeches is written in the first register by accident. All the welcoming language is host language; it quietly keeps the door in the host's hand and it asks the people sitting in front of you to be grateful guests on the day they stopped being guests. But then the flip side: if I actually talk to them as members, I have to grant that they now have standing to tell the rest of us we're wrong about the border, which is a strange thing to say into a room that has my brother in law in it and a judge who has to stand up right after me and swear them in.

So: four or five minutes, short plain sentences, since a third of the room will be hearing it through a whisper translation or not really hearing it. No nation-of-immigrans line, no melting pot, no quotes from anybody, no naming philosophers, no lecture, and nothing that makes my mother in law's being there sound like a favor we're doing her or like a crime. It also can't be a protest speech — it's my wife's afternoon, she waited fourteen years, I want her to enjoy it. But I don't want mush either. And I'd rather the thing never leaned on the word welcome at all, unless you can make it earn its keep.

Can you write it?
