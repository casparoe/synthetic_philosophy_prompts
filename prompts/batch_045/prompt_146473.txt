I am writing a paper on assertions that outlive their own evidence — the kind that get entered into clinical handoffs, deposition transcripts, lab notebooks, incident postmortems, and shared institutional knowledge bases, and are then read months or years later by people who have none of the original speaker's evidence and no way to recover it. Before I can say anything useful about that case, I need to get clear on the older dispute it sits downstream of, so I want you to reconstruct that dispute as the document its participants should have produced.

The two parties are the knowledge-norm camp — the view that a speaker may assert that p only if she knows that p, and that this is the norm distinctive of assertion rather than one norm among many — and the reasonableness camp — the view that what the norm requires is that the speaker's confidence in p, given her total evidence, clear a threshold fixed by the context, that knowledge is at most a sufficient condition for permissible assertion, and that a speaker can assert permissibly while failing to know (the Gettier-style and lottery-style speakers being the contested exhibits). Assume both parties are sophisticated: the knowledge-norm theorist treats knowledge as an unanalysed epistemic state rather than as justified true belief plus a fourth condition, and the reasonableness theorist is not impressed by the request to decompose knowledge. Nothing in what follows should rest on who historically held which view; characterise the positions rather than attaching names to them, and do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I have no way to check them and I do not want them.

Deliver the joint statement, in this order.

1. A numbered section of claims both parties can sign. I want these to be load-bearing rather than diplomatic: what assertion does that other speech acts do not, why both camps condemn the same easy cases, what kind of norm is in dispute (a norm of permissibility or defect, not of all-things-considered moral or legal wrongness), whether knowledge is agreed to be sufficient, what each camp concedes about the other's strongest case, and anything about the epistemology of testimony or of reliance that both need. If you think some apparent agreement is fake, say which and why.

2. A disagreements table. One row per residual disagreement; each row gives the claim in the knowledge-norm theorist's own preferred wording and the same claim in the reasonableness theorist's preferred wording, each phrased so that side would actually sign it and neither is a strawman. Mark, in a separate column or a short note after the table, any row you judge to be a merely verbal dispute, and say what substantive question lies underneath it.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. My candidate list, which you should cut, merge, add to, or re-order as you judge best, with a sentence of justification whenever you depart from it: whether knowledge is closed under conjunction and under universal generalisation, and how much of the dispute rides on closure; whether the norm proper to assertion has an audience or uptake component at all, or is purely speaker-side; whether the normative unit is the speech act at the moment it is performed or the whole epistemic transaction of which it is a part; whether the norm is constitutive of assertion or merely regulative of it, and whether that distinction is doing real work in the arguments either side gives; whether the disagreement bottoms out in a prior disagreement about what knowledge is, and if so whether the reasonableness camp's counterexamples only bite against a decompositional account the knowledge-norm camp has already rejected; and what kind of data — case judgments, linguistic evidence, the practices of criticism, retraction, and hedging — could in principle settle any of this.

4. For each crux, one test both parties would accept as bearing on it: a case, an argument, or a kind of evidence, together with each side's prediction written down in advance, in the form "the knowledge-norm theorist predicts X; the reasonableness theorist predicts Y." Where a test cannot honestly be constructed, say so and say what that reveals.

5. Two cases of my own, which I want built into section 4 as tests, since neither camp has addressed them. First, the durable record: a radiologist reads a scan herself, in good conditions, and enters into the patient's shared record a single undated line stating that there is no intracranial haemorrhage; she knows this when she writes it; eighteen months later an emergency team reads that line, takes it as current, and decides against imaging. Is the act of writing governed by the same norm as an oral assertion to a colleague standing at her shoulder? If the norm is satisfied at the time of writing, is there any residual normative failure, and does it attach to the writing, to the record-keeping practice, or to the reader's reliance? Would either camp's answer change if the same sentence were written into a medium designed to persist rather than spoken, and if so, what does that show about the norm? Second, the batch release: a laboratory director signs a statement that every one of twelve hundred vials in a lot was held continuously within a temperature range, on the basis of individually reliable automated sensor logs that she knows are subject to occasional undetected gaps, so that her confidence in the universal claim is very high but below certainty — roughly the confidence you would have in any single ticket's losing a large fair lottery. Is the signature permissible? What does each camp say, and what does each camp say about the fact that a great deal of ordinary institutional reporting would be impermissible if the other camp is right?

Finally, the part I most want you to commit to. Where no formulation both sides would sign exists, say so plainly instead of papering over it with a vague sentence neither would accept. Rank the cruxes by how much of the dispute you think turns on each, and give reasons. Then, in a clearly marked closing section separate from the joint statement, give your own verdict: which side has the better case overall, on which crux it is won or lost, and what specific finding — the outcome of one of the tests above, or an argument you can state — would change your mind. Say explicitly where you are relying on a contested reading of one of the positions, and where your verdict depends on a further question you have not settled. Attach rough confidence levels to your main claims; do not substitute "it depends" for a line where a line can be taken. Aim for about 1,300 words.
