I've been building a small embedded controller — a gridworld agent that maintains its own forward model of the transition function and represents itself as one more object in the grid — and my reading group and I backed into an argument we've started calling the Self-Knowledge Bind. Each step looks defensible on its own; the conclusion I cannot accept. I want to know which step to reject and what rejecting it costs me.

(1) *Embedding.* Any physically implemented decision-maker is part of the causal order it deliberates about. Its decision procedure is a physical process, and its output is an event in the environment.

(2) *Open options.* To choose by comparing expected utilities, the agent needs, for each candidate action it is choosing between, a distribution over outcomes conditional on that action. Equivalently: its world-model must represent more than one of its own candidate outputs as genuinely possible. Call this the open-option condition.

(3) *Accuracy.* A world-model earns its keep by being accurate, and the environmental variable an agent is best placed to get right is its own output, since the process producing it is right there inside it. An ideally accurate model of an environment containing the agent assigns probability 1 to the action the agent will in fact perform and 0 to the alternatives.

(4) *Conditioning needs positive probability.* Conditioning on a probability-zero event is undefined. So an agent whose model is accurate about its own action cannot evaluate the expected utility of the actions it did not take; there is nothing to condition on.

(5) *The Bind.* So the embedded agent chooses between two failures. Either its model is accurate about its own output, in which case deliberation over actions is undefined; or deliberation is defined, in which case the model is knowingly wrong about the one variable the agent knows best. What makes this a bind rather than a puzzle with an answer is that the failures trade off: the better the self-knowledge, the less room for deliberation, and the more it deliberates, the more it must corrupt its model. Self-knowledge is penalized, which is backwards.

(6) *It reaches past decision theory.* If (1)–(5) are sound, then any controller carrying an internal model of the system it controls, including itself, is in trouble, and the more accurate that model, the worse off it is. But internal-model control is ordinary successful engineering, and my agent demonstrably does better with a self-model than without one.

(7) *It proves too much.* Nothing in (1)–(5) turns on anything special about agency. Take any settled fact a model knows: an accurate model assigns it probability 1, so conditioning on its negation is undefined, so no one can ever think about what would have happened had it been false. Counterfactual reasoning about well-known facts is something we do constantly and are often right about. If the argument works, all counterfactual thought collapses, not merely self-directed deliberation.

(8) So something has to go.

I would rather keep (1), (2) and (3) than accept (7), and I would rather not surrender (6), since I have the runs in front of me. But I cannot locate the load-bearing step. Part of me suspects a slide between (2) and (4) — between needing to *think about* what would happen if I did a, and needing to *condition on* a inside one probability distribution — but the slide may be in (3) instead: perhaps accuracy about what a model represents is compatible with representing one's own output as uncertain, because the model is partial.

One move I have heard and do not find satisfying: that a system which feeds a prediction of its own output into the production of that output cannot in general be reliably predicted by any process it can run inside itself, so the first horn of (5) is unreachable. That seems to me to rescue the argument rather than answer it, since it leaves the second horn standing. If you think that move is the answer, say so and say why I am wrong about it.

Where exactly does the argument break? Take a definite position: name the step or the transition you reject, explain why that step looked fine — which nearby true claim it is liable to be confused with — and then price the alternatives you do not pick, telling me what I would have to believe if I rejected each of the other steps instead, and why that is worse. In particular, tell me what my agent is actually doing on a run where its self-model is right about what it will do and it does it anyway: is it deliberating, or is the deliberation a story I am telling about a lookup table? Commit to one answer, with your reasons.
