I'm a PhD student in comparative-historical sociology. My dissertation argues that a budgeting procedure adopted in one agency during a fiscal crisis and still in place thirty years later is path-dependent: contingent in its origin, locked in thereafter, so that the counterfactual question of whether something like it would have arrived anyway should be answered no. At a workshop an interlocutor asked what my mechanism was; when I described it, she said the picture I was relying on is the adaptive-landscape one — a ball on a bumpy slope, a nudge at a fork deciding which basin it settles into, gravity doing the rest. I would rather audit that picture than defend it. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the ball, the terrain, the nudge, gravity, the basin, and friction, and which of these my case lacks. Two specifics. First, my sequence looks reactive rather than self-reinforcing — each step provokes a response that reconfigures the setting for the next, rather than a single mechanism reproducing the outcome — and it is not clear the picture can represent that at all, since its whole point is that the terrain stays put while the ball moves. Second, locked in means in the picture that the ball cannot get out, but in my material the practice persists only because officials keep re-enacting it and could in principle coordinate their way into a different one, and their expectations partly constitute it; so what does gravity map onto, and is it importing something my case does not have? Finally: does the picture support the strong conclusion I need, or only the weaker one that this particular arrangement was contingent while some functionally equivalent one would likely have emerged anyway — and does locked in say anything falsifiable unless a scale of perturbation is specified? If it cannot carry my conclusion, propose a better analogy.
