I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently started sitting in on a philosophy reading group, and this term we have been working through the situationist critique of virtue ethics: the argument that the experimental record on how far people's conduct is consistent across different situations gives us no reason to believe in stable character traits at all, and therefore nothing for an ethics of virtue to be about. I am meant to supply something light for the last meeting of term, and I would like your help with it.

What I want is a joke that lands with people who actually know the dispute, one whose punchline turns on the real content of the disagreement rather than on the standby material. Jokes about philosophers who never leave their armchairs, about psychologists who torment undergraduates, or about the word character having two senses would each be greeted with the silence they deserve.

Two lines of thought occur to me, and you should use either, both, or neither as you see fit. The first is borrowed from my own trade: in the laboratory one prepares a system before one measures it, and a preparation is not a discovery, and I have come to suspect that the critique attributes to the sample a property of the apparatus — it arranges the circumstances, then reports the conduct that follows as a finding about the person placed in them. The second is one I take to be available to the virtue ethicist herself: if a virtue is a kind of practical wisdom rather than a reflex, then of course right conduct tracks the situation, and a person whose behaviour were wholly indifferent to circumstances would not be a paragon but a fanatic.

If it is of any use as material, our group has also taken to scoring one another, half in earnest, on a homemade consistency coefficient: a number between nought and one meant to capture how much of what you did was down to your character as opposed to your circumstances. Nobody has been able to say what the units are.

Please give me exactly five numbered candidates, each a joke of one or two sentences, each turning on a different joint in the dispute rather than five variations on a single gag, and each tellable aloud in under a minute. No introduction, no conclusion, and no notes afterwards explaining why any of them is funny. I will pick whichever one survives the room.
