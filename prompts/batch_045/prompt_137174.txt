I direct the research programme at a small philanthropic fund with roughly six million dollars to spend over three years on reducing catastrophic risk from advanced AI. We are deciding whether to make unilateral action by frontier AI developers a focus area; if we do, we can fund about eight projects. Our grantees would be philosophers, decision theorists and social scientists. We do not fund technical ML work, and our grantees would have no privileged access to labs' internal risk assessments — only to what is public or obtainable through interviews.

The frame I keep returning to is the unilateralist's curse: when many agents share an outcome, and any one of them can alone take an action whose upside is bounded but whose downside is catastrophic, the action gets taken whenever any single agent judges it worthwhile, so the group is effectively governed by its most optimistic member, and the aggregate expected value can be strongly negative even though each agent's own estimate is positive. When I try to apply this to AI developers I run into three complications, and I want to know which is worth spending money on.

First, symmetry. Not acting is also a unilateral decision — a lab that declines a capability push while others proceed is helping determine the outcome too. If the argument is fully symmetric it seems to license general paralysis, so something must break the symmetry, and I cannot tell whether that something is decision-theoretic (an asymmetry in option value, or in the value of information generated by acting) or empirical (a fact about how the actors are in fact distributed, or about how one actor's restraint changes another's behaviour).

Second, the second-order curse. The standard remedies — coordination bodies, shared red lines, public commitments, incident reporting — are themselves unilateral actions taken by whichever actor builds or joins them, and can be harmful if that actor's judgment about them is wrong. If the remedies reproduce the disease, the whole programme of work may be self-undermining.

Third, an endogenous actor population. The number of agents able to take the relevant action is not fixed, and is partly a function of what the visible actors do: open releases, publications and talent diffusion can add actors, while visible restraint may add or subtract them. I suspect this changes both the magnitude of the curse and which interventions help, but I can't see how much of the problem it dissolves.

Which of these should we prioritise, or which fourth line of work am I missing? I want a defended ordering rather than a survey, and I want the ordering to be sensitive to the fact that I can only fund conceptual and social-science work with no inside access. Please don't cite literature or suggest reading; I know that literature. Argue directly.

Structure your reply in four named sections, of roughly these lengths:

1. The agenda, about 600 words. Three or four lines of work in priority order. For each, say concretely what a funded project would do over three years and what it would hand back to me at the end — a theorem, a model, a dataset, a decision rule, a set of conditions.

2. Criteria and trade-offs, about 300 words. Which criteria you are ranking by — how many other questions turn on this one, tractability in three years for philosophers and social scientists without inside access, neglectedness — and where those criteria pull in opposite directions.

3. What to drop, about 150 words. The line of work you would cut as lowest priority, and why settling it would not change much downstream.

4. Falsifiers, about 150 words. What evidence over the next two years should move items up or down the list.
