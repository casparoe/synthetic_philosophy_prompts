At my local rationalist meetup last night two people got into it over the Fermi paradox and I ended up refereeing, badly. Reconstructing it as fairly as I can:

Marin: the Great Silence is close to zero evidence, because we've barely looked. A few decades of radio SETI, narrow bands, most of the sky never surveyed, and sensitivity such that we'd only have caught something considerably more conspicuous than we are, and only if it was nearby. Finding nothing after inspecting a handful of grains of sand isn't surprising under any hypothesis, so there's nothing here to explain.

Dev: that reply works against we listened and heard nothing, but that isn't the observation. The observation is that we are not standing inside an alien artifact. Take any civilization that spreads at even a hundredth of light speed — the galaxy is only about a hundred thousand light years across, so it's crossed in ten million years, which is a rounding error next to the age of the galaxy. So unless expansion basically never happens, someone should already have been here, and no amount of future telescope time changes that. The paradox doesn't depend on our search at all.

Marin: sure, but now you're extrapolating a very specific human pattern — that technological societies expand into available resources and keep expanding for millions of years — and you have zero instances of it. We're arguing about a rate times a probability with a sample size of one, and the one sample was selected for being a planet that produced observers, which is exactly the selection effect that makes the base rate unusable.

Dev: the selection point is real, but it cuts my way too. Consider timing. We find ourselves about 13.8 billion years after the Big Bang, and star formation still has a very long time to run — trivially longer than what's already elapsed. If observers were spread evenly over that stretch, showing up this early is wildly atypical. One clean explanation: civilizations that expand eventually fill the universe and prevent new ones from arising, so almost everyone who ever lives lives shortly before that happens. On that story our earliness is evidence *for* expansionist aliens arriving fairly soon, not against them, and the silence is just because they haven't got here yet.

Marin: right, and notice what that move does. Anthropic selection gets invoked to defuse the timing observation, but then the they should already be here observation has to be recomputed inside the window that selection hands you — and inside that window, of course we see nothing. It looks like the selection effect is being spent twice.

After that they mostly repeated themselves. There's one thing neither of them touched that I keep turning over and genuinely can't tell whose side it helps: every factor people argue about — how often life starts, how often it gets intelligent, how long technological societies last — is about how many civilizations *originate*. But what our searches could ever constrain is the origination rate times the average length of the phase in which a civilization is detectable at interstellar distances. And there's a boring, non-zoo-hypothesis story on which that phase is short: you get more efficient, you beam instead of broadcasting, you shift to low-power spread-spectrum links, you move industry off-planet and radiate less waste heat per unit of computation. If the loud phase is a few thousand years, the galaxy could be crowded and still look dead to us. What I can't work out is how that interacts with Dev's split: it seems to gut the signal-based silence completely, leave the visitation-based silence untouched, and possibly break the earliness argument, which needs aliens that are visible from far away. But it might also mean the silence was never able to discriminate between hypotheses in the first place, in which case the paradox was always just a wide posterior wearing a hat.

Please adjudicate. No citations or reading lists, I just want the logic. Sections, roughly:

- What's actually being claimed — ~150 words. Separate out the distinct observations and arguments in play, since I think at least two different things are being called the Fermi observation.
- Move by move — ~450 words. For each of the four moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two of justification, and say whether the two sides are actually disagreeing or talking past each other.
- The loud-phase idea — ~350 words. Take my wrinkle seriously: which strand of the observation it hits, whether it's really distinguishable from the zoo hypothesis, and whether it defuses the puzzle or relocates it.
- What both of them missed — ~250 words.
- Bottom line — ~100 words. Who's more right overall, and why.
