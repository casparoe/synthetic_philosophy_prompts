Here's the thing I can't get past. Take a forecaster. Ask her a coarse question — will there be an outbreak? — and she says 0.6. Score her with the familiar quadratic/Brier rule, where your score is 2 × (your credence in the cell that turned out true) minus the sum of your squared credences across all the cells. Fine, she gets a number. Now ask the same person the same territory but carved four ways (strain A with travel restrictions, strain A without, strain B with, strain B without), so her 0.6 is spread across four cells, and score her on *that* partition instead. Nothing about her evidence changed and nothing about the world changed, but the number moves — and as far as I can tell it keeps moving as you keep carving finer. Work the numbers on the page: I get 0.5 for an even 50/50 split over two cells, 0.25 for an even split over four, and 1 for flat-out certainty no matter how many cells you spread the zeros across — which seems to mean that how you *extend* a coarse credence to a finer carving matters a lot. Call this the "carving problem." I've been calling the principle that a decent theory of epistemic value shouldn't care how you slice up logical space "carving-invariance," and I want to know whether it's a real requirement or whether it's just a confusion of mine.
