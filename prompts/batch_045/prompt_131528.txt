I write a column on the human sciences, and I am closing a piece on what, if anything, economists mean by utility — specifically on the revealed-preference maneuver, in which one stops treating utility as a felt quantity that causes choices and instead starts from observed choices, treating utility as merely a numerical index that represents them. The consequences are what my piece is about: the index carries no content beyond the ranking it encodes, it is untouched by any increasing transformation of the numbers, it supports no claim about how strongly someone wants anything, and it grounds no comparison between one person and another.

I would like to end with a single joke that lands with readers who actually know this material, rather than with a generic gag about economists lacking a heart. Constraints:

Set the joke in a concrete, ordinary situation in which a bureaucratic or commercial actor is, for legal or methodological reasons, forbidden from asking people what they want and must infer everything from what they do — purchases, clicks, appointments kept. Invent the institution if that helps.

The punchline must turn on the actual content of the doctrine, not on its reputation. Pick one of these three features and tell me which you are using: that the index survives any increasing transformation, so the numbers say nothing beyond order; that it only supports claims about options an agent was genuinely offered, never about intensities or about counterfactual menus; or that observed choice cannot separate what someone wants from what they take to be available or permissible.

The humor should come from taking the view seriously rather than from treating it as stupid. No puns on utils, no canned economists-and-physicists setups, no economist-is-heartless material, and nothing that requires the audience to recognize a named theorem, paper, or author.

It must be tellable aloud: setup in two or three sentences, punchline in one, ideally as a short exchange between two people.

After the joke, three brief notes: which feature the punchline turns on and how; what an economist in the room would recognize as the real point behind the laugh; and where the joke cheats, that is, which element of the doctrine it overstates or applies outside the range where its advocates would accept it. If one of the three features resists comedy, say so in a line rather than forcing a joke out of it.
