You are a philosopher working at the intersection of criminal law theory and experimental philosophy. Think carefully before you answer: start by listing, in a few bullet points, the candidate accounts of intending that could be in play here (aim-or-purpose accounts, foresight-plus-certainty accounts, moralized or otherwise thick concepts of intentional agency, and views on which intent-talk is dispensable and the law is really just grading culpability directly), and note which features of the practice below each account predicts.

Consider this well-established legal practice. First, in many jurisdictions a jury is permitted though not required to find that a defendant intended a result when that result was a virtually certain consequence of his voluntary act and he knew that it was. A man who plants a bomb on an aircraft in order to claim insurance on a parcel, knowing the crew will certainly die, can be convicted of murder even though he wants nobody dead and would have preferred that they survive. Second, the same systems insist that motive is irrelevant to guilt: that you aimed at a good end, or would have welcomed avoiding the bad one, does not stop you from intending the means, and comes in only at sentencing, if at all. Third, the mirror image is simply absent: no doctrine anywhere converts a foreseen good outcome into an intended one, and no legal purpose has been found for which it would matter.

Now imagine a jurisdiction that closes the gap with a rule I will call constructive beneficence, enacted for sentencing mitigation only: a defendant who caused a prohibited result earns a reduction if some good outcome was a virtually certain consequence of the very same act and he foresaw it. A hacker breaks into a hospital network for profit and thereby, to a certainty, triggers a mandatory audit that catches otherwise fatal untreated conditions in a dozen patients. A firm dumps a chemical to cut costs and thereby, to a certainty, wipes out an invasive mussel that was collapsing the river ecosystem.

Now answer the following. What conception of intentional action does the existing practice — oblique intent plus the irrelevance of motive — presuppose? Be explicit about what it strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with, and about where it is underdetermined, so that several rival theories of intending would license it equally. Does it commit the law to the view that intending a consequence just is acting in the belief that it will near-certainly obtain, or can it be read as using foresight merely as evidence from which a jury infers a purpose, or as bypassing intention altogether in favour of a direct judgement of culpability? How should the bad/good asymmetry be read in light of the Knobe effect, where people say that a chairman who knowingly wrecks the environment as a side effect of profit did it intentionally, but deny this when the side effect is beneficial: is that evidence that the doctrine has quietly latched onto a moralized folk concept, or a performance artefact that tells us nothing about the concept the law deploys? Finally, would constructive beneficence actually be workable? If the asymmetry in folk attributions is real, fact-finders may stubbornly refuse to say that the hacker intended to save those patients. Say what that refusal, or its absence, would show about whether the doctrine we already have rests on a genuine psychological category or is a legal construct that merely borrows the word. Argue from the content of the practice; do not appeal to case names, empirical citations, or literature.
