You are a philosopher of science who also works on statistical methodology and meta-science, and I'd like you to think carefully and work in stages: first a rough, candid diagnosis of the pattern in the cases below, then a cleaned-up statement of the principle, then the cases it settles that I haven't given you. Don't just name-drop a framework and stop; I want the clauses.

Some background on where this comes from: I'm an assistant professor in a psychology department, and I spent most of a long departmental meeting arguing with two colleagues about how we should weigh evidence in the disputes that keep coming up in our literature. One colleague's position was essentially "preregistration plus big samples solves it — anything not preregistered is just storytelling." The other said the real villain is underpowered designs and that a single well-run study with a tiny effect estimate tells us more than a dozen small significant ones. I pushed back on both, and by the end of the argument I realized that I was issuing a string of verdicts that I couldn't state as a rule. The two of us did agree on one thing, though: that we can't go on treating the published literature as a simple record of findings, because the selection process into journals is itself part of the evidence, and because a lot of what gets published is the output of designs that had plenty of freedom in which outcome to report, which comparison to run, which participants to drop, or when to stop collecting. My own tentative principle was this: a result counts as evidence for a hypothesis only if it would survive being asked, in detail, what else the researchers could have done and didn't report — i.e., evidence is a property of a process of inquiry, not of a number. I like that principle, but I don't think it actually delivers all my verdicts, and I'd rather find out where it breaks than keep asserting it.

Here are the cases, with what I said about each. (1) A parapsychology paper reports a precognition effect from roughly a thousand participants across a series of experiments, with tiny p-values, explicit predictions, and — by the standards of the field — careful procedure and analysis; my gut says this is not evidence that the effect is real, and not merely evidence that gets outweighed. (2) A famous two-minute bodily-posture manipulation was said to change hormone levels and risk-taking on the basis of a study with about two dozen people per cell; a later preregistered replication with several times the sample, run in a different lab with a slightly different posture procedure, found essentially nothing, and I want to say this replication counts substantially against the original claim. (3) The same effect is then retested across a few dozen labs, each running the identical protocol, and the results scatter: a handful of labs clearly positive, a handful clearly negative, most clustered around nothing. I don't think this scatter counts much against the original; I think it counts as evidence that the effect, if it exists, depends on something about the setting that nobody has identified — and I notice that this sits badly with (2), where I was happy to let a null count against the effect. (4) A large preregistered study with well over a hundred thousand participants finds an effect that is significant at p just under .05 but so small that no one would design an intervention around it. My verdict: it is real evidence that the effect is not exactly zero, and my colleague's "that's just noise dressed up as a finding" is wrong — the design could have failed to detect it and didn't. (5) A meta-analysis of a literature finds a pooled effect comfortably different from zero, but a p-curve analysis of the same set of studies shows the significant p-values bunched just under .05 rather than spread toward zero. I don't count this as evidence for the effect. (6) A single paper reports three small studies, all significant, with p-values of about .04, .03, and .04. I want to say this is weaker than one adequately powered preregistered study at p = .001 — but I can't say why, since on my tentative principle nothing about the first case looks like a reporting sin: all three studies are right there in the paper. (7) The case we did agree on: a preregistered study with a couple of hundred participants finds that a hormone manipulation shifts risk-taking with p = .004. Both of us said: that's evidence, tentative but real, even though we'd bet against it surviving a decade. (8) The case I go back and forth on: an original study and one preregistered replication disagree, one significant and one null, both competently run, both with decent power. What should an editor do — publish the pair and describe the literature as unsettled, or treat the disagreement itself as the finding? And what does your principle say about a case nobody in my department could agree on: after a second, even better powered replication also comes back null, are we ever permitted to conclude the effect is absent, or does the mere possibility of an unidentified moderator keep absence permanently off the table?

So: what principle, or small set of rival principles, captures these verdicts?
