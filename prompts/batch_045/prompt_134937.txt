I work on programming language implementation — compilers, runtime systems, and the operational semantics of the languages I implement — and I'm fluent in the usual machinery: abstract machines and definitional interpreters, small-step and big-step semantics, observational and contextual equivalence, full abstraction, compiler correctness stated as preservation of observable behavior, the fact that extensional equality of input-output functions is an extremely weak notion of "same expressive power," and the entirely routine practice of extending a language with primitives that are not definable in it (floating-point instructions, syscalls, foreign function interfaces, unchecked escape hatches). I have no philosophy background beyond one graduate theory-of-computation course. Please argue rather than cite: I have no library access, and citations I can't check are useless to me.

What I want restated in that vocabulary is the debate over the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis, in three parts. First, the core claim: that the functions computable by a Turing machine are exactly the functions a human could compute by mechanically following a finite, explicitly stated procedure with unbounded paper and time. Second, the stronger claim sometimes distinguished from it: that anything computable by any physical process whatever is Turing-machine computable. Third, the question of what kind of claim the thesis is — a definition that fixes the meaning of an informal phrase, an empirical hypothesis about the world, a theorem in disguise, or something else — together with the style of argument often offered for it: that several independently motivated formal characterizations (general recursive functions, the lambda calculus, Turing machines, register machines and others) all turned out to pick out the same class, and that this convergence is evidence rather than accident.

Translate each of these as faithfully as you can into my vocabulary, without smuggling my framework's assumptions in and calling them translation. In particular: state what would play the role of the thesis in a setting where the objects of study are languages, machines, and semantics rather than intuitive notions of procedure; and be explicit when the only available restatement changes the subject.

There is one further claim I want run through the same translation, because it is the part I care about. Call it the interface thesis: the proposal that the right question about physical computation is not which functions a physical system can compute but which interaction behaviors it can realize, where a behavior is an unending exchange with an environment that must be responsive at every finite stage — some finite amount of output by each finite time, and always ready to accept more input. A colleague argues that this is a genuinely different claim from the Church–Turing thesis, that it survives even if the Church–Turing thesis is true, and that the standard framework is silent about it because it is built around functions on finite inputs whose answers arrive at the end. Under the translation, is she right? Does the distinction have a real counterpart in my vocabulary — the difference between a partial function and a productive process, between definability in a language and what a runtime that keeps a process alive indefinitely can sustain — or does my vocabulary collapse the two claims into one, and if so, is that because the distinction was never real or because my vocabulary is extensional by construction?

Then give me the residue report I actually want. Say which parts of the debate map cleanly onto distinctions I already make, which map only with distortion and what the distortion consists in, and which have no counterpart at all — my suspicion is that a claim relating a formal definition to an informal practice has no home in a discipline where the thing a specification is checked against is almost always another formalism, and I want to know what the nearest usable substitute is (validation of a specification against a corpus of hand-worked cases? differential testing against a reference implementation? something better?). I also want a hunch of mine tested: that talk of physical processes exceeding Turing machines looks banal in my vocabulary, since every serious language is extended with primitives it cannot define, and that whatever is substantive in the stronger thesis must therefore live at the level of what implements the primitive rather than at the level of the language. Tell me if that hunch survives contact with the debate or if it quietly concedes the point at issue.

Finally, the verdict: where the residue is a defect of my vocabulary and where it is a sign that the original claim was less crisp than it looked, and — if the translation shows something the philosophers' framing hides — what that something is.
