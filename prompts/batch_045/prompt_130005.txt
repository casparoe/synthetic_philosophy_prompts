I'm a first-year PhD student in cognitive science sitting in on an interdisciplinary reading group on cultural evolution, and I keep getting stopped in my tracks by the same sentence. Last week the discussion turned to why wildly improbable claims propagate so efficiently online while careful corrections mostly don't, and one of the senior people in the group ended the argument with the line *memes are like genes*: whatever replicates is what there is more of next generation, so of course truth has nothing to do with it — selection doesn't care whether a meme is true, only whether it copies. Earlier in the same term she had used the very same sentence for what looks like a different purpose: to argue that cultural change is genuinely Darwinian and not merely change over time, because cultural items, like genes, are replicators with lineages, inheritance, and heritable variation, and so the whole apparatus of population thinking is supposed to transfer. I can't tell whether those two uses are the same claim wearing different clothes, and I suspect the sentence is doing a lot of quiet work in both places.

What I'd like is a menu, not a verdict on who was right. Please set out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical, and give each a status: platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and contested, or straightforwardly false. I don't want to hand you my own list, but I'd want the distinctions I keep tripping over to be covered — *like genes* in the sense of having a lineage and a transmission chain; in the sense of being copied with fidelity high enough for cumulative, design-like complexity to accumulate; in the sense of being particulate rather than blending (and what happens to the analogy if cultural transmission is mostly reconstructive and the variants you get out aren't the variants you put in); in the sense of being a unit of selection with interactors and phenotypes further down; in the sense of varying randomly with respect to fitness; and in the sense of being *selfish*, i.e. of having interests that can come apart from the interests of the person carrying them. Say for each reading whether it is even coherent, whether it is testable in principle, and what kind of observation would tell it apart from its neighbors.

To make that concrete rather than abstract, here is a pair of hypothetical findings I invented to poke at the analogy, and I'd like each reading checked against them. Suppose that in a long-isolated island chain, canoe-building techniques improve measurably in hydrodynamic efficiency over eight centuries while ritual narratives in the same communities change just as rapidly but with no discernible directional trend at all. Suppose also that a costly ritual practice which measurably reduces the number of children its bearers raise to adulthood nevertheless spreads across the whole island group within two centuries, because it is preferentially performed by the most prestigious craftsmen and prestige-biased copying swamps the fertility cost. For each reading: does the first finding support, undermine, or simply not touch it? Same for the second? And — this is what I actually care about — which reading, if any, would really license the conclusion she wanted, namely that we should *expect* cultural selection to be indifferent to whether a meme is true, as opposed to licensing only something much weaker, such as that some cultural items spread for reasons unrelated to their truth, or that transmission mechanisms like copy-the-prestigious and copy-the-prevalent can beat out copy-what-works?

Two more things, if you can fit them in. First, the same person sometimes switches to the variant *ideas are like viruses*; I have a feeling that slogan carries different baggage — horizontal rather than vertical transmission, no shared fate with the host, host damage as the expected outcome — and I'd like to know whether the two slogans pull in genuinely different directions or whether the switch is just rhetoric. Second, a verdict on the dialectic: if the readings that would get her to her conclusion are the strong ones (selfish replicators whose interests diverge from ours, transmission biased in ways indifferent to truth), and the readings that are safe are the weak ones (lineages exist, variants spread at different rates), is the slogan functioning as an argument at all, or as a way of importing the conclusion under cover of an analogy nobody in the room is going to dispute? A table would suit me best: reading, precise statement, status, prediction about each of the two island findings, and whether it supports the debate-ending move.
