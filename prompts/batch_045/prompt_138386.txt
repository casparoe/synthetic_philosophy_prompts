I have recently joined the Standing Committee on Membership and Conduct of a national learned society — roughly nine thousand members, an annual meeting, a journal, a book prize, and a job list — and as the junior member I draft the recommendations. Our next case has split the committee in pre-meeting emails, and I would rather write a memo that reasons from our own record than one that just asserts a conclusion. So I want the structure of our past decisions made explicit before I commit to anything.

The precedents, all from the last four years, all decided by voice vote with short written reasons:

1. Member A, senior, used his allotted Q&A time after a junior member's paper to call her subfield "advocacy with footnotes" and invited the room to laugh at it. Verdict: removed from the following year's program; membership, submission rights, and journal access untouched; the committee published a statement calling the conduct unacceptable.

2. Member B, a graduate student, referred to a senior figure in the field with a slur in a public post, then deleted it and apologized unprompted within two days. Verdict: private letter of reprimand; nothing public.

3. Member C, mid-career, over two years and after two warning letters, used the members-only listserv four times to assert that scholars from a named region are constitutionally incapable of rigorous work, in deliberately inflammatory terms. Verdict: expelled; no reinstatement conditions stated.

4. Member D convened a society-sponsored panel arguing that the field should abandon one of its standard methodological requirements; members complained that the panel's premise was itself illegitimate and lent credibility to bad work. Verdict: no sanction. Reasons: the committee does not sanction topics or positions, only conduct in and around the society's own venues, and D had followed the approval process.

5. Member E, a non-member invited to give the plenary, was the target of a petition after a national outlet covered a blog post in which he argued that a named hiring program should be replaced with anonymized review. Verdict: invitation stood. Reasons: the society does not withdraw invitations over positions taken outside its venues, and a plenary slot is not an endorsement. His post said nothing about the capacities of any group.

Two further facts about the record: in four years the committee has never sanctioned anyone for what they wrote in a book or a peer-reviewed article — every sanction has followed either speech in a society venue or a targeted personal attack — and the committee has never specified what, if anything, an expelled member could do to come back.

The new case, Member G: mid-career and tenured, no current students, no editorial position. Eighteen months ago he published a 300-page monograph with a university press — full scholarly apparatus, a long statistical chapter — arguing that the field's outcome gaps across a named group are largely heritable and that the field's hiring targets therefore rest on a false premise. There are no slurs, no named individuals, and no claims about any colleague's competence; the register is dry throughout. The book sold a few hundred copies and drew no notice in the field until a large-circulation outlet ran a piece on it, after which a member filed the complaint that brings it to us. G has never raised this material in a society venue and has never posted about it. Asked to retract or apologize, he declined, saying the argument is offered in good faith on the evidence; but he has accepted an invitation to a published exchange with two critics in the society's journal and has agreed to any conditions on its format. The book is currently shortlisted for the society's triennial book prize, and the prize committee has deferred to us. Two last facts: the society adopted, a decade ago, a formal statement committing it to the full intellectual parity of the group in question; and the book's central empirical claim appeared in the society's own journal in 1996, in an article by a then-distinguished member, never retracted and never sanctioned. Finally, G is securely placed — expulsion would cost him very little materially, whereas expulsion would very likely have ended Member B's career.

Please give me, in American English and in 700 to 1,000 words, reasoning from this record rather than from the literature, and citing nothing:

First, the two or three rules that fit all five precedents — set each out in general terms, the way a future committee could apply it without asking us. Distinguish, where it matters, rules about withdrawing the society's own platforms and endorsement from rules about what membership itself certifies, and from rules that calibrate the sanction to how much it actually costs the person sanctioned.

Second, what each rule predicts on G, treated as three separable questions: membership, the prize, and whether the committee should issue a statement and of what kind.

Third, your best guess, with the closest precedent identified and an honest account of where that analogy breaks — in particular whether the middle option (retain membership, decline the prize, issue a scoped statement) is a genuine position or just two incompatible rules stapled together, given what the committee said in case 4.

Fourth, what the committee would be committing itself to under each option: what principle we adopt by expelling for off-platform scholarship, what we adopt by declining to act at all, and what we adopt by the middle path.

Fifth, the single change to G's facts that would best tell the two live rules apart, why it discriminates, and — if no such variation would separate them — what that tells me about whether our record supports any rule at all. Say also whether the 1996 article changes anything, and whether the fact that expulsion would barely touch G while it would have ruined B is a reason to adjust the sanction or a reason to ignore it.
