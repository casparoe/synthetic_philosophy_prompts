I'm a philosophy PhD student writing on the epistemology of mathematics, and I've managed to get myself into an argument with two people in my department whose judgments I otherwise trust, about how much confidence we are entitled to have in the coherence of our foundational theory. My supervisor treats the question as effectively settled and puts the risk somewhere around one in a billion; a friend in the mathematics department, who is otherwise no sceptic about set theory, says two percent and shrugs when pressed. I find myself around half a percent and unable to say what my number is made of, which is why I'm asking you. So: give me your own credence — a single number — in the proposition that ZFC (Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with Choice, as actually deployed by working mathematicians) is inconsistent, where that means there exists a finite sequence of applications of its axioms and rules of inference terminating in an explicit contradiction. I don't want a hedged "it depends on what you mean by consistent"; stipulate whatever you need to stipulate, say what would in principle count as the question being resolved either way, and then give me the decomposition that produces the number.

Here are the sub-questions I think the number has to be built out of, though please add any you think I've left out or reweight the ones I've got. (i) Base rates: unrestricted comprehension and Frege's system collapsed, and collapsed within a few years of being seriously used, because the contradiction was sitting in a single simple principle near the surface. How much of that episode transfers to a layered theory like ZFC, whose consistency is bound up with the iterative conception of set rather than with one axiom? (ii) Search exhaustion: after roughly a century of intensive use and the formalisation of large libraries in proof assistants, how likely is it that a contradiction would have surfaced if one existed — and note that this depends on the length of the shortest contradiction. I want this split, and I want a name for the second half: call it silent inconsistency, the case where ZFC is inconsistent but the shortest derivation of a contradiction is longer than anything we could ever write down or machine-check (say more than 10^40 symbols). How much of your probability mass goes there, and is silent inconsistency even a problem worth caring about, or does an instrumentalist about mathematics get to shrug it off? (iii) The ceiling on evidence: given Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, if ZFC is consistent it cannot prove its own consistency, so all our evidence is relative — equiconsistency results, inner models, forcing — or comes from stronger theories, which is circular in the relevant sense. How much does that structural fact cap the confidence available, independently of how good the evidence is? Relatedly, Gentzen's ordinal analysis of arithmetic in the 1930s reduces the consistency of Peano arithmetic to transfinite induction up to a particular countable ordinal; does that give PA a kind of evidential anchorage that ZFC lacks? And please respect the ordering constraint: since ZFC proves the consistency of PA, your credence in PA's inconsistency cannot be lower than your credence in ZFC's — if your two numbers violate that, say which one moves. (iv) Consistency versus soundness: much of what people mean by "we might be wrong about arithmetic" may really be worry about ZFC being consistent but unsound at the level of simple existential claims — proving that some decidable property has an instance when in fact no number has it. Price that separately, since it is a different failure mode with different practical consequences. (v) Stronger axioms: does passing from ZFC to ZFC plus large cardinal axioms, or to the universe axioms algebraic geometers sometimes help themselves to, raise the risk, because there are more principles that could collide, or lower it, because the hierarchy has proved remarkably well-ordered and no collision has ever been found?

Finally, three short things. Tell me which single term in your decomposition the final number is most sensitive to. Tell me which of my two colleagues — the one at 10^-9 and the one at 2% — is most likely disagreeing with you about a matter of substance rather than a matter of temperament, and where exactly that disagreement lives. And tell me what one piece of hypothetical evidence would move your number the most: a contradiction machine-checked inside a mainstream proof assistant's standard library, the discovery that two large cardinal axioms in the accepted hierarchy are mutually inconsistent, another century of uneventful use, or something I haven't thought of. Please reason it out rather than reaching for what the literature says; I'm interested in the structure of the argument, not in who has argued for what.
