ok so I have to give a short "where I stand" thing in a philosophy of science seminar I'm doing part time (I was a bench scientist for years, left the lab a couple of years back) and I'd rather be torn apart now than on thursday. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. I'm a realist. The unobservable stuff our mature, successful theories posit is real, and those theories are at least approximately true of it. What moves me is the no-miracles thought — if the posits weren't latching onto anything, the predictive success of science would be a fluke on a scale I can't swallow.

2. But I also think the pessimistic meta-induction is the strongest argument on the other side, and I don't think the usual realist answers to it are as good as realists make out. The way I'd put the argument: what went was always the *theoretical* part — the posits nobody could ever get at directly. The empirical regularities, the instrument readings, the protocols, they mostly survived. So the lesson of history is something like: trust the phenomena, don't trust the posits.

3. I think the observable/unobservable distinction is basically worthless epistemologically. Seeing a track in a cloud chamber is just seeing, with a longer causal chain. There's no bright line, it's all degrees, and a realist who grants that the distinction is principled has already given the empiricist too much.

4. I don't think "approximately true" is something anyone has actually made sense of, so I'd rather not lean on it. Every version I've seen either collapses into plain truth or into "some unspecified chunk of it is true," and neither can do the work realists want it to do.

5. What really confirms a theory is novel predictive success — predicting something the theory wasn't built to fit counts for a lot; accommodating data you already had counts for much less, maybe for nothing.

6. Reference for theoretical terms survives theory change. That's why I'm happy saying the electron was discovered rather than invented, and why I think "gene" still picks out the same thing even though molecular biology completely changed what we say genes are.

7. But I also think a theoretical term gets most of its content from the theory it sits in, which is why I have some sympathy with the incommensurability people — "mass" in 1700 and "mass" in 1905 don't seem to me to be quite the same concept.

Does this hang together? I have a feeling 2 and 3 might be fighting each other but I can't say why, and someone in the group told me 6 and 7 flatly contradict and I think she's wrong but I can't quite defend it. Go through it and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like they are — please rank them by how bad they are rather than listing everything as equally fatal, and for each one say what would have to give and what I'd be paying to give it up. I'd rather keep 1 if I can, but if 1 is the thing that's load bearing I want to be told. And if there's a single revision that tidies up the most, say which.

The case that made me care, if it's useful as a test on whatever you propose: for about six years I worked on an enzyme activity we'd purified out of tissue. The purification protocol worked every single time, the assay gave beautiful dose-response curves, we predicted inhibition constants before measuring them and were usually right. Then it turned out the activity belonged to a different protein than the one named in all our papers — we'd been manipulating, and successfully predicting the behaviour of, the wrong molecule for years, and none of it stopped working. Whatever fix you suggest should be able to say something about that one, because it's the one I actually believe.
