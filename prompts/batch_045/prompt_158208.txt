Write a dialogue between a Hartian legal positivist and a Fullerian procedural natural-law theorist. They are jurisprudential advisers to the Meridian Transitional Tribunal in the months after the collapse of the Meridian Regulatory Autocracy, and their task is to argue out how the Tribunal should treat the decrees issued by the Autocracy's final system of rule, known as Lex Automata, and how it should rule on the prosecution of Ilse Vantol.

During the last three years of the Autocracy, the regime governed through Lex Automata, an automated system that issued standing civic decrees. Two provisions are central. First, any person assigned a Civic Risk Score above 0.82 was classified a latent subversive and lost the protections of ordinary criminal procedure; the Bureau of Algorithmic Compliance could then impose preventive detention, expropriation of property, or transfer to labour camps. Second, any citizen who formally reported another person for alleged subversive propensity received full statutory immunity from prosecution for the act of reporting and a monetary bounty. On their face, these decrees seemed to satisfy the requirements Fuller associated with the internal morality of law: they were general in form, publicly promulgated on the civic portal, formally prospective, stated in clear numerical thresholds, not openly contradictory, possible for citizens to comply with in the sense that no one was commanded to do the impossible, stable in their announced content for two years, and congruent with official action, since Bureau officials, in nearly all documented cases, imposed the authorised consequences on those who crossed the announced threshold. Behind this surface, however, the weighting of the Civic Risk Score was never disclosed, and the Bureau retained unreviewable discretion to adjust scores in secret; later audits showed that the relatives of known critics were routinely nudged above the threshold.

Ilse Vantol, in the regime's final year, reported her husband to the Bureau after he privately criticised the Autocracy. She acted in reliance on the published immunity provision. Her husband's score was thereafter adjusted above 0.82; he was detained and died in a labour camp. Vantol has now been charged under an ordinary pre-Autocracy statute, never repealed, that criminalises unlawful deprivation of another's liberty. The Tribunal has not yet decided whether the Lex Automata decree was valid law at the time of her report. If it was valid law, her report was legally authorised, and convicting her would require either a frank retroactive statute or a refusal to give the decree legal effect for the purposes of the prosecution. If it was not law, because it failed the internal morality of law or otherwise lacked legal validity, then the old statute applies to her conduct without any retrospectivity problem.

The dialogue should present both advisers as equally competent exponents of their traditions. Neither should be a strawman; each should be capable of stating the other's view in a form its proponent would accept, and each should press the strongest version of their own position. They must engage in genuine back-and-forth rather than parallel monologue: every speech should respond to the specific argumentative moves the other has just made.

In particular, the exchange should work through the following matters, with each adviser revising their position in response to the other:

- Whether the decrees count as law depends, for the positivist, on the regime's rule of recognition and the social fact that officials accepted Lex Automata outputs as authoritative, irrespective of their moral atrocity; and, for the Fullerian, on whether the system satisfied the principles of legality, including generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, consistency, possibility of compliance, stability, and congruence between rules as announced and as administered.
- Whether the secrecy and secret adjustability of the Civic Risk Score defeat congruence and intelligibility, or whether they are merely defects in the evidentiary or administrative process that leave the announced rule intact and applied.
- Whether a systemic, unreviewable discretion to manipulate the triggering fact is better described as incidental corruption within a legal system or as a structural failure that removes the directives from the province of law altogether.
- How each tradition handles the moral and legal dilemma of retroactivity: the positivist should defend the Hartian view that it is more honest to punish Vantol under an openly retroactive measure, frankly acknowledging the moral cost, than to pretend the decree was never law; the Fullerian should defend the view that declaring the decree a nullity is not a dodge but the correct account of a system that had so far departed from the morality of order that it ceased to be a legal system, and should explain why this does not simply relabel a hard moral choice as a legal one.
- Whether the principles of legality apply to individual rules or to a legal system as a whole, and what follows for the Lex Automata decrees if the Autocracy's system was partly legal and partly a sham.
- The significance of Vantol's reliance on the immunity provision, and whether her expectation of immunity could ever be a legal expectation if the decree was not law.

The dialogue should end either by isolating the precise jurisprudential first principle on which the two advisers remain divided, such as whether law is ultimately a matter of social pedigree or of a normative claim to fidelity grounded in the internal morality of law, or by sketching a ruling or course of action for the Tribunal that each adviser can accept as a workable modus vivendi without either abandoning their theoretical commitment. The closing lines should make clear what each side still concedes and what it still denies.
