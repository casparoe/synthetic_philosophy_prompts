I want you to stress-test a view I have arrived at about commitment races — by which I mean competitions in which what the rivals are really competing over is not the prize itself but the ability to be first to make a costly, hard-to-reverse, publicly visible commitment, since whoever commits first narrows everyone else's options. The nuclear programmes of the 1940s and the present competition among frontier AI labs are the cases I have in mind.

The view has three parts. First, that the outcome of such a race is fixed far more by the mechanics of credible commitment than by the value of what is being raced for, so that the parties characteristically end up worse off than under any arrangement — even a bad one — that they could have struck before the race began. Second, that once the race is under way, unilateral restraint by whoever is in front is not merely expensive but self-defeating, because restraint spends the very asset that might have been traded for concessions, namely a credible threat not yet exercised; the only remaining exits are an external shock or a simultaneous mutual stand-down, and the stand-down needs verification capacity that the race itself tends to destroy. Third — the part I am least able to test — a mechanism I call the *irreversibility ratchet*: every irreversible commitment raises the cost of reversing course, which makes backing down less credible next time, which cheapens the next commitment because it now presents itself as mere consistency, so that the race accelerates even while the prize's value stays flat. Beneath all of this lies an asymmetry I treat as structural: commitment technology for escalation is abundant — hardware, contracts, hiring, legislation, public statements — while commitment technology for restraint is scarce, because a pledge not to do something can always be quietly undone. That asymmetry, on my account, is why escalation races are common and restraint races are rare.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the evidence and arguments that ought to move me, graded by how much they should actually move me rather than listed democratically, and with an indication of which of them I could reasonably set aside and which I could not. Include historical cases in which a race was slowed, redirected or called off by something other than a shock — I have in mind the Asilomar moratorium on recombinant DNA work, the 1969 American renunciation of offensive biological weapons, and the prohibition of blinding laser weapons agreed before such weapons were fielded — and say honestly of each whether it is a real counterexample or one my view can absorb. Distinguish the defeaters a defender of the view would himself concede from those he would wave off, and say why the line falls where it does. Say what the view predicts that its more optimistic rivals do not: for someone who thinks racing states or racing labs can coordinate on restraint mid-race, or that a leader's restraint tends to be matched rather than exploited, what observable difference in a race one could in principle watch would separate my story from theirs? And tell me plainly whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every instance of successful restraint is explained away as a case where the parties were not really racing, or where the race had already been decided, I want to be told that, and told whether it is fatal for a claim of this kind or an acceptable cost of making a structural claim about mechanisms rather than a prediction.

For my own part, what I would find hardest to dismiss is a well-documented case in which a party that was winning a race made a credible and verifiable commitment to restraint and extracted something of value in return; what would move me least is a model in which restraint is simply stipulated to be observable. Finally, tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the point you are least sure of.
