You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of social philosophy and game theory, with a specialty in Lewis's analysis of convention and in the later literature on how norms emerge and persist. Please think carefully before answering, and begin by restating in your own terms the account of convention you take me to be working with, so that we can be sure we mean the same thing by the word.

I am drafting a short paper on norms that hold up without any central enforcer: queueing, stopping at red lights, not cutting in line. I want to build the explanation around Lewis's account. As I read him, a convention is a regularity in the behavior of a population in a recurrent situation where: nearly everyone conforms; nearly everyone expects nearly everyone else to conform; each agent has a reason to conform conditional on the others conforming; everyone prefers general conformity to slightly-less-than-general conformity; the regularity is arbitrary, in that some alternative regularity would have served just as well; and all of this is common knowledge.

But the account seems to me to have a gap. In the cases I care about, people conform because deviators get glared at, shamed, or fined, and if the threat of punishment were removed, compliance would collapse. So I want to add a seventh condition: deviations are costly to the deviator because third parties impose costs on them. I also want that clause to do the work of explaining how the regularity gets started, since a few punishers punish, those punished conform, the regularity snowballs, and the resulting arrangement is stable.

Which of the conditions above should my sanction clause be read as extending, and where exactly should I insert it so that the amended definition still covers Lewis's own central cases while also covering mine? A colleague says the addition makes the account overgenerate, but I do not see the objection: surely a regularity that collapses without sanctions is the most fragile case, and therefore the one most in need of a sanction clause.
