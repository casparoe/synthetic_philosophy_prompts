I'm in the final year of my PhD and I'm giving a job talk in three weeks on the third chapter of my dissertation, which is about the inefficacy objection: the argument that since my purchase of factory-farmed meat, or my own emissions, or my own vote, makes no difference to whether anyone is harmed, I have no individual duty to abstain. My chapter's thesis is that the debate has been conducted as if all collective harms had the same causal structure, and that once you sort them into three, the standard expected-difference reply turns out to be the right answer in only one of them. In the continuous case — emissions feeding into atmospheric concentration and thence into welfare — individual contributions aggregate into a smoothly graded function, and the familiar thought that a tiny chance of mattering can be multiplied by a very large harm does the work it is supposed to do. In what I call the lumpy case, of which meat is my central example, it does not: a single consumer's abstention changes nothing today, because supply does not respond continuously. A friend of mine in operations research described to me the kind of ordering rules such systems actually run on — order-up-to and threshold-plus-replenishment policies, case-pack minimums, batch sizes fixed by contract rather than by demand — and on rules like those the system resets in discrete jumps, so my abstention is invisible until enough abstentions have accumulated to push some replenishment decision down by one notch. My positive claim is that the notch is deferred rather than lost: removing my demand shifts the distribution over when thresholds get crossed, so that under sustained abstention the expected reduction in production over a long enough window converges on roughly what the naive per-unit estimate predicted, except that the difference is finally made by somebody else's purchase at a later date rather than by mine. I call this the diachronic difference reply, and I claim that it answers the inefficacy objection for lumpy goods without leaning on the astronomically small pivot probabilities that the standard reply depends on. For the third case — one-shot legal thresholds such as a referendum, where there is nothing analogous to an inventory to defer into — I concede that no causal reply works and argue that whatever duty there is must be grounded non-causally, in fairness or in participation in a scheme, and I expect that concession to be the softest part of the talk.

The visit is to a department that is not my own, and the audience is mixed. There is a senior consequentialist who regards the inefficacy objection as settled by expected-value reasoning and is likely to say I have invented a problem in order to solve it; a Kantian who thinks the entire dialectic concedes far too much to consequentialism and will ask why the size of the difference I make should bear at all on what I owe anyone; a political philosopher who works on structural injustice, thinks individual-consumption framings are ideological, and will press me both on complicity and on whether my account quietly demotes institutional and regulatory duties; a formal decision theorist who will object that my convergence claim needs assumptions about the stability of demand that real inventory systems do not supply, and that my probabilities about threshold-crossing are no better grounded than the pivot probabilities I claim to be avoiding; and an applied ethicist with effective-altruism sympathies who will want to know what any of this implies for what we should tell people to do and what we should fund, and whether the diachronic story makes individual offsetting, corporate campaigning, or neither look like the better intervention. The format is twenty minutes plus ten for questions, and when I gave this at my home-department works-in-progress I lost forty minutes to the question whether threshold-crossing is a causal notion at all, which I would rather not repeat.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view and not a summary of the literature — assume I have read the standard material, and do not cite anything or attribute any position to a named author. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, in roughly the order in which they are likely to arrive and ranked by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that person would put it rather than in my vocabulary, and for each a reply I can give in about two minutes plus a candid note on where that reply is soft, where it begs a question, and where I should simply concede and retreat to something weaker.
