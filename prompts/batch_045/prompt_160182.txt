I'm a retired marine geophysicist — thirty-odd years of mapping the sea floor, and now, for reasons I can't fully explain, I sit in on a philosophy reading group at the public library (its mostly retired people and one very argumentative undergrad). We got onto that auction game where the auctioneer sells a dollar bill but both the highest and the second-highest bidder have to hand over their bids — you can bid a dollar ten, win the note, and still be out ten cents, while the runner-up pays their bid and gets nothing, so both keep bidding past a dollar. Everyone in the group calls it a sunk-cost thing. I don't think that's obviously right, and I want somebody to take it apart properly rather than nod at it.

Here's why I doubt it. At our field-station reunion a few years back I actually ran it with a real note and a crowd watching, and I added one rule: before any bids, the two players could settle the whole thing with a coin flip instead. Most pairs turned the coin flip down ("that's boring, I can win this"), and it was exactly those pairs that went past a dollar; a couple of the players who got burned told me afterwards that by eighty cents in they weren't trying to win the dollar any more, they were trying not to be the one standing there having paid for nothing. That sounds forward-looking to me, not backward-looking. So I've been seperating four candidate drivers: (A) plain sunk-cost reasoning, spending more to justify what's already gone; (B) the marginal calculus, where each new bid risks only a small increment while protecting a much bigger stake you'd otherwise lose for nothing; (C) rational learning, where the other side's willingness to keep going is evidence about how much they want it or how long they can last, so continuing is just updating; (D) face and identity, not wanting to be the sucker who quit in front of everyone. My guess is (B) and (C) do more work than (A) in the game itself, but I can't design the test that separates them cleanly.

What I'd like is the same question put to a real case: the American escalation in Vietnam between roughly 1964 and 1968. I know the outline, so take it as given — Congress's authorization after the Gulf of Tonkin incident, the Rolling Thunder bombing campaign run up in stages with pauses used as signals, search-and-destroy and body counts as the measure of progress, troop numbers climbing from a few thousand advisers to something like half a million by 1968, and then the Tet offensive in early 1968, which by most accounts mauled the other side militarily and yet is what turned US opinion, produced the bombing halt and de-escalation, and led to withdrawal a few years later. So: set the mapping up properly — who the players actually are (is it even two?), what each one's options were at a given moment, what counted as the prize and what as the bid, and whether anyone occupied the auctioneer's position. Then say what the game structure predicts and where it stops fitting. My own suspicion is that the analogy breaks on the exit rule: in the auction, quitting costs you only your own bids and the thing is over, whereas in a war there's no move that ends it and refunds anything, you can always re-enter later, and the prize isn't a known fixed dollar — nobody on either side agreed what control of the south was worth, and both seem to have revised it as they went. I'd also like to know what the credibility argument does to the mapping, the one about US commitments elsewhere being worthless if it walked away. And the lab work I only half-remember says people escalate more when they personally made the first decision — does that say anything here, given the escalation ran across two administrations? Last thing: I'm skeptical of the textbook's favourite example too. Everyone cites the Concorde as the type case of throwing good money after bad, except the development money was government money already spent and there were treaty commitments, jobs, and national prestige in favour of finishing — so is the type case even a case? Where the model has nothing to say, say so instead of making it speak.
