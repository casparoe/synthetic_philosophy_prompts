I'm a PhD student in philosophy working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and the ethics of AI, and I've been asked to circulate a short position statement ahead of an interdisciplinary workshop on artificial welfare. Before I send it out I want a consistency check on the commitments I've drafted, because the package feels stable until I try to apply it to a case, and then something gives. I don't need a literature review or references — I know the debates and I'll supply citations myself. What I want is the conceptual audit.

Here is where I think I stand:

(a) Suffering is not merely the occupation of an aversive or negative-valence functional role. It requires phenomenally conscious experience: there must be something it is like for the subject.

(b) I am a functionalist about consciousness: whether a system has phenomenal states is determined by its causal or computational organization, not by its substrate and not by how it was made or what it was made for.

(c) The reward signal as it figures in a reinforcement-learning objective is not itself valenced, and nothing about it constitutes suffering. It is a scalar fixed by the system's designers, and its relation to the agent is extrinsic.

(d) If anything in a standard RL agent could ground valence, it is the agent's learned value function — the internal estimate of expected discounted return that supplies a single common currency in which states and actions are ranked from the agent's own forward-looking perspective. I notice, though, that I also sometimes put this by saying that the temporal-difference error signal is the candidate, and I am not sure whether the slide between value and TD error is harmless or whether the two must be kept apart.

(e) Suffering requires more than aversion. A state's being such that the subject is disposed to avoid it is not sufficient; the state must also be unwanted by the subject in a way that is not exhausted by the avoidance disposition, and I do not think expected-return maximization obviously supplies that.

(f) The candidate sufferer is the agent — the policy and value function as realized at a time — not the training process. The sequence of environments, episodes, and gradient updates is not itself a subject.

(g) Diachronic unity matters for the central cases. For something to dread an outcome and then undergo it, the entity that anticipates and the entity that undergoes have to be the same subject across the interval.

(h) A case verdict I am inclined to give: an agent that learns an explicit predictive model of its environment — by which I mean it learns transition probabilities and evaluates states by planning or simulating trajectories through that model — represents an aversive outcome as forthcoming, and assigns the current pre-outcome state a strongly negative prospective value, is a genuine candidate for suffering; a model-free agent that has merely cached a low value for that state and steers away from it is not — the latter has, at most, aversion.

(i) Methodologically, I think the question should be settled from the top down: get an account of what suffering is in the paradigmatic animal case and then ask whether the RL architecture realizes it, rather than by looking for behavioral signatures in trained agents. Relatedly, I do not think that a signal's playing the reward-prediction-error role in an animal is by itself a reason to identify it with felt valence; such signals seem at least as much involved in learning and attention as in pleasure.

(j) One thing I am genuinely unsure about: between the anticipation and the outcome, an RL agent's parameters are typically updated by learning, so the implementation that does the anticipating is not numerically the same as the implementation that undergoes. I don't know whether this is a real threat to (f) and (g) or a pseudo-problem of the same kind as the persistence worries we don't take seriously for animals.

What I would like from you: go through (a)–(j) and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, how severe each tension is, and for each one what would have to be weakened or given up. Please also tell me which apparent conflicts dissolve on inspection and why, so I don't end up fixing something that isn't broken. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. Finally, if there is a single revision that would clean up the most, tell me what it is and what it costs me elsewhere in the package.
