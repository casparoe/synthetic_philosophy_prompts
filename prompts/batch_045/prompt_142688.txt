I teach a graduate seminar in epistemology and I'm setting the final take-home exam: 48 hours, no collaboration, answers capped at 1,500 words. I need one question on luminosity and the KK principle (Kp → KKp) that can't be answered by summarizing the Williamson-style margin-for-error argument and announcing that knowledge isn't luminous.

The seminar has been working with a toy model, and I want the question built around it. A subject's evidence at a case w is represented by the set R(w) of cases compatible with it; R is reflexive, and knowledge is treated as factive evidential support, so that S knows p at w if and only if p is true at w and p holds throughout R(w). A condition C is luminous if and only if, whenever C obtains at w, R(w) is a subset of C.

The crux I want students to confront is this: the margin-for-error argument is often presented as though it quietly refutes KK, but on this model the two theses are in a much tighter relation than that. KK is valid on a frame if and only if R is transitive, and the anti-luminosity argument derives its entire force from the failure of transitivity, since small differences in how things stand are evidentially indiscriminable while large ones are not. So the argument does not refute KK as a corollary; it forces a choice between KK and intransitivity, and the real question is what each horn costs. In particular I want students to see why the natural KK-preserving repair — read in a position to know off the transitive closure of R rather than off R itself — collapses in the sorites series, because the transitive closure of a chain-connected space is the universal relation, which leaves no room for non-trivial knowledge.

Second, a model answer of roughly 600 to 700 words, including both directions of the equivalence result and a statement of the collapse argument.
