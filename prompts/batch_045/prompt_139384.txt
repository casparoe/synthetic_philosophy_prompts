I serve on the grants committee of a private foundation that makes about forty awards a year in areas connected to long-term risk and global priorities. We have before us a full proposal, *Cooperation Without Contact*, requesting $1.8M over three years from four researchers in decision theory and machine learning. It asks for support in studying whether, and under what conditions, it can be rational for an agent to bear costs for the benefit of agents it cannot causally influence, when the agents' decision procedures are likely to be structurally similar or otherwise correlated: spatially separated civilizations, agents inside simulations, and independently developed AI systems are their paradigm cases. The investigators argue that if such acausal cooperation is rational, then because the reference class of correlated agents may be enormous, even a modest probability of the relevant correlation justifies expenditure at our scale.

Three features of our situation make this more than a straight call on the merits.

First, the money available to us this cycle comes from a restricted gift whose instrument requires that grants support work with an identifiable mechanism linking the funded activity to the benefit sought. Our counsel reads that as requiring a causal pathway. The investigators reply that this begs the question, since the benefit is supposed to flow through correlation between decision procedures rather than through causation, and that a donor drafting those terms decades ago could not have contemplated the issue.

Second, the investigators concede that we will never receive evidence about whether the work paid off: if acausal cooperation works, the benefits accrue to agents we cannot communicate with, and if it fails, nothing observable happens either. One trustee treats this as disqualifying — a funder that can never learn whether it succeeded cannot, she says, be acting responsibly with other people's money. Another trustee, a committed evidential decision theorist, answers that our own decision is itself part of the pattern: a foundation that declines because it demands feedback thereby becomes evidence about what structurally similar funders do, and she claims this makes the no-feedback worry incoherent rather than disqualifying. A third asks why, if this style of reasoning pays, the agents and institutions that already employ it are not visibly better off than the rest of us.

Third, the proposal has two workstreams — a formal one on the rationality of acausal cooperation, and an empirical one on whether independently trained systems with correlated architectures in fact converge on mutually beneficial policies — and the investigators have told us the two are inseparable and that they will not accept a partial award. Several of us suspect the empirical workstream would satisfy the gift's mechanism requirement while the formal one would not.

The committee is split evenly, and the split does not track expertise.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the trustees and to the applicants before Thursday, no more than about 1,200 words. It should state a concrete bottom line — fund, decline, or fund a specified reduced or conditioned version, with a figure and conditions where applicable — and it should:

1. keep the three questions that our discussion has run together separate: whether acausal cooperation can be rational for an agent deciding for itself; whether it can be rational for a fiduciary spending other people's money under a restricted gift; and whether a body that can in principle receive no feedback may responsibly act at all. Say which of the three your recommendation actually turns on.
2. be followable by a trustee who has never encountered Newcomb's problem, without flattening the decision theory into something simpler than it is.
3. give the strongest consideration on the losing side in the form in which the applicants or the trustees would press it, and say what would have to be true for it to carry the day.

Do not settle the matter by observing that the idea sounds strange, and do not lean on citations: we cannot check them and our trustees will not be moved by them. If the honest recommendation is one we cannot in good conscience execute — declining while telling the investigators we would fund under a different decision theory, for instance — say that plainly and explain why.
