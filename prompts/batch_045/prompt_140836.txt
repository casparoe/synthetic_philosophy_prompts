I'm writing a short conceptual appendix for a policy institute that is drafting a housing and pension proposal in which some benefits would be keyed to generational cohort rather than to age, income or housing-cost exposure, and before I sign off on the framing I want to know how much weight the category can actually bear. So here is the proposition I want a number on: that generational cohorts — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, on whatever cutoffs you prefer — are real kinds in the sense that matters here, meaning that the boundaries track something in the world (shared causal structure, projectible similarities, a common explanatory history) rather than being intervals drawn for convenience and then filled in by journalism, marketing and self-description. Please give me your credence in that proposition, decomposed rather than bare: one sub-question at a time, a rough probability for each, an explicit statement of how the terms combine, and a sentence of justification for every number. I'd be grateful if you wrote the whole answer in British English.

The decomposition I keep reaching for runs roughly as follows, and part of what I want from you is a verdict on whether its structure is right. First, how much turns on the metaphysics of what a real kind has to be — mind-independence, projectibility, homeostatic clustering, or something weaker — that is, on the probability that any purely demographic grouping could qualify on the best available account. Second, how much turns on the identification problem: since cohort, period and age are linearly related, any apparent cohort effect is formally entangled with age and period effects, and the standard ways of breaking the tie rest on assumptions the data alone cannot settle. Third, how much turns on whether a cohort's causal commonality derives from events rather than from the calendar interval itself — call this Event-anchored versus Calendar-anchored cohorts; school-entry years, conscription classes and the entrants to a labour market during a hiring freeze seem to me to be anchored one way, while the familiar generational labels, and the boundary disputes that produce micro-generations such as Xennials or Zillennials, seem anchored the other. Fourth, how much turns on the interactive-kinds point: if classifying people changes how they understand themselves and thereby makes the generalisation true, does that confer reality on the kind or disqualify it? Fifth, how much weight you would give the diagnostics I am tempted to lean on — that the popular cutoffs have been redrawn at least once without new psychological evidence, that cohort vocabularies look national rather than global, and that a real kind should presumably make the number and edges of the generations determinate rather than negotiable.

I should say where I stand: I am at about 0.3, a colleague whose judgement I respect is at about 0.65, and I suspect we are not really disagreeing about the demography at all. So please tell me where a disagreement of that shape most likely lives, which single term your own figure is most sensitive to, and what kind of result would move it most — a natural experiment, a solution to the identification problem that does not smuggle in its answer, or something in the metaphysics. Two last things: say explicitly whether your terms are jointly necessary conditions that multiply, or independent routes any one of which could suffice, since I think that is exactly where my colleague and I come apart; and close with one paragraph on whether a credence of 0.3 or one of 0.65 should make any difference to the proposal, given that rules keyed to birth-year bands may generate whatever real cohort structure they presuppose.
