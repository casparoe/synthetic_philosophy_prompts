I convene a small philosophy-and-literature reading group, and I am writing the handout for a session that pairs H. G. Wells's short story "The Country of the Blind" with Molyneux's problem — the question, put to Locke, of whether a man born blind who can tell a cube from a sphere by touch would be able to tell which was which by sight, before touching them. We have no library access and no one in the group can look anything up, so I need a reading that doesn't depend on citations or on the empirical case literature: I half-remember that the modern cataract-surgery evidence is disputed and sparse, and if it comes in at all I want it only as a foil. What I want is a reading of the story in terms of the problem, of roughly the length I could hand out and discuss in an hour: which scenes carry the philosophical weight, what the story shows or complicates about the question, and where an honest critic has to admit the story resists the reading.

The angle I have been turning over is that Wells's story runs Molyneux's question in the direction the philosophical literature almost never does. Nuñez is not a Molyneux subject: he is an adult with a lifetime of visual practice who is dropped into a world whose paths, kerbs, windowless houses and routines have been built for touch and hearing, and who must acquire by hand and ear what he already has by eye. I've come to think that the interesting version of the problem for the story is not the classical shape question — can the newly sighted subject match a felt cube to a seen cube — but what I've been calling the affordance version: could a newly sighted person tell by sight, without touching, that a thing is liftable, that a gap is passable, that a surface will bear weight, that a person is nearby? Wells's valley is precisely an environment whose affordances are haptic and auditory, and Nuñez's constant stumbling is the evidence. I'd like you to say whether that reframing changes the force of the problem or merely relocates it. I also want three smaller threads pressed: the person-level case, whether Nuñez could pick Medina-Saroté out by sight having only touched her face and heard her voice, and whether the story gives us any grip on this at all; the testimonial case, whether the villagers could acquire the concept of seeing from Nuñez's reports alone, and whether their failure to do so is a point about testimony and concept acquisition or merely about their conservatism; and the credit question, whether a subject who sorted the cube correctly at first sight would thereby be applying the concept cube visually, or whether something less than conceptual could produce the same performance.

Two further things I'd like handled explicitly. First, the surgery: the village doctor declares Nuñez's eyes diseased, he consents out of love, and then he flees at dawn rather than lose them. Does anything a philosopher could conclude about cross-modal transfer bear on whether sight is worth keeping, or is the story tempting us into a slide from an epistemological question to an evaluative one — and if it is that slide, does Wells make it knowingly? Second, a member of the group who has been blind since birth objects that Molyneux's question is asked by sighted people about blind people and virtually never in reverse, and that this is why the story is the right pairing; but I worry the asymmetry cuts deeper, since the villagers have had a lifetime to adapt and Molyneux's subject has had no time at all, which may mean the story is not really a case of the problem in either direction. Say whether you think it is evidence, an intuition pump, or something else. Please work from the 1904 Strand version, in which Nuñez climbs out alone and the story ends with him lying smiling under the stars; I know Wells later expanded it with a rockslide and an escape for Medina-Saroté, and I'd like a sentence on whether using the revised ending would change what the handout should claim. Describe the scenes rather than quoting them — I'll supply the passages myself — and correct me quietly if I've got a detail of the plot wrong.
