My philosophy reading group (mostly non-specialists, plus one retired biologist) has me defending a particular account of what life is, and I want to stress-test it rather than keep polishing it. Call it the maintenance view: something counts as alive just in case it is the kind of thing that can die, and a thing can die only if its continuing to exist depends on its own ongoing activity holding it together against decay — so the mark of life is the standing need for self-repair, not metabolism as such, not replication, and not the capacity to evolve. What I like about it is that it has clean answers where the usual definitions get clumsy: a mule or a sterile worker ant is obviously alive even though it cant reproduce, a virus seems to fail because outside a host cell it isn't holding itself together at all, and I dont even have to decide what the standard textbook definition (a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution) implies about a single organism that will never have descendants.

What I want from you is what would count against it. I mean the defeaters with teeth, not the objections I've obviously already fielded. Which borderline cases actually brake it — a cryptobiotic tardigrade or a bacterial spore with no measurable metabolism for centuries, a brain-dead body kept oxygenated on a machine, a cell whose membrane is maintained entirely from outside, some future software agent that earns money to pay for its own compute and stops existing if it stops earning? Which intuitions about organisms, death, and persistence is it quietly committed to and therefore stands or falls with? Does it collapse into one of the rivals it was supposed to replace — the autopoiesis story, metabolism-first views, the evolution-capacity definition — so that I've just renamed someone else's theory? And have my three hedges (ongoing activity, dispositional activity, activity under the right conditions) qualified it into something that cannot lose? I'm personally most moved by cases where the verdict would change what we'd actually do: whether to declare someone dead, whether a Martian sample is a biosignature, whether a seed bank preserves life or only its ingredients. Be willing to tell me the view is well insulated, and whether that's a real problem for a definition of this kind or just what definitions are like.

Give me a table with exactly these columns: case_or_objection | what_would_have_to_be_true (what the world has to be like for this to count against me) | severity (fatal / serious / awkward / doesn't really bite) | would_a_defender_concede (yes, grudgingly, or no — and why) | best_available_reply | live_or_merely_logically_possible. Rank the rows with the most fatal first. Then one short paragraph after the table: the single defeater you'd actually bet on, and the smallest revision to the view that would make it risk something real.
