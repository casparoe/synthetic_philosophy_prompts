My flatmate and I spent a solid two hours after the pub last night going round in circles about whether a heart that was assembled molecule by molecule in a lab — never evolved, never selected for anything, sitting in a creature that will never reproduce — would still have the function of pumping blood. I said obviously yes, it does something; he said no, nothing is *for* anything there. We got nowhere, mainly because we kept sliding between 'what it does' and 'what it's for' without noticing.

I've got to run a fifty-minute seminar for second-year phil of biology students on the etiological vs causal-role accounts of function next week, so give me the plan, not the explanation. Specifically:

- what has to be in place before either account even looks like an answer to a question — I think the load-bearing bit is that they're answering different questions (why is it there at all / what does it contribute to some larger capacity), and I want that ordering to do real work rather than be a preamble
- one case to carry the whole thing, and what such a case must have and must lack. Is the heart actually the wrong choice, given they'll all say 'pumping, obviously' and switch off? I want something with two real effects where only one is pre-theoretically 'what it's for', no designer anywhere in it, and enough room to run the same case through both accounts
- the misconceptions in the order they'll actually form, with where in the sequence to head each off: 'function just means what it does', 'the evolutionary one smuggles in purpose or a designer', 'if it has a function it must be helping the organism right now', and the one I'm least sure how to handle — that the causal-role account hands out a function to everything, including the noise the heart makes
- where the pair stop disagreeing about facts and start disagreeing about words, and whether to say that out loud at this level
- what to flatly refuse to get into in fifty minutes
- one question at around minute forty that would tell me the distinction has landed rather than them having memorised 'selected effect vs current contribution'
