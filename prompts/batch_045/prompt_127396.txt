I'm retired now — thirty-odd years recording from visual cortex, so I come at this stuff from the wiring side rather than the armchair side — and a few months ago I started going to a philosophy reading group at the local library. Last week we got onto disjunctivism and the argument from illusion and two of the members, Marta and Dev, got properly stuck into each other afterwards over coffee. I'd of thought it was a settled thing, but they both kept making points the other couldn't answer, and I honestly couldn't referee it. The case they were fighting over was the usual one: a hallucination of an apple subjectively indistinguishable from really seeing one.

Marta opened with the standard argument: such hallucinations are possible; if two experiences can't be told apart from the inside, then whatever makes the experience the kind of experience it is must be present in both cases; so seeing the apple and hallucinating the apple share a common mental factor (a sense-datum if you like, or at least a common phenomenal character or representational content), and the difference between them is extrinsic — whether the shared factor got caused the right way and whether the world happens to cooperate. Dev came back saying "indistinguishable from the inside" is a claim about discriminability and not about kinds, and that the perfect hallucination is anyway a myth: real seeing is coupled to the scene, so as you move your head the shading, the parallax and the highlight keep changing in ways that are supplied by the world and don't have to be generated internally, whereas a hallucination has to be synthesised by machinery with finite bandwidth and will degrade if you probe it hard enough — long enough, actively enough, in enough fine spatial detail. So, he said, the premise is only true for thin, brief samples of experience, and the argument needs it for the experience as a whole. Marta then said that even if he's right that changes the subject, and offered a case of her own: record the whole pattern of activity across the visual hierarchy while someone looks at an apple, and later stimulate those same neurons with that same pattern, whatever the technology turns out to be. Now the proximal neural state is the same as in the good case and only the causal history differs; so either you say the resulting experience is a different *kind* of experience, which makes the character of an experience depend on extrinsic history (Swampman with a visual field), or you say it's the same kind, in which case you've granted the common factor. Either way, she said, disjunctivism either collapses into a causal or externalist version of representationalism — a common-factor theory that just insists on reserving the word "perception" for the lucky case — or it's simply false. Dev's reply had two halves. First, replaying a snapshot can't reproduce the ongoing loop through the body and the world, and if you imagine replaying the whole loop you've stopped imagining a hallucination at all. Second — and this was the bit nobody answered — the order of explanation runs the other way: a hallucination *of an apple* is only identifiable as that by reference to what seeing an apple is; it's the degenerate case, the thing that happens when the apple isn't there but it otherwise goes as if it were. So hallucination can't be an independent datum from which to infer something about perception, because the notion of hallucination already borrows from the notion of perception, and the argument is circular, or at any rate can't deliver the reading where perception turns out to be just a special case of the shared factor. Marta said that's an point about the order in which we find things out and not about what things are — plenty of scientific kinds were first picked out via a broken or poisoned version of the mechanism and then turned out to be the general category — and that if Dev's own positive claim is that seeing differs from hallucinating because seeing is coupled to the world and poised to guide action, that's a functional difference, perfectly compatible with the experiences being intrinsically the same kind of thing. At that point I put in that in the lab we never had a bright line between signal and noise, and they both talked past me, so that was the end of it.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and tell me who's right about what — not just who wins, but where each of them is onto something real and where each is quietly sliding? I'd also like to know what, if anything, they're *both* missing: my suspicion, from years of arguing with colleagues about whether we were measuring a mechanism or just a correlation, is that they never settled what a theory here is supposed to be for, but I can't turn that into a proper point. Please don't give me a reading list or tell me which papers to look up — our group has a strict no-name-dropping rule and I can't get to a university library easily anyway — just work with the arguments as I've written them. And whoever referees has to commit, so finish with one last line and nothing after it, in exactly this shape: Verdict: <one of: Marta, Dev, neither> — <one short clause, six words at most>.
