I got into an argument with a friend about eight months ago that we still haven't settled, and I want to know what kind of argument it actually is — not who's right about the topic, but why two reasonably careful people can go round and round on it and never touch each other. Please don't reccomend me things to read; I'm about to be somewhere with no internet for a while and I want your own diagnosis, not a reading list.

It started from a thing I half-remembered from a podcast: an economist named Sen, I think around 1970, apparently proved that you can't have both a bit of individual freedom and the principle that if literally everyone prefers X to Y then the group should prefer X to Y. The example was two guys, one prude and one letch, and one copy of a certain famous dirty novel: each of them gets to decide whether *he* reads it, the prude would rather nobody read it but would rather read it himself than let the letch enjoy it, the letch would most like the prude to read it, and you end up going in a circle: nobody reads beats prude reads beats letch reads beats nobody reads. Fine, cute. But then I tried rebuilding it myself to check I'd understood, and I came up with two versions of my own that I can't tell whether they make the result more worrying or less. Version one: two neighbours, each of whom has an absolute say over the colour of his own front door and nothing else — noone is claiming any say over the other's door. But they're nosy: one wants his door to clash with the other's, the other wants his to match, and there's one pair of arrangements both of them rank the same way. I get the same circle again, and my friend says that shows the whole thing has nothing to do with rights and is just a fact about people having preferences about other people's business; I say it makes it worse, because now there's no meddling or paternalism anywhere in the setup and you still can't put the outcomes in a consistent order. Version two: if you let the two of them decide one at a time instead of "all at once", the circular thing seems to partly dissolve — in the book example you get a perfectly definite outcome, just a worse one for both of them than a different outcome they'd both rather have; in my door example it doesn't settle at all, there's no arrangement where both are willing to leave their own door alone. So it looks to me like what's doing the work is the demand that the group's rankings line up tidily, or that everything be settled in one shot, rather than anything about freedom.

So: what kind of stalemate is this? Is it just a fight about what the word "right" or the word "liberalism" is being made to mean inside a mathematical model; is it that the model itself smuggles in the requirement that a group have one tidy ranking, so the result is an artifact of the setup rather than a discovery about freedom; is it a genuine clash between two things we both value; or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading — one asking how a committee should aggregate votes, the other asking what it is to have a right at all? My own hunch is that the real dispute is about whether a right is supposed to *pick an outcome* or just *take some options off the table*, and that everything else is downstream of that. Tell me if that's right, where each of my two versions lands relative to it, whether the bits I noticed are things people already have names for or whether I've made an error somewhere, and — given your diagnosis — what, if anything, would actually count as somebody making progress on this rather than just restating their side louder.
