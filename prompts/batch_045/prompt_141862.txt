I write a fortnightly column on science, technology and ideas for a national magazine, and a growing share of my pieces turn on a claim of the form "the system itself is political": a scheduling algorithm, a prepaid utility meter, an exam-proctoring tool, a hospital triage score. Every time I let that sentence into print I am making an attribution — that a political effect I can observe (some group's options narrowed, some group's dependence deepened, some group's standing lowered) would not have arisen, or would have arisen differently, had a differently built artifact been installed in the same institution. My technical sources push back on this in one direction ("we only optimize what we were told to optimize") and my editors push back in the other ("so you're saying the object has opinions"). I need something better than my instinct.

I already know the canonical argument that artifacts can carry politics, and its two chestnut cases — the parkway built with bridges too low for buses, the harvesting machine that reorganized a rural labor market. Please don't rehearse them, and don't give me a survey of the literature, a reading list, or citations: I can't check a bibliography against the sources I have on deadline, and it wouldn't help me with the cases in front of me.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run. Concretely: I get a proposal for a piece, I have roughly twenty minutes and access to public documents plus one or two interviews, and I need to decide whether the political effect belongs to the artifact, to the institution that deployed it, to the arrangement the two are embedded in, or whether I can't tell — and then write one or two defensible sentences. Please give me:

The questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops early with "don't make the attribution," and where it hands the decision back to me because it is a values question rather than a factual one. Each step should end in a short phrase I could lift into the copy.

Two failure modes the procedure has to block. It must not let me drift into talk about what the designers secretly intended — I can't establish that, and it isn't needed. And it must not let me write as if the object itself has agency or a will: the artifact's contribution has to be cashed out in things I can check — what the thing physically or logically requires of the people around it, what it makes costly, what it makes impossible without a second party's cooperation, what it forecloses if it is withdrawn.

One distinction I've been developing on my own and want you to sharpen or reject rather than ratify. Call it the upkeep question: an artifact can be politically quiet at the moment of installation and become politically loaded later, because keeping it working requires a continuing relationship with some party who can stop providing something. On this view the interesting questions are not only "what did it do when it arrived" but "what has to keep happening for it to keep working, who can stop that happening, and what is left behind on abandonment." My instinct is that this is a genuinely different location for the politics than either the thing's design or the deployment institution's policy — that it catches cases like a device that is fine until its vendor goes bankrupt, or a system that quietly transfers power to whoever controls its data refresh. If it collapses into one of the other locations, say so and tell me which; if it is real, give it its own step and say what evidence would satisfy it.

Please run the finished procedure on three of my own cases:

1. A transit authority buys a system that builds bus operators' rosters from predicted ridership. Operators describe unpredictable split shifts, twelve-hour unpaid gaps, and second jobs they can no longer hold. The vendor says it optimizes for coverage against constraints the authority sets; the authority says the constraints come from the contract and the budget.

2. A "smart" electric bicycle battery that unlocks its full range only through the manufacturer's app. Riders who bought the bikes outright find, after the manufacturer enters insolvency, that the batteries will not charge to full capacity and no third party will service them.

3. A university adopts a commercial AI essay-detection service. Non-native English speakers are disproportionately flagged, and there is no route to contest a flag. My strong preference — and I'll be honest that it's partly that it's the better story — is to write that the detector itself is the thing doing the harm. If your procedure tells me the politics sit elsewhere and my headline has to change, tell me plainly and show me the sentence I should write instead; I would rather be wrong in the draft than in print.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which cases it will systematically misclassify, which judgment calls it dresses up as steps (I suspect choosing the comparison class and choosing whose options count are both smuggled in), and where an editor simply has to decide. Keep the whole thing under about nine hundred words, in prose with numbered steps rather than a diagram.

End with a single machine-readable line giving the label your procedure assigns to case 3 — one of ARTIFACT, INSTITUTION, ARRANGEMENT, UPKEEP, or UNSETTLED — in the form \answer{LABEL}.
