I help run a monthly film night attached to my university's undergraduate philosophy society: one film, someone from the department invited along, and about twenty minutes of setup from me before the lights go down. Next month is Tenet, and I'm the one doing the setup. I've been reading LessWrong and its descendants for something like a decade, so my instinct is always to find the crisp distinction underneath a mushy question, and I'd rather spend the twenty minutes on one sharp thing than on a survey of the thermodynamic arrow that half the room will already have absorbed from YouTube explainers.

The sharp thing I keep circling is what I've started calling *retrospective deliberation*. If the standard entropic account of records is right, then the direction in which an agent's memories and traces accumulate is fixed by the direction of local entropy increase, which means an entropy-inverted agent remembers what is, from our point of view, still to come and acts on reasons that are located later in our time than the action itself. Every inverted character in this film is in that position — that is what makes the temporal pincer work, and it is why the forward characters are told to keep things from themselves — and the limit case is Neil, who near the end chooses to go back and do something that, from where we are standing, is already over by the time he chooses it; if I have the mechanics of that scene wrong, correct me, but I think the shape of it is right. So: does the film ever let an inverted character deliberate under genuine uncertainty, or is it consistent in treating them as agents whose reasons are downstream of their own outcomes? If it is consistent, is it accidentally saying something interesting about whether rational deliberation requires an open future — something like a dramatized version of the worry that a perfect predictor destroys the possibility of choice — or is it just cheating to preserve suspense? And is there a reading on which the inverted characters are the only genuinely free agents in the film, because the ones moving forward are the ones who cannot know what they are bringing about?

The second thread I want is about what the film's fatalism is actually commitment to. This is a single-timeline picture: nothing anyone does changes anything, and there is a refrain about what has happened having happened. But determinism is time-symmetric, so a block universe by itself cannot explain why anyone experiences the past as settled and the future as open, and the usual move is to explain that felt asymmetry via the thermodynamic and record asymmetry rather than via determinism. I would like the setup to make that point and then ask whether the film actually cashes it: are the inverted characters shown experiencing our future as fixed and our past as open, or are they just given normal-looking forward planning with a few props turned around? I am also curious about the formal conceit — the title from the Latin word square, the names borrowed from it, the habit of replaying a sequence from the other direction, the cars and the battle running in two directions at once — which looks like an image of the standard modern package: time-symmetric local dynamics plus a low-entropy boundary condition, with the arrow imposed from outside rather than baked into the processes. Does the film earn that, or does its visual language of smoke un-smoking, glass re-forming, and explosions running backward depict a world in which the asymmetry is intrinsic to processes after all, in tension with the palindromic framing?

Two last things. First, the honest part: where does this reading break, and where is it me imposing a distinction the film cannot support? If you think the whole exercise is a category error because the film's inversion is just time-reversed kinematics with an entropy word stapled to it, say that and tell me how to handle it in the room — is there a version of the discussion that survives, or should I pivot? I have also been unable to shake a deflationary reading on which the film is not about time or entropy at all but about asymmetric information: every advantage in it is an advantage in who knows what and when, and the machine's real function is simply to point someone's knowledge asymmetry the other way, which would make the physics incidental. I cannot tell whether I resist that because it is wrong or because I want the film to be about entropy. Second, a constraint: no quotations and no bibliography — I cannot check the screenplay, and I will do my own reading if I want to follow something up.
