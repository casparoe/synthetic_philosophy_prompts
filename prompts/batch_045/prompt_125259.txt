I work in distributed systems — consensus protocols, partial synchrony, failure detectors and quorums, safety versus liveness, and the standard impossibility results about what can be agreed when messages may be delayed, reordered or lost. I have been reading about Aumann's agreement theorem: the result that agents who share a prior cannot disagree about the probability of an event if their posteriors for that event are common knowledge between them, together with the companion claim that such agents should not expect to profit from trading merely on the strength of their private information. I do not want this explained to me in philosophers' terms; I want it restated in mine.

Please translate the following one at a time: (i) the theorem itself; (ii) the shared-prior assumption, and the doctrine that goes with it, that any residual difference between rational agents' opinions must be traceable to differences in the evidence they have received rather than to differences in how they started; (iii) the requirement that posteriors be common knowledge, as opposed to known to depth one, or two, or any finite depth; (iv) the no-trade corollary. For each, give the closest structural counterpart in a message-passing model — nodes with local state; an agent's information partition as what that node can and cannot distinguish, given its message history; a common prior as an identical initial configuration; a posterior as local state after some sequence of deliveries — then say what you had to distort to make it fit, and what has no counterpart in that vocabulary at all.

Two points I especially want the residue report to address. First, my field has results to the effect that in an asynchronous system with even one possible crash failure no non-trivial common knowledge is ever attained, and no deterministic consensus protocol is possible. If that is right, the antecedent of the agreement theorem is unsatisfiable by any physically realised system. Does that leave the theorem vacuous as a claim about real agents, or is the philosophers' notion of common knowledge doing different work from ours — and if so, what exactly is the difference? Second, I deliberately build systems in which the nodes disagree: diverse replicas, ensembles, quorum decisions where I want independent failure modes rather than convergence. If persistent disagreement between like-minded agents is supposed to be rationally indefensible, is my engineering practice irrational, or does the theorem have nothing whatever to say about it?

Conclude by telling me, for each piece of residue, whether you take it to be a limitation of my vocabulary or a sign that the original claim is less determinate than it is usually taken to be.
