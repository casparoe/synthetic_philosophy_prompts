Here is what I want analyzed. I work on research-integrity policy for a consortium of journals, and I keep running into a puzzle about what our authorship rules are really saying.

The standard regime, which most biomedical and social-science journals follow, has four conditions for being named an author of a paper: a substantial contribution to the conception or design of the work, or to the acquisition, analysis, or interpretation of its data; drafting the work or critically revising it for intellectual content; approval of the version to be published; and agreement to be accountable for every aspect of the work, so that questions about the accuracy or integrity of any part of it get investigated and resolved. The same guidance adds that each author should be able to identify which co-author is responsible for which other part, and should have confidence in the integrity of the others' contributions. Separately, one individual is designated the corresponding author and takes primary responsibility for all communication with the journal. Alongside this, many journals now publish a contributor-role statement: a fixed taxonomy of roughly fourteen roles — things like conceptualization, methodology, data curation, formal analysis, software, writing, supervision, funding acquisition — under which a person can be listed for a role without being an author at all.

The regime is honored partly in the breach. Some papers in physics and genomics have bylines with thousands of names, where nobody could identify which co-author is responsible for which part, let alone vouch for all of them. Journals also routinely permit posthumous authors, who cannot approve anything or be accountable for anything. And most journals now state that an AI system cannot be an author, usually on the grounds that it cannot consent to the work or take responsibility for it — even when the system did much of the analysis and drafting.

Now the proposal I have to evaluate. To deal with papers with more than twelve contributors, our consortium is considering what I will call distributed guarantee authorship. The byline would carry the name of the collaboration rather than a list of people. Each individual would be registered as a guarantor of record for an explicitly enumerated set of components — specific figures, tables, datasets, code, or sections of the argument. Each guarantor would sign a statement of mutual reliance: they certify that their component was produced in reliance on the others' components, and that they would have withdrawn it had they known some other component to be unsound. Corrections, expressions of concern, and retraction notices would attach to components and, derivatively, to their guarantors. No individual would vouch for the paper as a whole; the collaboration, listed as author in the indexing databases, would be the entity that owns the retraction — though the only sanctions available to us would still fall on individuals.
