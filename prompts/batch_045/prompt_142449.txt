Apparently in Jain philosophy there’s this practice where you’re not supposed to just say a thing is or isn’t; instead you tag the claim with a qualifier that means something like conditionally or in some respect, and then pick one of seven modes. The seven modes are, roughly: conditionally it is; conditionally it is not; conditionally it is and is not; conditionally it is inexpressible; and then conditionally it is and is inexpressible, conditionally it is not and is inexpressible, and conditionally it is, is not, and is inexpressible. The whole outlook is called anekāntavāda, or many-sidedness, and the seven-mode scheme is saptabhaṅgī. They also contrast a partial standpoint, called naya, with a fuller or more authoritative take on the thing, pramāṇa, and the idea is that ordinary language only ever gives you the partial standpoint version.

I’m definately missing something, because on the surface it just sounds like fancy word games. Here’s what puzzles me. The practice looks like it’s doing something stronger than ordinary perspectivalism, but I can’t tell exactly what. It affirms and denies the same thing, and then it uses a sentence to say the thing is inexpressible, which seems either inconsistent or at least super tricky. It also has this weird fourth mode where the thing is inexpressible, and then it combines inexpressible with is and is not, instead of just stopping at the first three. So can you reconstruct the implicit theory here? What view of truth, contradiction, and the limits of language does this practice only make sense under? Does it strictly presuppose that reality itself contains contradictory aspects that our yes/no logic can’t capture, or could a plain flat relativist or contextualist view explain the whole thing, where every claim is just true relative to a standpoint and there’s no real contradiction because the standpoints never apply at once? And what does the practice strictly presuppose versus what it merely fits with? For instance, does the inexpressible mode force a commitment to reality outrunning language, or is it just a way of marking that some standpoint-combos are too messy to assert?

Now I’m gonna add a wrinkle, because I don’t want the stock answer that it’s all just perspective. I want to split the many-sided view into two versions. Call the first flat many-sidedness: there’s a bunch of standpoints, all on the same level, and a claim is true or false only relative to a standpoint, and that’s it—no standpoint is ever assessed by another standpoint. Call the second stacked many-sidedness: standpoints themselves can be evaluated from further standpoints, so you get a hierarchy, and you could even apply the same seven modes to the choice of standpoint, not just to the object. If you do that recursively, I guess you get something like a standpoint about a standpoint about an object, and maybe that second-order assessment is itself only conditionally true; call it recursive naya if you want a label. My real question: does the traditional sevenfold practice strictly presuppose stacked many-sidedness, does it merely sit comfortably with flat many-sidedness, or is it philosophically underdetermined so several theories would license it? Point to specific bits of the practice—especially the fourth mode, the combined seventh mode, and the naya/pramāṇa contrast—that tell us which. If we dropped the implicit theory and replaced it with flat many-sidedness, what would have to change in the practice? Would we lose the inexpressible mode, or could we keep it as a polite way of saying we don’t know which standpoint matters? And if we go stacked, does the practice need an infinite tower of standpoints, or is there a natural place to stop?
