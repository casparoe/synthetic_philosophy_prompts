I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas, mostly demography, longtermism and the effective-altruism-adjacent beat — and I'm working on a piece about how people who worry about the very long-term future think about population size. I keep running into the repugnant-conclusion argument: start with a modest population of very happy people, imagine instead a vastly larger population of lives that are barely worth living, and the total-welfare view seems to say the second is better, which everybody I talk to feels in their gut is wrong — but every fix anyone proposes has its own absurd consequence. I've been kicking around a home-baked fix of my own and I want to see whether it survives contact with someone who actually likes the total view, so I need you to play that person for me.

You're an unnamed philosopher who's spent years arguing that what matters is the total quantity of welfare, and that the repugnant conclusion, once you get clear about what's actually being asked, isn't repugnant at all — it's just what you get when you stop smuggling in the question "what would it be like for *me* to live there." One request: I already have a reply to "your view makes the value of a life depend on facts about other people's lives" — average utilitarianism does exactly that too — so if that's where you're headed, bring something more.

My opening, then. Call it "no extra credit for copies": the value a life adds to a population is discounted by how much of what makes it good is already instantiated elsewhere in that population, so the billionth life at the same low positive level — say +1, where 0 is neutral — adds almost nothing, while a life whose good is realized in a way nobody else's is adds its full value. On this view a huge population of genuinely varied, mutually irreplaceable lives really is better than a small one, so I'm not an average utilitarian in disguise; but the standard comparison never gets going, because in the standard comparison the enormous population is described only as "the same barely-worth-living quality of life," and I think readers picture a homogeneous mass. The bit I like most: when I've described the huge population to friends as ten trillion lives all at +1 but realized in completely different ways — different projects, different pleasures, different everything, just the same net level — the revulsion softens quite a lot, which is some evidence that what people are reacting to is the degeneracy and not the headcount. So: tell me why no-extra-credit-for-copies is wrong, and be good at it.
