im in a philosophy of mind seminar this term and we did the access/phenomenal distinction and global workspace theory last week, and I keep going round in circles about what actual clinical practice is committed to, so I want to use it as a test case.

Here is the practice. Someone survives a catastrophic brain injury: eyes open, sleep-wake cycles, no command following, no purposeful movement. The diagnosis turns on whether you can get anything out of them — follow this command, blink twice, say your name. That is what gets scored at the bedside. Lately there is a second channel: put the patient in a scanner, ask them to imagine playing tennis versus imagine walking through their house, and if the activation patterns come apart on request, they get reclassified as conscious even though nothing changed behaviourally. I think people call that covert awareness. Same shape in anaesthesia: depth is titrated to movement, to haemodynamic response, to some processed EEG number, and accidental awareness during surgery is treated as a serious harm. So: access is the test. But experience is what everyone agrees is at stake. When people argue about these patients they are arguing about whether there is someone there, whether they are suffering, whether it is OK to withdraw assisted nutrition — not about whether a representation has been made available to downstream consumer systems. And note the thing that bugs me: once the scanner channel is found, clinicians and families talk as if the patient had been conscious the whole time, trapped and frightened, when by hypothesis the only thing that changed was our access to them. Meanwhile the same teams still sedate and give analgesia to patients they have officially diagnosed as not conscious, because you cannot rule it out.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory. Does the practice presuppose something like the identity of phenomenal consciousness with access or with global availability, or is its real commitment only epistemic — access is the one evidence channel we have, plus a brutal cost asymmetry between under- and over-attributing? Be strict about the difference between what the practice needs and what it merely sits well with, and tell me which views it actually rules out. Also disambiguate for me, because I think three different things are getting collapsed in seminar: Block's access consciousness, global broadcast as a mechanism, and plain behavioural responsiveness at the bedside.

Two cases I made up, please take them seriously. Call the first silent broadcast: the workspace is fully intact, ignition and broadcast and downstream consumption all happen normally, but every output channel is destroyed, and there is in principle no measurable signature, not even a neural one, that distinguishes the patient's mental activity from noise. Call the second an orphaned island: a sensory system produces structured, valenced, miserable experience that never reaches the workspace and leaves no trace in any downstream system at all — no ignition, no availability, nothing. Is the first patient conscious on a global workspace view, and is the practice systematically wrong about them if so? Is the second one suffering? Which of the two does the practice have to have a view about, and which of the two does more work in showing what the practice is committed to? My suspicion is that GWT is not the view that best fits the practice but the one that makes it look incoherent, but I can't get the argument straight.

Then: if we swapped the implicit view for a strict access-identity view, what would actually change at the bedside, and what would change if we swapped it for a view on which orphaned islands are real and common? And be honest about where the practice is underdetermined and several theories license the same behaviour — including whether the precautionary reading explains everything and the metaphysics never shows up at all.
