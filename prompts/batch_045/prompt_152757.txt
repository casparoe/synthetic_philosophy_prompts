Consider prioritarianism in its standard cardinal form. For an outcome x, its value is V(x) = Σᵢ f(uᵢ), where uᵢ is person i's lifetime well-being, taken to be interpersonally comparable and cardinal, and f is increasing and strictly concave, so that a given increment of well-being contributes more to value the lower the level at which it accrues. It is frequently claimed that prioritarianism thereby escapes the levelling-down objection that burdens teleological egalitarianism: because f is increasing, moving a better-off person down to the level of a worse-off person, while improving no one, strictly lowers V. I want your credence that this claimed escape does not survive scrutiny.

The proposition, stated precisely: prioritarianism is not materially damaged by levelling-down considerations. Equivalently, the negation I want you to assess is that prioritarianism is materially damaged — that it generates levelling-down-type verdicts which are sufficiently counterintuitive that a reasonable inquirer, having fully grasped the structure of the cases, should lower their credence in prioritarianism, relative to its principal rivals, by at least 0.15. As a resolving condition: I will treat the matter as settled affirmatively if, over the coming decades of this debate, a clear majority of those working on distributive axiology come to accept both that the verdicts in question are genuinely entailed by prioritarianism itself and that they count substantially against it.

Two cases, with everything not stated held fixed. Assume 0 is the neutral level for existence, so that a life at 1 is a life barely worth living and adding it is not a harm to the person added.

Outcome I: two people, at levels 10 and 5.
Outcome II: three people, at levels 5, 5 and 1.

Moving from I to II makes the person at 10 worse off, leaves the person at 5 exactly as they were, and adds a third person at level 1. No one who exists in both outcomes is better off; one such person is worse off; the only other change is an addition. With f(u) = u^0.5, V(II) ≈ 5.47 against V(I) ≈ 5.40, so prioritarianism ranks the levelled-down, augmented outcome above the status quo. More generally, with f(u) = u^a, the ordering in this case flips at a value of a only slightly above one-half — I make it roughly 0.52, and I would like you to check that arithmetic as part of your answer. Now add a loss-aversion parameter: let the disvalue of a person's fall from u to u′ be λ times its prioritarian magnitude f(u) − f(u′), while gains carry weight one. Then II outranks I only if λ is below f(1)/(f(10) − f(5)), which under f(u) = u^0.5 is about 1.08. So a very modest asymmetry between losses and gains suffices to block the verdict.

My own credence that prioritarianism is materially damaged here is about 0.55; a colleague whose judgement I respect puts it at about 0.2. Give me your number, not a survey of positions, and give me the derivation. Specifically:

Decompose the question into the sub-questions your credence actually rests on, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each, and then show how they combine. I take the following to be candidate nodes, which you should use, amend, or replace as your own reasoning requires: whether prioritarianism really does entail the Outcome II verdict as opposed to its being an artifact of how the view is extended to variable populations; whether the person-affecting thought that nothing can be better when no one is better off and someone is worse off has independent force as a constraint on the axiology of outcomes; whether what is doing the work in Outcome II is the priority weighting at all, rather than the variable-population structure that also generates the repugnant-conclusion-style results; whether any nearby variant — loss-asymmetric prioritarianism, a critical-level or sufficientist threshold, a priority-plus-equality hybrid — retains whatever motivates the view while avoiding the verdicts; and whether the levelling-down intuition is evidentially weighty or is instead explained away by norms against interference, or by a general status-quo or non-maleficence heuristic.

Then: which term is your total most sensitive to, which term are you least confident about, and what single hypothetical argument or finding — empirical or conceptual — would move your number the most, and in which direction? Finally, given the 0.55 and the 0.2 above, tell me which of those nodes you think the disagreement between my colleague and me most likely lives in, and why.
