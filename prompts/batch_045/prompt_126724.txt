I am a PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on advance directives and the authority of the earlier self, and I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of a practice I keep circling around. The practice is the ordinary one in which a competent adult records instructions about future medical treatment and those instructions are later treated as binding once the person has lost decision-making capacity: clinicians and families are told to follow them, and in most settings the earlier instruction overrides both the judgement of what is now best for the patient and the patient's own contemporaneous wishes. I want to know what has to be true for that to make sense.

Four features of the practice strike me as doing the real philosophical work, and I would like your reconstruction to account for all four.

First, the timing. As long as the person remains competent, an earlier directive has no authority at all: a competent patient who changes their mind is treated as having revoked it, whether or not they ever got around to tearing up the form. The earlier self's word becomes binding at exactly the moment it becomes unrevisable. I have started calling this the answerability condition, and it seems to me to be backwards relative to every other domain in which one person's decision binds another — contracts, promises, votes, instructions to an agent — where authority standardly lapses, or at least weakens, once it can no longer be revisited or questioned. The one familiar exception I can think of is the testamentary will, where we do let a dead hand dispose of property. So: is the medical directive best modelled on a will, and what does that comparison buy and what does it break? A will is usually explained by convention, by the owner's ongoing interest in the disposition of what was theirs, or by the needs of survivors — none of which is obviously an explanation in terms of the signer's persisting agency.

Second, the asymmetry between refusing and requesting. An advance refusal of even life-saving treatment is treated as close to absolute. An advance request — keep me on the ventilator indefinitely, give me this intervention whatever the clinicians think — is not: the standard response is that clinicians owe only what is medically indicated, so the earlier self can veto but cannot command. That asymmetry looks to me like evidence about what the practice is really for.

Third, the hard case. An elderly patient with diabetes and a gangrenous limb said, years earlier and while fully competent, that she would never want to be kept alive in a demented state. She is now demented, appears content, says cheerfully that she wants to live, and resists the treatment plan. Standard practice asks the surrogate to exercise substituted judgement — to ask what she would have wanted, not what is best for her now, and not what she now says. Whatever the practice presupposes, it presupposes that the content demented woman's present utterance loses to a sentence she uttered years ago.

Fourth, the psychiatric variant. In a number of jurisdictions a person with recurrent psychosis can execute a clause consenting in advance to admission and medication during a future episode, including medication they will refuse at the time — the earlier self authorises the coercion of the later self. I do not need the jurisdictional details and I would rather you not depend on them. My question is comparative: in the psychiatric case the later refusal is discounted because it is taken to be the product of an impaired episode, whereas in the dementia case the later refusal is discounted without, I think, any claim that the person is not still a competent-enough agent with genuine present welfare. If those two cases are justified differently, then the practice is not one thing.

Here is what I want from you. Set out the implicit theory the practice needs, adjudicating between at least these families, which I will name in my own terms so we can avoid the literature: (a) a continuity or identity view, on which the earlier instruction binds because the later patient is, or is continuous enough with, the person who signed, and it is that relation that carries the authority; (b) a will-based or agency view, on which the authority is the authority of an act of will — a standing exercise of self-government, on the model of a promise, a trust, or an authorisation to an agent — and does not depend on any claim about who the later patient is; (c) a side-constraint or jurisdictional view, on which the practice is not really honouring the earlier self at all but enforcing a right against bodily interference whose content is fixed by the last competent expression; and (d) an instrumental view, on which the point is predictive accuracy, fewer family conflicts, and less burdensome bedside deliberation. Then tell me which of these the practice strictly presupposes, which it merely fits comfortably with, and which it rules out. In particular: does the practice require anything like a psychological-continuity account of identity, or would a biological or animalist account license it just as well? Is it consistent with a view on which the demented patient is no longer the same person as the signer?

Then use the four features as tests rather than illustrations. Does the refusal/request asymmetry tell against the will-based view — is a will that can only say no really a will? Does the answerability condition tell against any view on which authority is supposed to track the persistence of the will? Does the psychiatric variant show that the earlier self has authority to coerce, or only that the later self's refusal is judged defective — and if the latter, what is doing the work in the dementia case? And be explicit about where the practice is silent: about the content of directives, about how much the signer must have understood, about what to do with a directive that the person would almost certainly have revised.

Finally, two probes. Suppose a jurisdiction enforced an escalator clause, in which the signer stipulates that any later objection to the directive is itself to be treated as evidence of impaired judgement and disregarded. Does your reconstruction permit or forbid that clause, and with what justification? And suppose a jurisdiction required reaffirmation in light of a structured encounter with people actually living in the relevant condition, on the grounds that most signers badly misimagine it. Would the theory you have reconstructed make that requirement mandatory, optional, or incoherent — and would the practice as we have it look unjustified if it lacks such a requirement? Close by saying what would concretely have to change in clinical practice if we replaced the presupposed view with a view on which authority tracks present welfare rather than a past will.
