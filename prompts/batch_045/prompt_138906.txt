I'm a backend engineer — about eight years in industry, mostly Go and TypeScript, comfortable with specs versus implementations, contracts and assertions, property-based tests, type inference, and enough formal methods to have read about model checking without ever having used it. No philosophy beyond what I've absorbed from argumentative people on the internet.

Here's where this came up. At a design review last week we were arguing about whether a service should keep retrying a downstream call indefinitely. One teammate said: telemetry can tell you what the service does, but it can't tell you what it should do — you can't get an ought from an is. Later, when someone proposed defining correct behavior as whatever the service had actually done over the previous ninety days, another teammate said that was just the naturalistic fallacy. Both lines were used as conversation-stoppers, and I don't think either speaker could have said what precise claim was being made. I also don't know whether the two of them were invoking the same distinction, or two different ones that happen to share a name.

I'd like the underlying positions restated in vocabulary I can actually reason in, and I'd like to know what gets lost in the restatement.

The claims I want translated, as faithfully as you can:

1. The is–ought gap — the thesis usually associated with Hume, that no conclusion about what ought to be follows from premises that only say what is.
2. The naturalistic fallacy in the sense Moore is usually credited with: the claim that goodness cannot be defined in terms of any natural or descriptive property, together with the thought that it always remains an open question whether something having that descriptive property is good.
3. The vernacular, appeal-to-nature sense of the naturalistic fallacy — inferring that something is good because it is natural, or bad because it is artificial — which I suspect is a different animal from 2 despite the shared label.

Please work in the following vocabulary: runtime behavior and execution traces versus written specifications; tests as samples of behavior versus requirements as norms; candidate invariants mined from traces versus specs that someone has actually accepted; types and type inference, including whether a value ever forces an annotation; assertions, contracts, and backward-compatibility constraints; and ought as it appears in a code review comment, where it is always relative to some spec and some stakeholder.

Three places where I don't trust my own mapping, and want you to test it rather than accommodate it:

(a) My instinct is that the is–ought gap is just the observation that no volume of tests, logs, or traces entails a specification: you can only check code against a norm you brought with you, or guess at one. That seems obviously true and not very deep. It also seems trivially easy to close — add a bridging premise like we want the service to stay available, and now is plus that premise yields an ought. If the gap closes that cheaply, what work is the thesis doing? Is it a substantive claim about reasons, or bookkeeping about which premises an argument needs?

(b) I think my framework may be smuggling something in. Every ought in engineering is hypothetical: relative to a spec, a goal, an owner. If the translation renders moral oughts that way, it has quietly assumed that all reasons are goal-relative and left no room for demands that bind you regardless of what you happen to want. Flag that explicitly instead of smoothing it over.

(c) Some of my descriptions already seem to contain norms: this function is broken; that isn't a real retry policy; this endpoint is underspecified. Defect words and function words look like they carry a standard inside a factual claim. Do such claims cross the gap, or do they merely relocate the normative premise into a definition? And is there an actual philosophical position that leans on this, or am I inventing one?
