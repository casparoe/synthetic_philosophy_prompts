After thirty-odd years of psychophysics and single-unit recording in the early visual system, I retired and began auditing a graduate seminar in the philosophy of perception. I have come to think that the dispute about whether cognition can penetrate perception is conducted with a taxonomy too coarse to settle anything, and I would like you to construct an argument for a conclusion I have been edging towards but cannot yet state cleanly.

The taxonomy I propose, which I ask you to adopt for the purposes of the argument and to say where it breaks down, separates top-down influences on perception into three kinds. Call an influence sensitivity-like when it alters the gain or the discriminability of a channel: the channel still says what it says, only more or less loudly. Call it criterion-like when the mapping from sensory evidence to reported content shifts because the costs of the two possible errors are asymmetric in the subject's situation — the predator you fail to detect is dearer than the rustle you mistake for one. Call it content-imposing only when the influence makes the experience represent a property it would not otherwise have represented, in a way that is not a function of what is there to be perceived: the novice who has been told that a rare warbler is in the tree and who thereupon sees the bird as one.

The conclusion I want argued for is that cognitive penetration raises no epistemically distinctive problem for perception. More specifically: (i) sensitivity-like and criterion-like influences, which I suspect account for most of the experimental battery — effects of hunger, of fear, of monetary or emotional valence, of induced expectation in ambiguous displays — are not corrupting at all, since a system optimised against an asymmetric loss function, or against its own noise, may reduce expected error by precisely such shifts; (ii) content-imposing influences, where they genuinely occur, create no new sceptical problem, because they merely make the resulting experience inherit the epistemic standing of the cognitive state on which it depends, so that the experience offers dependent rather than independent support for the belief it prompts; and (iii) the correct upshot of the whole debate is therefore neither that perception is encapsulated nor that it is penetrable and hence epistemically compromised, but a revision of the independence condition on perceptual justification — roughly, that an experience can justify a belief only if the experience is not an epistemic descendant of that very belief.

Please build the argument so that a scientifically literate reader who has read nothing in the area can follow it. Define every term of art as you go, including informational encapsulation, experience as opposed to judgment, and justification as you understand it. Do not lean on who first said what: I have no interest in the history of the debate and no way to check it.

Two things in particular the argument must survive. First, the objection I keep meeting in seminar: if the experience inherits its standing from the cognitive state, and that state was itself shaped by earlier experiences of the same kind, then what we have is a feedback loop rather than a foundation — circularity with extra steps, and allegedly worse for being sub-personal. Second, a pair of cases I cannot get out of my head: the field ornithologist who, after forty seasons, simply sees the bird as a warbler, and the novice who sees the same bird as a warbler because he was told to expect one. The two are, so far as I can tell, indistinguishable from the inside, and they differ in no respect that the word penetration marks. Your argument should say what the epistemically relevant difference between them is, and why that difference is not simply the difference between a true belief and a false one.

Aim for roughly seven hundred to nine hundred words of argument, the denser the better. I would much rather have one objection answered properly than three gestured at.
