My reading group does one of those big "how did moral change actually happen" books every year, and last night we got completely stuck on something that I've been chewing on since, mostly because I dont have the vocabulary for it. The book's headline claim, as I took it, was that the arguments people give for ending a practice aren't usually what ends it: the practice is already on its way out and the arguments show up afterward and get the credit. I more or less buy that. But the thing I keep noticing when I read about the ends of duelling and of slavery feels more specific than that, and I want to know whether it's a thing with a name, or whether I'm just confusing three ordinary things for one.

Here's the shape of it. Take a gentleman in the 1700s who gets insulted and has to fight. Ask him why and he says, basically, because otherwise he'd be a coward — and that one word is doing all the work, the whole institution rests on it. Now hand him a flawless argument that duelling is just murder, that the insult can't really touch his worth, that the law forbids it, and so on. From what I've read, a great many of these men could have granted every step of that argument and still turned up at the field, because the argument never touches the part where the word grabs him. And what eventually ends the practice isn't anyone defeating the case for duelling — nobody really did, it more or less stopped being made — it's that two or three generations later the same word can be said out loud, by people who still think cowardice is a bad thing, to an audience that still thinks so too, and it simply doesn't grab anymore. The belief didn't change; the grip did. The slavery case looks similar to me: you find plenty of slaveholders saying in writing that they wish the whole thing were over and would be glad to be rid of it, people who already share most of the descriptions and some of the values of the folks attacking them, and it runs on anyway for another couple of generations, and then it stops in a way that looks abrupt if you were expecting arguments to be what did the work.

So: is there an established name for that specific thing — a consideration coming loose from a person who still endorses it? I've tried poking around and I keep getting pointed at words that are nearly right and not quite. It isn't weakness of will: these people don't experience themselves as falling short by their own lights, they feel entirely in order. It isn't ordinary hypocrisy. It isn't just interests, since in both cases the practice cost the people doing it a great deal and they knew it. It isn't quite a plain change in fashion or in what's admired, because that story says people came to want something different, whereas this is more like the reasons themselves quietly ceasing to be reasons for them while remaining reasons in the abstract. There's also a rival-sounding story that it's about whose respect you need — the practice dies when the people you want to be well thought of by stop being the people who thought it was required — and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a competitor to my description or the same thing said better. And I'd like it seperated from a second thing I can't tell apart from the first: the case where people go on saying the words and presumably believing them, but stop being able to say them out loud in a way that would justify anything, so the norm becomes embarrassing rather than false, and the practice dies in public a good generation before it dies in private. Is that the same mechanism somewhere else, or a different one? I'm not after a label on its own — I want the carving: what exactly the thing is, what it isn't, which established concepts sit nearest to each of the two, what each of them leaves out, and if there really is no established concept for what I'm pointing at, tell me that and just invent one with a definition and some boundary cases.
