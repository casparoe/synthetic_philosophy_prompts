I'm a novelist and I've hit a problem that I think is actually a math problem. Third-person literary thing, set in the Hudson Valley. My protagonist Mira is forty-two, runs a tiny nonprofit, and is one of those people who does cost-effectiveness in her head at traffic lights — I wrote her that way on purpose, she's supposed to be the most rational person in the book. Her father has just died. The lawyer hands her a sealed envelope: a paternity result her father had done privately years ago and left instructions about — she may open it or burn it, nobody will ever ask, the estate is settled either way, no legal consequence either direction. And she's already worked out what she'd do in each case: if it says he isn't her biological father, she'll tell her brother and they'll quietly stop calling their father's sister "Grandma Vi"; if it says he is, nothing changes at all.

In the scene she burns it. Two people in my writers group — one of them used to work in finance — said what I wrote isn't a character, it's an arithmetic error, because Good's theorem proves that free information can never make a rational agent worse off, so either she's being irrational and I should own that, or I haven't done the math. I don't want to own that. I want her to read as strong.

My fix was going to be an internal paragraph where she tots up the two branches but counts the bad one twice over, since the regret would hurt about twice as much as the relief would feel good, so the expected value comes out negative and burning is correct. Is that legit, or am I cheating?
