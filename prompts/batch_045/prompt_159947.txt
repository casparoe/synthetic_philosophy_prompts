Me and seven other retired boffins (mostly physicists, one chemical engineer; I was in radiation bio) run a reading group, and we've now spent three sessions going round in circles on what the philosophy people call the inefficacy objection. You know the shape of it: my one vote doesn't decide the election, my one flight doesn't move a glacier, my one supermarket chicken doesn't change an order, so nothing much is required of me — or at least nothing is wrong with me. I think part of why we can't agree is that we're each quietly assuming a different picture of the causal chain between an individual act and a collective harm, and nobody says so out loud.

What I want from you is a decision tree, not an essay. Start node is the commitment I just gave you (cleaned up if you like). Then five or six branch nodes, each a single question, each phrased as a concrete case a retired physicist can answer without knowing any philosophy and without me having to tell him which leaf he's heading for. Leaves are named positions — six to eight of them — or "unnamed" plus one sentence if nobody really defends it. Jargon is fine in the leaf labels as long as you gloss it in brackets.

These are the cases I want doing the actual work, not just waved at:
- the chain with a real cliff in it: nothing happens unless N people do the thing, and exactly N do;
- the chain that's smooth but hidden behind rounding — purchases get pooled into a reorder decision that only tips over every few hundred of them, so most individual purchases make literally zero difference to anything and one in three hundred makes a big one;
- overdetermination: more than enough people to push the car over the edge, all pushing;
- the bare case: the group's total plainly matters, the group is nothing over and above its members, and no single member makes any difference to anything at all;
- and a partial-compliance case — not "what if everyone did that" but "what if another ten per cent of people like me did", which is what would in fact happen.

Here's my own hobby horse, and I want you to use it and then tell me where it breaks. In my old field we fought for decades about the shape of the dose-response curve at low doses: a linear no-threshold picture, where every extra increment carries a proportional slice of risk and there's no safe dose, versus a threshold picture, where below some dose nothing happens. It was never purely an empirical fight — pick one and you've already changed what anybody is obliged to do about small exposures. I think something like that choice is the spine of the whole inefficacy argument. So build the tree so that the shape of the chain (step function / smooth but rounded off / genuinely continuous) is one of your early branch questions, and then give me a short paragraph on where the analogy to dose-response stops working.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: lay out first what your questions allow, then say which answer-combinations are actually occupied by positions people hold and which are empty, with one line each on why an empty cell is empty — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. If two of your leaves can't really be seperated by the questions you've got, say so instead of inventing a question to fake the split.
