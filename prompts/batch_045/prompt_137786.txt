Hey — I teach a methods seminar for history and philosophy undergrads, 16 people, one two-hour session with a short break in the middle. I want an activity that makes them *feel* hindsight bias and the narrative pull in historical explanation, rather than one where I stand up and announce that both exist.

Last year I ran the lazy version: showed them a well-known financial crash, asked what caused it, and everyone recited the same three causes with total confidence. Useless. So the hard constraint is that the sequence has to be genuinely unknown to them — invented, synthetic, no real case study, nothing they can half-remember from school — and ideally the facilitator shouldn't know how it ends either, so I'd like some randomisation device that decides the outcome live, in the room.

What I want them to come away seeing, precisely: not "prediction is hard" (they already believe that and it's boring), but the *explanatory* version — that once an outcome is known, which facts feel causally load-bearing shifts, and a smooth story starts acting as its own evidence. I also want two things kept apart in the design rather than collapsing into one blob called "bias": creeping determinism about the outcome itself ("it was bound to happen, I basically knew"), and narrative salience in the causal account ("the dramatic decision in episode 3 is what did it"). If you think a third thing belongs, seperate that out too: the "someone should have predicted this" move, where hindsight quietly turns into blame — that's the version that actually shows up in their essays.

An idea I've been playing with, use it or bin it: run a synthetic sequence — say a made-up republic going through a currency-and-election crisis, eight episodes on cards — with a hidden causal model behind the scenes in which most of the real causal weight sits on something boring and structural (a slow trade or demographic trend) and almost none of it sits on the dramatic choices the characters make. Participants allocate 100 points across candidate causes in foresight, seal the sheet in an envelope, the sequence runs, then they reallocate in hindsight. If I'm right the hindsight allocations drift toward the dramatic episodes and away from the boring structural factor — away from the truth rather than toward it — which is a much better punchline than "people are overconfident". And before opening the envelopes they should write down what they think they originally said, so they catch themselves rewriting their own memory.

What I need: the scenario skeleton and the hidden weight table, materials, any roles, a minute-by-minute timeline for the two hours, whatever payoff or scoring scheme you'd use with no real money involved, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the specific errors. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I'm expecting at least one sharp student to name the whole point in the first five minutes and defuse it, and at least one person to refuse to allocate points because the numbers are invented. And be honest about what the exercise can't reproduce: real history has no known generative model, the dice make luck visible in a way actual contingency isn't, and once they know the outcome you can't un-know it, so a true within-subjects comparison is out. The randomisation might also pick the less dramatic branch — tell me how the debrief changes if it does, since it has to work either way.
