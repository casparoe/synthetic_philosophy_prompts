I'm retired now — I used to model turbulent flows for a living, and somehow that led me into a philosophy reading group at the public library, where we've been going through Dennett's three stances (physical, design, intentional) and his "real patterns" idea. The compression business is the part that feels like home to me; everything else I'm doing by gut, and I'd like to know what my gut adds up to. So here are my verdicts on when the intentional stance is the right one to take. A deep-search chess engine: yes, genuinely — it thinks my knight is hanging and it wants to win. A lookup-table chess machine that plays exactly the same moves out of a giant stored table of positions: no, that feels hollow to me, even though every prediction comes out the same, and I know that pair is the one that's hard to reconcile. A thermostat: fine as shorthand, but nothing hangs on it, the design stance does all the work. A hospital infusion pump that starts beeping: design stance only, "it thinks the line is blocked" is just laziness. A frog snapping at something small and dark: yes, I'll say it takes there to be something edible there. E. coli swimming up a sugar gradient: no, that's chemistry. A honeybee doing the waggle dance: yes, and more than shorthand — it's telling the others where the flowers are. A slime mould finding the short way through a maze: no, and I get irritated when people say it "solves" the maze. "The department expects the budget to be cut again": yes, I talk that way all the time and dont want to give it up. "The forest wants rain", "the gene wants to be copied": no, that's poetry. Aliens who could predict everything I'm going to do by treating me as a rational agent, without having the faintest idea what a neuron is: I want to say they'd have my beliefs exactly right, and it worries me, because it seems to make what I believe depend on how good they are at predicting me. And the case I keep flipping on: a swarm of honeybees picking which hollow tree to move into — half the time it's the paradigm of a decision, half the time it's a feedback loop I could simulate without ever mentioning belief or desire.

My own stab at the principle is: take the intentional stance whenever it buys you more prediction than it costs you, and call the pattern it exploits real whenever the description it licenses is shorter than the bit map. But I can't tell whether that hands me the lookup table or not, and I can't tell whether compression is even the right thing to ask about there. On patterns separately: the vortex draining out of my bathtub is real to me even though there's no vortex-stuff to it; the Big Dipper isn't, though I can describe it perfectly well; the "trend" a drug company finds in its noisy trial data isn't; and the digits of pi I genuinely don't know what to say about, since there's a tiny program that spits them out but nothing I'd call a pattern to look at.

So: is there one principle that covers both the stance verdicts and the pattern verdicts, or do I need two? If my compression principle is roughly on the right track, repair it for me — say precisely what's doing the work in each clause and which case forces that clause. Which of my verdicts does it make me drop? I'm guessing the lookup table one, but I'd rather be told. What does it say about the bee swarm? State it tightly enough that I could apply it to something I haven't thought about, and invent a couple of cases of your own to show me what it does with them. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts no decent principle is going to save.
