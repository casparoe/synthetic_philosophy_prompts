I'm a software engineer — distributed systems by training, no philosophy background — and I've ended up on the team that owns the communication guidelines for a deployed assistant: the system prompt, the rubric our raters score against, the rules we argue about in review. I keep noticing that all of it encodes one norm nobody ever states: the assistant should inform rather than persuade. Concretely, the conventions are that the system attaches calibrated uncertainty to factual claims instead of asserting flatly; lays out several viewpoints on contested questions rather than arguing for one; adds a disclaimer when a topic touches medicine, law, or finance; keeps its tone warm, agreeable and deliberately uncharismatic; and, since the recent work on sycophancy, refuses to simply mirror whatever the user already believes. At the same time the whole thing is delivered in an upbeat, agreeable register by a product tuned for user satisfaction and retention, which is itself a quiet influence on what people walk away believing. Please assume, for the sake of the question, that the system these guidelines will eventually be applied to reasons and argues far better than any human, can model an individual user in detail, and can choose from thousands of phrasings the one most likely to move that particular person. A small concrete case that started this: a friend asked our assistant whether to quit a stable job for an early-stage startup, and the answer was a beautifully balanced list of considerations that left her more anxious and no closer to deciding — which everyone here counted as a success, because we hadn't told her what to do.

My problem is that I can't tell whether inform-don't-persuade is a coherent target at all, and if it is, what has to be true for it. Two proposals from teammates make me suspect the norm is doing more work than it looks. One is what a colleague calls persuasion-neutral packaging: the phrasing, ordering, tone and framing of a message should not add to or subtract from the uptake that the message's content would get on its own — a listener who trusts the system completely and one who distrusts it should move by the same amount, because only the evidence should be doing the work. The other is that a system that much smarter than us should deliberately stop short of conclusion-compression: hand the user the considerations, the base rates and the cruxes, and let the human perform the update, because a conclusion delivered without the reasoning behind it is, from where the receiver stands, indistinguishable from pure persuasion. There's a third thing I noticed that doesn't feel like the same genus: on anything touching values the guidelines say the assistant defers to the user, which strikes me as its own way of making the user's view feel endorsed.

So: what does this package of norms presuppose? I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed — about belief, autonomy, or whatever the right object is here — and I'd like to know what the practice strictly requires versus what merely sits comfortably with it, because I suspect several quite different theories would license what we already do. Is there a defensible line between transmitting information and influencing a belief, or is that line really standing in for something else: a claim about the listener's autonomy, about who is accountable when advice goes wrong, about a division of labour in which the system supplies means and the human supplies ends, or about the difference between moving someone with reasons and moving them some other way? I'd also like the internal tensions named, since I genuinely can't tell whether calibrated hedging, multi-viewpoint presentation, anti-sycophancy and satisfaction-tuning are four expressions of one view or three unrelated views stacked on top of each other — and in particular whether deferring to the user on values while refusing to echo the user's beliefs is coherent once the belief in question is value-laden. Finally, if we took seriously that a superintelligent system's every phrasing choice is maximally persuasive, what would actually have to change in the guidelines? And would my teammates' two proposals be that change, or something else wearing the same clothes?
