I am finishing a long feature on the welfare of fish and crustaceans in aquaculture, and I have run into a problem I would like help with before I file. Going case by case and recording my own gut reactions, I find that the reactions are stable and fairly confident, but they do not line up with any of the principles I keep hearing from the people I interview. I would rather state the principle I am actually using than smuggle it into the piece unstated. So: below are my verdicts, then a principle a researcher offered me when I pressed him. What I want is a principle, or a short list of candidates, that captures as much of the pattern as possible, stated precisely enough that a reader could apply it to a case I have not listed, together with an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why.

1. In shrimp hatcheries, breeding females have an eyestalk crushed or cut in order to induce spawning. My verdict: this is among the worst practices in the sector, worse per animal than nearly anything else I have looked at, and I would lead the piece with it.

2. Warm-water fish such as tilapia being crowded into nets and then chilled in an ice slurry until they die. Verdict: very bad, though I notice I rank it below case 1 even though it is a drawn-out death applied to vastly more animals.

3. Months of life at high stocking density, in a schooling species that shows no territorial aggression and no measurable preference for more space. Verdict: mildly bad; I would not campaign on it, and I am not sure I would spend a paragraph on it.

4. The same densities, for the same duration, in a species that defends territories and fights when crowded. Verdict: considerably worse. I cannot say what changes between 3 and 4 other than the animal's own frustrated motivation.

5. Feed withdrawn for a day or two before slaughter, as a matter of routine, to empty the gut. Verdict: barely registers with me at all.

6. Electrical or percussive stunning followed by exsanguination on an otherwise well-run farm. Verdict: close to a solved problem, and I feel strangely untroubled by it. Yet this ends the animal just as dead as case 2 does, and the difference is only competence of method. I do not know whether to conclude that most of my horror in case 2 was about technique rather than about killing.

7. A decapod boiled alive. Verdict: not acceptable, and I react physically to the description. But my confidence that a shrimp or crab has any subjective experience at all is perhaps forty percent, and I cannot argue it higher from what I know.

8. A genetically edited line of farmed fish in which the neural machinery required for pain has been disabled, so the animal cannot suffer while being otherwise normal and active. Verdict: not merely permissible but close to a triumph; I would rather the industry moved in this direction than shrank. I take it that 7 and 8 are the pair my other verdicts cannot jointly support, since 8 treats the absence of sentience as settling everything and 7 extends full protection on quite thin evidence.

9. The arithmetic puzzles me. Case 1 affects a small number of animals for a short time. Chronic crowding affects essentially the entire population for months. On any additive reckoning the aggregate of mild discomfort should swamp the ablations, and yet my verdicts rank the ablations higher. I appear to weight intensity far above duration-and-number in a way that the additive story does not license.

The case I keep going back and forth on: a closed-containment salmon operation with genuinely low densities, no chemical or mechanical delousing, and reliable instant killing. It will be built only if there is demand for it, and the fish in it would not otherwise exist. I cannot decide whether this is a welfare win that displaces worse product, or simply more animals being bred and killed, and I do not know which way my own verdicts push.

The principle I was offered, in my paraphrase: what matters is expected suffering, understood as the product of intensity, duration, number of animals, and the probability that the animals in question are sentient; and where a practice would be indefensible if performed on a mammal, treat it as indefensible unless there is positive evidence that the animal cannot suffer. My suspicions, which I would like you to confirm or dissolve rather than take from me: the first clause seems to make cases 3 and 4 nearly equivalent, since duration, number and probability are held fixed; the second clause sits badly with the probability multiplier in the first; and the whole thing may save verdict 1 only by treating the ablation as far more intense than anything else on my list.

What I would like back:

Two or three candidate principles, each stated precisely, with each clause tied to the specific verdict that motivates it, so I can see what would be lost by dropping the clause.

Each principle's verdicts on cases I have not listed: bivalve farming, oysters and mussels, and the small wrasse or lumpfish stocked in salmon pens to eat sea lice, which die in large proportions. Please also generate at least two further cases a reporter could plausibly encounter and give the verdicts.

The flip-flop case, decided each way if the principles differ.

For each principle, which of my verdicts it overturns, and whether the overturning is cosmetic or the loss of something I actually believe.

Whether the tension between 7 and 8 is a genuine conflict in my commitments or a verbal one, and if it is genuine, which verdict you would advise me to withdraw and what I would then have to say in the piece instead.

Finally, a one-sentence or two-sentence formulation I could put in a magazine, accurate to whichever principle you recommend, that does not collapse into a slogan and does not imply a confidence about crustacean sentience that the evidence does not support.
