I read about decision theory and microeconomics in the evenings for my own amusement — I have no formal training in philosophy or economics, I work in logistics — and a friend of mine who does data science and I have been going back and forth on something for months without getting anywhere. I'd like your credence, and the reasoning that produces it, on a specific claim.

Here is the case we keep circling. Two tenants, Ana and Bo, live in identical apartments, face identical prices and identical budgets for ten years, and each week each of them buys a grocery basket. Their receipts are, we may suppose, byte-for-byte identical: for every price-and-budget situation that arises over the decade, they walk out with the same basket. On a revealed-preference account, then, the same utility function gets attributed to both — and as I understand it that function is pinned down only up to a positive affine rescaling, i.e. multiplying all the numbers by a positive constant and adding a constant, which is why I take the numbers themselves not to say anything about how strongly either of them wants anything. Now suppose a new product appears in year eleven and their records diverge sharply: Ana switches to it at once and drops a staple she had bought every single week for ten years, while Bo never touches it. My friend thinks that settles everything — the records diverged, so the utility functions were different all along and revealed preference simply hadn't been handed enough data to separate them, end of story. That strikes me as missing the actual puzzle, which is the counterfactual version: suppose neither of them lives to see year eleven, or the product is never invented. My credence is around 0.65 that there is still a determinate fact of the matter about how each of them would have ranked the new bundle against her old staple, and that this fact is not settled by the ten-year choice record even together with the usual consistency condition. My friend is around 0.2, on the grounds that a utility function is just a compact encoding of choice dispositions and that asking what someone values outside every situation they ever encountered is asking about nothing.

So give me your own number on that claim — call it U: for an agent whose choices across all the budget situations it actually faced are fully recorded and satisfy the standard revealed-preference consistency condition, there is a determinate fact about how it ranks options it never faced, and the record plus consistency does not settle that fact. But I want the decomposition, not just the headline: whether the class of options the agent never faced is even a well-defined domain for a utility function to range over, or whether the domain is fixed by what the agent actually encountered; whether the ranking question and the intensity question deserve separate probabilities, because I suspect they come apart and that my friend is implicitly only answering the ranking one; how much weight to give the empirical point that real agents violate consistency to some degree, and whether that is noise or a reason to doubt the whole apparatus; and how much turns on whether there is any independent route — introspective reports, some common neural currency, whatever — to how strongly a person values something, as opposed to just which way they'd go. Give a number and roughly one line of justification per term, say how you're combining them, then name the term you're least confident about and what would move it most. Finally, tell me where you think my 0.65 and her 0.2 actually disagree: is it a substantive disagreement about U, or are we just using the word utility to mean different things? And if anything in my setup is off — including that business about affine rescaling — flag it briefly before you start, but please don't spend the whole answer on it.
