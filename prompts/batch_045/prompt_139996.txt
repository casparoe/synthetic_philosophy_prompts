My friend Dani and I got into a long argument last night and neither of us would give. She does reliability work on a fleet of industrial pumps; I do anomaly detection on a big software system nobody fully understands. Her team had two sites flag the same anomaly — identical vibration signature, same slow temperature ramp — and it turned out one was cavitation from a partly clogged intake and the other was a worn bearing. She said: different mechanism, obviously, different parts doing different things. I said: from the point of view of everything we can measure and everything we can do about it, they're the same thing — same signature, same fix, same time to failure. She said a mechanism is like a subroutine: it's the same subroutine iff the same code is running, no matter which hardware it's on, and the code is the parts and what they do, not the symptoms it throws off. And she was leaning on that to argue you should never pool anomaly data across sites, or trust a fix validated at one site to work at another, until you've actually identified the mechanism — which in my system, where there's no spec and no one wrote the thing on purpose, sounds like it cashes out to: never.

Can you audit the subroutine analogy for us? I want the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the source code, the hardware, the compiler, the bug, the debugger's breakpoint, the spec — then which features of written code it smuggles into the target that a cell, a trained model, or a legacy codebase doesn't have: a single privileged description, modular parts with clean interfaces, an author's intent that makes some behavior count as an error. Then the part we actually cared about: which of those disanalogies matter for her conclusion and which are just noise. I also started suspecting that "same mechanism" is naming several relations that can come apart — same fix works, same forecast, same story you can tell an engineer — and that we were sliding between grains without noticing, since at one resolution both her failures are just "too much load on the shaft" and at another they have nothing in common. Does either of our conclusions survive once that's on the table, and if the subroutine picture is carrying more than it can bear, what's the better analogy?
