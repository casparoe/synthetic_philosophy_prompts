I'm teaching the moral psychology half of a practical reason seminar this term and I'm sick of the usual diet — Newcomb, Ulysses and the sirens, chocolate-or-fruit — so I want to run a real organizational episode through both the akrasia literature and the hyperbolic-discounting literature and see what breaks. The case is the Y2K remediation effort, roughly as I remeber it: two-digit year fields were baked into a lot of financial and infrastructure code; people were warning about it from the late 80s on; most big organizations didn't seriously fund remediation until about 1998–99; the money went out in a frantic backloaded spike; 1 January 2000 came and went with remarkably few serious failures; and the popular verdict afterwards was that the whole thing had been hype. Take that shape as given even if my numbers are off — I don't want this to turn on how many billions were spent — but do tell me if I've got the shape wrong.

What I want first is a disciplined mapping onto hyperbolic discounting: who the agent is, what the option set looked like at, say, 1995 versus 1998, what the payoffs were, what the model actually predicts about the timing of remediation, and whether the naive-versus-sophisticated distinction does any work here. This is the bit I keep going back and forth on with a colleague in econ: on the face of it Y2K is the ideal test case for the standard prescription, because a hard, immovable, universally known deadline is about the strongest commitment device there is — and the scramble happened anyway. So does that show the organizations were naive about their own future discounting, or does it show the mechanism wasn't present bias at all but coordination, free riding, vendor capacity, consultants whose price spiked as the deadline neared, and the expectation that if it were really catastrophic somebody else would have fixed it? And can you separate out what a time-consistent exponential discounter with real option value from waiting would have done — waiting to find out how bad the bug actually was is informative, after all — so I can see whether the observed backloading really discriminates between the models or whether I'm reading a signature into noise.

Then the akrasia side, where I suspect my own framing is shaky. The Socratic denial that anyone acts against their own better judgment, and the Davidsonian puzzle about how that could even be possible, are built for a single agent with a single all-things-considered judgment at the moment of action — but the 1995 planners who judged early remediation best are not numerically the same people as the 1999 crisis coders, and a firm isn't an agent with one judgment at a time. So: was this weakness of will at all, or just a change of mind by a successor? Does the multiple-selves picture that hyperbolic discounting invites dissolve the puzzle or relocate it one level up, and does doing that buy anything or just rename it? Two structural features I want you to take seriously rather than pat me on the head about. First, the cost here was a cliff, not a slope — call them cliff tasks, where all the cost lands at a fixed date and early action buys only risk reduction, versus slope tasks, where deferring makes the cost grow continuously; tell me whether the hyperbolic model has anything distinctive to say about cliff tasks or whether it is silent there. Second, the aftermath: call it counterfactual opacity, the fact that successful prophylaxis destroys the evidence of what it prevented — is the "it was all hype" verdict something the discounting model predicts (a present-biased agent weighing a salient cleanup bill against an invisible disaster), or an availability effect I'm illegitimately folding into discounting? And if the pattern is real it should recur: does it, in pandemic preparedness or whatever else you'd set beside it?

Be explicit about your confidence while you do this. Where are you extrapolating from the model rather than reporting what it says, where is it silent, and if my setup is confused — if you think the akrasia literature has no business being applied to organizations, or that hindsight cases like this one can't discriminate between discounting models, or that I'm equivocating between firms and their members — say that plainly instead of politely playing along. I'd much rather be told the question is bad than get a tidy answer to it.
