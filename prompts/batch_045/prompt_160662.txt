Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot: In Praise of the Music of Language (Basic Books, 1997) is a long meditation built out of dozens of competing English renderings of a single short Renaissance French poem by Clément Marot — the one Hofstadter nicknames Ma mignonne, in which a poet urges a sick young woman to recover. A reviewer in The New York Times objected that the source poem is little more than a charming trifle whose charm cannot be separated from its form. That objection sharpens the translator's problem rather than dismissing it: for a poem with almost nothing to say, the form may be nearly all there is to preserve, and form is exactly what a new language resists.

Assume you are rendering such a poem into English and you cannot keep everything. I want ratings and a ranking, not an essay.

Some vocabulary, which I am stipulating rather than taking from any source:

- A constraint is audible if someone who has never seen the original can notice its violation simply by hearing or reading the translation — a rhyme that does not land, a line that will not scan.
- A constraint is diaphanous if its violation shows up only when translation and original are placed side by side — a syllable count that drifts, a refrain that arrives one line late, an acrostic that dissolves.
- A constraint is inherited if it belongs to the source poem, and phantom if it is contributed by the target tradition instead: English readers hear regularly rhymed, end-stopped iambic quatrains as poetic in a way that has no counterpart in the French.

1. Tier list. Sort the following nine features into tiers ordered by how long you would hold on to them as pressure mounts, from the last thing you would surrender to the first thing you would jettison. Give one sentence per tier, and flag any item you take to be a phantom constraint rather than an inherited one.

- the rhetorical situation: who is speaking, to whom, and to what end (a poet urging a sick young woman to recover)
- the refrain, recurring at the same positions in the poem
- the rhyme scheme: the pattern of which lines rhyme with which
- the rhymes themselves being full rather than slant or eye rhymes
- a steady English metrical pulse, say a regular iambic beat
- line-by-line correspondence with the French syllable count
- the alternation of masculine and feminine line endings
- register: the light, unpretentious, conversational tone
- wordplay on the addressee's name or on a key word

2. Scores. Rate each of the following compensatory moves from 0 (never admissible) to 100 (indistinguishable from straightforward fidelity), with one line of justification each. Treat the moves as independent: score each as though it were the only liberty taken.

a. Replacing a pun the French supports and English cannot carry with a different pun of your own invention.
b. Moving the poem's concrete details to another setting or period so that a needed rhyme becomes available.
c. Keeping the rhyme scheme exactly while letting one key noun drift to a near synonym.
d. Holding the original's syllable count line by line and letting the rhymes turn approximate.
e. Using deliberately old-fashioned English to signal an old poem, even though the French was not old-fashioned to its first readers.
f. Adding a stanza the original does not contain so that the refrain can land once more.
g. Preserving the form perfectly while redirecting the poem to a different addressee — a city, a language, a dying craft — instead of a sick young woman.

3. One number. Given your answer to part 1: if a translation preserved the form essentially perfectly while substantially rewriting what the poem says, what fraction of the original's value would you judge to survive? Give a single number between 0 and 1, and a short defense of it.

Two further requests. First, tell me where you are confident and where the ordering is close: mark any tier boundary you would not defend under mild questioning. Second, if the task as I have posed it rests on a confused premise — if, for instance, there is no purpose-independent answer to what should be surrendered first, or the audible/diaphanous distinction cannot do the work I am asking of it — say so plainly and explain why, rather than politely producing the ranking anyway; you may take that stance for part of the request or all of it.

Please answer in American English.
