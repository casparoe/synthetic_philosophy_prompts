Bit of a panic — I've somehow agreed to do a fifty-minute guest session next week on a second-year biology module called Concepts in Biology, and it's the first time I've taught anything on my own (I'm a first-year PhD in HPS, my supervisor volunteered me without asking). The topic is biological function: the disagreement between the etiological or selected-effect account and the causal-role account. I don't want a reading list or a lecture script; I want a plan for how to actually build the thing in the room. There'll be about thirty of them, they've done natural selection and a bit of physiology and one genomics module where people toss around the word function constantly, most are either applying to medicine or heading for ecology, and the convenor — a behavioural ecologist who thinks function just means adaptation and that philosophy is what you do when you can't get grant money — will be sitting at the back. Two or three of them have read some popular evolutionary stuff and will say, quite confidently, that a thing's function is obviously just what it was selected for. I've got a whiteboard and no slides.

What I mainly want help with is the shape of the explanation. My instinct is that whatever comes first has to be that the two accounts were built to answer two different questions — roughly, why is this thing here at all, versus what is this part contributing such that the system containing it can do what it does — and that most of the cross-talk between the two camps and basically all of the confusion in a room like this comes from those two questions getting welded into one. But I genuinely don't know whether that belongs at the start as the frame or at the end as the payoff, and whether saying it too early just sounds like a cop-out to students who want to be told who's right. The case I'd like to hang everything on is a duplicated gene: an ancestral gene does job A, it gets duplicated, one copy carries on with A, the second copy drifts, picks up a mutation that makes it bind a new substrate, and that binding is genuinely useful to the organism right now — except the copy is still at low frequency and its presence in the population isn't explained by that benefit yet, so the etiological verdict looks like something like not yet, while the causal-role verdict looks like yes, it has a new function already. Is that the right case to carry the whole fifty minutes, or does it smuggle in too much too early? Should I open with something blunter and then complicate it? And where does the handover sort of case fit — a trait that was selected for one thing, now does a different thing, and is currently being maintained for the new thing? Give me the order the pieces have to go in and why each one depends on the one before it, not just a list of things to mention.

Then the misconceptions, which I'm more worried about creating than about answering. I think I'll manufacture at least these: the teleology panic, where they hear selected for and decide I've smuggled purpose or designedness back into biology; the idea that the causal-role account is committed to every last thing an organ doing being one of its functions and is therefore just silly; the thought that the whole dispute is verbal and nothing is at stake; and the one I find hardest, where they conclude that on the etiological account you can't say what a newly discovered structure is doing until you know its selection history, which they'll take as a reductio when I suspect half the literature takes it as a feature. Tell me where in the sequence each of those should be headed off rather than waited for, and what I should say to the convenor's version of the point, which is that this is all obvious and functions are adaptations. Also: what should I openly refuse to settle in fifty minutes and say out loud that I'm refusing — I'm guessing the malfunction and disease stuff and anything about mental content, but I'd rather you judged. And I need one question to put to them at the end that would show me they'd got the disagreement rather than just memorised which account is which.
