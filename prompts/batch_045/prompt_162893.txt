Hi — I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I'm halfway through a 3,000-word feature on escalation of commitment (working title: 'Good Money After Bad'). My editor insists the classic lab demo goes in, so I've got a long section on the dollar auction: a $1 bill goes to the highest bidder, but the runner-up also has to hand over their bid and gets nothing, so the bidding creeps past a dollar and people end up paying bizzare multiples of the prize to get out. I've been using it in drafts as, basically, the cleanest illustration of the sunk-cost fallacy anyone has ever built, and I'd like to keep that line if I can — it's the sentence my editor highlighted.

The trouble is that when I write down everything I actually believe for the sidebar box, it doesnt obviously add up:

(1) the dollar auction is the purest demonstration of the sunk-cost fallacy we have;
(2) sunk costs are irrelevant to rational choice, full stop — money already spent shouldn't appear anywhere in the decision;
(3) a lot of what gets labelled escalation of commitment in the wild is NOT irrational: finishing the thing has option value, quitting has real career and reputational costs, and the people closest to a failing program usually have information the auditors don't;
(4) the dollar auction is a fair model of those real cases, which is why it earns its place in a piece about them;
(5) the only rational move in a dollar auction is not to bid at all, and I want to tell readers exactly that;
(6) sunk costs can nevertheless be rationally relevant when other people can see you pay them, because visible expenditure credibly signals that you'll stay the course and deters rivals — I use this to explain standards wars and why firms keep funding a format everyone knows is dead.

A reader who emails me a lot also pointed out that if everyone took (5) seriously, the first person to raise a hand would get a dollar for nothing, which felt like a gotcha but I can't tell whether it is. And I've started using a distinction of my own in my notes — 'rule-driven escalation' (you keep going because the payoff rule makes stopping the worse option) versus 'face-saving escalation' (you keep going because booking the first loss is unbearable) — and I genuinely can't tell whether that distinction rescues (1) or quietly kills it.

So: can you audit the set before I file? Which of these really conflict, which only look like they do, and for each real conflict what would have to give and what it costs me journalistically — I'd rather drop a cute line than a true one, but I also don't want to spend 400 words on a game-theory subtlety nobody asked for. Rank the tensions by severity instead of flagging everything as equally bad, say for each what the cheapest fix is, and tell me whether one revision would clean up several at once. My gut says (1) and (3) are the shaky ones, but my gut has been wrong before, so don't take my word for it.
