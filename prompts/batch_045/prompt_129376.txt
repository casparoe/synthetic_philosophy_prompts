I'm a high school senior doing an independent reading project on theories of well-being, and my teacher has me trying to pressure-test a view she calls tranquilism: the claim that, where a person's welfare is concerned, only two things matter — the absence of craving and the absence of suffering. The things we normally count as positive welfare, like pleasure, achievement, love, friendship and knowledge, matter only insofar as they bear on those two, and maybe not even then. Rather than argue with her about it, I want to try the view out on someone who never had to face its modern form. So: how would Arthur Schopenhauer, writing as he did in the first half of the nineteenth century, have reacted to the following?

Suppose that by the 2040s there are three safe, cheap and reversible biomedical interventions for wanting. The Source-cutter lowers the intensity of desire itself: people on it still act and still reason, but they no longer hunger, strive, worry, compete or fall passionately in love; they simply do the next reasonable thing. The Sting-cutter leaves desire exactly as strong as it was and leaves the enjoyment of getting what you want intact, but removes the unpleasantness of not getting it: frustration, longing, envy and anxiety stop hurting. The Second-order version, which is the one my teacher likes, leaves both the desire and its sting in place and instead removes the minding — the way you can be fully aware of an itch and simply not care about it. And suppose a government funds whichever of these people choose and evaluates the programme with a national well-being index that tracks only two variables: average intensity of craving and average intensity of suffering.

What I cannot work out is which way Schopenhauer would jump. His own position sounds almost tranquilist already: happiness on his account is only ever negative, satisfaction is just the removal of a want and is instantly replaced by boredom, and life swings back and forth between suffering and boredom — so a population of people who want nothing looks like exactly what his pessimism was recommending. But three things pull the other way, and I would like you to weigh each of them. First, he holds the will to be the thing-in-itself, outside space, time and causality, so I suspect he would say that no molecule can touch it and that these interventions only change how the will shows up in a particular person's experience; if that is right, does it make them worthless to him, or merely insufficient, and what would he say a genuine quieting of the will actually requires? Second, boredom is itself a form of suffering for him, so does the Source-cutter risk manufacturing the very condition he thought was inescapable, and would that lead him to rank it below the Sting-cutter or the Second-order version? Third, he treated aesthetic experience and compassion as the only real escapes from the will, and both seem to need strong desire of some kind — so does his pessimism end up recommending an intervention that destroys the only things he thought made life bearable?

Then the practical question I keep going in circles on: given how little faith he had in schemes for social improvement, would he have supported a state-funded programme of this sort at all, or would he have seen it as the one reform genuinely worth making? And where, if anywhere, would he break with a present-day tranquilist who says that any two of these options producing the same totals of craving and suffering are equally good no matter what else they do to a life — a nation of people who write symphonies and a nation of people who sit quietly in the sun comes out the same on the index, and I want to know whether he would accept that or call the index a fraud. I would much rather have the argument than a summary of his philosophy, and I would be grateful if you stuck to his actual metaphysics rather than the sort of thing Nietzsche would go on to say about the last man.
