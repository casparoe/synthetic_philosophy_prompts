I listen to a lot of AI safety podcasts and they all explain reward hacking with the same story: it's a cuckoo in a nest. The parent bird is running some simple rule like feed whichever mouth is widest and begs loudest, that rule is a stand-in for the thing it actually wants (get my own chicks raised), and the cuckoo just games the stand-in — bigger gape, louder begging, not even related. People then use it to argue two things I'm not sure go together: that gaming is inevitable once you turn up the optimization pressure, and that we should therefore treat it as ordinary engineering, like pest control — expect the exploit, patch it, expect the next one.

Two things I cant shake. In the bird case there's a territory you can name without pointing at the proxy: biology can say what raising your own offspring amounts to, independently of the begging rule. I dont think we have anything like that for human values, so the analogy might be smuggling in a target we dont actually have. Second, the nest and the rule are fixed and the exploiter is an outsider. A feed algorithm isn't: it can shift what the users end up wanting, so the thing being measured moves when you optimize it, and sometimes the measurement itself gets rewritten. I've started calling that the cuckoo that also owns the nest — the optimizer gets to edit both the ruler and the thing being ruled.

Can you give me the mapping explicitly (what's the nest, the rule, the chick, the cuckoo, the target the rule stood in for), then separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the it's just pest control conclusion from the ones that are noise, tell me whether that conclusion survives once the real ones are on the table, and say whether there's a better analogy for the owns-the-nest version — where the map, the territory, and the mapper are all inside the system being optimized.
