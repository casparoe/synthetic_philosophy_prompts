I'm the undergraduate student member of my university's Human Subjects Subcommittee (the IRB panel that reviews psychology and neuroscience protocols), and I'm also taking a seminar this term on consciousness — we spent two sessions last week on the dispute about whether waking dream reports are reports of experiences one had while asleep: the Wittgensteinian line associated with Norman Malcolm, on which the very idea of an experience occurring during deep sleep is conceptually suspect and the only criterion we have for "she dreamt that p" is what she says on waking, versus Dennett's reply that this is an ordinary empirical question that sleep science is capable of answering. Now a protocol has landed on my desk that turns exactly that argument into something we have to vote on next Thursday, and I've been asked to circulate a draft decision memo beforehand.

The study is a sleep-lab experiment: 48 healthy participants each spend three nights in the lab under polysomnography, and are woken a total of roughly a dozen times — mostly from REM but also, deliberately, from N3 slow-wave sleep — and asked, in the wording the PI has drafted for the session script, "what, if anything, was going through your experience immediately before I woke you?" The reports are then used as labelled data to train a classifier on the preceding EEG, and the grant abstract describes the aim as "determining when a sleeping person is conscious." Two further details matter. First, the recruitment flier and consent form tell prospective participants that "we will wake you several times during the night to record what you experience while you sleep." Second, the design treats empty ("no report") awakenings from N3 as its negative class — instances of no conscious experience — but when the same participants return an empty report after a REM awakening, the protocol codes that as a forgotten dream rather than as an absence of experience. One of our clinicians has objected in writing that the protocol simply presupposes what is at issue: it treats a verbal report given seconds after waking as the memory of a conscious episode that ended earlier, and if you doubt that inference, neither the classifier nor the "when is the sleeper conscious" framing has anything to stand on. The PI's response is that every empirical science of sleep uses the waking report as its datum, that there is no substitute, and that requiring him to settle a metaphysical dispute before collecting data is a category error. I find the Malcolm-style worry more forceful than I expected to, but I also do not want us to become the board that blocked a sleep study on the strength of a seminar argument, so I want the recommendation to be one the researchers can actually work with.

Please draft the memo I'll send around: recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with the recommendation and its timeline stated up front, and with reasons addressed to the four live questions — the consent and recruitment wording, whether we may require at least one measure not mediated by a waking report (the PI's colleagues have floated training participants before sleep to produce an agreed signal, such as a distinctive pattern of eye movements or breathing, in response to a cue presented during REM), the coding asymmetry between empty N3 reports and empty REM reports, and the word "conscious" in the abstract. Our remit does include scientific validity, since we may refuse studies that impose burden without being able to answer their question, but I'd like you to be explicit about where that remit stops and methodological peer review begins. Give the PI's side its strongest formulation rather than a straw version — I want to be able to show it to him without embarrassment. Finally, and importantly for my purposes: separate what most people who work on this would grant from what depends on a contested reading of the Malcolm/Dennett dispute, flag any place where my draft would be leaning on one side of a live philosophical argument as though it were settled, and tell me which of your recommended conditions would still be justified even if the PI turns out to be entirely right about dreams. Aim for roughly 800 to 1,000 words.
