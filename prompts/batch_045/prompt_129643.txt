im on the student committee that decides where our winter fundraiser money goes this year (about $4k, which feels like a lot to us and is obviously nothing in the grand scheme), and we've been told to score causes with the ITN thing — importance, tractability, neglectedness, multiply them together, go with the biggest number.

The way one of the seniors explained it (he did a gap year at some effective giving nonprofit) is the part I can't stop thinking about. He said the landscape of causes is basically a market. Money already flows to the causes everyone's heard of, those are picked over, the easy gains got arbitraged away years ago, and the real finds are the underpriced assets nobody is bidding on. So on his version neglectedness isn't just one factor out of three, it's the signal that tells you where the mispricing is, and a cause being popular is actually evidence against funding it. Someone else in our group put the same idea as picking low-hanging fruit — don't climb the tree, grab what's nearest the ground.

It's persuasive but I'm not sure it survives, and it matters because our three finalists seem to be neglected for totally different reasons:

1. A weekend backpack food program run out of a church near school. Not neglected at all, everyone donates to it, and their actual constraint is fridge space and too many volunteer slots, not money.
2. Lab supplies plus a training session so a vocational school in another country can run its own water quality testing. None of us had heard of it, our faculty advisor found it on a mailing list. Super unphotogenic, no emotional story, and there's a matching grant that only unlocks if the total raised hits $3k — so either we push it over the line or it does literally nothing.
3. Printing and mailing a local-language health newsletter a few times a year. Basically nobody does this anymore, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's because nobody thought of it or because people tried it and it does nothing.

Two things bug me. First: in a real market, if an asset is cheap it's either mispriced or it's junk, and telling those apart is exactly the research the analogy was supposed to save you from. Second: our advisor mentioned that if we fund (2), maybe the funder who would have paid for it just moves their money somewhere else and we've accomplished nothing — which sounds like it has a market version but I can't work out what it is.

Can you audit the analogy properly? I want the mapping written out explicitly — what plays the role of the price, the asset, the trader, the spread, the efficient-market assumption, liquidity or room for more funding, whatever else fits — and then where the mapping breaks. But the part I actually care about: which of those breaks change what we should do with the $4k, and which are just the usual "well, no analogy is perfect" filler. Does the senior's conclusion — that an unpopular cause is itself a reason to fund it and you don't need to know much else — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the market picture doing all the work? And if it doesn't survive, is there a better analogy that would actually guide the decision (the fruit version seems to smuggle in that the fruit is just sitting there and will still be there next month, which isn't obviously true of a funding gap — handle them together or separately, whatever's cleaner), or is the honest answer just "do the research"?
