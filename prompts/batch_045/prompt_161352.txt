I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training beyond a couple of podcasts, and I've got an argument going at work that I can't settle.

The setup: my friend keeps saying the wavefunction isn't a thing in the world at all — it's just a probability distribution, like a weather forecast saying 40% chance of rain, or like the odds on a betting market. On that picture nothing weird happens in a measurement: you look, you find out where the particle is, you update your numbers, same as you'd update a forecast by opening the window. He uses this to conclude that there's no measurement problem, nothing spooky, "collapse" is just bookkeeping, and that people who talk about the wavefunction being real are committing a category error — like a gambler who thinks his credences are a physical object he left on the table.

Someone else in the thread says the opposite: the wavefunction is a field, basically like the electromagnetic field, spread out in space, physically real, interferes with itself, and collapse is a real physical event we just don't understand yet.

My own attempt, because I think in systems terms: the wavefunction is like a materialized view or a lazily evaluated value. It's a real data structure sitting on disk, physically there, doing causal work in the query planner, but it stands in for something you haven't materialized yet. Query it and it resolves to a concrete row. So it isn't in your head, but it also isn't the underlying data. I like that it splits the difference, but my friend says it smuggles in exactly the thing under dispute, because a view is derived from the base table, and whether the wavefunction is derived from something deeper is precisely the question.

There's a third thing I half remember, and this is probably garbled: someone sketched an argument that the "it's just your information" story can't be right in general — if two different wavefunctions could both be compatible with the same real physical state of a system, then for several systems prepared independently there'd be some joint measurement outcome that each wavefunction says is impossible but that has to happen anyway, so you can't have it both ways. Where does that kind of argument actually bite, if it's a real thing? Does it kill my view analogy, my friend's forecast analogy, or both?

The other place I think his picture is in trouble is interference. Probabilities of alternative routes just add; amplitudes apparently do something else, and I can't see how "I don't know which slit it went through" generates fringes. He says that's a quirk of the bookkeeping, not a problem. Is that the crux of the whole thing, or a red herring?

What I'd like from you:

- Where each mapping breaks, and for each break whether it actually matters for the conclusion the analogy is being used for, or whether it's cosmetic.

A materialized view is recomputable from base data — is the wavefunction recomputable from anything, or is it the bottom layer?
