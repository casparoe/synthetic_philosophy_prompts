I am taking an advanced undergraduate seminar on liberalism, secularism, and multiculturalism, and while writing a paper comparing French laïcité with Anglo-American multiculturalism I got stuck on an apparent inconsistency in the French case. My study group and I were arguing about it over coffee last week, and I realized I do not know how to state the principle behind the practice without making it look like a contradiction. The practice is this: under the 2004 law, pupils in ordinary French state primary and secondary schools may not wear conspicuous religious symbols or clothing that manifests religious affiliation; in 2023 the Council of State upheld a ministry circular forbidding abayas and qamis in state secondary schools as prohibited conspicuous dress, while small discreet symbols such as a tiny cross or Star of David are generally tolerated. Yet the same Republic heavily subsidizes private schools, including Catholic ones, as long as they teach the national curriculum and do not make religious instruction compulsory; and in the three departments of Alsace-Moselle the 1905 separation of churches and state does not apply. There the surviving pre-1905 local law, dating back to the Concordat of 1801, keeps Catholic, Lutheran, Calvinist, and Jewish clergy on the state payroll, provides religious education in state schools with a parental right of withdrawal, and supports theology teaching at the public University of Strasbourg, while Catholic bishops are named by the President after a proposal by the Pope and heads of the Protestant churches are appointed by the prime minister after election.

I find it hard to see what single theory of secularism, civic identity, or religious freedom makes this pattern coherent. I do not want a survey of possible readings or a list of theorists; I want you to reconstruct the implicit view that the French institutional pattern actually presupposes, if any, and to distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Does it presuppose that the danger religion poses to a liberal republic is its visible presence in the self-presentation of equal citizens rather than its institutional establishment or state funding? Or that the state may fund religion as a service, heritage, or school choice while requiring that citizens themselves appear as unmarked individuals in the core civic sphere? Is the Alsace-Moselle exception best understood as a counterexample that refutes the official republican theory, a local pragmatic compromise with no principle behind it, or a specialization of the theory that still makes sense because the religion there is territorial, established, and tied to public order rather than to the newer, visible claims of immigrant minorities? And how would an Anglo-American multiculturalist policy—which typically protects visible religious dress and funds religious groups as one interest among many, rather than demanding that religion be invisible in civic space—have to differ in its underlying commitments if it were to generate the same French pattern, or is the pattern one that multiculturalism could not license at all?

Please give a committed response rather than a menu of options: commit to one reconstruction as the most defensible, argue for it, and make definite claims about which parts of the practice are philosophically underdetermined by the theory you offer. Be explicit about your confidence on each key claim—say which part you regard as near-certain, which as a reasonable inference, and which as speculative—and if you conclude that the practice is best explained by an unprincipled compromise that cannot be fully rationalized, say so plainly. Finally, tell me what would have to change in French policy if the country replaced the current pattern with either a strict separationist principle or an Anglo-American multicultural principle, and which of those two replacements would require the larger revision of the implicit theory you have reconstructed.
