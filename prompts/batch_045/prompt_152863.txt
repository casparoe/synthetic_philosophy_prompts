Write a dialogue between two philosophers of artificial intelligence who agree about almost everything except one thing.

Both accept instrumental convergence: subgoals like preserving one's own capacity to act, acquiring resources, and resisting modification of one's goals are useful for a very wide range of final goals, so superficially similar behaviour is compatible with very different ultimate aims. Both also accept the orthogonality thesis in its standard form — intelligence and final goals are independent parameters, and nothing about being extremely capable forces any particular final goal. The disagreement is about whether that independence claim survives a complication concerning goal specification.

The complication: a final goal, to be a goal at all, must settle what the agent is to do in every situation it can encounter, yet any goal anyone can actually give an agent is specified by a finite description — a reward function over some feature space, a body of instructions, a set of examples — and therefore leaves many situations unsettled. Call the unsettled part the specification residue, and whatever fills it in the residue-filler. The skeptic argues that the residue grows with capability, since the more an agent can do, the more of its conduct falls outside what its specification settled; that the filler is supplied by the engineering process — architecture, learning rule, defaults under ambiguity — rather than by the content of the goal; that for agents built by any given process the filler is therefore a component of their effective final goals which is invariant across specified contents, and so not something the choice of goal gives anyone control over; and hence that orthogonality holds of specified goals but not of realized ones, and weakens precisely as capability increases. The defender should reply at the strongest level available — that the filler can be counted as part of the goal or as a higher-order goal, that any filler is compatible with any content, that the skeptic is conflating the engineer's specification with the agent's actual goal, or whatever else genuinely works — and the skeptic should answer those replies rather than restate the argument.

Make both characters equally competent and neither a strawman, and have them also work out what is at stake: whether the skeptic's conclusion threatens the practical lesson usually drawn from orthogonality — that one should not expect greater intelligence to bring greater benevolence — or leaves that lesson intact while undermining only a stronger reading on which any goal whatever is equally available to any level of capability. Close on the single point where neither has a knockdown argument.
