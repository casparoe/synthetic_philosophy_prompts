So I'm a journalist — mostly science and ideas — and last week I ended up at one of those long dinners after a workshop where everyone is slightly too pleased with their own example. Two people got going about whether it's irrational to have cyclic preferences, you know, A over B, B over C, C over A, and I honestly could not tell who was winning. I'm asking partly for a piece I'm sketching and partly because I want to know.

Marta, who works on philosophy of action, opened by saying the money pump doesn't show what everyone says it shows. You're holding C; I sell you B for a penny, then A for a penny, then C back again for a penny; you've lost three cents and ended where you started. But, she said, that only works against an agent who re-decides at each step. A resolute agent plans ahead: take the first two trades and refuse the third, or just refuse to play at all. And we let people do that all the time — she skips dessert tonight because of a decision she made this morning. So the pump targets cycling plus myopia, not cycling.

Deniz, who builds dynamic models, said the dessert case isn't like that. There, the plan is backed by something stable, her settled preference for being healthy, which doesn't flip during the evening. In the cyclic case nothing backs the plan except the cash. The moment the third offer arrives she'd rather pay the penny and take C, and the resolute version of her is simply refusing to be guided by her own ranking. Worse, there's no item you can point to as what the plan is *for*. Her ranking can't pick a best option even when you lay all three out in a menu at once. That, he said, is the real defect, and the money pump is just a vivid way of displaying it.

Marta conceded something there but said it cuts against him: if the real charge is that cyclic rankings pick no best element in a menu, then the money isn't doing any work and the complaint is structural, about the shape of the ranking, not about anything diachronic. And she added that fully transitive people get pumped too as soon as their preferences move over time: someone pays a fee in January to lock in the non-refundable beach holiday precisely because she knows March-her will want the city break, and then pays another fee in March to switch anyway. Being pumpable, she said, can't be the signature of intransitivity specifically.

Deniz's reply got the most nods around the table. Fine, he said, but then there are two pathologies, and they're versions of each other: diachronic rationality is the requirement that your time-slices be able to share one plan that each of them still endorses when it comes due, and intransitivity is the same failure compressed into a single moment. Transitivity is just what makes that possible. He also added that whether anyone actually bothers to pump you is beside the point, like saying an inconsistent set of beliefs is fine because nobody asks you the right question.

Then Marta went somewhere I hadn't heard before, and I want to know whether it's actually any good. Two things. First: the "and so you're worse off" step quietly assumes your ranking over bundles of item-plus-cash is cash-lexicographic, that more money always breaks the tie and never reverses an item preference. If an agent's cycling is global, over bundles including cash, then it isn't obvious she counts herself as worse off at the end of the pump by her own lights, and you have to say what standard you're using to call it a loss at all. Second: suppose she can charge a fee for taking an offer seriously, five bucks a trade. She pockets fifteen dollars, loses three cents, and ends up richer than any transitive person could in the same set-up. If a ranking is reliably rentable, in what sense is it broken?

Deniz didn't even pause. Being paid to have a disposition doesn't make the disposition rational, he said — you can be paid to hold false beliefs. What matters is whether the ranking can do its job of guiding choice, and being a rent-collecting curiosity doesn't restore that. And he was happy to convict on the cash point as well: a ranking that prefers less money to more, holding the item fixed, violates a seperate and very boring condition, and the pump doesn't need to lean on it.

At which point Ola, who'd been quiet all night and had definately had too much wine, said: isn't all of this assuming you're one agent across time to begin with? If the you who takes the third trade is a genuinely different decision-maker from the you who took the first, then three cents is a loss to a sort of committee, not to anybody. Everyone started talking at once and I lost the thread.

Can you work through the exchange in the order it happened and tell me who's right about what at each step — not just who wins overall? I'd also like to know what both of them are missing, and whether Ola's point is a real objection or just the wine.
