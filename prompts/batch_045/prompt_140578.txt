ok so i'm in the philosophy club at my school, and the senior who runs it is really into this stuff. Last week he showed us a video essay about vague words (like "heap", "tall", "bald") and then explained it himself, and i'm not sure i buy his version of it. I don't know enough to tell where he's summarizing and where he's quietly changing the view into something easier to knock down. Roughly, what he said was:

"Vague words cause a problem because of the sorites paradox: take a heap of sand, remove one grain and it's still a heap, do that over and over and you end up with a single grain, which obviously isn't a heap. So one of the assumptions has to go. There are two main camps. The epistemicists say the cutoff is real — there's some exact number of grains where 'heap' stops applying — but it's a brute fact about the word that we can never discover, because any two neighbouring piles look the same to us, so no evidence could ever tell us where the line is. Vagueness is really just ignorance. The supervaluationists say there's no cutoff: instead there are lots of precisifications, one for every way you could possibly make the word precise, and a sentence is true if it's true on all of them, false if it's false on all of them, and neither if they disagree. So in the fuzzy middle 'that's a heap' is neither true nor false — which means supervaluationists give up the law of excluded middle, since 'either it's a heap or it isn't' stops being true. That's why supervaluationism is the sane option: it lets you say there's genuinely no fact of the matter in the middle. Epistemicism is stuck saying that when you call a borderline guy bald you're either right or wrong and nobody can ever know which, which is absurd, so the view is basically dead."

A couple of bits smell wrong to me, especially the excluded middle one (that seems like a way too expensive price for the "commonsense" theory to pay) and the "brute fact nobody could ever have evidence about" one — but maybe that really is what they think?

Also, afterwards he made us do this thing i keep turning over. He had a set of shuffled pictures of sand piles and we went through them one at a time, saying "heap" or "not a heap" for each one without being able to compare it to the others. Nobody saw the series in order. Everyone's line ended up in a slightly different place, and we all thought that was fine. But two things: (1) we all thought the kid who called a three-grain pile a heap was just wrong, not "entitled to his own cutoff"; and (2) when one guy called a pile a heap early on and then a slightly bigger pile not a heap later, everyone thought he'd messed up and he sort of agreed — but if there's no cutoff and no fact of the matter in that zone, what did he get wrong? My friend says this definately proves epistemicism is right. I don't think it proves anything, but i also can't see how the supervaluationist explains it.

So: which parts of that summary would somebody who actually holds each view accept, which parts would they say misrepresent them, and is the "so epistemicism is dead" move still fair once the misrepresentations are fixed? And does the shuffled-piles thing actually favor one side, or do both have a reply — including to the bit where everyone's line is different but a few lines are still wrong? (Also, side question: if the cutoff is supposed to come from how people use the word, whose line counts when everyone's is in a different place?) Please tell me if i've got something backwards. thanks!!
