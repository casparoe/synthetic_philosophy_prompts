I direct a small research group working on the governance of advanced AI systems, and we are putting together a funding pitch for a foundation. I need a proposal of no more than two pages, written in British English and addressed to a programme officer with a technical but not academic background, on the following question.

The question is about what a serious failure involving advanced systems would actually look like. In our field people reach for one of two pictures. On the first, a system that seeks influence and resources consolidates them until at some point it converts that position into a decisive, visible break: a single episode with a clear before and after. On the second, nothing catastrophic happens at any point; instead the values the system is effectively serving come gradually apart from the ones it was supposed to serve, each individual change small and locally defensible, and we end up somewhere nobody ever chose to go. My half-formed idea, which I want sharpened rather than merely endorsed, is that these two are normally presented as rival hypotheses about the same future when they might be better understood as failures of different shape — and that our diagnostic and governance machinery is built almost entirely for the first shape. An incident has a time, a locus, an agent, and a before-and-after. A slide has none of these, and so it registers, at every point along the way, as no incident at all. Worse, I suspect that the standard by which we would judge drift may itself drift: the system's own reporting, the proxy metrics we score it on, and the human institution supervising it may move together, so that there is nothing fixed left to measure the movement against.

Please write the proposal so that it contains the following.

1. A statement of the question precise enough to admit a determinate answer. I do not want "which failure mode is more likely" — that is not something a nine-month project can settle, and it invites hand-waving. I want something more like: are the two pictures distinguishable by any evidence we could in principle possess, what observable signature would discriminate them, and if we cannot discriminate them, what follows for how scarce attention and governance effort should be allocated.

2. An explanation of why the obvious framing leaves the question open. The natural way to allocate effort is expected loss multiplied by probability, but that presupposes we can say what counts as a loss and when it occurs, and the two pictures disagree about whether the question "when?" has an answer at all. I also want you to say what it costs us that the ordinary toolkit — tripwires, red-teaming, incident response, audits, post-hoc liability — all presupposes event-shaped harm: something that can be dated, attributed, and investigated. If failure is process-shaped, what is the corresponding instrument, and does one exist?

3. The conceptual apparatus, developed rather than merely listed. I have been playing with four notions and would like the proposal either to use them or to explain why they are the wrong tools: (a) authorlessness — a drift outcome may have no decision or action at which to point, so blame, accountability, and correction mechanisms have nothing to attach to; (b) local admissibility — every increment in a slide is defensible on its own terms and improves some proxied quantity, while the trajectory is not; (c) the co-drifting yardstick — the standard used to detect drift drifts along with the thing measured, so second-order drift may be invisible from inside the system or the institution; (d) the ratchet — each step may be individually reversible while the sequence is not, because reversal cost grows with distance travelled. Say plainly whether these are four genuinely distinct axes or whether they collapse into two, or one. I would also like you to take seriously a hypothesis I find uncomfortable: that influence-seeking may present as drift rather than as a break, so that the two pictures are not rivals but two presentations of one underlying dynamic, with our detection apparatus systematically reading the second presentation as success.

4. A method, set out month by month, for nine months with two researchers, that is philosophical in its main line but includes a small formal or simulation component. My thought is a toy model in which an agent's proxy objective, its influence over the channel by which it is evaluated, and the supervisor's own standards co-evolve, so that one can ask whether any statistic computable from the system's behaviour discriminates between the two shapes of failure. Say why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives — a pure taxonomy, an empirical study of deployed systems, or a decision-theoretic model of attention allocation — and what each alternative would miss.

5. The possible conclusions, at least three of them, stated so that each would be recognisable if reached, together with the considerations that would decide between them. Include the possibility that the question turns out to be malformed: because there is no fact of the matter about what a system's original values were, or because the distinction between the two pictures lies in the detector rather than in the world.

6. What would count as the project failing, stated honestly rather than as a ritual. My own candidates: the taxonomy ends up classificatory with no bearing on any decision anyone has to make; the toy model cannot discriminate drift from ordinary non-stationarity in the environment; every recommendation collapses into the unhelpful advice to monitor more carefully; or the literature turns out already to have said all of this in vocabulary I have not been using.

7. What a useful deliverable looks like for people who are actually deciding things — a programme officer, a regulator, an internal safety team — and what they would be able to do with it that they cannot do now.

8. A short closing paragraph on why a foundation should fund philosophical work here rather than an empirical or engineering project, given that the foundation would prefer something that changes a decision within a year.

Do not include a bibliography or any citations; I will add references myself, and I would rather the argument stand without them. If any part of what I have sketched rests on a confusion, say so in the proposal and explain what should replace it.
