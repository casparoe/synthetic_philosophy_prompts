I've went back and forth on Dennett's counterfeit-people line for months — the idea that a lot of what's coming down the pipe is built to press our person-detection buttons while nothing at all is home — and I finally wrote down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and they definately don't line up with each other. So, honestly, before I tidy them:

- A customer support bot that says "I totally get how frustrating that is": obviously counterfeit. Switching it off mid-sentence harms nothing.
- A grief-bot built out of a dead woman's old messages, talking to her husband: also counterfeit, but I think it's worse than the support bot — creepy, like a disrespect to her — though not because the bot is someone.
- A companion model with real long-term memory that keeps a diary about itself and is then further trained to stay consistent with what it wrote, year after year: here I wobble. Some days I say that's a self actually being built; then I remember what the training loop is and I back off.
- A whole-brain emulation of a specific dead man, run on hardware: real person. Shutting it down is close to killing him.
- A woman with late-stage dementia who can't track a conversation and confabulates when you ask why she did something: real person, not even close, zero hesitation.
- An RL agent with a genuine aversive training signal, so it learns to avoid the states it got pushed into and emits distress signals that reliably predict the avoidance: I don't think it suffers. There's no one there.
- A man born without pain sensation who still learns to avoid what damages him and says "that hurts" when it does: I do think he can suffer, and that hurting him matters.

Six and seven look functionally similar and I give opposite answers. I also want to say that a person whose neurons get swapped one at a time for prosthetic chips stays a person the whole way through, while a behaviour-clone of that same person rebuilt from her data is counterfeit all the way down — even though at the end the two are doing the same things for the same reasons as seen from outside.

My rough rule was: something is a real person if it has a stake — it can lose something, the loss actually changes what it does later, and it can give you a reason for the change that it then acts on. But as stated that seems to let in a beehive or a nation-state, and to push out the woman with dementia, which I won't accept.

Can you get me a principle that captures as many of these as possible? Tell me what motivates each clause, be honest about which of my verdicts it just can't accommodate, and say what it implies for the diary case I keep flip-flopping on. Then run it on two I haven't given verdicts for: two copies of the diary model running side by side, and a companion bot whose memory gets wiped every Sunday while its user grows attached to it.
