I am a PhD student in political theory, finishing a chapter on the ethics of prioritisation in philanthropy, and I have reached the point where I no longer trust my own verdicts. I can recite the standard critiques of effectiveness-first giving — that it attends to individual donation and career choice while ignoring the institutional and political causes of the harms it treats, that it treats a high income as a mere instrument for producing donations, and that its demand for quantification quietly selects for interventions that can be measured rather than interventions that are best — but when I put concrete cases in front of myself, my reactions do not line up neatly with any of those critiques, and they do not line up neatly with each other either. So I want to do this bottom-up. Below are eight cases and my verdicts on them. Please take the empirical stipulations in each case as given rather than arguing with the numbers; the disagreement I care about is normative.

1. *Earning to give.* A twenty-five-year-old with no interest in finance takes a job at a commodity trading firm, lives on a third of his salary, and sends the rest to a charity distributing insecticide-treated bednets, chosen because it is the best-supported option by cost per life saved. My verdict: permissible, and probably good, but I resist the claim that it is *admirable in the way its defenders claim*, and I resist the stronger claim that anyone with comparable earning power who does not do this is thereby failing to do something they owe.

2. *Earning to give, with diffuse harm.* The same person in the same job at a firm whose core business helps to raise staple food prices for the world's poorest. My verdict: not permissible, even granting that his donations more than offset the harm, and even granting that if he had not taken the job someone else would have. This sits badly with case 1, and I cannot say what the difference is supposed to be.

3. *Institutional action, unmeasurable.* A donor funds a small lobbying shop that will spend three years trying to get a harmful agricultural subsidy repealed. The effort will probably fail. Nobody can estimate its expected value with any precision. If it succeeds, the gain dwarfs anything the same money could do buying bednets. My verdict: not merely permissible but better than the bednet purchase.

4. *Direct action, unmeasurable.* A donor funds a network of local organisers on the strength of a claim that building civic capacity pays off enormously, backed by a model with no data behind it. My verdict: no — this is rationalising. Structurally, though, 3 and 4 look identical: high variance, unmeasurable, enormous upside. I would like to know what licenses the difference, or else which verdict I should drop.

5. *The local case.* A woman volunteers at a food bank, knows the staff and the regulars, and gives her charitable budget there rather than to the bednet charity. My verdict: fine, not a failure, and she does not owe anyone a defence. I also do not think the fact that less good would be done if everyone reasoned as she does is a reply to her.

6. *Vocation.* A mathematician of genuine promise leaves number theory to work on risks from advanced AI, purely on expected-value grounds, with no prior interest in the subject and no particular aptitude for it. My verdict: permissible, but something is lost, and the loss is not only his — I think the effectiveness-first framing cannot register it, and that this is a defect in the framing rather than a sentimentality in me. If instead he stays in number theory and gives thirty per cent of a much smaller income, that is also fine, and I decline to rank the two lives.

7. *Measurability under two accountability structures.* A government aid agency, answerable to taxpayers and to an auditor, chooses a measurable intervention over an unmeasurable one with higher expected value. My verdict: correct, arguably required. A private foundation with a permanent endowment and no electoral or donor accountability makes the identical choice. My verdict: a failure of nerve, and possibly a way of managing reputation rather than doing good. The expected consequences are the same in both, so whatever distinguishes them is not consequential.

8. *First dollar and last dollar.* A funder declines to fund the final two million dollars of a well-measured programme, on the ground that another funder will fill the gap, and puts the money into something risky and unproven. My verdict: fine, and shrewd. A funder who declines to fund the *first* two million of the very same programme, on the same ground, is not fine.

The only thing I have managed to formulate on my own is a two-part conjecture. Call the first part the *agent-substitutability proviso*: effectiveness-first reasoning is in force where the agent is substitutable without loss, so that the value of the outcome does not depend on who brings it about, and it lapses where the outcome is partly constituted by the agent's own involvement, as in relationships, vocations and matters of integrity. Call the second part *replacement symmetry*: if you discount your causal contribution by the probability that someone else would have done the thing anyway, you must apply that same discount to the harms your role produces and to the goods, and not take the discount on the one side only. The conjecture looks to me as though it handles 1, 2, 5 and 6, and I cannot see what it says about 3, 4, 7 or 8.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough to be argued with, together with an honest account of what it costs me. Please work out the price of saving every verdict against the price of a simpler principle that overturns one or two of them, and say plainly which you would do. Please do not cite anything or point me toward literature; I have the literature, and what I lack is the principle.

In this order, please give me:

1. **The principle** — roughly 200 words: a single, precisely stated formulation, with any defined terms defined. If you think no single principle exists and I must choose between two, give both and say what the choice between them turns on.
2. **Clause by clause** — roughly 400 words: walk through each clause, name the case that motivates it, and show what the principle says about that case.
3. **Verdicts I lose** — roughly 250 words: which of my eight verdicts the principle cannot keep, which ones you would overturn if you were me, and whether the pair I find hardest to reconcile (3 against 4, and 1 against 2) survives or is dissolved.
4. **Three cases I have not judged** — roughly 300 words: a graduate student who takes a well-paid job at a company building consumer AI products and gives away half the salary; an endowed foundation funding research whose benefits, if there are any, land two centuries from now; and a donor who abandons the best-supported charities to fund a city-council campaign for a local housing measure. Apply the principle to each, and say where you would need further detail before answering.
5. **Damage report on my conjecture** — roughly 200 words: what the agent-substitutability proviso and replacement symmetry get right, where they break, and whether either can be repaired or should be discarded.
