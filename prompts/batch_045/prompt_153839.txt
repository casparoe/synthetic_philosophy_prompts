I want a specification, not a survey. The target is an account of what an agent ought to do when she discovers that someone disagrees with her — call it a theory of disagreement response — and I want it stated as criteria first, so that I can grade the existing views against the list afterwards rather than inherit their framing.

Purpose, since adequacy is relative to it: I want a theory usable by a bounded agent in real time. My cases are ordinary ones. A referee report rejecting a paper I take to be sound. A colleague whose judgment I respect and who has seen the same data I have. A political argument with someone I take to be intelligent, informed, and sincere. A market price implying a probability different from mine. I want the theory to tell me, in each, whether to move, how far, and why — or to tell me that no such verdict is available and what the best available surrogate is. If the honest answer is that no usable rule exists, I want that said, with the argument.

Take as background Aumann's agreement theorem: if two agents' credences for some event are common knowledge between them, and both credences come from a shared prior by conditioning on their respective private information, then the two credences are equal — together with the companion result that the same assumptions rule out speculative trade between them. The usual slogan drawn from this, that rational agents cannot agree to disagree, strikes me as both too strong (it seems to forbid disagreements I am not willing to call irrational) and too weak (it says nothing about what to do before the fixed point, or when the fixed point is unreachable). So: what must a replacement deliver?

Here is my provisional list of the cases and distinctions such a theory has to get right. Treat it as raw material — correct it, extend it, and strike anything that is a prejudice of one camp rather than a genuine desideratum.

1. Finite, sequenced exchange. The theory must give verdicts for two rounds of exchange, not only for the fixed point, and must say what to do when the answer depends on who announced first.
2. Signal quality. It must distinguish learning a genuine report of someone's credence from learning a signal with no evidential weight — four hundred anonymous accounts, a paid disagreeer, a randomizer — and it must do so without handing me a general license to dismiss whoever I please. Case: my colleague of twenty years versus a bot.
3. No cliff. It must not make the verdict discontinuous in the depth of higher-order knowledge, i.e. hypersensitive to the difference between my knowing your credence and my knowing that you know that I know it. If no such robustness is possible, say so.
4. Unshared frameworks. It must handle disagreement between agents who do not share a space of states or a partition of it — two clinicians working with different disease taxonomies, two researchers with different candidate variables — and must not silently assume a common one.
5. No spinelessness. It must not, under repeated disagreement, drive every credence toward a fixed point regardless of the evidence. Case: a long sequence of apparent peers who each disagree with me after seeing my evidence.
6. No arrogance. It must not let me retain my credence by an independently formed judgment that I am the reliable party, unless that judgment is itself answerable to the theory. Case: the crank and I each think the other is the crank.
7. Channel separation. It must separate disagreement as evidence about the world from disagreement as evidence about my own reliability, must say whether those two channels are independent, and must address the worry that using the disagreement twice double-counts evidence I already had.
8. Belief versus action. It must not conflate differing credences with differing loss functions: two agents with the same credence and asymmetric error costs will act as if they disagree. It must say whether its output concerns credence, action, or both.
9. Output type and self-application. It must specify what it delivers — a point credence, a permitted interval, a constraint on a set of credences, or a decision — and whether the verdict remains stable once I apply it to myself.
10. Consistency with aggregation and trade. It must not imply that prediction aggregation, markets, or mutually beneficial trade are incoherent, and it must say what legitimates aggregation even when individual disagreement is not irrational.
11. Responses other than reweighting. It must allow that the right response may be to gather new evidence, defer to an institution, or suspend, and must say which applies when.
12. Disagreement that survives full data exchange. It must handle agents who have seen all the same data but weigh it differently, and must say whether that disagreement is rational; if it is, the theory must still say something about it.

For each requirement you keep, give the case that motivates it and mark it non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Then the part I most want: which subsets of the requirements are jointly unsatisfiable? I have four suspicions — that the no-cliff requirement cannot be had together with anything that recovers exact agreement at exact common knowledge; that no spinelessness and no arrogance squeeze every candidate to nothing; that real-time usability cannot be had together with making the verdict depend on the disagreeer's actual reliability, which I cannot fully ascertain; and that permitting rational persistent disagreement under unshared frameworks is in tension with no arrogance. Tell me which of these are real, and if the list can only be satisfied by dropping something I have called non-negotiable, say which, and what dropping it costs. Finally: which single requirement does the most exclusionary work?

Only then, and briefly — a couple of lines each, a table is fine — grade against the list: the Aumann picture itself (share a prior, exchange everything, converge); the split-the-difference-with-an-acknowledged-peer view; the total-evidence view on which disagreement is just more evidence to be weighed; the permissivist view allowing a range of rational responses to one body of evidence; and the view that disagreement is purely higher-order evidence about your own reliability. No citations or reading lists, please — I want the criteria, not the literature.
