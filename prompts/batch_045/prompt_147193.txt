For the past three years I have sat on my university's research committee, which disposes of a bequest for doctoral funding. Last year we voted between (A) twenty studentships outright and (B) a hundred studentships with probability 0.10, twenty with probability 0.89, and none with probability 0.01; we chose A. This year, on a separate budget line, we voted between (C) twenty studentships with probability 0.11 and none with probability 0.89, and (D) a hundred studentships with probability 0.10 and none with probability 0.90; we chose D. Our treasurer, who knows that I teach philosophy, has asked me to adjudicate. He says the two votes cannot both be right: whatever we were doing in preferring A to B, we reversed it in preferring D to C, and the minutes should be reopened. Members have replied that a one-in-a-hundred chance of returning nothing to the donor is not something the committee could ever have justified to her, and that in any case our resolutions are recorded and we are bound by them.

I voted with the majority on both occasions and cannot see that I have contradicted myself, but I cannot say why not either. Two features strike me as possibly relevant, and I cannot tell whether they are: that three of the eleven members were different at the two meetings, and that a recorded resolution is the nearest thing a committee has to a binding commitment. Should we reopen the minutes? And if not, what ought I to say to the treasurer?
