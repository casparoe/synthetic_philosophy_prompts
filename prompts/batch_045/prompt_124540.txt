I work in quantitative finance, do a little systematic sports betting on the side, and recently got dragged into an argument with two friends — one who says you should always maximize expected value, and one who says you should always maximize the expected growth rate of your wealth, the log-utility rule that tells you to stake a fixed fraction of what you currently have. I realised my own gut reactions to concrete bets fit neither of them, and I would rather find out what I actually believe than keep gesturing. So here are my verdicts, on cases where I trust the stated probabilities and the bets are genuinely favourable, with my net worth around $100k throughout: (1) A thousand sequential independent even-money bets where I have a real 60% edge and I pick my stake each round — betting everything every round is insane, even though its expected payoff dwarfs everything else available; something like 15–20% of current wealth feels right, 50% feels reckless, 2% feels pointlessly timid. (2) The very same bet offered exactly once, stake fixed in advance at 20% of my wealth — fine, I take it; at 100% of my wealth — bad, but a shrug rather than a horror, and clearly less bad than going all-in a thousand times. (3) A thousand of the same 60/40 bets, but the house fixes the stake at $10 and winnings are never reinvested, each bet settling separately against a bankroll that is large relative to $10 — I take all thousand, I do not thin them out, and I cannot see what the growth-rate story is even meant to apply to here. (4) A one-in-a-million shot at a billion-times payoff, ticket costs a dollar, offered once — I take it, and honestly I would put $100 on it, which I gather is about a thousand times what the growth-rate rule recommends, roughly ten cents; ten cents strikes me as absurd. (5) A one-in-a-million shot at averting an existential catastrophe, where the payoff is something like 10^15 future lives rather than money and the stake is a grant I control — I fund it, at a level that looks fanatical by the standards of case 1; I am not taking 20% of anything, I am writing a very large check. (6) Risk-sharing: five hundred of us each independently face the same favourable gamble and have agreed in advance to split total proceeds equally — my verdict is that each of us should stake far more than any of us would alone, close to everything in the limit, so that the aggregate looks like plain expected-value maximization and the caution from case 1 has apparently dissolved. (7) Everything in case 4 stays the same except that I now have a guaranteed income and a benefactor who covers my floor whatever happens — my willingness to stake rises, and not by a little, and what bothers me is that it now depends on accounting decisions, whether I count my pension, my house, my future earnings, none of which changes any real payoff. (8) If the bet pays out in a hyperinflating currency I obviously switch to real consumption units; but I do not know what the corresponding move is in case 5, where the thing that compounds is not my money.

The principle I have been trying on for size is this: maximize the expected growth rate of whatever quantity actually compounds multiplicatively across the series of decisions you are making; when payoffs are additive in something that does not compound, maximize expected value in that thing instead; and when there is only one decision and the stake is small relative to everything you care about, expected value is fine again. I think cases 4 and 5 break it, and I do not think it explains 6 and 7 at all — but cases 2 and 3 make me suspicious that repetition is not really what is doing the work, since going all-in once does not feel much better than going all-in often, and a long sequence of small fixed-stake bets does not feel like a growth-rate problem at all.

What I want is the best principle you can state that captures these verdicts, or the best two if the verdicts really cannot be had together. Please give me: first, a paragraph of at most 150 words stating your single favoured principle in plain English together with a formula, and saying explicitly whether it sides with the growth-rate view or the expected-value view in case 5; second, a row-per-case table with exactly these columns — Case (my number), My verdict as I gave it, Verdict of your principle, Fit (exact, strained, or conflict), Clause doing the work, and What would have to be added to save my verdict; third, a JSON object with these fields: principle_formula (a string), clauses (an array of objects, each with clause_text, motivating_case, verdicts_saved, and cost_if_dropped), unlisted_predictions (an array of objects, each with case_description, prediction, and confidence as a number between 0 and 1), verdicts_abandoned (an array of objects, each with case, my_verdict, and why_no_clause_saves_it), and numeraire_handling (a string saying what your principle does about the accounting-dependence in case 7). For the unlisted predictions I am most interested in two: a thousand-round sequence where a single loss ends the sequence entirely, an absorbing barrier rather than a shrinking bankroll; and a one-shot longshot where the alternative use of the stake is a donation that converts dollars into lives linearly. And if the only way to keep all eight verdicts is a principle with an extra clause that does nothing but rescue one of them, say so plainly and tell me which verdict you would drop instead — I would rather give up an intuition than carry an ad hoc clause.
