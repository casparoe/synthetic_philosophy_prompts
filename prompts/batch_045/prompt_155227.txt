**Two distinctions, not one**

It is now common ground that cognition makes a causal difference to what we experience: expectation, mood, verbal framing and training all change how things look, sometimes apparently all the way down to early vision. The interesting question was never whether that influence exists but what it does to the standing of experience as a source of justification. I want to argue that the debate has been run as if there were one distinction here, penetration versus mere causal influence, when there are really two, and that once they are separated the familiar examples sort differently than either side expects.

Call it *content-transfer* penetration when the cognitive state contributes its content to the content of the experience: you come to believe that the shape in the hedge is a person, and the shape comes to look like a person. Call it *calibration* penetration when the cognitive state alters a setting without contributing content: a scaling factor, a prior, a threshold is reset, and the same face, with the same muscular configuration, comes to look tense rather than neutral. Both are penetration in the ordinary sense, but only the first obviously raises the epistemic worry, since only in the first does experience seem to inherit the correctness or incorrectness of a belief.

I think that is backwards. The worry attaches to neither of those properties but to a third, which I will call *evidential entanglement*: the experience's content depends counterfactually on the cognitive state in such a way that, had the cognitive state been different while the stimulus was held fixed, the content would have been different, and this dependence is not mediated by any improvement in how well the experience tracks the very property it represents. Where experience is evidentially entangled with a belief that P, the experience cannot serve as an independent tribunal for P, and treating it as evidence for P double-counts: support the belief already possessed is routed through the experience and then counted again as fresh support from the world.

Two consequences follow, and they are the point of the paper. First, the well-known argument from the inertia of illusion was always a non sequitur. That an illusion survives knowledge shows only that penetration is not all-purpose; it is silent about entanglement, since an experience can be causally stubborn and evidentially entangled at the same time. Second, the argument from perceptual learning misfires in the opposite direction. Training raises tracking, so the entanglement condition fails: the expert's experience is more reliable, not less, and calibration by expertise is not the kind of dependence that disables the tribunal.

If this is right, the right question for any candidate case is not whether cognition reaches into perception but whether the influence raises or lowers the experience's counterfactual reliability on the property represented. Penetration is not a kind of causal influence at all; it is a kind of epistemic dependence. Cases where the influence improves tracking are not penetration but *tuning*, and my suspicion, which I will not defend here, is that the great majority of the celebrated examples are tuning.
