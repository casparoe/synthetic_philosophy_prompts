I am closing the final session of a graduate seminar on Dennett's three stances — physical, design, and intentional — and on the account of real patterns those stances were developed to support, and I would like to end with a poem rather than a summary slide.

Write a sestina: six unrhymed six-line stanzas in which the same six end-words rotate according to the standard pattern, followed by a three-line envoi. The end-words are:

pattern, physical, design, intend, compress, noise

Three constraints on the content.

First, one target, and not a chess-playing computer or a thermostat. Choose a single system that has neither a designer nor beliefs of its own, but toward which all three stances can nonetheless be applied in earnest — an ant colony's foraging trail, a city's traffic at rush hour, a price series, a river delta, your choice. Name it in a one-line epigraph and stay with it for all thirty-nine lines.

Second, get the machinery right. The physical stance predicts a system's behavior from its physical constitution and the laws governing it; the design stance predicts from the assumption that the system is designed to do what it does and will do it, barring malfunction; the intentional stance predicts by treating the system as a rational agent, working out the beliefs and desires it ought to have given its situation and needs, and predicting that it will do what it ought to do to get what it wants. A pattern, on the associated view, earns the title real when invoking it buys you a cheaper description of the data than you would have without it — when redescribing the system in those terms genuinely compresses rather than merely relabels.

Third, the rotation has to earn its keep. Nothing about the target changes from stanza to stanza; only the order of the six words does. Use that: the same six words in a new arrangement each time should read as the same system redescribed at a different level of abstraction, and by the envoi the reader should have noticed that pattern and noise have drifted toward each other — that what counts as noise from one stance is what a rival description leaves out, not the absence of structure. The envoi's last line should contain both words.

The point of the poem, and the part I most want handled carefully, is a case the compressibility test does not settle: two descriptions of the same data that compress it equally well but cannot both be right about what is going on, one available only from the intentional stance and the other only from the design or physical stance, with nothing in the data and nothing in the test itself to break the tie. Do not resolve the tie in the poem, and do not stage it as a refutation of the view: render it so that a reader who knows the literature recognizes what is at stake in it, and a reader who does not still reaches an ending rather than a shrug.

Aim for something that stands on its own for someone who has never heard of the stances: concrete throughout, no jargon applied as ornament, the six end-words falling where they would have fallen anyway had you not been bound to them.

After the poem, add a short note — two or three paragraphs at most — doing two things.

Separate the settled from the contested. I want to be able to tell, reading it, which of the poem's commitments are just the apparatus and which are live disputes among people who work on these views: whether real patterns secures a robust realism about intentionality or only an instrumental license to go on talking as if; whether the compressibility test is stance-relative or circular in a way that leaves it unable to adjudicate between rival descriptions; whether three stances is the right number at all. Do not present a contested reading as what Dennett or the field has established, and do not flatten the disagreement into a balanced both-sides summary.

Then flag where the poem takes liberties: any place where it leans on a reading of the stances or of real patterns that a proponent might disown, or where the verse required saying something sharper than the view actually says.
