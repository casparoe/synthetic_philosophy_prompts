I run a volunteer listening hour at a small residential care home — an hour of recorded music on Tuesday afternoons for a dozen or so residents, most of whom pick the records. Last spring, after a documentary series made public a long pattern of abuse by a singer whose voice nearly all of them grew up with, I quietly dropped him from the rotation and replaced him with a bandleader against whom comparable allegations had been made a decade earlier, and whom I left in place without thinking about it. Both moves felt right at the time and they still feel right, but I can't explain the difference to a room of eighty-year-olds who want their records back, and I've been going round in circles with two friends about it for months. One friend says I'm just refusing to fund or signal approval of a living perpetrator; the other says it's guilt by association dressed up as principle. I don't think either is what I'm doing, and the case I keep coming back to is this: the same singer's most famous track, played as a clip inside the documentary about his abuse, bothered no one at all — several residents asked to hear it again afterwards. The sound is identical. What changed was the situation of being addressed by it.

Here is the pattern as I've managed to describe it to myself. The refusal doesn't track how bad the person's acts were, and it doesn't track whether the work resembles or expresses those acts: a man who wrote tender love songs gets dropped as readily as one who wrote violent ones, and his tender songs go with him. What it seems to track is how much the work's appreciation depends on you extending something to the artist as a person — warmth, trust, charm, the sense of being spoken to and of answering back. Intimately-addressed music, confessional songwriting, comedy, a charismatic screen performance, memoir: dropped. Abstract instrumental music, a tightly-constructed score, a dance band heard as an ensemble sound: kept, even from the same biography. It isn't consequentialist, because in the case that started this no money reaches anyone, the artist is dead, and I'd bought the records decades ago — and because the refusal survives being entirely private, where nobody is watching what I signal. And crucially it isn't a judgement about the work: I don't think the record got worse, and I say so, and the residents agree with me, and still don't want it played. What they object to is what listening to it puts them in — something like being on warm terms with him, accepting something from him, letting his voice in the room on the old footing. So when people tell me to separate the work from the artist, it sounds like an answer to a question nobody asked. And then the feature that really puzzles me: nothing about the recording changed when the allegations came out. Either the work acquired a property it didn't have, or I acquired a reason — and I can't tell which, but the difference seems to matter for whether this is a fact about art or a fact about me.

So: is there an established concept in the philosophical literature on art and ethics for this specific thing — the way a work's moral history bears on whether one can accept an interpersonal stance toward its maker, independently of any claim about the work's value and of any downstream consequences — and if so, how exactly is it carved off from the neighbouring views? I have in mind three that I don't think fit: the debate about whether ethical flaws make a work worse as art (I'm explicitly not claiming it's worse); complicity or boycott arguments, which turn on money, encouragement, or what my listening says to others (dead artists, private listening, no payments); and the worry that I'm reasoning illegitimately from where a thing came from to how good it is (I'm making no claim about quality). If the fit is imperfect, tell me which of these is nearest, what has to be adjusted in my description to make one of them fit, and where my version overflows it. If there is no established concept, coin one and define it tightly enough to classify the boundary cases: a performance in which the warmth belongs to a character rather than to the performer; a track heard as documentary evidence rather than as a record; an artist whose wrongdoing was unknown when the work was made and who died before it was known; a listener who has never learned the performer's name and loves the voice; and a work made collaboratively, where the invitation seems to come from a group rather than a person. Please also be explicit about your confidence: distinguish the parts of your answer that report an established distinction from the parts that are your own construction, say which part of the carving you'd abandon first, and tell me what would change your verdict — what would make you conclude instead that my "pattern" is really just one of the views I've dismissed, or that it's two separate phenomena I've run together, or that the interpersonal-uptake variable is doing no real work and something else explains my two Tuesday decisions.
