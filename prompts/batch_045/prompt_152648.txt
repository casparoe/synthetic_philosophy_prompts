I'm defending the third chapter of my dissertation next week in the formal epistemology seminar here, and honestly I'm more nervous about the Q&A than about the chapter. Setup:

Audience is about twelve people. Four or five are card-carrying formal epistemologists who grew up on Aumann-style partition models of knowledge and possible-worlds semantics for belief, and who think a modal logic plus a Kripke semantics is the only serious way to model a doxastic state. There's one logician who thinks any non-normal modal logic is just giving up with extra notation and will say so within the first five minutes. Two are mainstream epistemologists who are quietly skeptical that any of this machinery tells us anything about what real people believe — they'll keep circling back to what the normative upshot is supposed to be. There's one AI person who works on bounded reasoning in agent architectures and will want to know whether any of it is implementable. And my advisor, who thinks the whole hyperintensional turn is a fad, will be sitting in the front row.

The thesis: logical omniscience isn't a defect to be engineered around, it's a symptom that we're representing the wrong thing. Standard models treat a belief state as a set of worlds, which automatically makes belief closed under logical equivalence and, with the usual axioms, under conjunction and modus ponens. My move is to index the doxastic state to a topic: the state only resolves distinctions that fall inside the topic currently in play, and closure under entailment holds only *within* a topic, not across topics. I call the resulting structures topical models, and the slogan version is that the agent believes every consequence of what she believes *about the same thing*. So someone who accepts all the axioms of a theory and the inference rules can still fail to believe some far-off theorem, because the theorem is about a topic the axioms only bear on indirectly. That's definately the part of the chapter I'm least sure about.

There's also a worked case: an agent who is shown a large finite graph (say, a few hundred nodes with a few thousand edges) piecemeal over an hour, believes each thing she's told as she's told it, competently draws short inferences from what she's been told, but hasn't assembled some global claim about the graph that would take a long chain of steps to get. I use it to argue that the omniscience the standard model foists on her isn't tracking anything real about her — it's an artifact of representing her state as one flat set of worlds.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Specifically:

- Each one phrased the way that person would actually put it. The formal people have thier own idiom — your accessibility relation is doing no work, that's just a neighbourhood semantics with a story bolted on — and the mainstream people have a completely different one. I want to hear the difference.

- The question I'm hoping nobody asks.

My own guess is the dangerous one is normative: if all I've done is describe a more finely-grained state, I haven't said anything about what the agent *ought* to believe, and the original worry about logical omniscience had a normative edge — logic is supposed to constrain belief, not just fail to describe it.
