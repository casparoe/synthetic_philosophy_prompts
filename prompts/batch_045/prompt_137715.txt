My brother-in-law is a therapist and every time we argue about this he falls back on the same pair of pictures: guilt is like a debt, shame is like a stain. He uses them to argue that guilt is obviosly the better moral emotion — a debt is about what you actually did rather than about how you look to other people, it has a definite amount, it can be repaid or forgiven, and it does not need anyone watching — while a stain is about appearances, it hangs around, and nothing you do really settles it. He is also half leaning on that old mid-century anthropology contrast between guilt cultures and shame cultures, which I gather anthropolgoists mostly dropped but which is still all over the management literature I read.

I write a fortnightly newsletter for people who run small organisations, and the piece I am stuck on is whether a team can deliberately build one of these rather than the other, so I need to know how much weight the two pictures can actually bear. Could you audit them for me? I want exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no summing-up at the end, in British English:

1. the mapping in compressed form — what plays the creditor, the ledger, the repayment or forgiveness, the audience, the washing;
2. the things each picture quietly imports that guilt and shame do not obviously have — I keep coming back to creditorless guilt, the sort you feel about someone who is dead or in survivor guilt, and to shame you would feel with no witness at all;
3. the single disanalogy that does the most damage to the way he is using them, and why that one and not the others;
4. whether the guilt-is-better claim survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time;
5. what either picture implies about whether a group can pick one — does the debt picture need institutions, someone with standing to discharge the debt, in a way the stain picture does not — and whether there is a better pair of analogies for what he is actually after.
