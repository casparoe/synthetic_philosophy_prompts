I take the minutes for the ethics committee of a public health system, and I have no vote in its decisions. At our last meeting two members argued past each other for forty minutes about a coverage question, and I have to write the discussion up in a way that says who actually scored points where. I'd like you to adjudicate the exchange as it happened. Please assess the arguments as they were actually made rather than as they might have been made better, and don't reach for what some author has said: I want the moves judged on their merits.

The committee is deciding whether to cover a drug with the working name RX-9. Taken within a few hours of a memory being reactivated, RX-9 dampens the affective charge attached to that memory while leaving its factual content intact; it does not sedate, does not impair new learning, and its effects on a given memory appear to persist after a short course. Three indications are on the table: (1) diagnosed PTSD following an assault; (2) an otherwise healthy person, six months after the end of a long relationship, who has lost weight and nearly lost their job, and who describes the memory as a wound that will not close; (3) hospital residents with no diagnosis who want to take it after a patient dies under their care, so that they can continue working night shifts without the death intruding.

The two members are Marta, a health-policy philosopher on the committee, and Dev, a psychiatrist who runs the trauma clinic.

(1) Marta: Indication (1) is therapy and (2) and (3) are enhancement, and the line I want is not a statistical baseline but a malfunction: in (1) a system that has stopped doing what it is for is being brought back to doing it, while in (2) and (3) the system is doing exactly what it is for and the drug is being used to alter how much a person is moved by something that is not going wrong. Therapy restores a person's capacity to be affected appropriately; enhancement resets the setting. A public payer owes people the first and not the second.

(2) Dev: The mechanism is identical in all three cases, and so is the benefit — less suffering, sleep, the return of ordinary functioning. If the intervention and the benefit are the same, the difference between (1) and (2) has to be located entirely in the story we tell about why the person is suffering, which is a judgment about whether their distress is warranted rather than a fact about what medicine can do. Telling the person in (2) that their memory system is functioning correctly and that they are therefore not a candidate for repair is a way of laundering a verdict on the legitimacy of their grief into a verdict about the scope of medicine.

(3) Marta: Granted that the mechanism is shared; the mechanism never settles these questions, since the same drug repairs a heart and lowers a blood pressure. What matters is the shape of the benefit. Plot benefit against the target capacity: in therapy the curve is steeply concave, rising quickly while the person is below their own earlier level and then flattening out; in enhancement it stays roughly linear past that point. That makes the distinction empirical and individual rather than metaphysical. The curve for (1) is concave. The curve for (3) is linear, because the residents want a lower setting for as long as the night rota continues, with no natural stopping point.

(4) Dev: That is clever, but it relocates the line rather than defending it. First, many people have no earlier level to be restored to — the memory predates any baseline, or the person has always been highly reactive — so their curve has no inflection point and the test returns nothing, even though we still have to decide. Second, if the line depends on the shape of one person's benefit curve, the same drug at the same dose is therapy for one patient and enhancement for another, and coverage becomes an official judgment about the contour of someone's inner life, which is more intrusive than the blunt question about disease you began by rejecting. Third, concavity is partly a fact about how much we are willing to supply: if the system will not fund repeat courses, the curve flattens by administrative decision and the same case flips from therapy to enhancement without anything about the patient changing.

(5) Marta: I will accept individualisation and even a partly administrative boundary, because that is how every threshold in medicine works; the cut-off for treating blood pressure is set by a committee, and nobody concludes that hypertension is not a real phenomenon. The distinction is not trying to carve nature at a joint. It is giving a public payer a principled ordering: we owe people restoration of what has gone wrong before we owe them improvements, and where the two cannot be told apart we default to the first. That ordering survives a fuzzy boundary. A fuzzy boundary is not the same thing as no boundary, and abandoning the ordering means every claim on the budget is just a preference.

(6) Dev: Then say that and drop the rest. What you have described is a rationing rule with a priority ordering, not a difference in kind between two kinds of intervention, and if it is a priority ordering then refusing (3) is not a claim about what the residents are owed but about what else the money could do — which means that under a different budget your not-owed becomes may-be-funded, and enhancement was never categorically outside medicine in the first place. And the worry you raised in the corridor, that funding (3) would make it harder for a resident to decline it, is a problem about rota设计与employers, not about the drug: prohibit any employer from asking whether someone took it and regulate the pressure directly. Once you do that, the case for refusing (3) collapses into the budget claim alone.

Please do three things. First, rate each of the six moves on a four-point scale — clearly right, more right than wrong, more wrong than right, clearly mistaken — with one or two sentences of justification per move, judging the move as stated and not the best version of it. Second, in prose, say who is right about what overall, which single move is the strongest argument in the exchange, which is the weakest, and what both parties are missing — in particular, whether the malfunction account and the priority-ordering account can be held together, or whether accepting one forces the abandonment of the other.
