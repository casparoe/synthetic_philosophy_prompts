ok so my school has this philosophy club thing and we got onto rigid designators last week and I tried to build an argument that the whole "necessary a posteriori" stuff is fake, but now I think my argument proves way too much and I can't tell which step is the broken one.

(1) A rigid designator picks out the same object in every possible world where that object exists.
(2) "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" are rigid, they're names, not descriptions.
(3) In the actual world they're the same thing (Venus).
(4) So they're the same thing in every possible world: necessarily, Hesperus = Phosphorus. True, but you can only find it out by looking at the sky. That's the famous part, I'm fine with it.
(5) Now let me make up a name by stipulation: "Bloppy" rigidly designates whatever is sitting at the bottom of the deepest part of Lake Superior, whatever that turns out to be — like naming a planet before anyone's actually seen it.
(6) Bloppy is rigid by stipulation, so if it turns out Bloppy is the sunken tugboat Mildred, then by the exact same move it's necessary that Bloppy = Mildred.
(7) But it's totally contingent what's at the bottom of that lake. It could have been a rock, or nothing at all. So Bloppy = Mildred is contingent, if it's true.
(8) Same for Hesperus and Phosphorus: the astronomers could have found two planets up there.
(9) So either the move from (1)-(3) to (4) doesn't really give you necessity, or else every single empirical discovery is a necessary truth, which is ridiculous.

I have a feeling (7) and (8) are cheating with the word "could," but I also really don't want to give up (7) — it's about as obvious as anything gets that there might have just been a rock down there. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? Also, is the Bloppy case secretly different from Hesperus/Phosphorus, or is that the whole point? And what even happens in worlds where Mildred was never built — does "Bloppy = Mildred" have a truth value there, and does that matter for whether it's necessary?
