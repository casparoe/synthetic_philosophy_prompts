Hey — I volunteer to run a monthly "eng and society" lunch thing at my company: usually 14-16 people, mostly backend and infra folks, one 60-minute session, and basically nobody has any philosophy background (me included, I write Go all day). We tried to talk a few months back about why people end up believing obviously false stuff online, and it went badly: half the room landed on "people are just tribal and dumb," the other half decided the fix is a browser extension that surfaces articles from the other side, and we never got past that. A friend of mine afterwards — she's a librarian, not a philosopher, so grain of salt — said something that stuck with me: that being merely *cut off* from good evidence and being *actively taught to distrust everyone who brings it* are two totally different failure modes, and that's why the "just show them the facts" instinct keeps backfiring, because in the second case the facts arriving from a suspect source make people dig in harder. I want the room to feel that difference in their gut rather than hear it from me, so I'm trying to build something they play rather than something I explain.

Constraints, in no particular order: no laptops or phones during the session (it's a lunch room and half of them would just start googling), no pre-reading, and nobody should hear either of the fancy two-word labels until the very end — I want them to notice the pattern in what actually happened first. The setup has to make both failure modes show up at once, in the same room, with the same evidence, so we can compare afterwards: one subgroup that simply never gets exposed to the contrary material, and one subgroup that does get exposed but has earlier in the hour been given some reason to write off whoever is delivering it. Ideally the disputed fact should be something boring and checkable right there in the room — how many red cards are in a face-down deck, whether a jar holds more than N marbles, whatever works — so the content can't be argued with and the only move left is to shoot the messenger. Then I want to literally score both subgroups on confidence vs. accuracy so the divergence is visible. I'd also like a wrinkle where the person bringing in the outside evidence is someone the group previously trusted, an insider who defects, so they have to either burn one of their own or update.

What I need: real rules, roles or hidden assignments, a timeline with minute markers for 60 minutes, materials I can print or buy, and a payoff/scoring scheme that works without money (bragging rights or a stupid trophy is fine). Then the debrief: how you'd map specific moments in the hour onto the two failure modes, the questions you'd ask to get them to articulate the difference themselves before you name it, what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it (I have at least one aggressive contrarian who will try to reverse-engineer the game while it's running and I don't want him to wreck it), and honestly which part of the real phenomenon an hour-long game just cannot reproduce, so I know what I have to say out loud myself at the end. Bonus if you can tell me how I'd know a month later whether it actually landed, since I suspect half of them will just enjoy the game and totally miss the point.
