I'm a junior, and I'm one of five students on the committee that allocates our school's activities fund. We vote on Thursday, and I keep changing my mind, so I would be grateful for your help.

Here is the situation. We have a one-time surplus of about nine thousand dollars, and two proposals are on the table for how to spend part of it. Both are for the same thing: a free after-school SAT and ACT prep course, which would cost roughly three thousand dollars for workbooks, practice tests, and small stipends for two senior tutors, and which would serve around a hundred and forty juniors who can't afford commercial prep. Nearly everyone agrees the course itself is a good thing. The disagreement is about what it costs the person it hurts.

Proposal A, from our treasurer: suspend the Hollis Award for this year. The Hollis is a three-thousand-dollar award our committee gives each spring to one senior with financial need. The recipient isn't announced until April, but it's widely expected to go to Danielle Reyes, whose father was injured last year and who has told our college counselor she is counting on the money for her enrollment deposit. The three thousand dollars would go to the prep course instead.

Proposal B, from another member: leave the award alone, pay for the course out of the surplus, and hold it in the library annex from three to five every afternoon. The annex is the only reliably quiet space in the building. Danielle uses it each afternoon for homework and for weekly meetings with the college counselor who is helping her with her applications; if the course meets there, she loses both, and the counselor says that would meaningfully weaken her applications.

There is also a possible Proposal C: a cheaper Saturday-morning version run by volunteers with fewer materials, serving maybe forty students for about seven hundred dollars, leaving both the award and the annex untouched.

Our treasurer says both A and B are fine, and that A is actually the more honest of the two. His argument is that we aren't trying to harm Danielle, that any harm to her is a side effect of helping a hundred and forty people rather than something we are aiming at, and that the benefit dwarfs the harm. He says his sister's ethics textbook has a name for this reasoning, something like the doctrine of double effect, and that it marks the difference between using someone as a means and merely foreseeing that they will be affected. A friend of mine says all of this is window dressing: in both proposals one identifiable person loses something large so that a great many people gain something small, and the language about not intending anything is just a way of feeling better about it.

I can't tell who is closer to right. I have four specific questions. First, does the treasurer's reasoning actually cover both proposals, or does one of them fail the very test he is appealing to? Second, does it matter that in Proposal A we don't know that Danielle would win the award — it seems likely, maybe four chances in five, but it isn't certain? Third, does the availability of Proposal C change which of the other two is permissible, or only which of them is best? Fourth, and this matters most to me: is the distinction the treasurer is leaning on a genuine moral distinction, or is my friend right that a difference in how the harm is causally connected to the benefit cannot matter that much?

After giving me your advice in prose, please end with a short JSON object, using exactly these keys:

1. "bottom_line" (string): how I should vote on Thursday, in one or two sentences.
2. "treasurer" (object with three string keys): "right_about", "wrong_about", and "verdict", where the verdict is one of "applies to both proposals", "applies to A only", "applies to B only", or "applies to neither".
3. "proposals" (array of three objects, one each for A, B, and C, in that order), each with these keys: "label" (A, B, or C); "who_is_harmed"; "causal_role" (whether the harm is a means to the good effect, a merely foreseen side effect, or neither, plus one sentence of justification); "verdict" (one of "permissible", "impermissible", or "unclear"); "decisive_condition" (the condition that settles it, or "none"); and "reason" (one sentence).
4. "risk" (string): whether, and how, it changes the analysis that the harm in Proposal A is only highly probable rather than certain.
5. "cheaper_alternative" (string): whether the existence of Proposal C affects what is permissible as opposed to what is merely best.
6. "is_the_distinction_real" (string): two or three sentences giving the strongest case both for and against trusting the distinction the treasurer is using.
7. "what_to_say" (string): one or two sentences I could actually say at the meeting.
