Hi — I'm a retired astrophysicist (spent most of my career on large-scale structure, nothing remotely philosophical) who's drifted into philosophy over the last couple of years, and I've got a question I can't get any traction on. There's a reading group in town — six or seven retirees plus two philosophy grad students who mostly roll their eyes at us — and we've spent about eight months on anthropic reasoning: the doomsday argument, Gott-style delta-t moves, and the presumptuous philosopher case where the philosophers refuse to look through the telescope because the bare fact that they exist already tells them the cosmos is enormous. And it has completly stalled in a way that feels different from other philosophy we've read. Nobody has shifted even slightly, and each side is convinced the other has made some elementary blunder about sampling. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict.

Here's my sense of the two sides, tell me where I've got it wrong. Self-sampling: treat yourself as a random sample from the observers in the relevant reference class, so finding yourself this early in human history is evidence about how many observers there will ever be. Self-indication: treat your own existence as evidence for hypotheses that predict more observers, so the fact that you exist at all boosts the big-cosmology hypothesis and cancels the doomsday effect. Fine. But then the argument just stops. One camp says the other is double-counting — you had to exist in order to be doing the reasoning, so your existence isn't news and can't be evidence. The other camp says the first is conditioning on something that was guaranteed in advance and is thereby throwing away perfectly good evidence. Then somebody notices that both answers depend on what the reference class is — all observers, or all beings capable of reasoning like this, or only beings in your exact epistemic situation — and we're back where we started.

My hypothesis, which I'd like you to push back on as hard as you like: this isn't a dispute about sampling at all, it's the measure problem in a different hat. In cosmology, when a theory predicts a huge or infinite set of events, you have to pick a measure before any probability comes out, the choice of measure does all the work, and the theory itself doesn't determine the choice. SSA and SIA look to me like two different packages of (sampling rule + prior over how many observers there are), and once you're free to set the prior you can reproduce either verdict with either rule — the disagreement has just relocated into the prior, where it's invisible. If that's right, the endless fighting about the reference class is a symptom: picking a reference class is a crude way of picking a measure, and everyone argues about the crude proxy instead of the thing doing the work.

Two more things, if you can. First, the bit nobody in the group will engage with. It seems to me there's an empirical question hiding in here that isn't cosmological at all and is testable on a table. Say I could run this: a coin is flipped, and depending on the outcome I create either one copy of a subject or a million copies, all with identical memories up to the moment they're asked how the coin landed, with the copies never meeting and no way to tell whether they're the original. SSA and SIA disagree about what the subjects should say, the experiment can be repeated, and one of them will be vindicated in that setting. So there is a fact of the matter about which rule is right in the tabletop case. My question is whether settling that settles anything about the cosmological case, or whether the two are different questions dressed up the same way — and if they are different, what exactly is the difference. Second, smaller: one of the grad students keeps insisting that SIA should really be counting observer-moments rather than observers, and that once you say that it starts boosting hypotheses on which you personally live an extremely long time, which sounds crazy to me but I can't put my finger on why. Real problem for the view or just a confusion? I'd honestly also settle for being told the whole question is ill-posed, but then I'd want to know what "ill-posed" means here, and whether it's ill-posed in the same way as asking for the probability that a randomly chosen integer is even.
