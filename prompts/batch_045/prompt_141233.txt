My opening move. Mises's claim was that if the state owns the factors of production there are no money prices for them — they're internal transfers, not objects of exchange — so there's nothing in terms of which to compare a tonne of steel used this way against a tonne used that way, and rational allocation is impossible. But a missing price is missing information, and information can be manufactured. Lange's board dosen't need real markets in capital goods: it runs a trial-and-error procedure. Announce a set of accounting prices, let each state enterprise report what it wants to buy and sell at those prices, nudge prices up wherever there's excess demand and down wherever there's a glut, and iterate. In Lange's day that iteration was done by hand and was hopeless; the man himself later said electronic computers would finish in hours what the market does only by a slow, wasteful process. Now I'll go further than Lange. Where the procedure needs agents to do the bidding, we have them: software bidders, trained on the recorded behaviour of a real economy that had real prices in it, running an internal auction a million times a second. That also kills the old objection that managers have no incentive to report truthfully to the board — we don't ask them, the auction gets the information out of them. And before you reach for the tin example from your 1945 paper: fine, someone somewhere learns that tin has got scarcer, but a price is just a number in a vector, and the vector is what my system updates. So socialism's problem was a search problem — search over an astronomical space of possible allocations — and search is exactly what computation is for. Once the bottleneck is gone, tell me precisely which step of my procedure breaks, and why running it faster doesn't mend it.
