ok so I just spent like two hours arguing with my friend (she's a child psychiatrist) about whether rising diagnosis rates are "actually more kids with it" or just "we got better at spotting it." She thinks it's obviously mostly better detection and that this is a boring measurement point with a boring fix. I think that for the kinds of things psychiatric categories are, "more of the thing" and "better at spotting the thing" aren't even separable in principle — the category and the people it sorts co-evolve, Hacking's looping thing. We got nowhere, and now I have to give the talk version of this and I'm more scared of the Q&A than of the talk itself.

The setup: 30 minutes plus 20 for discussion, at the mental health services / psychiatric epidemology research seminar here. Twenty-ish people: mostly quantitative types who run cohort studies and think in terms of constructs, measurement invariance, and predictive validity; four or five clinicians who still see patients; one health economist. Nobody's hostile, but the room is allergic to anything that smells like anti-psychiatry or "mental illness is just a social construction," and several of them were around for the last DSM revision and have settled into treating diagnostic categories as useful fictions you pick, defend briefly, and then get on with your actual study.

My thesis in three steps: (1) the standard question "has true prevalence gone up, or is it just diagnosis?" presupposes that the kind has a prevalence independent of our sorting practices — for interactive kinds that presupposition fails, so the question is underdetermined, not just hard. (2) The bit I think is actually new: the loop doesn't only run through clinicians and patients any more, it runs through screening instruments, risk scores built out of health records, self-tracking apps, and intake tools that both classify people and shape what those people say to the next classifier — and because those models get retrained on data their own outputs helped produce, the thing doing the sorting and the thing being sorted are updating on each other faster than any clinical concept could stabilise. (3) So there's no true trend to recover by measuring more carefully; the only way to get comparable numbers across years is to freeze the instrument, and that's a decision, not a discovery.

What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. Give me the six or seven objections I'll actually get from this room — not the ones I'd get from philosophy of science people, I know those already — ordered by how much damage they'd do, each one phrased the way that person would really say it rather than in my vocabulary (I expect at least: "isn't this just measurement non-invariance, which we have tools for?", "isn't this just feedback in deployed prediction models?", and "what exactly is your estimand?"). For each: a reply I could give in about two minutes, and where that reply is soft. Also tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes to keep the room, what I should absolutely not give up, which single reply has to land for the rest of the talk to matter, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks — I think it's some version of "if there's no fact of the matter about the trend, are you saying the needs-assessment numbers my whole field produces are worthless?", but tell me if you think the real one is worse.
