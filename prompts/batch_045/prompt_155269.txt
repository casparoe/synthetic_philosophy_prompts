I'm a software engineer who does volunteer data work for a small island-conservation charity. At a project meeting two of the staff ecologists argued past each other in a way I couldn't adjudicate; call them R and S. The proposal on the table was a two-year reversible pilot: on a small predator-free island, automated feeders dispense a compound intended to blunt the unpleasantness of pain in the resident rabbits while leaving intact both the sensory signal of tissue damage and pain-driven learning, so an animal still notices and protects an injury but suffers less from it. Welfare would be tracked by behavioral proxies, and the program can simply be switched off.

(1) R: wild suffering is vast and almost nobody works on it; the pilot is cheap, bounded, switchable at will, and in a system small enough to watch, so even a modest expected reduction justifies running it.
(2) S: "watched" is doing all the work here. There is no validated welfare metric for a wild animal, so you cannot measure your own outcome variable. And the island isn't really closed — birds, storms and disease cross the boundary — so reversibility only helps if you'd notice harm early enough to act, which on your own assumptions you would not.
(3) R: I concede the metric is weak, but that objection would have blocked anaesthesia and farm-animal analgesia too, where we likewise started with coarse behavioral proxies. Time budgets, wound-grooming, stress markers, foraging-under-threat: that is not nothing. The answer to a bad ruler is to build a better one, not to declare the quantity unmeasurable.
(4) S: those proxies were calibrated in domestic animals against a shared human benchmark; in the wild you cannot calibrate them, and averaging hides exactly what matters, since a population can look calm while a subset is in agony. Worse: blunt the sting while keeping the signal and you risk animals over-using injured limbs and dying sooner. Since most wild juveniles die early anyway, you may be converting suffering into extra dying rather than removing it.
(5) R: the sting/signal split is the whole point of the design. And your aggregation worry is a boomerang — if we cannot tell whether welfare goes up or down, we also cannot tell whether a wild life is on balance worth living, so "leave nature alone" leans on the very measurement you call impossible. Doing nothing is an intervention with an unmeasured outcome too.
(6) S: I accept the symmetry, but it doesn't favor you. We already disrupt these animals' habitat, so the burden of proof sits with whoever wants to add a new, unvalidated program imposed on beings who cannot refuse. And the real failure mode isn't one botched pilot — it's that "ecosystem welfare management" becomes a standing mandate with no one inside it positioned to say stop.

They asked me to referee because I'm the outsider in the room; I have no stake and no settled view. Please go through it move by move rather than declaring a winner overall.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no preamble and no closing summary: (1) the closed-system and reversibility claim; (2) the unmeasurability objection and R's domestic-animal analogy; (3) the aggregation and pain-function objection; (4) the symmetry and burden-of-proof exchange; (5) what both of them are missing that a referee should point out.
