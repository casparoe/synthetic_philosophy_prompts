Running a rewatch night for my local rationalist-ish meetup next month and we're doing Predestination (the 2014 one with Ethan Hawke and Sarah Snook, based on that Heinlein story). We picked it because its the most extreme case of a self-causing self I know of: one token who is their own baby, their own mother, their own father, their own recruiter, and eventually their own target — and crucially with no duplication and no branching. John/Jane is never two people at once, just one person with a really weird causal history.

I want to read it through Hofstadter's claim that the 'I' is a self-referential symbol — the tangled hierarchy idea, the symbol that can never be fully cashed out without going round again, a kind of quine that reproduces the system that produced it — and I want the reading to actually say something about that claim rather than drape it over the film as decoration.

Let me stipulate names so we don't talk past each other. Call a world-loop a loop in the causal chain: the token goes around, the case file closes on itself, the person is their own parent and their own worst enemy. Call a model-loop the Hofstadter-ish thing: some subsystem carries a representation of the whole that carries it, and the 'who' is supposed to be what falls out of that closure, with the downward-causation-ish feel that comes with it. Call a rewrite the situation where the representation of 'me' has to be rebuilt wholesale because everything it was tracking has changed — which is roughly what the orphanage-to-Jane-to-John storyline is.

The thing I keep getting stuck on — call it the loop test — is whether the film's world-loop does any of the work Hofstadter wants the model-loop to do. My read going in is no, and that the movie is therefore evidence against him: here is a character with a maximally strange causal history and a completely ordinary first-person. Nothing about being your own father changes what 'I' picks out; the pointer is just the usual indexical pointer at a usual self-model with unusual content. But I can see the opposite reading, and the thing that pushes me toward it is the end, where he's standing in front of the Fizzle Bomber — who is his own future self — being told he was always going to become him and choosing to shoot anyway. There his self-model has to contain a representation of someone he hasn't become yet and is actively trying not to become, which feels like the loop finally reaching inside the head instead of being bolted on outside it. Argue for one of these, and tell me where the other one is strongest.

Also: someone in the last thread said the whole 'the self is a strange loop' line is unfalsifiable poeticism, a vortex with no mechanism, no more explanatory than Dennett's center-of-narrative-gravity thing. I don't think either of us knows what would settle that. But does the film give either side anything? Is there a scene that makes the Hofstadterian's claim feel earned, or one that makes the deflationist's point for them?

Give me two or three scenes to point the group at, say where the reading breaks down or the film cheats (I'd especially like to hear whether the movie secretly leans on branching logic it disavows, since it insists there's only ever one of him), and finish with whatever the film shows about the self that the philosophy doesn't. I've read GEB cover to cover and I've read the identity-over-time literature, so please don't spend paragraphs recapitulating either, and I can't check the disc, so describe scenes rather than quoting anything.
