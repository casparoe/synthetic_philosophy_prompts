I spent thirty-odd years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist — critical exponents, renormalization-group arguments, a great deal of time waiting for a neutron beam — and since retiring I have been sitting in on the metaphysics reading group that meets in the old seminar room down the hall from my former office. My colleagues there are entirely fluent in talk of grounding, full and partial, and of fundamentality as ungroundedness, and I have discovered that I hold quite firm verdicts about particular cases while having no idea what general principle, if any, they add up to. I would be grateful for help extracting one. Please do not send me to the literature: we have a good library and I can find the references myself; what I lack is a principle stated tightly enough that I can apply it to a case nobody has handed me.

Here are my verdicts. (1) The sample's being at temperature T is fully grounded in the microstate of its constituents together with the dynamics — there is nothing over and above. (2) The sample's being in the ferromagnetic phase is also grounded in the microstate, but collectively, in a way no description of a handful of constituents captures, and the very same phase can be realized by utterly different microphysics. (3) The value of a critical exponent — say the one governing how the correlation length diverges — I want to say is not explained by the microscopic details at all: change the lattice, change the atoms, and the exponent does not move. It is explained by facts about large-scale structure: the dimensionality of the system, the symmetry of the order parameter, the range of the interactions. I hold (2) and (3) together and can feel them pulling against each other, since (3) seems to say that a fact grounded in the micro facts is explained by something the micro facts do not determine. (4) The fact that a phonon of such-and-such wave-vector is present in the crystal is grounded in the motion of the atoms, and I am a realist about phonons; yet I resist saying the phonon fact is nothing but a fact about those atoms, because different atoms differently arranged would have served — so here my verdict is that the ground does not pin down the grounded fact in the way (1) suggested. (5) When two gauge-related potentials, A and A plus the gradient of a scalar function, describe one and the same state, I want to say the surplus structure is not real and is grounded in nothing — and yet the gauge potential does genuine explanatory work in the Aharonov–Bohm effect, which makes me want to take that back. (6) The negative result I once published — that our sample did not superconduct down to 1.2 kelvin — strikes me as a perfectly real fact about the world, and I am content to let it be grounded in the micro-history together with the laws; but the fact that there are no magnetic monopoles in the laboratory I am not content to ground in anything at all, and I cannot see what separates the two. (7) That the two-dimensional Ising model has a phase transition at a definite temperature I take to be a fact grounded, if in anything, in the mathematical structure alone, with physical samples inheriting it by instantiation; I am no Platonist, but I balk at grounding a mathematical fact in physical ones. (8) Finally, my professional instinct that "more fundamental" means "smaller scale" has collapsed on me: in the renormalization-group picture the large-scale fixed point is the robust, explanatory item, and the microscopic details are what wash out. Relatedly, the electron's mass is a brute parameter within our best theory and is also said to be explained by its coupling to the Higgs field, so "fundamental" in the physicist's sense and "ungrounded" in yours seem to me to come clean apart.

So: is there a single account of grounding and fundamentality — about whether the relation is many-one, whether it is hyperintensional, whether grounds must determine what they ground, whether facts about absences can have positive grounds, whether fundamentality is bruteness or something more like independence or counterfactual robustness under change of description — that delivers these verdicts, and if not, which verdict should I surrender first and what exactly would surrendering it cost me? I especially want to know what the best candidate predicts for the case I keep going back and forth on: whether the low entropy of the early universe is a fundamental posit or a grounded consequence of something deeper, and whether calling it brute would mean ungrounded or merely not derived within any theory we presently possess.
