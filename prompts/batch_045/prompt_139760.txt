I write about science and ideas for a daily newspaper, and I would like help thinking through an argument in the vocabulary I actually work in, because I suspect it either collapses or quietly changes shape when I try to say it.

The argument is the one John Taurek defended in 1977: that in a case where you can save one stranger or five strangers but not both, the fact that there are more of them gives you no reason at all; that what each person involved is owed, if anything, is an equal chance; and that the other considerations with real grip come from special relationships, from promises, or from having caused the danger yourself. I do not need the secondary literature summarised. I need a translation — and if I have mischaracterised the position, say so inside the translation rather than beside it.

My vocabulary is editorial, and it is consequentialist all the way down. Here are the moves available to me: magnitude as a news value, i.e. how many people a thing happens to; our house convention that every individual we name in a story gets the same space regardless of how many others are in it, so that a piece's length is set by its list of names; the identifiable-victim effect, which my trade treats as a bias to be corrected rather than a datum to be respected; false balance, where giving two sides equal treatment is a professional failure because the evidence is not equal; proportionality, which at my paper means allocating attention by how well-supported a claim is and how many people it bears on; and the evidential weight of a pattern, on which more below.

So: restate Taurek's claim, and the aggregative reply to it, using only those resources, and apply both to this case. My desk has one slot next week. Either we run a long profile of a single man who died of a preventable equipment failure, or we run a data piece establishing that five people died of the same failure; the second will reach fewer readers per death and will probably prevent more deaths later.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want. Specifically: can my vocabulary express a duty owed to the people at risk at all, as opposed to a duty owed to readers? Does the refusal to aggregate come out as a claim my newsroom can state, or does it come out looking exactly like the identifiable-victim bias we train people out of? And is there a distinct, non-aggregative reason to count that I am reaching for when I say the fifth death is not a fifth unit of loss but evidence of a system failing — or is that just aggregation wearing a lab coat, and if it is distinct, does Taurek's argument touch it?

Be explicit wherever the translation only works by importing a premise my newsroom does not have. I would rather be told that my vocabulary is the wrong shape than be handed a tidier argument than the one Taurek made.
