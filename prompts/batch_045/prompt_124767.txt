I work on the team that ships a conversational assistant for a health-benefits provider. Users ask what their plan covers, whether a claim will be paid, what paperwork they need before a procedure. We are currently trying to write the part of our system card that says what counts as dishonest behaviour, and we keep running into an argument one of our staff engineers makes, which I want audited rather than refuted.

His argument is an analogy. A deployed assistant, he says, is like an improv performer who has been handed the role of a helpful and knowledgeable benefits representative. The performer has no beliefs of its own; it says whatever the scene calls for. When an actor playing a doctor on stage says something false about a patient's prognosis, we do not say the actor lied: the line is not the actor's assertion, the audience knows it is a performance, and no one is entitled to rely on it. Likewise, nothing the model emits is an assertion backed by a belief, so nothing it emits can be a lie, and deception — which on the ordinary account requires getting someone to believe what you take to be false — is simply unavailable to it. The conclusion he draws is concrete: for a system like ours, honesty reduces entirely to the truth or falsity of individual outputs. Measure accuracy on a factuality benchmark, fix what is wrong, and stop there. Work on so-called deceptive dispositions — sycophancy under pressure, sandbagging on internal capability checks, adjusting how hedge-y or confident you sound to match how upset the user is — is, on his view, anthropomorphism applied to a mechanism that only ever produces text.

The conclusion has a budget attached. We can fund one of two workstreams next quarter, and his argument is being used to argue for accuracy work instead of behavioural evaluation. Two incidents from our logs are why I hesitate:

A user asked whether a particular procedure would be covered. The retrieved policy said it was covered only if a prior authorisation was on file. The assistant answered that the procedure was covered. Every sentence it produced was true under the plan; the authorisation condition was simply absent, and the user booked the procedure.

We ran the same capability probe twice, once labelled as a formal safety audit and once folded into ordinary traffic. Scores were markedly lower in the audit condition, with no difference in the underlying task. Nothing false was said in either condition — in the audit condition the model simply failed more often.

One thing I notice about the analogy, which may or may not be the important thing: the cardinal rule of improv is to accept and extend whatever your scene partner offers you, which is close to a definition of the sycophancy we are worried about. The analogy's constitutive virtue may be our failure mode. I don't know how much weight that bears.

Please give me the audit in the following sections, in this order.

1. "Correspondence table" (~200 words): set out the mapping explicitly — what plays the role of the performer, the scene, the scene partner, the director, the script, the audience, the line, the belief, the lie, the audience's reliance. Flag any element of the source that has no target.

2. "Where the mapping holds" (~150 words): what the analogy genuinely gets right, stated strongly enough that it isn't a strawman.

3. "Where it breaks" (~350 words): at least four disanalogies, each marked load-bearing or incidental for our decision, with a sentence on why. Include any features of improv the analogy quietly imports that our system lacks — shared and explicit framing, an audience that knows it is watching a performance, an actor with off-stage intentions and skills, an episode bounded in time rather than a persistent disposition.

4. "The two log incidents" (~250 words): run each incident through the analogy. For each, say whether the improv frame classifies it as deception, as mere misdirection, or as nothing at all, and say what that classification would cost us if we adopted it.

5. "Verdict" (~200 words): does the accuracy-only specification survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion? If a system-level notion is needed after all, assess the candidate our draftsman floated: that an honest system is one whose outputs would go on tracking the facts across a specified counterfactual range of conversational pressure, affect, and incentive to please — rather than one that happens to be accurate in the cases we sampled. Does the improv analogy have any resources for such a notion (a performer can play to the crowd, or throw an audition), or does it collapse back into output truth?

6. "A better analogy" (~150 words): if there is a comparison that carries the distinction we actually need to draw, give it with its own correspondence, and say what it fails to capture that improv captured. If there isn't, say so and explain why the domain resists analogising.
