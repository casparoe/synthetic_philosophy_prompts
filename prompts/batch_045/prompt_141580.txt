I am a third-year computer science student, currently enrolled in a seminar on agency and AI safety, and I work roughly fifteen hours a week as a research assistant in a campus lab that builds language-model agents for multi-step office tasks: booking rooms, triaging a shared inbox, drafting and sending routine correspondence on someone's behalf. I also run the weekly annotation shift, which means that in practice I am the person who decides, on a Monday morning with four other undergraduates waiting for instructions, how a new batch of tasks is going to be supervised.

The seminar has given me two ideal types that I can state but not use. In a goal-directed setup we write down an objective or a reward, let the agent optimize against it, and inherit robustness wherever the objective is right and trouble wherever it is wrong: specification gaming, proxy-chasing, pressure to acquire leverage over whatever measures the objective. In an approval-directed setup we do not try to state what we want; we state a procedure for getting a designated approver to endorse the agent's action in context, and the agent is trained and selected to produce actions that approver would approve of, given what that approver can actually see. This buys us something when the goal is hard to state, and it costs us the approver's myopia and blind spots, which become part of the target rather than being corrected by it. My supervisor adds a third consideration I find persuasive but vague: an approval target is cheap to revise later, since we can rewrite the approver's instructions, while an outcome target requires rebuilding the verifier — so when we expect our own understanding of a task to change, the choice is partly a bet on which mistake is cheaper to undo.

What I do not have is any way to make this choice per task, and I have to make it ten to fifteen times a term. The constraints are: about twenty minutes per decision; an audience of undergraduates with one hour of training and no theory; a total annotator budget of twenty hours a week, so anything that requires dense human judgment per action is expensive; and a graduate-student mentor who will sign off on a one-page form but will not sit in the room. Decisions are revisited at the end of term, if at all.

Please write me the decision procedure I should be running. I want the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a no, where it terminates in "use both, with the outcome as the objective and approval as a constraint," and where it has to hand the question to a person because it is a question about values rather than facts — those hand-offs should be explicit and should say who decides. I would like it grounded in the distinctions above: the gap between what an approver can see and what actually matters downstream; the difference between a task being hard and its outcome being cheaply and non-gameably checkable; whether a less-informed second approver would rank the same candidates the same way, or whether the signal is mostly style; and whether the people bearing the consequences of a bad action are represented among the people doing the approving. Where my supervisor's reversibility point belongs, put it in; where it is a red herring, say so.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show the form filled in.

1. A calendar agent that negotiates meeting times and sends rescheduling emails. We have already collected six weeks of approval data on this and it is the lab's main demo.
2. A support-ticket triage agent for the department's help desk, where closure without reopening within forty-eight hours is logged automatically and could serve as the outcome signal.
3. An agent that drafts written feedback on project proposals for the seminar I am taking; the course staff are interested in piloting it, and my instinct is that this is the clearest possible case for approval-based supervision, since there is no ground truth to optimize.

I want at least one of these to come out in a way I will not like, and I want you to say plainly if that case is the calendar agent, given the annotation time already sunk into it. Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do: which failure modes it will be blind to, which tasks it should never be applied to, and which of its steps are judgment calls written in the grammar of a checklist.
