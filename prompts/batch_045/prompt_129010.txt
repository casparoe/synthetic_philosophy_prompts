I think I built an argument that shows the doctrine of double effect is secretly consequentialist, and I can't find the broken step. Help.

(1) DDE: you may φ only if (a) φ is the sort of act that's independently permissible, (b) your end is good and the harm you foresee is neither your end nor your means to it, and (c) the good is proportionate to the foreseen harm.
(2) Nothing in (1) cares whether the mechanism is a bodily movement or a not-doing — the conditions are about your end, your foresight, and the balance. So run omissions through the same test. (Motivation: if the intended/foreseen distinction is doing the work people say it does, it can't matter whether the death you see coming arrives by your hand or by your not moving.)
(3) Diverting in the 5-vs-1 trolley case clears all three. Everyone agrees.
(4) So not diverting has to clear them too: the point of not diverting is that the one lives, and what you foresee is five deaths. Five deaths for one life isn't proportionate. So not diverting is forbidden, i.e. diverting is obligatory.
(5) Now the hospital case. The surgeon leaves the vagrant alone. The point of that is the vagrant's life; what he foresees is five deaths. Also disproportianate. So the surgeon is required to cut him up.
(6) That's insane, and it's the exact opposite of what DDE was supposed to deliver.
(7) So (1) or (2) is false.

Here's the bit that makes me think the trouble is worse than just (2). Make the switch rest the other way: the points are already set so the trolley will go onto the one-person track, and diverting it back onto the five is a plain bodily action whose foreseen effect is five deaths and whose point is sparing the one. DDE now says don't touch it. In the ordinary version, where you have to throw the switch to divert, DDE says diverting is permitted (not required). Same people, same deaths, same agent, same foresight — differs only in which way the hardware happens to sit. So either DDE manufactures a duty where everyone hears only a permission, or its verdicts are hostage to a baseline.

I'd like to keep (1) — my chapter is built on it — and I really don't want to reach for the doing/allowing distinction, since I've got a section arguing it does no independent work. Which step do you actually reject, why does it look innocent, and what's the cheapest thing for me to give up? If the answer is clause (b) or (c), tell me which reformulation still gets (3) right without giving me (5).
