Here is where I have landed. I am writing these up as the core commitments of a chapter on moral worth, and I want to know whether the package is jointly tenable before I build an argument on it.

1. Worth tracks motives, not merely outcomes. An action has moral worth only if the agent acted, at least in part, because of the considerations that make the action right. I am not requiring that duty be the sole or dominant motive, and I am happy to allow mixed motives; I am only denying that conformity to duty, however reliable, is enough.

2. The difference-making test. An agent acts for a reason R only if R is counterfactually difference-making for what she does: had R not obtained, with her other motives and the rest of the situation held fixed, she would not have acted as she did. This is my account of what acting for a reason consists in, and I am relying on it throughout.

3. A verdict on symmetric overdetermination. A nurse reports a colleague's medication error. She does it both because it is the right thing to do and because she wants to cause trouble for someone she dislikes. Either motive on its own would have been fully sufficient; she is not the sort of person who needs both. I want to say her report has full moral worth, and specifically that the presence of an independently sufficient bad motive does not reduce the worth of the action by one degree.

4. Gradability. Moral worth comes in degrees. Two agents who both act on the right reason can differ in how much moral worth their actions have, and it is worth having a theory that can say how much.

5. Anti-luck. The moral worth of an action cannot vary with anything that lies outside the agent. Two agents cannot differ in moral worth solely in virtue of a difference in their situations, in what other people happened to do, or in what would have happened had things gone otherwise.

6. A verdict on the whistleblowers. Wanda leaks because it is right, but she would have stayed silent had leaking not also advanced her career. Walter leaks for the same reason and would have leaked at real cost to himself. In the actual case the two acted alike and acted on the same reason. I am nonetheless strongly inclined to say Wanda's action has less moral worth than Walter's, and I want to keep this verdict.

7. Motivational opacity. Agents are unreliable reporters of their own motives, introspection is systematically subject to self-serving misattribution, and there is no procedure available to agents or to observers that settles, in a given case, what someone acted for.
