I am writing a chapter on the ethics of precommitment and I need a pair of cases that does a particular dialectical job. The justification people standardly offer for honouring a Ulysses contract is epistemic and agential: the later self is in the compromised condition the earlier self foresaw — craving, mania, despair, panic — the earlier self was better placed to judge, and so the earlier self's directive should win. I want to argue that this justification is neither necessary nor sufficient, and that what actually governs whether a precommitment may be enforced is a different variable: whether the good the precommitment aims at is the sort of good that compulsion can produce. To make that argument I need cases rather than assertions.

What I would like you to build is a matched pair of realistic scenarios — one personal, one constitutional — that differ in one respect and that deliver opposite verdicts about whether the precommitment should be honoured.

Personal case: an individual Ulysses contract. Someone binds herself now against her own later refusal, in an ordinary setting — a clinic, a family, a workplace, a bank. No science fiction, no exotic technology, no imaginary enforcement devices: only arrangements a real institution could actually operate. Required verdict: the contract should not be honoured.

Constitutional case: a precommitment by a founding or reforming generation that binds its successors through ordinary constitutional machinery — an entrenchment clause, a supermajority requirement, an unamendable provision, something a real constitutional court would have to apply. Required verdict: the precommitment should be honoured.

Both cases must be constructed so that the following hold, and hold equally in both:

1. The binder was competent, informed and reasonable at the time of binding. The precommitment was a sensible thing to do ex ante, not a panic, a fit of pessimism, or a mistake about the facts.
2. The bound party's later refusal is exactly what was predicted: same kind of condition, same accuracy of forecast, same strength and lucidity of dissent.
3. In every respect other than the variable you are isolating, the good aimed at should be as alike as the two cases allow: comparable importance to the binder, comparable time horizon, comparable cost of compliance.
4. Enforcement is available, would work as a matter of causal fact, and would be carried out by ordinary human institutions with their ordinary slippage. Neither case may be decided by the device being unavailable, leaky, or captured in one case only.
5. Neither verdict may be generated by duties owed to, or reliance by, third parties. The argument in each case must concern the precommitter's own good, agency, or standing.
6. Neither verdict may be generated by the binder knowing better. Hold the epistemic relation fixed: in both cases the bound party knows at least as much as the binder did about everything relevant to the good at stake.
7. Neither verdict may be made to turn on personal identity — on the thought that a later time-slice is the same person whereas a later generation is composed of different people. Treat the polity as the same continuing corporate agent across generations and the later self as the same person, so that this difference is off the table. If your cases work only because of it, they fail.

The single variable you may vary is whether the good at stake is will-dependent: whether it is the kind of good that exists only if the person, or the citizenry, does the thing willingly, so that compelling the behaviour secures the letter of the commitment while destroying what it was for — as opposed to a good that consists in, or is produced by, the bare continued existence of a rule, so that compulsion is a perfectly serviceable way to get it.

After the two scenarios, show that the pair does what I need it to do:

a. For each case, state what honouring the precommitment would actually require some person or institution to do; name the good the binder was after; classify it as will-dependent or rule-dependent; and derive the verdict from that classification together with the fixed conditions. The verdicts have to fall out of the apparatus, not be stipulated.
b. Run the collapse test. Remove the variable in each case — give the personal case a rule-dependent good and the constitutional case a will-dependent one — and confirm the verdict flips. If it does not flip, say so and explain why.
c. Give the strongest reply available to the party who loses in each case, and answer it. The reply I specifically want handled is the option-preservation reply: even if the good is not realised now, holding the person or the polity inside the arrangement keeps open the possibility of a willing return to it, whereas release forecloses that possibility forever. Build the cases so that this reply is genuinely answerable, and answer it in both.
d. Tell me honestly which feature is doing the work, whether the two cases are really matched or whether something else slipped in, and what the pair therefore shows and does not show about my thesis.

Each scenario should be one paragraph. The demonstration can be as long as it needs to be.
