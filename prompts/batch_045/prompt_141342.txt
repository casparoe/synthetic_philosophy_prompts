Hey — I'm putting together a set of eval prompts on decision theory, and for this one I want something that a model cant just answer by reciting the standard Newcomb setup, so here's a design job instead of a question.

Build me a Newcomb problem with no predictor in it. Not "the predictor is only 90% accurate" — literally no agent, no simulation, no device that represents you, no intentional vocabulary anywhere in the causal story. The correlation between what you choose and what's in the box has to come entirely from a common cause. Constraints: one paragraph, ordinary stakes (money, a job, an inheritance, whatever), no time travel, no quantum stuff, correlation strong but not perfect — say roughly 0.9 — and you have to state how the correlation is actually generated and give me real numbers for the payoffs, not "huge" and "trivial". It's going to be a discussion opener for a reading group that's already sick of the boxes-and-perfect-predictor version, which is why cheating matters here: a case that just renames the predictor counts as a failure.

The hard part, and the thing that makes it worth doing, is that the case has to pass two tests that pull against each other.

(1) The lesion test. If I swap your common cause for a brain lesion that causes both the choice and the payoff, the intuitive verdict should flip. In your case it should be obvious that you take the one box; in the lesion version it should be obvious that it makes no difference what you do. If the verdict doesn't move between the two, you've built a lesion case wearing a hat.

(2) The deflation test. Strip every agential and intentional word out of your description of the mechanism — predicts, expects, believes, reads, models, is confident about — and check whether the correlation is still there and still doing the same work. If it evaporates, you sneaked the predictor back in through the scenery.

Verdict requirements: evidential decision theory says one-box, causal decision theory says two-box, and the disagreement has to fall out of the case rather than be stipulated by how you describe what the agent knows.

After the paragraph: state each theory's verdict with the actual reasoning — the partition over states, the probabilities, which term in the expected-value calculation is the one that differs — then say precisely which single feature of your mechanism is keeping the case on the right side of both tests, show what happens to both verdicts when you delete just that feature, and then give the strongest attack a causal decision theorist has. Not the lazy one where they just assert their own theory; the one where they argue your case only looks like a Newcomb problem because of how you framed the agent's evidence. Then tell me definately whether that attack actually lands or whether you can answer it.

Start straight in on the scenario — first line is the setup itself, no preamble, no "here's a case", no restating what I asked for — and for every bit of analysis after it, lead with the verdict and justify it afterwards.
