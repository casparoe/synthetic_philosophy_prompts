I work as a story editor on factual drama — the sort of thing that carries a caption about being inspired by real events — and my partner is an archivist, so the two of us have been arguing, on and off for about two years, about what exactly we owe the real dead when we turn them into characters. I have got into the habit of writing down my verdicts on individual cases rather than defending general principles, and I would now like to know whether there is a principle behind them, because I suspect that at least two of them cannot both survive.

The verdicts, as I have them. A composite character who stands in for a whole team of scientists in a miniseries about a nuclear accident: fine, provided the programme says so somewhere. Reconstructed dialogue for a meeting that certainly happened but whose words were never minuted: fine. Moving a real person into a room they were not in, so that two documented events can share a scene: fine, that feels like punctuation. An invented love affair between two real people who certainly knew each other: fine, with a bad conscience. The same invented affair presented in a realist docudrama that will be most viewers' only source on the pair: not fine. A fabricated specific act of cruelty — a real person shown doing something vile that they did not do, though they did do other vile things of that kind, so the scene is representative: not fine, and worse than the invented affair, and I cannot say why. Depicting a real person as having committed a crime they were tried for and acquitted of: not fine, and worse again. Depicting a real person as having had a secret sexual relationship contrary to the sexual identity they avowed in public and around which they organised their life: not fine — but that is, on the face of it, the very same act as the invented affair I called fine, and I cannot get the two verdicts to line up. Inventing a cruel act about a Roman emperor who spent his life courting fame: I barely mind. Inventing the same act about a private person who died two centuries ago, never sought notice, and left no descendants and no community that remembers her: I mind a great deal — and since neither of them can be harmed, and no living person is distressed in either case, I do not know what my reaction is even tracking. And last: a biopic of a memoirist who spent his life publishing a self-aggrandising and largely false account of himself, which invents scenes in order to puncture the myth — fine, and I am not sure whether that is because the subject forfeited something by lying or because the invention there is restorative rather than accusatory.

The three candidate principles I have been trying out are these. First, a structural one: what matters is not how many facts are changed but where in the narrative's causal architecture they sit — inventions that decorate are permissible, while inventions that supply the motives for the person's actual deeds, or that become the reason the audience thinks she did what she did, are falsifications of the person. Second, counterfactual consent: would this person, judging by the values she actually held and avowed, have accepted this depiction of herself — where the relevant question is not whether she would have enjoyed it but whether the depiction is one she could have recognised as hers. Third, an epistemic one about the audience: the wrong is a function of how likely the work is to become somebody's settled belief about the person, so it tracks genre, publicity, and whether a rival account is in circulation. Please test these against my verdicts, repair them or replace them, and tell me honestly which verdicts I have to give up: I would much rather have a principle that overturns one or two of them than a principle with a list of exceptions as long as the list of cases. Then I want it applied to the case I go back and forth on. A documentary about a writer who died in the 1990s, made with the co-operation of her estate, uses a voice model trained on her archived interviews to read aloud, in her own voice, passages from her private diaries — sentences she wrote, in her own hand, but never spoke and never published — and presents this as her reading them, with a caption explaining the synthesis. I have felt both that this is the most respectful possible use of an archive and that it is a violation unlike any of the others, and I cannot tell which of those thoughts is the sentimental one. And please say what your account predicts about a case I have not listed: say, a drama about a musician who died three years ago, made against the wishes of her adult children, which depicts a documented episode of which they are ashamed.
