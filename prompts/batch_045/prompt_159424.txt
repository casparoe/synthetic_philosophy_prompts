I got into an argument at a meetup last weekend over a hypothetical I've been turning over ever since, and I'd like a cleaner verdict than the one we reached. The setup: a very large, well-run study follows every child born in a single country in a single year — call it 200,000 of them. They attend schools that are close to identical in funding and quality by that country's standards, none of them are malnourished, and none are exposed to anything a reasonable person would call deprivation. At sixteen they all sit the same exam, and an ordinary twin-and-family design on this cohort returns a heritability of 0.7 for exam performance. A separate DNA-only estimate computed from common genetic markers in the same cohort returns 0.3. Someone at the meetup then said that, given the 0.7, most of the gap between this country's average score and a much poorer country's average score must be genetic. I said that doesn't follow. He said the number would look basically the same if you ran the study in the poorer country. I said that doesn't follow either. We then spent an hour generating increasingly baroque versions of the same disagreement, and I came away able to recite every standard caution — variance versus means, populations versus individuals, within-group versus between-group — without being able to say which caution actually bites on which inference. So I want the cautions cashed out as numbers rather than slogans.

Here is the scaffolding I've started using, which you should adopt provisionally and then tell me at the end whether it is earning its keep. I think people are conflating three parameters. First, the ratio parameter: the quantity actually being estimated, the share of phenotypic variance in this population across this range of environments that is attributable to genetic differences — a ratio of variances, not a decomposition of any individual, and always carrying a suppressed subscript for the population and its environment. Second, what I've started calling transport weight: the degree to which the ordering of genotypes by phenotype survives a specified change of environment, i.e. how badly the reaction norms cross when you move the population elsewhere. Third, mean-shift headroom: how far a specified intervention could move the population mean, which is a question about means and, as far as I can tell, close to orthogonal to any variance ratio. My working hypothesis is that nearly every bad heritability inference is an inference that quietly substitutes transport weight or mean-shift headroom for the ratio parameter, and that the ratio parameter on its own licenses far less than people want from it.

So: here are eight claims, all of which I have seen made or half-made with a straight face, and I want you to rate each on a 0-to-100 scale for how much of the inferential work the 0.7 genuinely does. Read the scale as: starting from someone who knows only that exam performance varies and is somewhat predictable from family background, how much of the distance from there to full belief in this claim does the estimate actually cover? Then sort them into tiers — roughly, licensed, underdetermined, actively blocked — with one sentence each naming which of my three parameters the claim really needs and whether the 0.7 speaks to that parameter at all.

1. For any given child in this cohort, about 70% of his score is due to his genes and about 30% to his environment.
2. Most of the difference between this country's average score and a much poorer country's average score is genetic.
3. Further equalizing school quality within this country would not accomplish much, because there is hardly any environmental variance left to work with.
4. The 0.3 from the DNA-only estimate and the 0.7 from the family design are estimates of the same quantity, so the gap between them is mostly sampling noise and rare variants, and should close as samples grow.
5. Because parents in this country partly sort on the traits that predict exam performance, the 0.7 is not the number you would obtain in a randomly mating population — and the direction of that discrepancy is knowable in advance rather than being a rounding error.
6. Some of what the design books as genetic is really environmental: a child's genetically influenced behaviour evokes different treatment from teachers and parents, and that causal pathway ends up scored on the genetic side of the ledger.
7. Heritability concerns variance within a population and says nothing about the mean, so there is no tension between a heritability of 0.7 for this trait and a cohort whose average score shifts substantially in a single generation.
8. Heritability is a property of the trait, so once it is pinned down here it can be carried over to other cohorts, other countries and other decades as a fixed background fact.

Three follow-ups, please. Steelman whichever claim you rate lowest — I want the best version of it, not the version that is easiest to dismiss. Then, if you had to collapse my three parameters into two, or add a fourth, say which and why; I would rather find out the scaffolding is wrong than have it flatter me. Finally, one number between 0 and 100: how informative is the bare fact that h² = 0.7 in this cohort, considered in itself, for the decision whether to fund an intervention aimed at raising exam performance? Defend the number. And please don't hand me citations, author names or reading lists — I'm not going to chase them down, and here they would function as borrowed prestige rather than as argument; I want the reasoning, not the pedigree.
