ok so I need a poem, and it's basically a last-ditch move in an argument I am currently losing. Context: last night me and my roommate — she's a cog sci PhD student, very into predictive processing, thinks most philosophy of mind is vibes — got into about a three-hour fight about Dennett's four kinds of minds, you know, the tower of generate-and-test: Darwinian creatures with everything wired in and selection doing the learning across generations, Skinnerian creatures who can be shaped by reward and punishment inside a single lifetime, Popperian creatures who let a hypothesis die in their head instead of dying in the world, and Gregorian creatures (named after Richard Gregory) who get their foresight by borrowing thinking tools — words, pictures, gadgets — that other minds already paid for. Her position is that the tower just stops there: everything after that is "culture," more tools in an already-Gregorian head, not a new *kind* of mind. My position is that something structurally new happens once the testing itself gets outsourced to institutions that outlive the tester — labs, archives, peer review, markets, code repositories, credit bureaus — so that a mind can act on forecasts whose grounds no single head contains, and whose errors get corrected by nobody in particular. I started calling this the Alexandrian creature, after the library. She said that's not a mind, that's an institution, and a person standing in a library is still just a Gregorian with better scaffolding. So: the poem has to earn the fifth one, or it should drop it.

Act as both a working philosopher of mind and someone who actually writes formal verse — real metre, not line-broken prose — and think carefully before you commit. Start with a short brainstorm: give me two or three candidate linking lines and one sentence each on what that line would make the poem do, then pick one and write the thing, then in three or four sentences tell me which line in each section carries the philosophical weight and where your version is quietly cheating. The form I want: five sections of six lines, loose iambic tetrameter, ABABCC, and — this is the bit that has to do the work — the last line of each section is repeated verbatim as the first line of the next, and the last line of section five loops back to the first line of section one, so the tower is literally cumulative and ends where it began. No headings, no labels, no footnotes: someone who knows the taxonomy should be able to say which section is which and why they come in that order, and someone who has never heard of Dennett should still just think it's a good poem. I'm going to read it aloud to close the last session of a graduate reading group I TA for (they spent the term on this stuff) and then send it to her as my closing argument, so it needs to work out loud.

Content constraints, section by section: (1) Darwinian — no verbs of learning, trying, practicing, or discovering anywhere in it; the wisdom is wired in and the cost of being wrong is paid in bodies. (2) Skinnerian — real trial and error out in the world, something gets rewarded or punished, and the creature itself is different afterward. (3) Popperian — the death has to happen inside the head, something that could have been an action becomes a thought instead, and the animal walks away. (4) Gregorian — it has to use at least one thing it did not invent and could not have invented, borrowed from another mind, and it should be clear that the borrowing is where the foresight comes from. (5) Alexandrian, mine — somebody acts on a forecast they cannot reconstruct, the grounds are distributed, nobody in particular fixes the mistake, and the turn should be that the new vulnerability is not being wrong but not being able to say why you're right; let the closing couplet stay open on whether that's a fifth kind of mind or Gregorian scaffolding all the way down, and lean whichever way the poem actually earns rather than toward my side. Please don't use the words "meme" or "algorithm," don't rhyme mind with find, and don't give me a final stanza that patly announces that culture is the answer.
