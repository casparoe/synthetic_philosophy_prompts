I have no academic affiliation — I work in IT project management and have been reading about federalism on my own for a couple of years, ever since I got pulled into a local argument about who should be setting school curricula in my state and discovered that the same argument is being had, in different vocabulary, all over the place. A small foundation that funds unaffiliated independent researchers has invited me to apply for a nine-month fellowship, and they want a two-page proposal (plus a short budget note). They say explicitly that they would rather fund one sharply posed conceptual question than a survey, and that they will notice if the question is fuzzy.

Here is my half-formed idea. Almost every formulation of subsidiarity I run into seems to presuppose a negative reading: subsidiarity as a reason for the higher level to keep its hands off. The EU test asks whether the objectives of a proposed action cannot be sufficiently achieved by the member states but can be better achieved at Union level; the German Basic Law makes the federation's use of its concurrent powers turn on whether federal legislation is necessary (Erforderlichkeit), a test the constitutional court mostly declines to police; American arguments tend to be about enumerated powers, about conditions attached to federal money, and about whether states can be conscripted into administering federal schemes. But the older Catholic-social usage the word comes out of is about subsidium in the sense of aid or help: the higher body exists to assist the lower unit to do for itself what it ought to do, which on occasion means the higher level must act — fund, train, set a floor, build administrative capacity — precisely so that the lower unit becomes genuinely able to act. Call these the abstention reading and the enablement reading. My suspicion is that a lot of talking past each other is explained by participants implicitly holding one or the other, and that the two can come apart in practice. There is also something dynamic I cannot get the standard tests to see: competences can be retained on paper while being hollowed out in fact, because the standards, the money, and the expertise have drifted upward. At that point the abstention reading's question of whether the lower unit could do the job has a self-fulfilling answer, and the enablement reading will license interventions that look, by the standard test, like straightforward centralization. So I have been toying with a counterfactual-capacity test: not could the lower unit do this adequately now, but could it do it after a realistic interval of actually being the one responsible, and what would have to be invested to make that true — which suggests the right answer can flip depending on the time horizon you choose, and that a principle silent about that horizon is indeterminate.

What I need from you is the proposal itself, about 1,200 words, in American English, written for a reviewer who is not a specialist in comparative constitutional law. (2) say why the existing answers leave it open — the necessity and better-achieved tests, the argument that federalism is adequately protected by the political process rather than by doctrine, the view that subsidiarity is a procedural or burden-of-proof rule rather than a substantive one; (3) set out a method for nine months of part-time unaffiliated work, say six to eight hours a week, English plus slow German, no archives, no interview program, no travel, stated month by month, and say why that method rather than the two obvious alternatives of a full four-system legal comparison or a purely normative argument from first principles; (4) describe a concrete deliverable, which I imagine as a decision procedure someone could actually apply to a new competence in order to decide what level it should sit at, together with two or three worked policy domains — I was thinking school standards, pandemic response, and climate targets — run through the machinery of all four systems (the American division of enumerated and reserved powers, Germany's concurrent-powers catalogue and the Länder's direct voice in federal legislation through the upper chamber, Switzerland's reworked division of tasks between Confederation and cantons with the cantons doing most of the implementing under fiscal equalization, and the EU's subsidiarity test with its national-parliament early-warning mechanism);
