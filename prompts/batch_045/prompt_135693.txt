I'm in the upper sixth and I help run my school's Philosophy of Science reading group — it's the advanced stream, and this term we've worked through Popper on falsification, Kuhn on normal science and paradigm shift, and Lakatos's attempt to steer between them. The group is examined at the end of term by a single timed essay: ninety minutes, twelve hundred words, twenty-five marks. The papers are marked by me and two other sixth-formers against a shared rubric, with our supervising teacher only settling disputes where we disagree. So I need the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme tight enough that three markers who are not experts would land on roughly the same number.

The difficulty I keep running into is that every Lakatos essay title I can find can be passed by writing out the vocabulary — hard core, negative and positive heuristic, protective belt, progressive versus degenerating problemshift — and then sorting some famous episode from the history of science into the boxes. I want a question where that manoeuvre isn't available, so I'd rather build it around a case that isn't in the literature at all and that candidates have to work on rather than recall. Below is a case I've invented; use it, and add one further detail if you think the crux needs it (tell me what you added).

The Selene Programme, in the physiology of sleep. Its hard core: during waking the brain accumulates a single blood-borne sleep-promoting substance; sleep just is the clearance of that substance; nothing else causes sleep onset. In the 1920s and 30s it predicts that transfusing fluid from a sleep-deprived animal into a rested one will send the recipient to sleep — corroborated, dramatically, in several species — and that the active factor is heat-stable and of low molecular weight, corroborated by fractionation. In the 1950s it predicts a hypothalamic sleep centre and that lesioning it produces insomnia; the prediction is corroborated, though the lesion work is later confounded, since the lesions were large and the effect is now attributed to damage to neighbouring arousal pathways. From the 1960s to the 1980s the substance is never isolated; every purification yields several active fractions. The belt is extended four times: there are several such substances rather than one; they act redundantly, so removing one changes nothing; the substance is produced in the gut and modified in the liver; and the old transfusion experiments were unreliable because the procedure itself produced the effect. Two genuinely new predictions are extracted from these additions — that double and triple knockouts will abolish sleep, and that a receptor antagonist will produce prolonged waking — and both fail. Meanwhile a rival, the Local–Circadian Programme, treats sleep as a property of local cell assemblies rather than of a circulating substance, predicts local sleep-like activity in cortical regions of an awake, behaving animal (confirmed in the late 1980s), and re-explains the old transfusion result as an artefact of the procedure; but it has not yet predicted anything new of its own, and it has no account of why lesions in the old locations produced insomnia. It is 1996. The Research Council must decide now whether to renew the Selene Programme's ten-year funding block. Its defenders say every mature programme passes through a degenerating phase and the rival has not earned the right to replace them. Its critics say the belt has been patched so often that nothing could now count against the hard core, and that the patches have stopped paying for themselves.

I want the question to force these five things, and I'd rather it force them than name them: (1) that candidates actually apply the apparatus to the case with discrimination — saying which belt changes were content-increasing and which merely protective, and why several of them might be both; (2) that they face the fact that a verdict of degeneration seems to be available only retrospectively, since Lakatos allows that a programme in trouble may revive, so an appraisal of episodes is not yet a decision rule for people standing inside one; (3) that whether this is one programme patched four times, a sequence of programmes, or one programme whose hard core silently changed from one substance to several flips the verdict, and that on Lakatos's own terms what counts as the hard core is settled by convention, so the framework can't referee that; (4) that since there is no abandonment merely because of anomalies, the Council's question becomes whether the rival has absorbed the Selene Programme's corroborated excess content, and Lakatos supplies no threshold for that and no guidance about splitting resources while the rival is still embryonic; (5) that if tenacity is in principle always potentially rational, the methodology threatens to license whatever scientists were going to do anyway, and a candidate should say what a non-question-begging reply to that would have to look like.

Deliverables, please. First, the question itself: one title, plus the instructions to candidates, pitched at the standard of a strong first-year university exam in philosophy of science. Second, a model answer of about a thousand words that takes a definite line on the 1996 decision rather than surveying options, and that contains at least two claims a well-prepared candidate could reasonably contest. Third, a marking scheme: twenty-five marks across five or six named moves with the marks each is worth; an explicit statement of what separates an A answer (eighteen or above) from a B (fourteen to seventeen), naming the specific move the A makes and the B doesn't; and, for each move, one line on the typical error — I especially want the scheme to penalise the candidate who calls every patch simply ad hoc without asking what ad hoc can mean without hindsight, the candidate who concludes that Lakatos gives no guidance at all, and the candidate who treats a degenerating programme as a refuted theory, which is the Popperian mistake Lakatos was arguing against. Finally, flag the two places where a good candidate might reasonably disagree with your own model answer, and say how markers should credit that.

Two constraints. The exam is closed-book with no notes and no library, so nothing in the question or the model answer may depend on naming papers, dates or textual details; justify everything from the case and from the architecture of Lakatos's position as it is normally taught. If some move needs a detail I'd have to look up, tell me rather than building on it — I don't want to discover in the marking that half the group wrote down a sentence I got wrong. Relatedly: our teacher mentioned in passing that Lakatos has some fallback about intellectual honesty for scientists who stay in a degenerating programme. I don't know whether that's a real feature of his position or a half-memory, and I can't check it in the exam hall. If it is real, I'd like the model answer to use it and to say why it still can't settle the Council's decision; if it isn't, say so plainly and tell me what to put in its place.
