Last night, over a very long dinner, I argued with a friend — a structural biologist — about whether DNA is really a code, and we got nowhere. She kept saying that the codon table is a mapping and mappings are codes, and that the rest is word-spinning; I kept saying that a mapping is only a code if something can go wrong relative to it, and I could not get her to say what goes wrong when a transfer RNA mischarges beyond the fact that we dislike the result. I think we were answering different questions, but I cannot yet say which.

So what I want is not a verdict but an instrument: a taxonomy of the positions on this question, built as a tree in which every branch is labelled with the claim that separates the leaves beneath it, so that I can locate both of us in the logical space and see what we were actually disputing. The audience is a mixed reading group of philosophers and working biologists, so the register should be plain but not condescending.

How I would like it built.

Work from the logical space first, and only then put names on things. Start from the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and afterwards situate the actual positions in that space, leaving the unoccupied combinations standing as empty leaves.

Five to seven branch nodes. Each must be a claim that someone could accept or reject without knowing what any position is called. The distinctions I care about include, though you need not give each its own branch: whether informational talk in molecular biology does explanatory work that could not equally be done by talk of structure, causation and reaction propensities; whether the codon-to-amino-acid assignment is merely not chemically necessitated, or is in addition conventional, in the sense of being held in place by something agreement-like rather than by accident plus inertia; what, if anything, makes a particular assignment the correct one — an actual history of selection, the present dispositions of the translational machinery, the intentions of whoever assembled the system, or nothing beyond the fact that it is what actually happens; whether a code essentially involves a sender and a receiver with something at stake, or whether a purely mechanical reading mechanism will do; whether the notion carries any normativity at all, so that a token can be misread or misexpressed relative to something other than our descriptions, and if so what fixes correctness; and finally whether the whole question is about the terrestrial system as it happens to be or about what any possible genetic system must be like. That last one matters to me, because I suspect my friend was answering the first and I was answering the second.

Cases the branches must be able to sort. Treat each as stipulated rather than as a claim about what anyone has actually built: a molecule assembled by chance in a prebiotic pond that is base-for-base identical to a working gene, with no history behind it whatsoever; a laboratory strain in which a codon has been reassigned wholesale to a non-standard amino acid, so that the same token now recruits something different; a single cell containing two parallel translation systems, one servicing its own chromosomes and one servicing a synthetic element with a different table, so that one and the same molecule is read two ways depending on which machinery gets to it; a hypothetical lineage in which stereochemistry forces a one-to-one codon-to-amino-acid pairing, so that nothing could be reassigned; a gene transferred into a distantly related species where it yields a misfolded, functionless product; a stretch of sequence with no downstream effect at all; an inherited methylation mark, or a prion conformation, that reliably makes a phenotypic difference; and a regulatory sequence switched on only by an environmental toxin, which raises the question whether the genome thereby says anything about the world outside the organism.

Eight to twelve leaves. Give a name only where you are confident a label is standard; otherwise mark the leaf unnamed. Every leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where a combination the branches allow is unoccupied, keep it as an empty leaf and say in a line whether it is empty because incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. One cell I particularly want you to consider, whether or not anyone occupies it, is the view that DNA is a code only relative to an interpreter who has a use for it — us, in the laboratory — so that codehood is a status we confer rather than one the molecule possesses. Where two named positions come apart nowhere in the tree, flag them as inseparable by these questions rather than manufacturing a distinction to separate them.

Then a placement key: six or seven yes-or-no items phrased entirely as verdicts about the cases above, with no item of the form do you think DNA is a code. Show the mapping from answer patterns to leaves, and if some patterns land on no leaf, say so and say what that reveals about the tree.

Finally, a short closing paragraph on which of these splits actually threaten ordinary molecular-biological practice and which leave the day-to-day work untouched.

Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list or any citations, and do not attribute views to particular authors or papers: I want the distinctions themselves, not the literature around them.
