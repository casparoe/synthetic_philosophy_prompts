I'm trying to get clear on a distinction in the dialectic over Frankfurt-style cases and the principle of alternative possibilities, and I keep failing to state it precisely. I'd like your help.

Fix the standard case: an agent performs some action for her own reasons; a monitor, of which she is unaware, would have forced that very action had she been about to decide otherwise; she never approaches that point, so the monitor never fires. Intuitively she is morally responsible. The usual explanation is that the monitor is idle: it makes no contribution to the actual causal history of her action, which is entirely her own, so responsibility is grounded in the actual sequence rather than in the availability of alternatives.

Idleness cannot be the whole story. Consider a threat that is equally idle: a gunman who would have shot her had she not handed over the wallet, and who never fires. There the intervener is just as inert, yet her responsibility seems diminished. So what exactly distinguishes the idle monitor from the idle threat? I can produce the one-line answer myself, namely that the threat figures in her deliberation as a reason while the monitor does not. What I want is that distinction stated at the level that makes the one-liner true. Is the relevant contrast causal, normative, or counterfactual? Must the intervener be a reason for which she acts, or is it enough that it is causally efficacious upstream of her reasons, or merely that she would not have acted as she did had it been absent? Say which, and say why the others fail.

Then use that distinction to diagnose two variants I have been playing with. In the near-trigger variant, everything is as before, the monitor does not fire and she acts for her own reasons, except that her reasons were finely balanced: a small weakening of them would have brought her to the point of deciding otherwise, and the monitor would then have fired. In the slack variant she was settled: no perturbation of her reasons short of a drastic one would have triggered the device. The modal facts are the same in both, since in neither could she have done otherwise. But my sense is that the two pull differently, and I want to know whether your diagnosis predicts that, and what it would mean if it does. In particular, if the intuitive force of these cases tracks how close the agent came to the trigger, then what they isolate may not be the irrelevance of alternative possibilities but the robustness of the actual sequence to perturbation of the agent's own reasons, in which case the leeway theorist has a reply I don't know how to formulate.

Please give me three things.

1. The distinction, stated as sharply as you can, with the rival formulations named and rejected.
2. The diagnosis of the near-trigger and slack variants, including whether the difference in pull is real or an artifact of how the cases are described.
3. The upshot: do these cases refute the principle of alternative possibilities, or only show that the kind of alternative whose absence matters is narrower than the principle's surface form suggests? Test your answer against a third variant in which the monitor blocks only her refraining, leaving her a genuine choice between two actions each of which she could have performed for her own reasons.

Constraints: 600 words maximum, three labelled sections, prose rather than bullet points, no citations or references to the literature, and no appeal to survey data about what ordinary people would say. Where a consideration would only matter on the assumption that determinism is true, flag that explicitly instead of bracketing it silently.
