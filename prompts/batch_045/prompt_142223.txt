I am trying to pin down an interpretive disagreement, and I want a number with its derivation rather than an essay.

The background, from the texts themselves. In the Guide's discussion of the sacrificial cult (III, 32), Maimonides says that "It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed"; that the prevailing worship of the day consisted in sacrificing animals before images in temples devoted to the stars; and that God accordingly "transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal" rather than abolishing the practice. He adds that abolishing it then would have had the effect a prophet would now have who "called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action." And in the Mishneh Torah (Laws of Kings 11:1) he rules that in the messianic era "the observance of all the statutes will return to their previous state. We will offer sacrifices, observe the Sabbatical and Jubilee years according to all their particulars as described by the Torah," in an age when, as he says a few paragraphs later, the messianic king brings all the nations to serve God together.

The claim I want your credence on — call it the counterfactual thesis — is this: on Maimonides' considered view, the commandment of animal sacrifice is explained wholly by Israel's antecedent habituation to sacrificial worship and by nothing about sacrificial worship as such, so that had that habituation been absent, no such commandment would have been given. Treat this as a claim about what his position commits him to rather than about what he would have avowed if asked, and take it as resolved by what the relevant chapters of the Guide, read together with the way the same institution is handled in the legal code, would show about that commitment. I do not assume it is the same as the claim that the cult has no continuing point once the habituation is cured — a tolerated survival can acquire a use — but if you think the two cannot be pulled apart, say so and say what that does to your figure.

I sit at 0.5. A colleague whose judgement I respect sits at 0.85, and I cannot locate where the difference lives. Give me your own figure, decomposed over whatever you think the load-bearing sub-questions are. The ones I would separate: whether the remarks about prevailing idolatrous custom are offered as a complete account of why sacrifice was commanded, or only of why it was commanded in the shape it was — these animals, this sanctuary, these seasons — with the bare institution resting on some other footing; whether the claim about human nature describes a contingent and in principle curable conditioning or a standing feature of embodied creatures, and how much that is settled by his own analogy to prayer and fasting; whether the cult is for him also a genuine instrument for establishing true belief rather than merely a survival; whether the messianic restoration of sacrifice in an age when idolatry has disappeared is evidence against the thesis, or neutral because a renewed commandment needs no concessionary rationale, or neutral because human nature is unchanged; and whether his whole project of giving reasons for the commandments is in the business of causal-historical explanation at all. Add any axis I have left out.

There is one case I have not seen addressed and that I want you to apply: suppose a generation raised from birth under the Mosaic dispensation, with no living memory of idolatrous sacrifice. Ask what the counterfactual thesis predicts about whether the cult would be abrogated for them, whether Maimonides could coherently say that, and how much of your final number rests on your answer. If you think the case is a bad test — because a concessionary rationale can persist as long as anyone alive remembers the old ways, or because a commandment once given is not the sort of thing that lapses — then say that instead, and say how far it lowers your confidence in the test itself.

For each term give a rough probability and one line of justification, then show how they combine; then tell me which single term your number is most sensitive to, which term you are least confident about, and what one piece of textual or argumentative evidence would move you furthest. Close by telling me which of the two of us at 0.5 and 0.85 you take to be the one who has misjudged it, and what exactly the disagreement is about.
