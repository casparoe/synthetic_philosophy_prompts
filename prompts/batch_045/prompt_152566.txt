I help run the neighborhood association for my area — nothing fancy, we meet one evening a month in the basement of a library, usually 20 to 35 people, mostly long-time homeowners, a handful of renters, one guy who does drywall and actually knows what things cost to build. I've got a 90-minute slot coming up and I want to do something other than the usual arguiing about a parking variance.

Here's what I've been chewing on by myself (no background in any of this, I just read a lot and think about it at night): whenever a building with, say, twenty apartments gets proposed on a lot near us, the people who show up are all people who already live here, and the people the building is for are not in the room and don't get a vote. So the meeting almost always outputs no, and it feels principled the whole time, because every single objection is reasonable on its own — traffic, shadows, the school, the sewer line. What I can't get anyone in that room to actually feel is that the outcome is mostly being set by who is allowed a say rather than by anyone's reasons, and that the objections being made would have blocked most of us when we moved in.

So: design a 90-minute activity for that group, on a fictional proposal of your choosing, using only stuff you could find in a library basement (no money, no projector, phones discouraged). Rules, roles or cards, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief. Nobody should hear the words NIMBY or right to the city until the debrief, and there shouldn't be a lecture up front at all.

Please build it around three things I've been messing with. First, the arrival test: any objection has to be restated in a form that would also have applied to the objector when they arrived, and someone has to say out loud whether it would have. Second, a spillover deck: when the room votes a project down, we draw a card saying where those households end up instead, and the cost has to land on someone the room can picture — a named relative of a person sitting there, whatever — so that the cost of no stops being invisible and delayed. Third, run the same decision twice with the franchise changed: round one only current residents vote; round two the households the building is for are represented too, and I want the people who have to argue for them chosen by lot from among the loudest opponents. Tell me honestly if that last bit backfires.

The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the problem, including the move where someone says I'm not against housing, I'm against this project. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it — assume at least one landlord who owns three rentals and two renters who might get priced out, and they already hate each other. And be frank about which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce at all and I'd just have to say out loud. If you can also work in a way to show the gap between what people say in the room and what they'll write down anonymously, do that too — I suspect that gap is half the story.
