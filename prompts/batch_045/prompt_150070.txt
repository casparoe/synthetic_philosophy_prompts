I'm the graduate TA for an upper-level seminar on philosophy of cognitive science — 18 students, mostly philosophy majors with a few psychology and linguistics students in the mix, one 90-minute session in a room with no screens. We've just finished the week on the extended mind: Andy Clark and David Chalmers's 1998 paper "The Extended Mind," the Otto/Inga case, active externalism, and the cluster of conditions they use — roughly, that the resource is constantly and easily available, that what it says is automatically endorsed rather than weighed, and that it has been taken up and trusted over time. Last term I tried a version of this that was just a memory task done first unaided and then with notes, and it taught nobody anything: everyone shrugged, said the notebook obviously isn't part of them, and a psychology major pointed out that all I'd measured was how much people like their own notes. I want a real activity this time. What participants should come away seeing is not that external aids help cognition (nobody denies that) but that the live question is which marks turn reliance into constitution, and that the marks in that standard cluster don't travel together: a resource can be one thing on one test and the opposite on another.

The idea I want staged is something I've been calling the three tests, and I'd like the game built so that students generate cases where they come apart rather than being told that they do. The substitution test: give a participant the same information they encoded, but in a stranger's notation, or in their own notation re-copied by someone else, or as an objectively better and more complete card than theirs — the question is whether the resource still guides them in the same way, and whether "it isn't mine" is doing the work or "it isn't good" is. The tampering test: return their own card with two or three entries silently altered in their own handwriting, and see whether the resulting error is owned ("I was wrong") or externalised ("my card was changed"). The friction test: keep the card but tax it — a retrieval delay, a cost in points per consultation, a partial or scrambled version — and see whether degrading the coupling degrades the sense that the belief was theirs. I want at least one condition that functions as a control against the "you just like your notes" objection, ideally one where sentiment and integration point in opposite directions. Practical constraints: ordinary classroom materials and paper only, no phones or laptops; a clear timeline across the 90 minutes with a build phase in which participants construct their own aid from scratch and can't bring anything in; roles for a few students as registrars who record answer latency, confidence and error-attribution so we have something to debrief with; a points payoff rather than money, with some way for participants to stake confidence so that endorsement strength is measured and not just accuracy; and no upfront lecture, with the words "extended," "parity," "constitution" and "coupled system" not uttered until after the final phase. Since the tampering phase involves deceiving participants, tell me how to handle that properly — what to say beforehand, and how the reveal is folded into the debrief.

Give me the full runnable package: rules, materials and setup, the timeline with minute marks, the payoff scheme, the assignable roles, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific parts of the thesis and its critics — including the objection that causal coupling to an external resource isn't the same as that resource being part of a cognitive system, and the worry that if notebooks and diaries count, there is no principled place to stop before address books, smartphones and the internet. Be honest about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and be frank about which feature of the dispute the activity cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply in discussion myself. Where you're designing under uncertainty, say so: give me the one design choice you're least confident about and the observation during the session that should make me abandon it mid-run and fall back to open discussion, and flag the premise the whole activity rests on — something like the assumption that how people attribute error and ownership is evidence about what their minds are made of — together with what happens to the exercise if that premise is false.
