I do HVAC for a living so I deal with practical science all day but I've never taken a philosophy class in my life, and I got into it with my brother-in-law Dale at a barbeque a couple weeks ago — he's a chemist, works in coatings or something I never really follow. He said something like "of course science tells us what's actually out there, that's the whole point of it," and I said I wasn't so sure, because it seems like every generation's science has turned out to be wrong about the deep stuff. We argued while the kids ran through the sprinkler, I wrote my argument down when I got home, and now that I read it back I think it proves way too much and I can't see which step to throw out:

(1) If a way of forming beliefs is reliable, then most of the beliefs it handed us in the past should still look true to us now — or at least close to true.
(2) The way science forms beliefs is basically "guess the best explanation for what you can observe," and that method handed us a long list of stuff we now think is just false: the luminiferous aether, phlogiston, caloric fluid, the four humors, atoms as little indivisible billiard balls, whatever. (Dale says some of my examples are sloppy and he's probably right, but he agrees with the general shape of it.)
(3) So that method isn't reliable, and by the exact same kind of reasoning our current best theories are probably false too — or at least the parts that talk about things nobody can see are probably false. (I looked this up afterward and I think this thing has a name, the "pessimistic meta-induction"? I might have the name wrong, don't worry about it.)
(4) Dale's comeback was that there's a real difference between a theory being handy for predicting and you actually being able to manipulate the things it talks about — you can spray electrons in a cloud chamber, he says, and nobody ever sprayed phlogiston. Call that the "you can poke it" reply.
(5) But I don't think the reply saves him: people were successfully manipulating stuff for centuries on theories we now think are wrong. Doctors drained swamps and the malaria rates dropped, and they thought they were getting rid of bad air. So "you can poke it" has a track record of failure too, and (3) stands.
(6) Except here's where I lose the thread: the claim in (2) and (5) that those old theories were wrong is itself something we only think we know by trusting current science. So if (3) is right, my own reasons for (3) get pulled out from under me.
(7) And worse: any method with a track record of error would be unreliable by this logic, including plain old induction — so the step from (1) and (2) to (3), which is itself an induction, would be unreliable too, and I'd have to stop trusting my own reasoning about everything, not just about science.
(8) That's obviously nuts. Dale is annoying but he isn't wrong about everything, and I don't want to end up saying we know nothing about electrons, or that I can't trust my own reasoning, or that my argument has no force against him at all.

So which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me — like, what do I have to swallow if I drop it? I'd definatly rather keep (2) and (5) than (7), and I'd rather not end up having to admit Dale was basically right. Two other things: Dale also pointed out that "atoms" is a bad example for me because we still believe in atoms, we just changed the story about them, and I didn't have an answer for that either. And if my argument does end up eating itself, is there a version that still works — like, is it okay if it's only a "your own commitments blow up in your face" kind of argument instead of a straight argument that science is unreliable, or does that make it worthless? Please don't spend time on whether my history is right or fix my examples, I want to know which step to reject and why that step looked fine when I wrote it.
