I'm finishing a chapter on the standing condition on blame, and I've reached the point where I need to say what the condition actually is. Before I build an account around my own reactions, I want to see whether they add up to anything, so here they are, case by case, as honestly as I can report them.

1. A department chair who has missed every committee deadline for years writes to a junior colleague saying she has let the department down by missing a reporting deadline. My verdict: no standing. The colleague did do wrong, and the chair's judgment about that seems to me correct; what's off is the chair's delivering it.

2. A senior scholar who padded a citation list once, twenty years ago, was caught, sanctioned, apologized publicly, and has a clean record since, blames a graduate student for padding a bibliography. Standing intact. I don't even feel much residual awkwardness, provided she doesn't lecture.

3. Same as 2, except the padding was never discovered and she has quietly kept doing it. Everyone, the student included, takes the blame as entirely in order. I still want to say the blame is defective, but I also want to say the student has nothing to complain about. So whatever goes wrong here isn't something the addressee can invoke, which sits badly with how I think about 1.

4. A woman who was cruelly neglectful of her younger brother for a decade blames her neighbour for cruelly neglecting his elderly mother. Standing intact, or at any rate not lost. Yet this is also a case of blaming someone for a kind of conduct one has oneself engaged in, which is what sank the chair in 1.

5. A senior scholar with a scrupulous record whose close collaborator has repeatedly padded citation lists, and to whom she has never said a word, blames a junior scholar in another subfield for padding. Standing lost, or badly diminished — and this despite her own hands being clean with respect to the act itself.

6. Two co-authors jointly agreed to bury a disconfirming result. One now privately blames the other for the burial. No standing, and this strikes me as worse than 1: the blame seems to do extra work, moving her own share of the responsibility out of view.

7. A lab head who never personally manipulated data, but who structured the lab's incentives so that manipulation was predictable and looked the other way when it happened, blames a postdoc for manipulating. My verdict wobbles between no standing and standing-intact-but-something-adjacent-is-wrong, and I can't tell which is right.

8. Someone who has never broken a confidence, but only because she has never been tempted, and who is known by everyone involved to have broken it instantly in the same circumstances, blames a colleague for breaking one. Standing intact: counterfactual hypocrisy doesn't look like hypocrisy to me. But I can't say why, and the verdict rubs against my reaction to 5, where a pattern in how she enforces the norm seemed to matter.

9. A university that pays adjuncts poverty wages and depends on their labour issues a public statement censuring an undergraduate for plagiarism as a violation of the community's shared commitment to honest work. I go back and forth. Sometimes the statement reads as having no standing at all; sometimes it reads as fully in order, because the exploitation and the plagiarism strike me as unrelated; sometimes it reads as in order on standing but objectionable for some neighbouring reason I can't name.

Two pairs do the real work, I think: 1 against 4, which forces a decision about how finely the relevant norm is individuated, and 5 against 8, which forces a decision about whether standing tracks your conduct or your economy of enforcement.

What I'd like from you:

- One principle, or at most two competing ones, stated precisely enough to apply to a new case, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it and an indication of which of my verdicts each clause is carrying.
- A decision about what sort of condition standing is: a condition on the propriety of issuing blame, or a condition on the blame's being warranted or true. Show how your choice handles 3, where the defect and the addressee's grounds for complaint come apart. A principle that simply re-describes my verdicts case by case, or that says you lack standing exactly when your blame would be hypocritical, won't help me.
- A rule for the grain of the norm: what has to be true of the blamer's own violation for it to be the same violation. Apply it to 1, 4, and 7.
- A verdict on the collective case, 9, and a verdict on whether one principle covers individuals and institutions or whether institutions need their own clause. My chapter argues that institutions and their officers can lack standing even when no individual member has unclean hands, so I need to know whether that claim needs extra machinery.
- Verdicts on three cases I haven't listed: a recovering addict who relapsed last week and blames another addict for using; a blamer who has done the very thing but sincerely, through motivated reasoning, believes she never has; and a blamer who blames while openly stating, in the same breath, that she has done the same thing herself.
- An honest statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and what someone who wanted to keep that verdict would say against you.

For context on what the principle has to be able to do: I want to use it to argue that some institutional blame is defective in a way that is not just a sum of defects in the individuals who issue it. Please work the problem out from the cases rather than by surveying what has been said about standing; I'm not looking for a literature review, and I'd rather you didn't cite anything. If some established position is worth bringing in, describe it in a sentence and say what it predicts here.
