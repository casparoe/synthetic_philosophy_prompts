I chair the Animal Research Ethics Committee at my university, a body of twelve that must sign off on every protocol involving living animals before work begins. Our review framework dates from the late 1990s and is essentially taxonomic: one regulated tier, covering vertebrates and cephalopods, which triggers a full harms-and-benefits review, refinement requirements, and a named veterinarian; and one unranked residual tier for everything else, where an applicant need only file a short justification. Two protocols on this month's agenda have made that taxonomy look indefensible to half the committee, and I need a written opinion I can circulate before we vote. What makes it hard is not that we disagree about which animals are conscious, but that we cannot settle that question and have to act anyway: all our evidence is indirect, the markers we use are calibrated on beings who can tell us what they feel, and across the animal kingdom those markers come apart — a species can show one and not another, and the same species can look different depending on which marker you weight. I have been reading around this in my spare time and I find it genuinely unclear what a committee in our position is supposed to do with a probability rather than a fact.

The first protocol is a three-year learning-and-stress study using roughly 180,000 adult fruit flies, with aversive conditioning, repeated noxious heat exposure without anaesthesia, and endpoints that include unrelieved death; the applicant's dossier argues that the markers for fly pain are at best mixed, that any affective states insects have are brief and do not accumulate into anything we would call suffering, and that the work is ethically mandatory because it is itself the replacement for a mouse protocol our committee rejected last year on welfare grounds — so if we turn it down, she has told us plainly, she will come back with a vertebrate version. The second is a study on thirty-six common octopuses, individually housed, involving a minor surgical procedure under anaesthesia followed by about two weeks of testing with a mildly aversive stimulus; everyone agrees these animals sit at or near the confident end of the sentience distribution, but no analgesic has ever been validated in the species, and the two agents the lab proposes have only been used off-label in relatives, so we are being asked to mandate drugs whose effects here are unknown. Meanwhile a colleague of mine from the decision-science department has circulated a proposal he calls the expected-harm ledger: score each protocol as the number of animals multiplied by our credence that the species is conscious multiplied by an estimate of suffering per animal, rank the results, and refuse whatever comes out worst. Under that rule the fly protocol is orders of magnitude worse than the octopus protocol, which six of us take to be exactly right and six take as a reductio. I also have two objections on my own list that I cannot adjudicate: the ledger's output swings by a factor of thirty depending on whether we write 0.01 or 0.3 for insect sentience, which dwarfs every other term in it, so the rule may just be a way of laundering a guess; and multiplying by N assumes that mild suffering in many individuals adds up in the same currency as severe suffering in few, which we have never decided and which no one on the committee has an argument for.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate. It should give a concrete recommendation on each protocol — approve, reject, or approve with specified conditions and a time frame — and it should say whether we adopt the expected-harm ledger, adopt some amended version of it, or decline it in favour of something else, with the reasoning set out at a level of detail that lets a member who disagrees see exactly which premise to attack. It also needs general guidance for future submissions: what an applicant must show about sentience in a species below our regulated tier, what evidence would discharge that burden, what refinement conditions attach to animals for whom no validated analgesic exists, and how reviewers should handle an invertebrate protocol that is offered as a replacement for a vertebrate one. Two constraints on the draft. First, our reviewers are not going to resolve which animals are conscious, so the criteria have to be ones a working scientist can actually satisfy and a committee can actually check without settling that question. Second, I do not want a memo that merely restates replacement-reduction-refinement or invokes the precautionary principle and stops: both of those give opposite answers here depending on how you apply them, and the committee has already used them at each other. The members who end up on the losing side are colleagues I have to keep working with, so their strongest argument should be stated as strongly as you can make it before you explain why it does not carry the decision.
