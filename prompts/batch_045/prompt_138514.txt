I want to practice attacking the Poverty of Historicism argument, and I want a real opponent rather than a punching bag. Please play a hard-line Popperian: you accept the three-step argument (the course of history is deeply shaped by the growth of knowledge; we can't predict by scientific means what people will come to know; so we can't predict the course of history), you won't give up that conclusion, but you'll happily concede points about how it should be stated and about what exactly it rules out. If I box you in you can restate the view more carefully, but you never abandon it. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to about a paragraph.

Here's my opening. Your argument at best shows that we can't predict the *content* of future knowledge. But a lot of lawlike claims about history only need its *existence* plus its gross structural effects: any society that sustains open criticism and cumulative inquiry for a couple of centuries will gain enormously in predictive and manipulative power, watch child mortality collapse, move most labour out of agriculture, and widen the range of effective choices available to ordinary people. None of that commits me to saying what anyone will believe. Its non-trivial, its testable, and it survives every step of your argument — so why isn't that a law of history?
