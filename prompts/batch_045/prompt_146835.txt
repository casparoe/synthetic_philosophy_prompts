I argued with a physicist friend about whether Newton's bucket shows that space is a thing, and he kept leaning on an analogy I hear everywhere: spacetime is just a field, exactly like the electromagnetic field. It exists in vacuum, it carries energy, it carries waves — gravitational waves are ripples in it — so it is as much a substance as the electromagnetic field is, and the bucket then becomes straightforward: the water climbs the wall because it is rotating with respect to the field itself rather than with respect to other matter. That is meant to settle substantivalism, and with it the claim that a single bucket in an otherwise empty universe would still show a curved surface.

I suspect the analogy is carrying more than it can. The electromagnetic field is defined over a background geometry that says where the field is and how it propagates; the metric field is what supplies that geometry, so saying that it exists in space looks close to circular. And having degrees of freedom of one's own is not obviously the same as existing independently of matter, which is the part the hole argument and the shift-the-universe-three-feet worry were about. So I would like an audit: what maps onto what in the field analogy, which features of the electromagnetic field it quietly imports into spacetime, which of those disanalogies matter for the conclusion he wants and which are irrelevant, and whether that conclusion survives once they are on the table.

The case I raised and could not settle was this. Take a bucket that is at rest with respect to the distant matter, but enclosed by a massive shell that is spinning, and stipulate that the water climbs part of the way — some fraction of the height it reaches when the bucket itself spins at the shell's rate. He said partial is nearly as good as full for Mach: distant matter is doing some of the work, so the bucket has stopped being evidence for absolute space. I said partial looks nearly as bad as none: if curvature remains while the bucket is not rotating relative to anything material, something non-relational is still setting the local standard of non-rotation, and the shell's contribution is just another field effect. What does that case actually establish, and does it bear on substantivalism at all, as opposed to on whether rotation can be defined purely as relative motion of bodies?

One last thing, because I think our exchange kept sliding between three claims: that rotation is absolute, that spacetime has degrees of freedom of its own, and that spacetime points exist independently of what occupies them. Which of these does the field analogy support, which does the bucket support, and which does substantivalism actually require? If the field analogy fails, is there a better comparison — the metric field compared to something other than another field?
