I serve on the acquisitions committee of a small municipal arts foundation, and we are currently deadlocked over whether to commission a large site-specific sculpture for the main square of our town. The complication is that the work does not yet exist: what we have is a maquette, a set of technical drawings, a written statement from the artist, and enthusiastic reports from two curators we have good reason to trust, one of whom has stood in front of the artist's last three installations. The commitment is expensive and effectively irreversible, and it has to be made many months before there will be anything to walk around. Roughly half the committee thinks deferring is plainly the responsible thing to do: the curators have vastly more experience, our mandate is to spend a budget well rather than to cultivate refined private opinions, and a trustee who refuses to vote until she has had her own encounter with the object is indulging a scruple at public expense. The other half wants to say that something has gone missing: that voting to spend a great deal of money on the strength of the claim that a thing is beautiful, while having no experience of it and no prospect of any, is either not really a judgment of taste or is a borrowed one that we cannot honestly present to the town as ours.

I have been reading about the philosophical debate over whether aesthetic beliefs can be acquired from testimony, and I cannot work out how much of it transfers to our situation, so I would like you to press on three things. First, I suspect we have been running together three different claims: that the finished work will be beautiful; that it will be beautiful to us, or move us, when we finally stand in front of it; and that once we do, we will be able to see what makes it good rather than only having been told. Tell me which of these, if any, trustworthy curatorial testimony could put us in a position to know, and which it could only give us a reason to act on, and why that difference matters for what we are entitled to say afterwards. Second, and this is the part I find genuinely puzzling: imagine we could instead consult a system that has been fitted to the recorded aesthetic responses of our own committee over many years and that predicts with well-documented reliability how each of us will respond to a work we have not seen — not that the work is good, but that we will judge it good, and roughly which features will be doing the work in our judgment. Is the standard resistance to aesthetic deference even supposed to be sensitive to that? As I understand it, there are three candidate objections in play: that no testimony can substitute for acquaintance with the thing itself; that even highly reliable deference is objectionable because a judgment of taste ought to be one's own in some stronger sense; and that what testimony supplies is at best a reason of the wrong kind, good enough for choosing but not for believing. Say whether the personalized predictor escapes any of these, and if it escapes one, whether that reveals the original objection was never really about how reliable the testifier is.

What I want, in the end, is the practical upshot. Describe the most important implications of all this for how our foundation should proceed: whether we should adopt a rule that at least one voting member must have direct experience of the artist's earlier work, whether we should frame our public justification in terms of what we believe about the sculpture or only in terms of what we expect we will come to believe, and whether the existence of a reliable taste-prediction tool would change what we owe the town by way of honest accounting. Do not stop at a survey of the available positions; commit to the line you think is correct and defend it, and be explicit about where you are less certain.
