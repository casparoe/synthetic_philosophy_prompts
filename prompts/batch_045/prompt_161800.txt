I am convening a small adversarial collaboration on a methodological dispute in AI alignment, and I want the joint statement drafted beforehand, so that both parties arrive to react to a text rather than to each other.

The two participants are (a) an assurance-first person, who holds that alignment work should be organised around worst-case reasoning: a safety case should as far as possible take the form of a guarantee that holds for every input, for every distribution shift inside a specified set, and for every strategy available to a system that is actively trying to defeat the measure; empirical evidence is admissible only as a way of discharging a stated assumption, never as the load-bearing element. And (b) a risk-management person, who holds that this demand cannot be met by anything recognisable as a contemporary machine-learning system, that what we can actually obtain are expected-harm estimates, monitoring, redundancy and iterative feedback from deployment, and that pressing for worst-case guarantees produces either paralysis or recommendations whose real content is simply not to build the system.

Having watched them talk past one another, I suspect three distinctions are being run together, and I want the document to prise them apart:

1. Worst-case as a standard of acceptability versus worst-case as a method of probing. One can insist on adversarial red-teaming, perturbation analysis and adversarial evaluation purely as a way of surfacing average-case failures, while still holding that the deployment decision itself should be an expected-value decision. I want the statement to record which of these each side is actually committed to.

2. Guarantee as mathematical certainty versus assumption-backed guarantee, where every load-bearing empirical assumption is listed in an explicit ledger and each is either independently checked or assigned a failure probability that is then propagated through the case. My guess is that once the ledger is forced into the open, the sides differ less about whether assumptions are permitted than about the discount applied to them: how much probability mass one may place on a load-bearing assumption being false, and who bears the burden of argument.

3. The tail-dominance argument versus what I have started calling the wrong-tail objection. If the harm distribution is heavy-tailed enough, expected-harm minimisation is itself dominated by the extreme tail and looks from the outside like worst-case reasoning, which suggests part of the dispute may be verbal — turning on whether average case means expectation under a well-specified distribution or what typically happens. But the risk-management side has a reply I want stated in its own words: that worst-case rigour applied inside a misspecified event space is rigour spent in the wrong place, since the probability mass that matters most is often on events the model does not contain.

Please write the document, in six hundred to nine hundred words:

- A numbered section of agreed claims that both could sign without hedging — covering at least the statistical character of deep learning, the tendency of optimisation pressure to move systems into the tails of their own evaluation distributions (so that an average-case guarantee is not stable under the optimisation that produced the system), the composability of partial measures and how far independence assumptions can be audited, and what a safety case is for.
- A disagreements table: for each residual disagreement, the proposition as the assurance-first side would formulate it and as the risk-management side would formulate it, each in wording its own side would accept, with a note on whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal.
- A cruxes section. For each crux: the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; a concrete case, empirical finding or argument that both would accept in advance as bearing on it — I would like at least one crux to be testable against evaluation-aware or sandbagging behaviour, and at least one to turn on the analogy with safety-critical engineering outside machine learning, where low failure rates are widely credited to probabilistic risk assessment, redundancy and design-level fail-safes rather than to worst-case proofs; and each side's prediction about the outcome, written down before the fact, so that neither can reinterpret it later.
- A closing note, from you as drafter rather than from either party, stating how confident you are that each side would actually sign the characterisations attributed to it, and singling out the one claim in the document you are least confident about.

Keep the whole thing at the level of concepts rather than the literature: no citations, no references to specific papers or authors, and nothing that turns on a result I would have to look up.
