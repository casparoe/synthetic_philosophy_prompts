I'm in a graduate reading group on the temporal structure of experience, and we have now spent two full sessions on the color phi phenomenon without moving an inch, which makes me suspect we are not disagreeing about anything we could settle. The case: a red spot is flashed at one location, then a fraction of a second later a green spot a few degrees away, and observers report a single spot that travels between the two locations and changes color somewhere in the middle of its path — that is, at a time at which the green flash has not yet occurred, so that the change apparently cannot have been derived from the second flash by any normal forward-looking process. The two live explanations are the ones Dennett labels, in the vocabulary he introduces in *Consciousness Explained*, as Orwellian and Stalinesque revision: on the first, the subject really does undergo an experience whose temporal features differ from what is later reported (roughly, something is first experienced differently and the record is then edited, so that the subject sincerely remembers a mid-path change that never occurred in experience), and on the second, nothing of that earlier sort is ever experienced at all — the visual system holds processing open until the second flash arrives and then constructs, once, an experience whose content already includes the change mid-path, so that there is no earlier draft for anyone to rewrite. Dennett's own move, as I read him, is that this is a fake contrast: on a multiple-drafts picture there is no theater and no privileged moment at which a content becomes conscious, and therefore no fact of the matter about which happened. The standard reply is that of course there is a fact of the matter — my experience has whatever temporal structure it seems to have, and it is not open to you to say introspection is wrong here while insisting that nothing whatever could reveal the error.

What makes our sessions stall is that every piece of evidence anyone proposes is symmetrically absorbable, and I want a diagnosis of why. Suppose we time the neural correlates and find the content-bearing activity only after the second flash: the Stalinesque says that is the construction, the Orwellian says that is the laying down of the memory that then gets edited. Suppose instead we find the first flash's content encoded early: the Orwellian says that is the experience, the Stalinesque says that is an early, pre-experiential draft that the later construction overwrites or never promoted. I tried to break the tie with what I call the interleaved-probe design: during the gap between the two flashes, require a speeded discrimination of the first flash's color on the side, on the reasoning that a subject whose experience at that moment already features a mid-path change should be measurably worse at reporting the red than a subject whose experience is still of a red spot. But each side can accommodate either outcome — the Orwellian by saying the probe damaged memory consolidation rather than experience, the Stalinesque by saying successful discrimination can be driven by subpersonal processing that never became an experience — and I cannot tell whether those replies are principled methodological caution (we have no access to experience except through report and action, so the question is hard) or an immunizing maneuver that reveals the question to be empty.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the debate bundles together three questions that nobody separates: an empirical question about the represented temporal structure of experience (what the visual system delivers as happening when), which strikes me as genuinely answerable and largely answered by psychophysics, and which recurs in the same shape in the flash-lag and related postdictive effects; a processing question about when in neural time the content-bearing events occur, messier but in principle tractable, despite there being no single clock for the whole brain and different pathways having different latencies; and a metaphysical question about when a content's *becoming conscious* happens, which is where the fight actually lives and which may be ill-posed not because consciousness is ineffable but because we are asking for a determinate instant at which a temporally extended, fuzzy-bounded process turns into an event of a given kind — like asking at which second a conversation became an argument. Competing diagnoses I can't rule out are that it is a verbal dispute in which the two sides apply different criteria for which neural events count as experiences at all, or that it is really a disagreement about evidence: about whether a subject's report is data about experience or only about memory, and so about whether introspection has the authority the anti-Dennett side grants it. I'd like you to tell me which of these, or something else, is doing the real work, judging by what each side actually commits itself to rather than by how the positions are usually summarized; to say whether the symmetric absorbability of evidence is a symptom of ill-posedness or of an epistemic limit on a substantive question; and, given your diagnosis, to say what would count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — or else to argue that no progress should be expected and say why that is not just a confession of failure. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning: no preamble, no restatement of my question, no headings, around 800 to 900 words.
