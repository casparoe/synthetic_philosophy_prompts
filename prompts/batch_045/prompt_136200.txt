I have no philosophy background whatsoever — I work in logistics — and about four months ago I fell into the doomsday argument rabbit hole via a video essay and have been boring my flatmates about it ever since. The version I've ended up beleiving is a bit different from the usual one, though, and I want it stress-tested. Here's my version: you shouldn't sample yourself from *all humans who will ever live*, you should sample yourself from all humans who are ever *in a position to ask this question* — call them askers. If the future is mostly full of emulated or uploaded minds, or of descendants who simply never do this kind of reasoning about their own position in history, then a gigantic future population contributes almost nothing to the sample, so it can't make my birth rank look suspiciously early. My conclusion is therefore narrower than the usual one: not "there won't be many more humans" but "there won't be many more askers after me." I like this because it seems to dodge the move where you say my own existence is more likely in a big world, so I shouldn't be surprised to find myself early — on my version, a big world only buys you more surprises if the extra people also get to ask. But it also opens me up in a new way: a friend pointed out that if the far future runs lots of fast digital minds and even a small fraction of them bother with anthropic reasoning, my escape hatch slams shut and the original argument comes roaring back.

The other thing that's nagging at me is calibration. The same shape of reasoning — you're somewhere in a process, assume you're not at a specially chosen point, infer how much is left — can be aimed at things where we can actually go check the answer: how long a TV show will run given you're three episodes in, how long a restaurant stays open given you're eating there on a random night, how long a species lasts given you find a fossil partway through its range, how many copies a book sells given you bought the 40,000th one. If that style of inference has a decent track record in cases we can check, I feel licensed; if it quietly fails everywhere except when it's pointed at our own extinction, I should probably drop the whole thing. My flatmates also raised two things I can't shake: whether it matters that I *know* I'm alive in the early 21st century with a population curve that's been exploding (doesn't that already explain why I'm early, without any doom?), and whether any of this changes what I should actually *do* — like, does it make any difference to how I vote or donate or whether I have kids, or is it just a fun fact that has no grip on anything?

So: what would count against my version? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely embarassing, and I want you to separate the ones that a committed defender of my version would honestly concede from the ones they'd shrug off. Please say which of my commitments are load-bearing — if the asker-sampling move turned out to be ad hoc, or to collapse back into the usual reference-class mess under a new name, or to be unfalsifiable because I can always invent invisible non-askers, I want that said plainly, along with whether unfalsifiability would actually be fatal for an argument like this one or whether it's just what these arguments look like. Don't bother with citations or paper titles or author names, I'll look those up myself; I care about the reasoning, not who said it. Please write in plain language and define anything technical you use.
