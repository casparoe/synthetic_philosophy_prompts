I'm in a small reading group on decision theory — an economist who thinks these cases are all obvious, a philosopher who keeps telling me my verdicts are mutually inconsistent, and me, increasingly unsure. I've started writing down my gut answer to each case separately, because every general principle I try on for size seems to break somewhere. Could you try to extract the principle that best fits the pattern below, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to a case I haven't seen, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot keep?

(a) Newcomb's problem with opaque boxes and a predictor with a long track record, say 99 per cent: I take only the big box.
(b) Same, except both boxes are transparent and I can see the million sitting in the big box: I still take only the big box and leave the thousand behind.
(c) Counterfactual mugging: a flawless predictor tossed a fair coin; if it landed heads, it handed me ten thousand dollars, but only on the condition that it had predicted I would hand over a hundred dollars if asked in the tails case. The coin came up tails, and here it is asking. I refuse — though I also think I would happily have installed a binding commitment device before the toss had one been offered, and it unsettles me that those two answers don't fit together.
(d) Hitchhiker: a driver who reads faces flawlessly drives me across the desert to town, having seen in advance that I would pay him a hundred dollars on arrival. I made no promise, there is no reputational consequence, and the town is full of strangers who will never hear of it. I pay.
(e) XOR blackmail: a flawless predictor sends me a letter demanding a thousand dollars, and the letter explains that it was sent if and only if exactly one of two things is true — either I have a rare condition that will cost me a hundred thousand dollars to treat whatever I do, or the predictor foresaw that I would pay. So if the letter arrives, I have the condition exactly if I am the sort of person who refuses; I feel the pull to pay in order to make the bad news not be true, and I still refuse.
(f) Control: exactly like (c), except the coin has not been tossed yet and will be tossed after I answer, with the ten thousand arriving if it lands heads and I paid. I pay.
(g) One our group made up: like (c), except the predictor never simulated me — it simulated my identical twin, raised apart, whom I have never met and who has never heard of any of this, and the ten thousand in the heads branch depended on what she would have said. I find myself inclined to pay, less firmly than in (f) but far more than in (c); if the person simulated is merely a stranger who happens to reason roughly as I do, my inclination drops to about nothing.

My own stab at a rule: cooperate with a predictor only when what it predicted is the very choice now in front of me, and when the reward lands either in the branch I am actually in or in one I cannot yet rule out — and never when the predictor's rule rewards me for a disposition that costs me. But that rule seems to say the same thing about (c) and (g), and my gut doesn't, and I can't tell whether (c) and (d) really differ in anything besides the fact that in (d) I already got the ride.

Please give me: one or two sentences stating the principle at the top; then a compact table with a row for each of (a) through (g), showing my verdict, the principle's verdict, and a note wherever the two come apart; then what the principle says about two cases I haven't listed — first, the predictor tosses the coin, it comes up heads, it hands me the ten thousand, and then, unusually, asks for the hundred anyway; second, the same as (c) but with a predictor who is right only 80 per cent of the time, where I especially want to know whether the principle yields a threshold accuracy at which paying becomes right and where that threshold falls; then one line on which of my verdicts I should retract; and a final line naming the view if it has a name, without letting the label stand in for the content. Under 700 words in total.
