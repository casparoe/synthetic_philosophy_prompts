A moral philosopher whose graduate seminar I am auditing has staked out a clear line across the standard cases, and I want to know where the line goes next. Her verdicts so far:

- Bystander at a switch: diverting a runaway trolley from five onto a dead-end siding where one track worker stands. Permissible.
- Driver in the same situation, turning the trolley himself. Permissible; she denies that his role makes a difference.
- Fat man on a footbridge, pushed so that his body stops the trolley. Impermissible.
- Loop variant: diverting onto a siding that rejoins the main line, where a heavy man's body is what will halt the trolley before it reaches the five. Impermissible.
- The same footbridge case executed by remote charge, with no contact and no personal force. Impermissible; she denies that contact or closeness is doing the work.
- A transplant-style case: killing one person to supply what five others need. Impermissible.
- She would divert to save two at the cost of one, but not to save one at the cost of one.

I take that record to rule out straightforward consequentialism, the personal/impersonal distinction, and any bare doing/allowing or contact-based rule. What survives, as far as I can tell, is some form of the doctrine of double effect, some form of a prohibition on using a person as a means read off the causal structure rather than off the agent's intentions, and some form of the principle that one may redirect an existing threat but not create a new one. Here are three cases I have built to pull them apart.

1. The grudge case. The brakes fail; five are ahead. The driver can divert onto a siding that runs into a gravel bank, which will stop the trolley. A worker is standing beside the bank and will be killed. His death does nothing to save the five, and the driver would divert into the bank just the same if the worker were elsewhere. But the driver also wants that worker dead and would divert onto him even if the line ahead were empty, and this desire is part of why he acts when he does. Same act, same causal structure, one extra intention.

2. The barrier case. A trolley is bearing down on five people in a cutting. You can release a heavy barrier from an overhead gantry onto the track; the trolley strikes it and stops short of the five. The impact will hurl the barrier into a worker on the embankment, killing him; his death contributes nothing to the rescue and is not needed for it. The question is whether the barrier's flight is the old threat redirected or a new threat created.

3. The doomed man case. The loop variant, except that the heavy man on the siding is already pinned under a fallen gantry and, as everyone can see, will be crushed to death within about thirty seconds no matter what anyone does. Diverting onto him stops the trolley and kills him perhaps twenty-five seconds early. Five are saved. Note that this case also forces a question about how the bad effect is to be described: is it his death, or only the loss of his last twenty-five seconds?

For each case, tell me what she will say and why, and set out what each of the surviving rules predicts so I can see where the predictions diverge rather than where they agree. Which of the three does the most diagnostic work, and is there a fourth case, which I would like you to describe, that separates the survivors more cleanly? I also want to know where her record simply runs out: which of these she could answer either way without contradicting anything she has said, and what she would have to add to her view in order to settle them.
