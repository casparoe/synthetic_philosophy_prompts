My brief:

I run a 75-minute session for about 16 undergrads in a Minds and Machines course. Mixed majors, mostly no programming, they've read Turing's 1950 paper and Searle's Chinese Room, and they've all used Google Translate but none of them have thought about it. Next week we're doing the shallowness debate about machine translation — Hofstadter's line of complaint that a system doing corpus-driven pattern matching isn't really translating, that it produces fluent output without any model of what the sentence is about and without any sense of which parts of the source the speaker was trading on. I want them to feel the force of that complaint instead of nodding along to it.

And here's the thing I keep going back and forth on, which I'd like the activity to actually separate rather than blur. I think the shallowness talk is secretly two complaints that everybody runs together:

(1) the no-world-model complaint — the system has no idea what the sentence describes, so it can't use facts about giraffes, doorways, or who is related to whom to resolve anything, and it fills the gap with whatever is statistically smoothest;
(2) the no-sense-of-relevance complaint — even when the relevant facts are in principle available to it, the system doesn't know which distinctions the speaker was leaning on, so it flattens a joke, a slight, or a piece of dry understatement into something literal and flat.

My hunch is that (2) is the deeper one and (1) is the one everybody argues about, and I want students to run into (2) in a way that genuinely surprises them — they should come out thinking the interesting problem is not the one they walked in expecting.

So: design an activity in which the students become the translation engine.

What I need:
- A corpus-only setup. Bilingual paired sentences in a made-up language, with a little internal grammar so pattern matching actually gets somewhere. Give me a small but usable sample — call it 15-20 pairs — plus the test sentences, and tell me what each item is designed to do to them.
- An information restriction that forces locality. Something like each person in a group only ever seeing a narrow window of the source sentence, or the sentence being passed down a line one chunk at a time with no going back. Whatever it is, it should make long-range dependencies hard for the group as a group, not just for an individual.
- A fluency trap: at least one item where the most corpus-likely rendering is smooth idiomatic English, is wrong, and is one the students will feel confident about. I want them to have the experience of being rewarded for a sentence they'd defend.
- A second phase where the restriction is lifted — they get the whole sentence, and they're allowed to use anything they know about the world — and where they still can't get one item right, because what's missing isn't information, it's relevance. Write that item out for me and tell me what it should have been.
- A scoring scheme that rewards surface overlap with a reference translation rather than getting the meaning right, because I want them to notice that the metric itself is pushing them toward shallowness. But calibrate it so they don't just give up and start messing about.

Also: a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, a list of what I need to print, the exact wording I read out at the start (including which rules I must definately not explain in advance), and a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto (1) and (2) above, and then onto the question whether what they were doing counted as understanding anything.

Two things I want you to be blunt about. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and what do I do in the moment — the failure modes, not the ideal run. Second, which feature of the shallowness debate this activity fundamentally can't reproduce. My worry is that my students obviously do understand language, so all I've built is a simulation of an information constraint rather than a simulation of a lack of understanding, and a sharp student is going to say so in the debrief and I won't have a good answer. Tell me how damaging that objection actually is, whether there's a fix, and if there isn't, what I should just say out loud to them instead.
