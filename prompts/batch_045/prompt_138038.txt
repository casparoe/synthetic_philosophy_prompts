I want to know what principle I am really running when I call a trait innate. Below are my gut verdicts on seven cases. Most I am confident about. Two pairs I cannot get to sit still together. At the end I have written the principle I half-believe.

**Case 1 — first language.** A child hears a few tens of millions of words of casual, ungrammatical, unfinished speech, is never told that a sentence is ungrammatical, and never encounters the crucial constructions in the relevant form, yet ends up with the same grammar as everyone else in her community. My verdict: innate, and the argument from the poverty of the stimulus works here.

**Case 2 — the boarding-school signers.** Suppose a school for deaf children brings together pupils who arrive with only improvised, idiosyncratic home-sign systems — no shared grammar, no morphology. Within a few years the younger pupils are producing a shared system with stable word order and agreement-like markers that appeared in none of the input, and they use it in ways the older pupils never do. My verdict: innate. This is the verdict I am most confident about, more than Case 1.

**Case 3 — prepared avoidance.** A hypothetical prey species — call them *dune marmots* — acquires a lasting avoidance of one particular colour pattern after a single exposure, while needing many pairings to learn to avoid any other pattern equally well associated with illness. My verdict: yes, but only of a learning bias, and I suspect the word "innate" is doing two different jobs here.

**Case 4 — the silver wren.** A songbird will not learn its song from loudspeaker playback, even when the playback gives strictly more, cleaner and better-spaced instances of the song than a live tutor does; it needs a live tutor during a narrow window. My verdict: the song is innate. Note that here the data were abundant, so the poverty of the stimulus has nothing to do with it.

**Case 5 — the gated call.** A burrowing rodent I will call the *kestrel squirrel* responds to a specific alarm call only if it heard that call from its mother during a three-week window; pups raised in acoustic isolation never respond to it at all. My verdict: not innate in the sense I care about. It is learning behind a gate, and I would not call it innate just because the gate has a schedule.

**Case 6 — the network.** A large neural network trained on hundreds of billions of words with no explicit syntactic supervision nonetheless comes to track long-distance agreement and generalises to constructions it has never seen. My verdict: not innate, and perhaps not even a well-formed question for the network. But I refuse to let this retroactively defeat Cases 1 and 2, and when I try to say why, I end up saying only that the network had vastly more data and vastly more computation — which sounds like an excuse. Call this the Parity Problem.

**Case 7 — the decoy.** Someone argues that children's knowledge that unsupported objects fall is innate, on the grounds that nobody teaches it and nobody is ever shown a counterexample. My verdict: no. That is a general-purpose learner meeting an extremely regular world. The "poverty" here is manufactured.

My tentative principle: *a trait T is innate in organisms of kind K iff no general-purpose learner, held to K's data budget and K's developmental deadline, converges on T across K's normal environments.* Call it budgeted underdetermination.
