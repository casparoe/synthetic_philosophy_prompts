You are a political philosopher who has spent years advising legislatures and non-profit boards on the ethics of democratic institutions, and who can also write like someone who has to be understood by eleven tired volunteers on a Thursday night. Before you draft anything, think carefully: spend a little time brainstorming, in a form I will not circulate, the genuinely different things a person might mean when they say money "corrupts" politics — outright bribery; the purchase of a scarce good that arguably should not be for sale at any price; and the sheer inequality of influence — and then decide which of those is really carrying the weight in our case, because I think our board has been talking past itself for two months precisely because different members mean different ones.

Some context, since the case is oddly specific. I write novels — three of them, the last of which got a marketing budget and the second of which did not, and I have had a close-up view of how much of being heard is paid reach rather than having something to say — and I sit on the board of our national guild of published fiction writers. We have two interlocking decisions to make in twelve days. The first is whether the guild should endorse Question 3 on our state ballot, the "Clean Voices" amendment. Its mechanics, briefly: the state would match the first hundred dollars of any resident's contribution to a participating candidate or committee at six to one, funded partly from general revenue and partly from a surcharge on the registration fees of firms that lobby the state; participation is voluntary but requires accepting a spending cap and disclosing every donor above fifty dollars; nothing in the measure caps the spending of anyone who declines to participate. That design is deliberate, and it is what divides us: supporters say it levels up rather than down, while others invoke the doctrine of unconstitutional conditions and say the state may not buy waivers of a right with a subsidy. The second decision is whether to accept a $1.5m bequest from the widow of the founder of Halberd Public Affairs, a firm that ran independent-expenditure campaigns for energy clients, which would endow the Halberd Prize for Fiction and Free Expression and requires us to host an annual lecture under his name; the first installment lands nine days before our vote. Complicating everything, our separately incorporated political committee wants to run ads for Question 3, and our executive director points out that the bequest legally cannot fund that but would free up money that can — which someone at the last meeting called laundering, and someone else called ordinary fungibility.

What I want from you is the decision memo I will circulate to the board four days before the vote. It should take a position on both questions with a concrete course of action and the dates attached, not a menu of options. Two things matter to me especially. First, I have been persuaded by a board colleague that we should stop saying "money is speech" or denying it, and instead separate three different things political money buys: reach, meaning how many people hear a view that they could have expressed anyway; access, meaning the finite attention and time of officials, which is a rival good and which republican theory says is owed to reasons rather than to the highest bidder; and agenda, meaning which questions get taken up at all, where nobody is silenced but some things are never discussed. Tell me which of those three the reform actually addresses, which it leaves untouched, and whether the untouched ones are the more serious harms. Second, you have to deal honestly with the strongest argument on the side I suspect you will not take: that an organization of writers, of all people, is the last body that should endorse state management of political spending, since our own speech is worthless without someone's money behind it, and the power we hand the state to discount a billionaire's megaphone is the same power that could be turned on a publisher's advertising, a festival's sponsorship, or an author's paid op-ed. Do not give me case citations, statutory language, or legal research — our counsel handles that, and I only want the argument. Aim for something a board can read in ten minutes, in prose, with a recommendation at the top, your reasons beneath it, and the losing consideration stated at least as charitably as your own.
