I write a fortnightly column for a science-and-technology magazine about ideas that escape their home discipline, and part of my job is vetting the op-eds, book excerpts and embargoed press releases we run. A few times a month something crosses my desk in which a source — a theoretical physicist, an AI researcher, a legal theorist, a central banker with a taste for big ideas — reaches for Gödel's incompleteness theorems outside mathematics: to argue that machine intelligence can never equal human understanding, that there can be no final theory of physics, that no legal, tax or regulatory code can ever be complete, that no model of the economy can be closed, that the universe cannot be a simulation. I don't want the blanket newsroom response that every such use is abuse, because some of them are doing real work; but I also don't want to print a sentence that would make a working logician wince. My constraints are fixed: roughly fifteen minutes per item, no mathematics beyond what I remember from school, fact-checkers with humanities degrees who are not going to read a proof, and a standards editor who signs off and whom I need to be able to justify the call to in a Slack message. So what I want is a triage procedure I can actually run — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no (cut the sentence), where it permits printing with a specified hedge, and where it has to hand the decision to a person, either because the remaining question is one of editorial judgment rather than fact or because it is a technical question that no amount of triage can settle.

The conceptual work is in deciding which distinctions have to survive the translation into something a non-specialist can run. My sense is that the load-bearing ones include: whether a formal system has actually been specified at all, and whether it is the kind of structure that could encode elementary arithmetic rather than, say, a chess engine or a spreadsheet; whether what is said to be missing is derivability inside that system, truth, or knowability by any mind; whether the argument needs the system to be consistent and what becomes of it if it is not; whether the target is held fixed or quietly re-specified every time a gap is pointed out, which is what happens in the Lucas-Penrose style of argument about minds and machines; whether the limitation is claimed of one system or uniformly of all of them; and whether the word doing the work is really incompleteness or rather undecidability, uncomputability or Chaitin-style irreducibility. A friend who works in formal epistemology gave me three rough tests she uses — she calls them the arithmetization test, the export step and the mirror test, the last being: take the human, or the physics community, or the legal system as the target and see whether the argument, if sound, proves the same limitation about us. Keep, correct or discard those, and add whatever checks they miss. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and give me two or three substitute sentences I can drop in when the verdict is that the analogy fails but the underlying point is fine.

Then run it, in front of me, on three items. First, a press release in which an AI company's chief scientist says the incompleteness theorems show that no system of rules can capture mathematical truth, and therefore no AI can have understanding and their product will always need a human in the loop — vivid copy, and my editor wants to quote it. Second, an op-ed from a well-known theoretical physicist arguing that because any sufficiently strong formal system leaves truths unprovable, physics can never have a final theory, only an endless succession of them. Third, and this is the one where I want you to be ruthless: a column of my own, half written, arguing that Gödel shows the dream of a complete and consistent regulatory code — a rule for every case, no discretion, no gaps — is not merely impractical but incoherent, so that discretion is a permanent feature of law rather than a regrettable one; a colleague muttered something about open texture when I described it, which I took as dismissal. Give me the honest verdict on all three, including whether mine has to be killed or can only survive as an explicitly labelled illustration, and then say what the procedure cannot do: which of these arguments it can only classify as beyond triage, where it will misfire on claims that are metaphorical on purpose, and when the right answer is to go and find an actual logician. Please reason from the structure of the theorems themselves rather than from anything you would need to look up — I have no way of checking a reference, and I would rather have the argument than the authority.
