I'm in a undergrad reading group on philosophy of mind / cognitive science, and last week we were going over the "Ant Fugue" dialogue in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach — the ant-colony one with Aunt Hillary and the Anteater. You know the setup: Aunt Hillary is a colony of ants, the individual ants are constantly dying and being replaced, the Anteater eats some of her and her ability to think or meaningfully say "I" degrades, and the whole thing is written kind of like a fugue where you can read the lines at the level of individual ants, at the level of the colony, or at some weird self-referential level. One person in the group kept repeating the old slogan "the whole is more than the sum of its parts" as if that settled the question of whether Aunt Hillary really has a mind over and above the ants, and someone else just said she is nothing but ants all the way down. I want to come back next session with something sharper then just picking a side.

So here's what I need: please take the slogan "the whole is more than the sum of its parts" and lay out, as a bullet-point menu I can bring to the group, the different precise claims it could be making in this Aunt Hillary context, ordered from the totally trivial to the wildly radical. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude / trivially true, substantive and defensible, or actually false or question-begging, and — this is the part I really care about — whether that reading would actually do the philosophical work my classmate wanted. That is, would it get you from "there is a bunch of ants interacting" to "there is a single thinking being / a mind / a self / a subject of experience here", or is it compatible with the reductionist just shrugging and saying, sure, and it is still nothing but ants?

Please make the menu fine-grained, not just "it means emergence". Some seed distinctions I had in mind: (a) the mereological point that a collection or structured whole can have properties (mass, location, throughput of food, robustness to damage) that no single ant has; (b) the predictive or explanatory-irreducibility claim that you cannot infer or fully explain colony behavior from ant-level descriptions alone, so you need a higher-level vocabulary; (c) the causal-powers claim that the whole can do things no part can do; (d) the semantic / symbol-system claim that the colony is a level of interpretation where patterns of ants function as signals that refer or mean things, so there is a language of the colony; (e) the identity-over-time claim that Aunt Hillary can persist through complete turnover of her ants, so she is a continuant distinct from any particular ant; (f) the consciousness / subjectivity claim that there is something it is like to be Aunt Hillary, a unified "I" with the strange-loopish self-representation Hofstadter is always on about; and (g) the spooky ontological-emergence claim that some new non-physical stuff or extra vital property pops into existence once the ants are arranged right. Feel free to add, split, or rename readings, but for each one tell me whether a reductionist could happily accept it, and flag which reading(s) people usually mean when they deploy the slogan to end arguments about ant colonies and minds. Finally, say whether that usual reading is actually supported by the Aunt Hillary setup or whether it just assumes the conclusion that the colony is a minded subject.
