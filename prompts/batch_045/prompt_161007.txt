I have been defending a position about the metaphysical basis of moral status — what makes a system the kind of thing whose welfare matters, and how much it matters — and I would like the strongest honest case against it. Not a survey of the dialectic, and not a list of rivals: I want to know what this view risks, and where.

The position, stated with the qualifications I actually rely on:

(i) Whether a system has moral status, and to what degree, is fixed by which computations it implements. The physical or chemical nature of the realizing substrate is irrelevant to status as such.

(ii) Implementation is not mere description-matching. A mapping from physical states to computational states counts as implementation only if the system's counterfactual behaviour mirrors the counterfactual structure of the computation: had the input been different, or had some intermediate state been different, the system would have passed through different subsequent states in the way the computation specifies. This is what is supposed to dispose of the familiar triviality worry that, under some mapping, any sufficiently unstructured system implements any computation.

(iii) Status therefore survives change of substrate, of architecture, and of speed, so long as the computation implemented is preserved.

(iv) Status is scalar and continuous, tracking something structural about the implemented computation — the degree of causal integration among its states, the richness and temporal depth of what the system models, or something in that neighbourhood. So there is no sharp line, but also no presumption that very different systems sit at the same point on the scale.

(v) Numerosity is handled by a distinctness condition that I have not yet spelled out. Running one mind's computation a million times over in lockstep on a single chip is not, merely by virtue of that, a millionfold increase in what matters.

Please give me the defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say what would have to be true for it to bite. I want you to separate three kinds. First, empirical: what findings about actual minds — about the neural basis of consciousness in animals we already grant status to — would tell against (i) or (iii) rather than merely against a particular implementation programme. Second, structural: is there a real prospect of a result showing that condition (ii) admits no non-arbitrary cutoff, so that the robustness requirement either trivializes back into the triviality worry or, once made precise, turns out to be a requirement stated over the causal dynamics of the physical system rather than over computations at all — in which case (i) is doing no work and the view has quietly collapsed into a rival that measures integration or causal density directly? If that collapse is the real threat, say so, and say which of my clauses would have to go.

Third, cases the view may simply be unable to describe cleanly. Here are four I keep circling; tell me which are genuinely independent problems and which are one problem in different costumes.

A. Overlapping implementation. A single physical process at a fine grain implements a rich self-modelling computation; some proper subset of the very same state-transitions, described at a coarser grain, also satisfies my conditions for implementing a far simpler sentient computation. Does the system contain one subject's worth of what matters, two, or something in between? It looks as though I must either count the same physical transitions twice or concede that the computation-based criterion is not what is settling the question.

B. Causally inert realization. A system in which the candidate computation is realized but nothing downstream depends on its fine-grained structure: the system would be behaviourally and causally indistinguishable if those fine-grained states were permuted in a way that preserved coarse behaviour. I claim status for it on the strength of (i) alone. What would make that attribution more than stipulation?

C. Attenuated realization. The same transition structure walked through over a million years with long dormant intervals, or spread across physically separated components whose links are slow enough that the system's states are never jointly present but only distributed across time. (iii) says speed does not matter; the causal story in (ii) says it might. Which clause breaks, and does the breakage have a principled location or only a continuum on which I would have to draw an arbitrary line?

D. Cheap multiplication. One chip, one mind-computation, a million lockstep instantiations from the same inputs. My (v) is a promissory note. What would a distinctness condition have to look like to cash it, and is any such condition derivable from (i)-(iv), or does it have to be imported as a separate principle — in which case the view's claim to be a single unified criterion is overstated?

Two further things. Tell me whether the view predicts anything its rivals do not, and therefore whether it risks anything at all: my sense is that (ii) and (iii) jointly predict a distinctive pattern of verdicts about slow, gappy, and distributed realizations, but I want to know whether a causal-integration theorist or a biological theorist could simply echo those verdicts, in which case the predictions discriminate nothing. And tell me honestly whether the view has been insulated by its own qualifications — particularly the slack in (ii) and the unfinished (v) — into something that cannot lose. If it has, say whether that is a defect here. This view is supposed to adjudicate decisions about actual systems carrying actual costs: an unfalsifiable criterion that always returns "more information needed" has a real opportunity cost, and I would count that as counting against it.

For what it is worth, the line of attack I would personally find hardest to shrug off is not any single case but an epistemic one: if the question which computation this system implements has no answer independent of our descriptive interests and our choice of grain, then the view gives me no rule for the situations in which I actually have to act, and its apparent determinacy in the easy cases is inherited from cases where substrate and computation happen to line up. If you think that argument is weaker than it looks to me, say why, and rank it lower.
