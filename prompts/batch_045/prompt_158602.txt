Our lab meeting has this running argument about emergence, and I keep losing it because of one sentence. Every time someone proposes that we explain a phenomenon by decomposing it — neurons, ants, whatever — our PI says "the whole is more than the sum of its parts" and the conversation moves on. Last week it was used to shut down a concrete proposal: a postdoc had suggested that a harvester ant colony's choice of foraging direction could be modeled as individual ants following simple local rules plus the geometry of the terrain, and the reply was that the colony "decides" where to forage in a way no individual ant does, so any ant-level model is by construction leaving something out and we should be hunting for a colony-level law instead. This week the same sentence got used to argue that since nobody can predict a large language model's capabilities before training it, there must be something ontologically extra in the trained system — which struck me as a leap, but I couldnt say why in the room.

Can you lay out for me, as a numbered list, the different precise claims that sentence could be making? I want the version where it's basically trivial (wholes have properties their parts lack, fine), the version where it's really a claim about our epistemic situation rather than about nature, the version where it's a genuine metaphysical claim about causal powers or downward causation, the version where it's just a claim about which vocabulary does the work in a good explanation, and any reading that's straightforwardly false because of something like additivity or conservation. Say for each whether it's defensible, and — this is the part I actually need — whether it would really support the move it was being used to make: the refusal of an ant-level model, and the jump from "we can't predict it in advance" to "there is something extra in the thing itself." Also tell me which reading people reaching for that sentence are usually reaching for, and whether there's a reading on which it's true but completely useless for settling anything.
