Here's something I keep going back and forth on and can't settle. Copyright treats independent creation as a complete defense: if you never heard my song and you write the very same song, you owe me nothing, and it doesn't matter how nearly identical the two are. Patent law runs the other way, since whoever makes or sells the thing is an infringer whether or not they ever heard of the patentee, so the second person to invent may get enjoined and can end up owing money. And trade secret law goes back to copyright's side, because working the secret out yourself or reverse engineering my product is a perfectly proper way to come by it. So of the three things we lump together as intellectual property, two only protect against a particular kind of taking, and one protects against a state of affairs.

A friend of mine, who isn't a lawyer, says this shows the things being protected differ in kind: copyright is about a relation between two people, I took from you, while a patent is about something out there in the world that a second person can arrive at on their own. I half buy that, but I can't tell whether the asymmetry is doing any real philosophical work or is just evidentiary, maybe because patents are published and searchable so you could have checked, while copying a song is almost impossible to prove directly, so access gets used as a proxy.

What does the asymmetry actually presuppose about what's owned, and what rival views of ideas and ownership does it quietly rule out? Is my friend's story one coherent account, or is the independent creation defense really just a proof device dressed up as a theory, and which parts of each regime are left underdetermined so that several theories would license them? And what would concretely have to change, in doctrine rather than in slogans, if a court took the relational view seriously and applied it to patents, or the object view seriously and extended it to copyright?
