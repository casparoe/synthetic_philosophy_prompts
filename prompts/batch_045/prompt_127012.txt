hey, I'm on the committee that hands out our university's access and participation money and we're going in circles. One member — genuinely reasonable, very stubborn — keeps reaching for the same picture: a degree is a race. Our job is to get everyone to the same starting line (fees covered, laptop, same maintenance grant), gun goes off, after that it's their business. Anything more is either patronising or an endless audit of how people spend their Tuesday afternoons. She uses it to argue for one off front-loaded cash and against compensating for anything that shows up later.

Three things make me doubt it but I can't get them into focus.

A decent chunk of the students we fund have disabilities or chronic illness where the cost isn't a one-off at the start, it arrives every week for three years — transport, care hours, extra time, equipment that wears out. In her framing that's bad luck mid-race, so it stops being fairness and becomes compassion and belongs in a different budget line. I don't see why the gun is where fairness ends.

Last year two students on identical grants both ended up unable to pay rent. One had put a big share into a genuinely expensive hobby. The other doesn't drive and lives somewhere with terrible buses, so a big share went on taxis. On the starting-line picture they were treated identically and that's the end of the story. That feels at once too harsh and too convenient.

And I think we're really arguing about what the unit is — money in the bank, how the person is actually doing, or what they can get done with it — and the race picture just buries that by making the only visible quantity distance covered per unit effort.

So: audit it properly. Give me the correspondence table — starting line, the gun, the track, effort, the finish line, the prize, the referee — mapped onto what we actually control (tuition, maintenance, disability support, teaching, assessment rules, classification, the job market after). Then tell me which features of a real footrace get smuggled in that a degree doesn't have. My three candidates: a race has one synchronised start and a three year degree is a stream of separate encounters, so there isn't one place where the starting line is; in a race everyone runs the same track, but we let people pick subjects with wildly different rates of turning money into a result, and I'm not sure that's the same as the expensive tastes problem or a different one, since the choice of subject is itself something we're supposed to be equalising; and a race rewards effort and nothing else, but nobody will say what effort even is for someone with depression or a 20 hour care load. Rank those by how much damage they do for the use she's making of it, and flag any I've missed.

Then the bit I actually care about: does her conclusion — front load it, never compensate for what appears later, never compensate for differences in what people need to convert cash into a degree — follow from the analogy, or is the picture doing the work? And if the race framing collapses, what's the better analogy for what this budget is for, with its own mapping, not just a vague it's more like a garden.
