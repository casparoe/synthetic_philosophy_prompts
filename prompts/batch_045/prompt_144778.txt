so I'm in a physics-of-information seminar this term (mixed phil + physics majors, mostly third years) and I keep noticing something I don't have a word for, and I genuinely can't tell if I've noticed something real or if I've just confused myself about the bookkeeping.

Here's the pattern. Every couple of weeks someone sets up a machine that converts information into work. We add up the energy, we find the machine can't actually win, and then somebody says where the cost got paid. My problem is that the place it got paid keeps moving, and it seems to move when we change the idealisation rather than when we change the machine.

Three cases from my notes:

1. The one-molecule-in-a-box setup. You learn which half the molecule is in, that's one bit, you extract kT ln 2 of work. The story we were given is that you pay it back when you wipe the one-bit memory. Then someone asks: what if the demon just has an enormous stack of blank bits and never wipes anything? And the answer we got was that now the cost is in the blank bits, or in whoever manufactured them, or — this is the bit I only half followed — in the fact that the memory has become correlated with the gas, whether or not you ever erase it. So the cost jumped from a step in the cycle to a state of a subsystem, and the cycle itself didn't change at all.

2. The feedback experiments they showed us, the bead in the laser trap being nudged by a controller that measures where it is. We were told the measurement step can in principle be done reversibly, so the cost isn't there; it's in resetting the controller afterwards. But there's also a version of basically this setup where the controller is just a long tape of already-prepared bits that the machine chews through, and then the cost is in preparing the tape — which is a different time, a different bench, and arguably before the machine existed. And if you let the tape be prepared by another such machine you just push the problem back one step. So the cost slid along the time axis as well as across subsystems.

3. The purely mechanical one, the little spring-loaded trapdoor / ratchet-and-pawl thing with no memory and nothing you would want to call a measurement. There the failure isn't an information step in any sense — the door is itself warm and jiggles. And yet when you add it up the number is the same kind of size as in the other two cases.

So: the total is invariant, the location isn't. The books always balance, but they balance by pointing somewhere, and the somewhere looks negotiable. It reads to me less like a mechanism (here is the thing that stops the demon) and more like a constraint on the accounting — you can always locate the cost, but which physical step is carrying it seems to be a function of where you drew the line between the machine and everything else, and of which limiting case you decided to take (reversible measurement? pre-prepared tape? unbounded memory?).

I've started calling it cost-sliding in my notes and my made-up test is: hold the machine fixed, change only the idealisation, see whether the identified location moves. In all three cases it does. That makes me want to say the location isn't a property of the machine at all — but I also don't want to conclude something dumb like the cost isn't real, because the total really is fixed and the machines really do fail.

What I'm after:

- Does this have a name, on either the physics or the philosophy-of-physics side? I should say I don't just want a label — a bare term would tell me nothing.
- Whether it has one or not, what exactly is it, and what is it not? It's obviously near the erasure-cost principle, but that's a lower bound on a kind of process, and what I'm circling is which part of the process the bound bites on, which feels like a different claim. It also reminds me of an ordinary conservation law, where you can always find where the energy went, and of the way heat-versus-work depends on where you draw the system boundary — but in both of those I think the decomposition is settled once you fix the boundary, whereas here the boundary choice and the idealisation choice seem tangled together in a way I can't unpick, and I want someone to tell me whether that tangle is the real phenomenon or whether I'm overreading it.
- Is anybody actually troubled by this? Does it put any pressure on the slogan that information is physical, or on the claim that the erasure cost explains why demons fail rather than merely being consistent with their failing? Or is the standard reaction just: yes, of course, it's a consistency condition and not a mechanism, and nobody ever claimed otherwise?
- If there's no established term, coin one and define it properly, and give me the border cases: a case that clearly is this thing, and a case that superficially looks like it but is really either two different machines being described, or an actual unresolved disagreement about the physics rather than a choice of description.
