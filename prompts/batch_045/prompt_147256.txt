Me and my flatmate got onto Bertrand's chord paradox at 1am and he covered the whiteboard with an argument I've been staring at since. Every line looked fine at the time and I don't think the conclusion can be right.

(1) Principle of indifference: if you have no reason to favour one of several mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive possibilities, split your credence equally.
(2) The old gerrymandering objection (coin: heads / tails / it lands on its edge / a bird eats it, so 1/4 each) doesn't bite, because (1) applies to the finest *symmetric* partition, the one whose cells can be mapped onto each other by a transformation that leaves everything you know fixed.
(3) Circle of radius 1, chord drawn 'at random', how likely is it to be longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle (about 1.732)? Perpendicular distance from the centre uniform on [0,1] gives 1/2. Angle at the circumference uniform gives 1/3. Midpoint uniform over the disk gives 1/4.
(4) He grants those are three different chance set-ups, i.e. three well-posed questions rather than one question with three answers. Then he says that's the *problem*. In any real case you aren't told the set-up. So: picture a machine in the next room that prints chords and has an unlabelled dial on top; each dial position wires it to a different set-up. We spun the dial without looking.
(5) The dial angle has a genuine symmetry — rotations — so (1) applies cleanly and gives uniform over angles. But the map from dial angle to chord-length distribution is some squashed nonlinear thing, so 'uniform over dial angles' isn't uniform over anything about the chord. Pick a wiring and you get an answer; pick another and you get another; by fiddling with the wiring he says you can land on pretty much any number between 1/4 and 1/2.
(6) So for any continuous magnitude you care about — rod lengths, tomorrow's temperature, the mass of some particle — your credence is either arbitrary (you picked a parametrization) or undefined (you didn't). Indifference never delivers.
(7) And it spreads to the discrete cases it was meant to ground. A die you've never seen: whether it's fair is a continuous nuisance parameter, a bias vector in a five-dimensional simplex, and 'no reason to favour one region of that simplex over another' is exactly the step that broke in (5). So P(six) isn't 1/6 either, and since indifference was the only source of priors, Bayesian inference is guessing with extra steps.
(8) He shrugs: maybe that's just true.

I want to keep (1), and I want a die I know nothing about to come out 1/6. Which step is doing the illegitimate work — I suspect something gets swapped around (5) or (6), 'no answer' versus 'arbitrary answer', but I can't make it precise. And whatever you reject, tell me what it costs: does the die survive, and am I stuck saying the three chord answers are all correct, all wrong, or neither?
