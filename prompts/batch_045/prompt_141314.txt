I'm finishing a thesis in legal and political philosophy on cognitive liberty, and I've hit the point where the chapter needs a specification rather than another survey. The chapter's question is normative and institutional: when should a court or a research-ethics committee treat interference with a person's equipment as interference with the person? Three decision contexts make this concrete. First, compelled production: whether the state may subpoena the contents of a neural implant, or of a personal assistant that has been trained on a decade of the subject's speech and now completes her sentences and retrieves what she is trying to remember. Second, injury: whether deleting or damaging such a resource is damage to property or an injury to the person. Third, testimony: whether a subject's sincere avowal of what she believes may be corrected by what the resource contains, in the way it can be corrected by a diary she wrote.

So fix the purpose as follows: the theory of cognitive extension I need is one good enough to underwrite an attribution of a resource to a subject for legal and moral purposes. That means it has to return verdicts in contested cases, its verdicts have to be stable enough to be administered by an institution, and it has to say whose system the resource is. I want the desiderata any such criterion must meet: what it must explain, which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must respect, and which distinctions it must collapse — each with the case or consideration that shows it is a requirement rather than a taste. Mark each as non-negotiable, as a preference of one camp, or as something you think should be struck from the list.

The minimum case set, which I'd like treated as the constraint on the criteria rather than as a scoreboard at the end:

- The notebook case, stated so nothing rests on familiarity: a subject with a severe memory impairment carries a notebook everywhere, updates it automatically, consults it without deliberating about whether to, and acts on what it says without re-checking; the contrast case is a subject who looks up the same information afresh each time in a public directory, and a coupling-only case where performance depends on an environment never shaped by or for her, such as a log kept by someone else or a sign she happens to walk past.
- The shared resource: one evolving external store jointly maintained by two collaborators, each of whom relies on it exactly as the notebook-user relies on the notebook. If it is part of one subject's cognition, is it part of both? Whose memory is it? I want to know what the criterion says here, because this is the case where a many-one relation between resources and subjects breaks the usual individualist framing — and it is the case my chapter most needs, since two people's data-protection claims can conflict over one artifact.
- The drifting resource: the store keeps changing while the subject sleeps — an assistant that rewrites its own summary of her standing commitments, or a notebook a colleague annotates overnight. Does she now believe what it says, or only when she next consults it? Does the criterion need a counterfactual condition (she would avow it on consultation), and how does that condition distinguish genuine standing from a subject who has been subtly steered by whoever controls the update?
- The inscrutable resource: the resource is reliable and, in use, transparent, but the subject cannot survey or articulate how it encodes what it encodes — a trained model whose retrievals she depends on. Trust-and-reliability conditions and accessibility-or-surveyability conditions pull apart here, and I want to know which of the two is doing real work and why.
- The withdrawal pair: remove the external resource and the subject shows a specific, temporally structured deficit that partly recovers over months; compare an internal resource lost the same way, a resected region or a skill degraded by disease. Whatever the criterion demands of the outer resource should not be something the brain's own parts fail, and it should not be so weak that everything passes.
- The dormant resource: satisfies every candidate condition but is presently in a drawer, or powered off, or out of range. Is extension intermittent, and at what temporal grain? This matters directly for the subpoena question, and I suspect no criterion gives a principled grain.
- The monitored resource: the subject uses the resource under surveillance, and its structure and contents have been partly shaped by the observer's anticipated reaction. Whose extended system is it? Does the answer change whether the state may compel its production?

The part I care about most is the conceptual sorting, not the scoring. Several demands in this literature look to me like they might be camp prejudices wearing the clothes of desiderata, and I want them either earned or exposed. Is the parity-style move — that a resource doing the work of an internal process should be counted as cognitive — a genuine requirement, or only a dialectical device that overgenerates once you grant that the skull is not a functional boundary? Is the demand for intrinsic, non-derived content a genuine constraint, or is it the anti-externalist conclusion packed into a criterion, and if it is the latter, what is the non-question-begging version of it? Must verdicts about cognition and verdicts about consciousness coincide, or is the requirement that extension show up phenomenologically an artifact of modelling everything on belief attribution? Must the criterion be applicable in advance by a researcher who does not yet know the answer, and must extension be an empirical discovery rather than something settled by conceptual argument? Is avoidance of cognitive bloat — refusing to count the laboratory, the internet, or the linguistic community as literally part of a mind — a requirement, or conservatism about where persons stop? And must the criterion handle endogenous change, practice and plasticity, with the same machinery it uses for external scaffolding?

Then the result I would most like, and which I want you to test rather than ratify. My suspicion is that some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, and that the list is therefore itself a finding. The trilemma I have in mind is: the criterion must not beg the question by simply equating the cognitive with the intracranial or with whatever plays a causal role; it must return determinate verdicts in the shared, drifting, inscrutable and monitored cases, because the legal purpose needs verdicts; and it must stay metaphysically neutral between functionalist, enactive and embodied accounts of minds. I doubt all three can be had, and I want the argument for the pair you think survives, plus the eight or ten lines of reasoning. There is a second tension I can't locate precisely: symmetry between inner and outer, no bloat, and any purely causal or functional criterion of constitution. Tell me which requirement you think does the most exclusionary work overall, and whether any desideratum on your list is met only by a view nobody actually holds.

Keep the scoring short, under a third of the answer, and a table is fine: a line or two each for a parity-style functionalist account, for the commonsense view that finds coupling where others find constitution, for a diachronic integration or systems-based account, for the enactive view that denies there is a representational core for a criterion to adjudicate, and for an enculturation or cognitive-integration view. I want the weight on the criteria and on the cases behind them. Do not name papers or attach references; argue the positions. About 1,400 words. Finally, since I have to defend my framing in the chapter: if fixing the purpose as legal attribution imports a demand for determinacy and interpersonal attribution that the right theory should refuse, say so plainly, and add a coda of five lines saying which requirement would drop out if the purpose were explanatory — helping a cognitive scientist decide what to model — instead of normative.
