I am in my final year of secondary school, and nearly all the technical vocabulary I have is self-taught from two years of building and submitting tabular machine-learning models rather than from any formal study of philosophy. Recently I fell into reading about the "accuracy-first" or epistemic-utility approach to belief: the view that beliefs come in degrees, that the epistemic value of a set of degrees of belief consists entirely in how near they lie to the truth, and that the familiar norms — that your degrees of belief should hang together like probabilities, and that you should revise them in a Bayesian way — are meant to be *derived* from that standard rather than assumed at the outset. I believe I follow the shape of it, but I keep suspecting that I am quietly reading my own framework into it. So I would like it restated, carefully and without flattery, in the vocabulary I actually use.

My vocabulary: a model that emits a vector of numbers over a fixed set of mutually exclusive classes; loss functions and expected loss; the empirical distribution of a held-out test set as the thing losses are averaged over; calibration and reliability diagrams; a penalty term added to a loss, and the hyperparameter that trades the penalty off against fit; constraints on a hypothesis class; optimisers; ensembling; cross-validation; and a fairly firm conviction that no loss function is mandatory in the abstract — its point is always what you are going to do downstream.

Please translate the following four things one at a time, and for each say whether the mapping is clean, strained, or forced.

1. Degrees of belief and the requirement that they satisfy the probability calculus — as a constraint on what my model is allowed to emit, and specifically as a constraint that is supposed to be *derived* rather than stipulated, which in my world is a strange aspiration, since a constraint is normally either part of the architecture or a penalty whose weight I tune.

2. Strictly proper scoring rules — as loss functions with the property that my expected loss is minimised by emitting my honest predictive distribution rather than a distorted one. Say what the analogue of "strict propriety" is in my terms, and what in my practice it corresponds to, if anything.

3. The dominance argument for probabilism — as a guarantee that holds *before* any averaging over a data distribution: for certain incoherent outputs there is a coherent output that scores better no matter which class turns out to be the true one. Explain what kind of guarantee that is in my vocabulary, given that I almost never evaluate a model against every conceivable labelling of the test set.

4. The argument for updating by a Bayesian rule — as the choice of a training or revision procedure made in advance, evaluated by the accuracy I expect it to yield from where I now stand, rather than by the accuracy of the model I end up with.

One concrete thing I want you to engage with, because it is where my own attempt at translation stalled. I took an incoherent output over three exclusive classes, say (0.7, 0.6, 0.1), and compared it with the rescaled, coherent (0.5, 0.43, 0.07). Under squared-error loss the coherent version looked better whichever class was true; under log loss it looked *worse* whichever class was true, which I did not expect. Tell me whether my arithmetic is right, whether the philosophers' argument is supposed to survive this, and what it means if the "just make it coherent" move works for one honest loss and fails for another.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which claims survive translation unchanged; which only survive if you smuggle in an assumption that is mine rather than theirs; which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — in particular, whether the step from "that output is dominated" to "you ought not to emit it" has any counterpart, given that in my world nothing is obligatory except relative to a loss. And a word-collision I want flagged: "accuracy" for me means the fraction of cases got right, which I gather is not the property their argument rewards.

Finally, give me your verdict on the residue: does it point to something my framework cannot say — a notion of being near the truth that is not just being useful, or a norm not backed by any loss — or does it point to something in the accuracy-first position that is less precise than it appears? Take a side, and say what would move you off it.

Practical constraints: I have no library access, so please do not give me reading lists, bibliographies, or citations, and do not tell me who proved what in which year. If a claim depends on who established it, flag that it is contested or the outcome of a technical debate rather than naming anyone. Where you must use a term I have not given you, define it in one clause. Around 900 to 1,200 words, please, and more than anything else: tell me where the translation fails, not where it succeeds.
