I'm revising a paper on biological function, and I've noticed that my own verdicts on cases don't line up cleanly with either of the two standard families of account — the selected-effects or etiological accounts on one side, the causal-role or organizational accounts on the other. I'd like to know what principle, if any, actually captures the pattern in my verdicts, and how much of the pattern it forces me to give up. I know the literature reasonably well, so please argue for the principle directly rather than pointing me to readings; I don't want a bibliography.

Here are the cases and what I find myself saying about each.

1. A human heart so damaged by disease that it cannot pump at all: its function is still to pump blood. It is malfunctioning, not functionless.
2. The sound that heart makes: not one of its functions, even though a cardiologist can reliably diagnose from it and a fetus appears to be soothed by it.
3. A mutation that arose one generation ago and that genuinely improves heat tolerance in the individual that carries it, and is doing so right now: not a function. The trait is useful, but it isn't yet for anything. I am least confident here.
4. A kidney grown from a patient's own cells on a printed scaffold, built to filter blood: (a) one that filters successfully has the function of filtering blood; (b) one that clots on implantation and never filters at all also has the function of filtering blood. It is a failed kidney, not a functionless lump.
5. An enzyme produced by twenty rounds of directed evolution in a lab, selected at each round for stability at high temperature: its function is to remain stable at high temperature — even though no organism ever benefited from this and no one designed the molecule.
6. The cells of the transmissible facial tumour that spreads through Tasmanian devil populations: cells whose lineage has been under strong selection for rapid proliferation and immune evasion, and which are extremely good at both. They have no function. It is a cancer.
7. The eyeballs of a blind cave fish, degenerated and buried under skin: no function now — even though they were selected for seeing in sighted ancestors. (I'm aware that calling them vestigial eyes seems to attribute the function anyway; I'd like that explained rather than explained away.)
8. Feathers: flight is among their functions now, even though, on the ancestry I accept, they were first selected for insulation and only later co-opted.
9. The protists in a termite's hindgut: their function is to digest cellulose — and I hear that as a function for the termite, not merely for the protist. By contrast, the vitamin K that bacteria in my own gut produce as a by-product of their own metabolism: not a function of theirs, not even for me.
10. The self-sacrificial defence behaviour of a sterile worker ant, which has no reproductive future of its own: its function is defence of the colony. The benefit accrues to a system larger than the bearer.
11. Termite-fishing in a chimpanzee population, socially learned and passed down across generations, with no genetic selection for it anywhere in the story: I go back and forth, and I'll say more below.

My own tentative principle: a trait T of a system S has the function of doing F just in case T's doing F is why T is there — that is, T, or the lineage T belongs to, or the design T was built to satisfy, was selected for doing F, where selection is meant to cover natural selection, artificial selection in the laboratory, and intentional design by an agent, and where the history settles the question rather than what T currently can do. Please test this against the list, and repair it rather than replacing it wholesale if it can be repaired. Two things I specifically want addressed: whether it needs a clause about whose benefit or which containing system is in question (cases 6, 9, 10), and whether it needs a clause about whether the selection has to be ongoing (cases 7 and 8 look like a pair to me, and I can't tell whether I have contradicted myself). I also want to know whether putting selection and design in the same clause papers over a real difference.

Please structure your reply as follows.

1. Bottom line — the recommended principle, stated in one paragraph in enough detail that it could be applied mechanically to a new case. About 150 words.
2. Candidates — two or three candidate principles (a selected-effects account, a causal-role account, and any hybrid you prefer), each in a sentence or two, with a one-paragraph statement of why the recommended one wins. About 250 words.
3. Clause by clause — each clause of the recommended principle, the case above that forces it, and what goes wrong if the clause is dropped. Include the malfunction clause, the beneficiary clause, and whatever handles vestiges. About 450 words.
4. Table of verdicts — one row per case, one to eleven, with my verdict and your principle's verdict side by side, and disagreements flagged. About 200 words.
5. Verdicts I should give up — which of mine the principle cannot accommodate, and, for each, whether you'd revise the verdict or the clause, with the cost of each choice. About 200 words.
6. Predictions — what the principle says about three cases not on my list, at least one of your own construction. Include a de novo protein designed by a computer to catalyse a reaction no natural enzyme catalyses, and say whether it matters that no one foresaw or intended that it also happens to stabilise a membrane. About 250 words.
7. The case I keep going back and forth on — termite-fishing. What your principle predicts, how much of the prediction is carried by a decision you had to make elsewhere rather than by the case itself, and whether this case is a reason to doubt the principle or just a reason to extend it. About 200 words.
