I spent a career in limnology and biogeochemistry, most of it reconstructing past lake chemistry from sediment cores, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a graduate reading group in archaeology. The word they use most often — more often than they define — is *collapse*. I would like a written dialogue that lets me see whether the disagreement I keep half-overhearing is a real one, and if so, where exactly it lives. Please write it as a conversation between two competent, mutually respectful specialists — give them names and a little professional background so it reads like an actual exchange rather than a set of position statements. One of them holds that collapse names a real, recurrent and measurable phenomenon in human history: a rapid and sustained loss of the differentiated institutional capacity a population can exercise, such that people in the generations after the event cannot do things their grandparents routinely did — raise certain monuments, maintain an administrative literacy, sustain long-distance exchange, feed a city of a given size. The other holds that the term does more rhetorical than explanatory work: that what the record shows is almost always transformation into a different scale or idiom of organisation, and that the appearance of loss is produced partly by what survives (monumental stone versus perishable fibre), partly by what archaeologists have historically valued (states, cities, writing), and partly by the grain at which the record is sampled.

Three things in particular keep bothering me, and I would like them to be the spine of the conversation rather than decorative asides. First, the grain problem. In my own field a signal that changes faster than the interval at which you sample it is indistinguishable from a step discontinuity, and a slow decline looks gradual or abrupt depending entirely on the resolution of the proxy. I want the defender of collapse to say why grain-dependence does not make the phenomenon unreal — measurement thresholds are everywhere in science — and I want the sceptic to show concretely how at least one canonical case could be an artefact of resolution rather than a real loss. Second, the cannot-versus-will-not distinction. A population may stop doing something because it has lost the capacity to do it, or because it no longer wants to, or because it has redirected the same capacity elsewhere. I would be grateful if you had them argue about whether the archaeological record can in principle discriminate these, and about what it would take for it to do so. Third, the base-rate problem. Every complex society eventually changes; I cannot see what the control group is supposed to be. If collapse is defined broadly enough, is there any society that has not collapsed? If the defender proposes a contrast class — transformation that preserves or raises complexity versus transformation that lowers it — the sceptic should press on whether complexity itself is a measure that carries the evaluator's preferences inside it, and the defender should have a real reply rather than a shrug.

At some point in the conversation I would like them to take apart a definition I have been toying with, which I offer in the spirit of a hypothesis rather than a settled view: collapse is a sustained, multi-generational reduction in the number of distinct capabilities a population can exercise, where the reduction is not compensated by the acquisition of new capabilities at a different scale or in a different idiom. Both of them should attack it, and each should repair it in the direction their own view needs, acknowledging what the repair costs them.

Please have them work through three specific cases and disagree about the classification of them: the southern lowland Maya centres where the inscribed monuments simply stop and the political centre of gravity moves north; the palace-centred economies of the eastern Mediterranean around the end of the Late Bronze Age, with their administrative literacy and long-distance metal trade; and Rapa Nui, where the statue quarries fall silent and many standing figures are later pulled down. I do not want them to agree on all three. I want at least one case that the defender concedes is better described as reorganisation, and at least one that the sceptic concedes is a genuine collapse — and in each concession I want it stated plainly what the conceder gives up by making it. They should also be made to confront the question of what the entity is that is supposed to collapse: a polity, a population, a set of institutions, a cultural lineage. The Maya-speaking descendants of the lowland centres are still there; it seems to me that this fact has to be answered rather than gestured at, and I am not sure by whom.

Both of them should concede something specific in the course of the exchange and explain the cost of the concession, and each should respond to what the other has actually said rather than to a convenient stand-in. Please end not with a resolution but with each stating, as precisely as possible, one finding of a kind archaeologists already routinely produce — no new technology, no future discoveries of a science-fiction sort — that would most move them toward the other's position. No citations or named authorities are needed; I am after the structure of the arguments, not the literature. Something in the region of fifteen hundred words, in dialogue form, with minimal stage directions and no summarising narration.
