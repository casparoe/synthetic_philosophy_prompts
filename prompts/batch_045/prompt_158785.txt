Something about US campaign finance law keeps bugging me. You can only hand a candidate a few thousand dollars per election, and corporations and unions can't give to campaigns at all — but anyone can spend unlimited amounts to elect that same candidate as long as they don't coordiate with the campaign. So the whole legal architecture turns on the coordination line, as if the same dollars were corrupting when handed over and just speech when spent in parallel. What theory of corruption does that presuppose? On the surface it looks like a quid-pro-quo view, but the disclosure rules dont fit that: forcing spenders to reveal themselves sounds like a worry about voters being misled, or about influence that never involves any bargain at all. And two other pieces pull the other way — foreign nationals are barred from giving or spending even independently, while a candidate can spend unlimited amounts of his own money on his own campaign. Can you reconstruct the theory the practice as a whole is actually committed to, seperate what it strictly needs from what it's merely compatible with, and say what would have to change if we swapped in a view where money damages political equality rather than creating bargains? One more thing I can't settle: does any of this require believing that money buys official action at some exchange rate, or does the whole thing work on a thinner story about access and attention?
