- copycat uses: a term gets stretched onto a new case and quietly inherits the inferential profile of the paradigm case — the same evidential standards, the same stock inferences, the same moral sting — without anyone checking that the new case has the features that earned that profile.
- fluid uses: the term never had a sharp sense anyway; its extension slides with what's at stake in the context, so the disagreement is mostly verbal.

Below are six uses of "manipulation" from that literature (paraphrsed, don't worry about sourcing them). For each: give me (a) a 0–10 score for how much of its bite comes from copycatting rather than from fluidity, and (b) one sentence on whether the slippage is load-bearing — i.e. whether the claim would collapse or visibly change shape if the term were pinned down. Then rank all six, worst slippage first.

1. A nudge manipulates, because it works by bypassing the agent's deliberation rather than engaging it.
2. Recommender systems manipulate users: they exploit weaknesses revealed by the users' own past behaviour.
3. Advertising manipulates by creating preferences the consumer would not otherwise have had.
4. The state manipulates citizens whenever it builds a choice architecture it could not justify to them in public terms.
5. Ulysses-style self-binding is a case of manipulating one's later self.
6. A speaker manipulates an audience when her message is framed so that a false claim looks obviously true.

Give me your own read rather than a survey of the field — no citations needed — and write it in British English.
