I am the senior linguist on a four-person documentation project for a severely endangered language — I will call it Tavan, a pseudonym — in an inland region I will also leave unnamed. Six fluent speakers remain, all over seventy; perhaps forty people have partial competence; there are no trained linguists and no reliable bilingual informants among them, and we work through a contact variety that is itself poorly described. My team is two linguists, one community researcher with a week of training and no theoretical background, and a volunteer who rotates every six weeks.

The stakes are unusual. Everything we gloss goes into a community-controlled archive, and from there into two downstream products that pull in different directions. One is a learner's dictionary and a set of school readers, where the community researcher and the elders' families have an effective veto over what appears. The other is a set of recorded statements from elders about places, boundaries and kinship, taken for a land claim, where the same glosses will be read as evidence by people who have never met the speakers and who will not read our caveats. So a gloss can be simultaneously too domesticating for one purpose and too cautious for the other.

What I need from you is a protocol, not an essay. I have read the literature on radical interpretation and I don't need it summarized or cited; I need it turned into something my team can run. Specifically: we finish a recording session and, that evening, by lamplight, offline, on paper, in about twenty minutes per cluster of related utterances, the person who was in the room has to work through an ordered set of questions and come out with a decision about how the utterance is to be glossed and tagged. Any entry that attributes a belief we take to be false, or that is tagged indeterminate, comes to me for sign-off. Assume the person running it has never heard of interpretation theory; every term of art has to be cashed out in instructions a careful non-specialist can follow.

There are five theoretical points I care about and want to see survive the translation into a checklist, together with the places where I suspect they cannot:

Charity. The protocol should start from the charitable reading and make departures expensive. But I want this handled as a global constraint on our whole manual for that speaker rather than sentence by sentence: give me a small error budget, denominated somehow, that a speaker's total attributions can draw down over a run of sessions, and a rule for what happens when it is exhausted — my instinct is that exhaustion should be read as evidence against our background assumptions rather than against the speaker, and I want you to tell me whether that is the right way round. Each withdrawal from the budget should have to be paid for by naming a specific mechanism that would explain the error in that particular case — poor light, misheard prompt, a known gap in the elder's information, intoxication, a leading question of ours — rather than by a general appeal to "they might be wrong."

The competing formulation of the same idea, sometimes called the principle of humanity, which asks us to make the speaker's attitudes the ones someone in their situation would have rather than the ones we take to be true. These two pull apart on our material, and the protocol has to tell the runner which one to follow when they do, and what to write down when they diverge.

Triangulation as the condition on reference. I want a voucher requirement: a term gets its reference fixed in the archive only where the speaker and a member of the team can be shown to have responded jointly to the same distal thing under at least two perceptually different conditions — seen and later heard, visited and later pointed to from a distance, present and then absent. Places that no longer exist, dead relatives, and things named only in narrative will fail this, and I need the protocol to say what we do with the large class of terms that fail.

Holism. Because a belief attribution and a meaning attribution cannot be settled independently, no verdict should be returned on a single sentence in isolation. Require a neighborhood test: propose the gloss, then substitute it into a fixed number of other already-attested sentences containing the same form and see what it does to them. Tell me how many is enough and what counts as a failure.

Prior theory and passing theory. Some of what we record is nonce — a metaphor, a joke, a malapropism, a kin term extended to an animal or a government official, an avoidance register used in the presence of a particular relative. The protocol must distinguish a gloss that goes into the manual from a gloss that is valid only for that session and that moment, and it must say what expires, when, and on whose authority.

I also want the protocol to be honest about itself. For each step, mark where a judgment is being dressed up as a procedure: where the "test" really amounts to someone's sense of what is plausible. Mark the points where the protocol hands the decision to a named person because the question is one of values — about what the community should see in print, or what we owe a speaker — rather than one of evidence. And mark where it stops with a no.

Then run it on three cases from our own material.

The first is from a land-claim statement. Speaker MT, a woman in her eighties, describing the boundary of her family's country: our first-pass gloss is that a rock standing in the water marks where the line ends, and that the country below it belongs to his brother. The rock was submerged when the dam was built roughly forty years ago; no one on the team has seen it and it cannot be visited; there are three candidate rocks in the old survey photographs. This is the sentence the claimants' submission turns on, and I already suspect the honest verdict is one I do not want.

The second is a causal claim I don't believe. Speaker PK says, in our first-pass gloss, that when the fish run it is because the old man under the falls has sung to them, and that if no one leaves tobacco there the fish will not come. Run the charitable reading, then the neighborhood test, then tell me whether the right move is to re-gloss the verb, to attribute a false belief and pay for it, or to tag it as something other than a causal claim. Be alert here: I can see that re-glossing until the sentence comes out true is the easiest way for this protocol to lie to me, and I want that failure mode named if it applies.

The third is a nonce use with a disagreement attached. In a session with a child present, a partial speaker uses the form normally glossed as mother's brother to address a dog, and later uses it of a parks official. Our community researcher says she is joking and asks that it not be written down. The recording is already in the archive. Decide what gets recorded, at what level of commitment, and who signs off.

After the three cases, tell me briefly what the protocol cannot do: the classes of question it is guaranteed to get wrong on this kind of material, the ways it will systematically distort what speakers say, and the point at which, in your view, it stops being a procedure and becomes a form for recording someone's intuition.

Format. Open with under 250 words on the translation itself: which of the five points above you kept intact, which you had to distort or drop to make a checklist, and what the distortion costs. Then give the deliverable as two JSON objects, and do not pad them with prose.

The first object, keyed "protocol", is an array of steps in the order they are to be run, each with these fields: step_id as an integer; name, a short label; question, the sentence the runner says or reads out; sufficient_answer, what counts as a pass in one or two sentences; fail_action, one of "stop_hold_indeterminate", "stop_refer_to_senior", "continue", or "log_and_continue"; budget_cost, an integer, zero where none; who_runs_it, one of "any_team_member", "linguist_only", or "senior_signoff"; and judgment_flag, one sentence identifying what is really being decided by taste at this step, or the string "none".

The second object, keyed "cases", is an array of three case objects, one per case above, each with these fields: case_id, one of "MT_boundary", "PK_fish", "nonce_kin"; source_line, the utterance as we first glossed it; context, one sentence on who spoke, to whom, and when; steps_run, an array of objects with step_id, finding, and passed as a boolean; blocking_step, the step_id at which the case stopped, or null; verdict, one of "adopt_gloss", "adopt_with_caveat", "session_gloss_only", "hold_indeterminate", or "refer_to_senior"; budget_spent, an integer; handed_to, the role that decides what the protocol did not, or null; residual_judgment, one sentence on the judgment the protocol could not remove; and unwelcome, one sentence on whether this is a verdict my team will resist and why, or the string "none".

Close with a short section headed "Limits", in prose, of no more than 200 words.

Use American English throughout — spelling, punctuation, and idiom. Do not suggest readings, cite anything, or rely on material you would need to look up; the whole point is that this has to work where there is no library and no signal.
