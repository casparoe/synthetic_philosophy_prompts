hey — our reading group is doing Wittgenstein's last manuscript (the one published posthumously as On Certainty) and we've all been told to circulate a one-page 'where I stand' note before the final session. Mine is below and I'd rather find out now that it doesnt hang together than in front of nine people.

1. Hinge commitments are genuine propositions — they have content, they're the sort of thing you can be certain of — but they are not the sort of thing you know or believe, and demanding grounds for one isn't a bad argument, it's a move that fails to get a grip.
2. No hinge rests on the others either. The system as a whole holds or it doesn't; there's no further fact about which single proposition is load-bearing.
3. Different communities and periods run on different hinges, and when they do neither side is making a mistake — there's no error to be found in someone whose hinges differ from ours.
4. But hinges can shift within a lifetime (conversion, the riverbed moving), and some shifts are improvements: the person who stops treating horoscopes as evidence has gotten epistemically better, not merely different.
5. Which propositions are in fact someone's hinges is an empirical question about what they do, not about what they'd avow — an anthropologist could get it right or get it wrong.
6. Radical Cartesian skepticism is a performative failure, not an alternative set of hinges we're obliged to tolerate.
7. A bit I made up: I've been calling it 'hinge drift' when a proposition starts life as an ordinary empirical result in some research field and, over two or three generations, ends up functioning as scaffolding — grad students who ask for the original evidence are told they've misunderstood the question, the studies stop being cited and eventually can't be reconstructed — and then the field quietly drops the proposition sixty years later and nobody can say what was wrong with it. I want hinge drift to be a real phenomenon and also to want to say that it's sometimes how a field goes wrong.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest revision for each? My guess is the trouble is in 3 and 4, or 3 and 6, but I genuinely can't tell which one I'd have to drop. Please rank the tensions by how bad they are, say for each what giving up that commitment would cost me (e.g. if I loose 3, do I loose my reply to the skeptic?), and then tell me whether there's one revision that cleans up the most at once. Also flag any pair that dissolves on inspection so I don't panic about it. One thing I want to keep whatever happens: I don't want to end up saying hinges are just very confidently held ordinary empirical beliefs. No need for citations or reading suggestions — just the audit.
