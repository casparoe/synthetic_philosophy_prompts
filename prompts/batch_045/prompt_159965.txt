I am writing a novel narrated in the first person singular by an ant colony — the Aunt Hillary conceit, a colony that speaks, remembers, and holds grudges, and whose member ants stand to it roughly as cells stand to me. Before I commit several hundred pages to that voice, I want the specification rather than the verdict: what would an adequate account of colony mentality have to deliver for it to be true, and not merely serviceable to a novelist, that the colony itself has a mind?

Please write the desiderata. For each one, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a taste, and mark it non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp. The purpose I am asking you to fix adequacy against is this: the theory must be good enough to tell me whether a first-person narrator of this kind can have a genuine standpoint — something it is like to be her — and if not, what voice I should use instead.

Distinctions I suspect the list must respect, offered as hints and not as constraints you are bound by:

- Having beliefs, desires, and intentions at all versus merely being predictively described as if one did. What would settle which side a colony falls on?
- Having a mind at all versus having a standpoint. A first-person narrator presupposes the second, and I do not know whether anything less licenses it.
- Unity. The colony has no central controller and no channel any single ant monopolizes. Is a bounded, integrated subject a real requirement, or a prejudice imported from animals with brains?
- Grain and tempo. The colony's deliberation runs through pheromone and takes minutes; its parts think in fractions of a second. Is slowness disqualifying, and does a theory owe us a principled answer about whose tempo counts?
- Memory. In my draft much of what the colony "remembers" is written into the architecture of the nest itself, outside every body. Is that memory at all, or a category mistake I should stop making on page?
- Nesting and persistence. The ants are themselves plausible subjects, and the colony survives the complete replacement of its members. Does the account owe us a verdict about how many minds are in the nest, and about whether the colony's survival is genuine continuity or mere succession?
- Error. A colony can act on a trail that leads nowhere. Must a theory let it be wrong, deceived, or irrational, rather than only broken?

Cases I want handled, including two from my manuscript:

1. A swarm settles on a new nest site when enough scouts accumulate at one candidate, although no individual insect ever compares the options directly.
2. Workers and a queen pull the colony's resources toward incompatible ends. Does the colony have one will, and is its "intention" anything more than a resultant?
3. Fission: the colony divides into two successor colonies with equal claim to her. Where did the subject go, and is "both, from now on" an answer a theory may give?
4. Two neighboring colonies are joined by a single shared trail, so that recruitment, foraging, and repair run as one traffic system while the queens and brood remain separate; the trail is later cut. One mind, two, or a third? What principle decides, and not by stipulation?
5. A parasite counterfeits the colony's recruitment signal and redirects her labor. Is being manipulable in this way something an adequate theory must explain as deception?

Then the parts I care about most: which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive candidates; whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — in particular whether a standpoint, a distributed subject with no bottleneck, and minds at both levels can be had together; and if they cannot, which one I should abandon and what that implies for the narration. A short scorecard of three or four of the standard positions against the list is welcome, but keep the weight on the criteria and their cases.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and do not restate my question. Please write in American English.
