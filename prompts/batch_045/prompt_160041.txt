I am writing a paper defending a weak knowability thesis, and I have been handed an argument against it that reaches conclusions nobody should want. I cannot locate the move that does the work. I would be grateful for a diagnosis, written in British English, set out as: which step you reject, why that step looked sound when I first read it, and what each of the available alternatives costs me.

My thesis, call it K: for every true proposition p, it is metaphysically possible that some agent or other knows p. Two clarifications that matter to me. First, the possibility is not anchored to any agent in advance: the knower may vary from case to case, so K does not say that there is some single agent who could know every truth. Second, the knower need not be anyone who actually exists, and the knowing need not happen at any actual time; merely possible agents in merely possible circumstances are allowed to do the work. I take it for granted that some truths are in fact unknown — truths about, say, the exact contents of a diary burnt unread, or about what happened in some unobserved corner of the deep past — and I do not want to give that up.

The argument I was given:

(1) Assume K. Let p be any truth that no agent in fact knows; by the datum there are such truths.
(2) Form the proposition q: p is true and no agent knows p. Given the choice of p, q is true.
(3) K applies to q, since q is true. So q is knowable: in some possible world w, at some time t, some agent a knows q.
(4) Knowledge distributes over conjunction, and knowledge is factive. So if a knows q, then a knows that p is true, and a knows that no agent knows p.
(5) From the second half of (4) together with factivity, no agent knows p; from the first half, a knows p. So in w at t, a knows p and no agent knows p.
(6) Contradiction. So q is not knowable after all; so, by K, q is not true; so, p being true, p is not unknown. Since p was an arbitrary unknown truth, there are no unknown truths: every truth is known.
(7) And nothing in (2) to (6) used anything peculiar to knowledge. Run the same reasoning with any operator that is factive and distributes over conjunction — perceives, proves, conclusively verifies, correctly recalls — and the modest thesis that every truth is perceivable, or provable, or conclusively verifiable, collapses in the same way into the claim that every truth is perceived, or proved, or conclusively verified.

My friend treats (7) as a point in the argument's favour: a result about the whole family of factive operators is, she says, more secure, not less. I am not convinced — I suspect the generality is a symptom rather than a credential, but I cannot say why.

One repair I tried, which she also rejects: index the knowing to times, so that K says only that every truth is knowable at some time or other. She reruns the argument with q as: p is true and at no time does any agent know p. Her conclusion is then weaker but still, she claims, intolerable — not that every truth is known now, but that every truth gets known at some time, so that no truth is permanently unknown, which rules out the diary and the deep past after all.

Please tell me which step you reject and why that step looked sound. Then price the alternatives for me. In particular I want your assessment of two moves I have been offered — restricting the quantifier in K so that it ranges only over propositions statable without epistemic vocabulary, and denying that knowledge distributes over conjunction on the ground that finite knowers are not logically omniscient — together with any further move you think is available and I have not named. I would also like to know whether the tensed rerun has the same source as the original collapse or whether the two come apart, and, if they do, which of the two conclusions I can live with. Finally: whichever fix you recommend, tell me what K is worth once it is in place — whether it still excludes the possibility of truths that are in principle beyond any inquirer, which was the point of wanting it.

Please write your answer in British English.
