I'm a fourth-year political theory PhD student, and on Thursday I'm giving a paper at the work-in-progress series we run jointly with the policy institute that shares our building — so the room will be maybe eight people: four or five policy researchers who are basically consequentialists and fairly EA-adjacent (they think in expected value, cost-effectiveness, counterfactuals, "what would you do instead"), two institutional economists who like second-best arguments about not being able to fix one distortion at a time, and one senior person in my department who reads Rawls sympathetically and thinks Burkean conservatism is mostly a vocabulary for not helping people. My thesis is that Chesterton's fence — the thought that you shouldn't remove some long-standing arrangement until you can say what it was doing there — is best read as an epistemic principle rather than a moral one, and more specifically: that established practices carry information in the form of constraints rather than beliefs, that this information is produced slowly by a trial-and-error process we mostly never see and is destroyed quickly when the practice is changed, and that therefore there is a defeasible presumption against reform whose strength scales with how much tacit, practice-embedded knowledge is plausibly in play, how irreversible the change is, and how long the practice has been running. The bit I think is actually mine is the asymmetry claim: I argue the presumption is grounded in the structure of the evidence-generating process and not in any asymmetry in the value function, which is what I say distinguishes it from status quo bias, loss aversion and the omission bias wearing a costume. I also lean on a Burkean point about the unit of evaluation being the practice together with its history rather than the policy considered at a time — the dead and the unborn as stakeholders in an institution nobody currently remembers choosing — and I add what I've been calling the repair blind spot: reformers score a practice by the goods it currently produces while being structurally blind to the failure modes it silently suppresses, which you can only discover by removing it and waiting. (A friend who does development economics and has watched a lot of well-meant reforms fail in the field is definately the reason I started writing this.)

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view: the seven or eight objections I'll actually get from this room, roughly in the order they'll arrive, each phrased the way that subgroup would phrase it rather than the way a generic philosophy audience would — I'd guess the consequentialists' version is about the expected value of information and about who bears the burden of proof, the economists' is about general equilibrium and partial reform, and the Rawlsian's is about whose interests the practice was serving in the first place and whether stability is even a virtue. For each objection give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, something I should concede up front rather than fight, and a note on where my reply is soft. Two I especially need: the objection that the tacit-knowledge move is unfalsifiable, that any practice whatsoever can be said to encode tacit knowledge and so my principle can never tell anyone to go ahead with anything; and the harder version of that one, which comes as a concrete challenge — name one reform you say the principle would have blocked that we now know was a mistake, and one it wouldn't have blocked.
