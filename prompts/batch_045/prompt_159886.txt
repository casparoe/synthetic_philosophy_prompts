I write about science and ideas, and I am working on a piece about the litigation now surrounding machine-learning and gene-editing patents. I keep running into a feature of how courts read patent documents that I cannot tell is incoherent or merely unfamiliar, and I would like the theory behind it set out explicitly.

In US patent practice a patent is really two things bound together: a long written description of the invention, and, at the end, a numbered set of claims. Only the claims define what the patentee owns. Their stated job is to give the public notice of where the monopoly stops. When a dispute arises, the meaning of the claim language is settled by a judge rather than a jury, and it is fixed as of the filing date: the words are said to mean what a hypothetical person of ordinary skill in the field would have understood them to mean at that time. What the inventor actually had in mind is officially beside the point; his own account of his intentions, and expert testimony about usage, count as extrinsic material and are supposed to be consulted, if at all, only after the intrinsic record — the claims, the description, and the back-and-forth with the patent office — has failed to settle the question.

Yet the same practice is author-sensitive in several ways that seem to pull the other way. If the written description expressly gives a term a special definition, that stipulation governs even when it departs from the ordinary technical meaning. If, while the application was pending, the applicant narrowed a claim or argued a limitation in order to get the patent granted, he is afterward barred from arguing that the thing he gave up comes back in under the doctrine of equivalents, which lets a patentee reach a device that is not within the literal words but performs substantially the same function in substantially the same way — though he can escape that bar if the equivalent in question was unforeseeable when he amended, which in practice means that the patent reaches devices built on technology that did not exist when the text was written. So the text is read through the eyes of a fictional reader at a frozen moment, but the rights it confers are expanded past the text toward the invention itself and contracted by the author's own drafting choices.

What theory of meaning does this presuppose? Is it genuinely committed to the claim that a text's content is fixed by the conventional understanding of a competent reader at the time of writing and not by authorial intention, or is that only a device that serves the notice function while the rest of the practice quietly relies on intention? Which parts are strictly presupposed, which merely fit comfortably, and where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that an intentionalist, a textualist, and a reader-relative theory could each license it? Can it be rendered coherent by separating the semantic content of the claims from the scope of the legal right they ground, and by treating the skilled reader as a normative construct rather than a prediction about any actual audience — and does the fact that the text's author is a committee of inventors, attorneys, and an examiner who negotiated it change what the practice can coherently presuppose about authorship? Finally, what would have to change if the anti-intentionalist reading were replaced by a thoroughgoing one, so that inventor testimony became central and meaning ran with what the inventors meant, and what would have to change under the opposite replacement, where only the ordinary meaning of the words counted and equivalents, stipulations, and estoppel were abandoned?
