I work in patient safety at a large teaching hospital, and I'm on the committee that is rewriting our incident-response protocol. The old protocol assumed, without ever quite saying so, that the right response to a mistake is guilt: you did a thing, you feel bad about it, you disclose it, and the system gets fixed. But the cases that actually reach my committee are increasingly ones where guilt looks like the wrong instrument, and after a long argument with a friend who facilitates restorative-justice conferences I've started to suspect we have the two emotions backwards in a systematic way. The tentative principle I keep coming back to is this: guilt is the fitting emotion when the agent's problem is that she has done something and knows it; shame is the fitting emotion when the agent's problem is that she is, or is becoming, a certain sort of person, or when her position has insulated her so thoroughly that the ordinary machinery of guilt never gets any purchase on her. I'd like that principle tested against my verdicts, repaired where it breaks, and pushed on the cases below.

Here are the verdicts, in the order I thought of them. (1) A night-shift nurse draws up the wrong dose, catches herself before it reaches the patient, and no one else ever learns of it; she lies awake for two nights. Guilt is exactly right here, and it would be wrong of us to drag her into any kind of public forum. (2) A senior surgeon is routinely cruel to residents; reporting channels exist but nobody uses them because he writes their evaluations. He isn't troubled at all. What is called for is shame — his being seen by his peers as the kind of man who does this — and I don't think guilt is even available to him. (3) A fifteen-year-old is caught shoplifting by her mother; her distress is entirely about having been seen, and it evaporates among friends who admire her for it. That isn't the good kind of shame, it's fear of exposure wearing shame's clothes, and what she needs is guilt. (4) A mid-level billing manager approved a coding practice that overbilled insurers; the practice predated her, everyone did it, nothing was at stake for her personally, and no identifiable patient was harmed. She feels nothing. Guilt seems like the wrong ask — there is no discrete act for her to feel guilty about — and what would be fitting is a sense of being someone who does that sort of thing. I hold this one least confidently. (5) A resident from a family where a mediocre evaluation is a catastrophe for the whole family's standing feels annihilating shame over an ordinary bad review. That shame is a malfunction, not a moral achievement — compare (2), where shame is exactly right, and I don't know what separates them. (6) Our weekly morbidity-and-mortality conference, where clinicians stand up and name their own errors in front of colleagues: good, and it produces far more disclosure than the anonymous hotline ever did. (7) A school I know of where children stand in assembly and confess their misbehavior to the entire school: abusive, and it predictably produces theatre from some children and hollowed-out humiliation in others. (6) and (7) look structurally identical to me and I can feel them coming apart without being able to say why. (8) A friend who, when I tell her something I've done wrong, immediately confesses something worse that she once did: her guilt is genuine but it's doing the wrong job, recruiting me as an audience for her.

What I'd like from you is a principle, or two or three competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could tell my committee what to do on Monday: the case that motivates each clause, what the principle says about cases I haven't listed, and an honest account of which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. The unlisted cases I most want predictions on are the collective and second-order ones — a hospital's own apology for a decade of avoidable harm to a specific group of patients; a clinician who feels annihilating shame over a harm she neither caused nor could have prevented; a clinician who feels nothing at all and delivers a flawless apology; and someone who feels guilt about a fault that isn't hers. Then the question I actually care about, which is whether a culture can choose: on your account, are there levers that reliably move a group toward one emotion or the other — who has power over whom, whether disclosure is anonymous or public, whether the physical setup lets people watch each other work, what the institution treats as the mark of a good person — or is choosing simply the wrong description of what a culture does, so that the most we can do is alter the scaffolding and wait? I'd rather give up (4), or my confidence about (6), than end up with something that retrofits everything.
