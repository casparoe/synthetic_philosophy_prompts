I am the patient-safety officer at a large teaching hospital, and I own the response our institution makes to individual clinicians after an adverse event. Every week my office handles one or two events serious enough to need a written response; for each one we do three things — a private conversation with the clinician involved, a note that goes into the event file and is read by the department chief and our legal office, and, in about a quarter of cases, something collective on the unit: a morbidity-and-mortality conference, a huddle, an all-staff email. I have ten working days and about forty minutes of my own time per case, the note needs the department chief's signature, and anything that reads as punitive gets contested, so the wording carries real weight. A few years ago the hospital bought into a just-culture framework, which on the ward has collapsed into the slogan that we target systems rather than people, and I have come to think that slogan is now doing damage: it is used to justify saying nothing at all to individuals. I see two failure modes. Some clinicians respond to our silence by vanishing — they stop reporting near-misses, go quiet in debriefs, get angry at anyone who mentions the case. Others have plainly learned the script: fluent apology, visible distress, no change in practice, flat event rates over three years. My reading is that we are recruiting one moral emotion when we want the other, but I have no way to tell, in advance, which one a given response will recruit. My team drafted a crude three-question rubric — is the object of what we say the act, the role, or the person? what does the clinician most want to do in the next hour, tell someone, repair it, or be somewhere else? would the same words land differently with five colleagues in the room? — and I want that either repaired or replaced with something I can defend to a chief of surgery.

What I am asking for is a decision procedure my office can actually run, grounded in what is genuinely known about the difference between guilt and shame rather than in vibes. Please give me the gates in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops and says escalate to HR or fitness-to-practice, and the points at which it must hand the decision to a human being because the question is one of values rather than facts. It has to fit on two laminated pages and be runnable in forty minutes by a rotating resident with a half-day of training, so I need you to tell me which distinctions must survive that simplification and which can honestly be dropped. Several things make this harder than a checklist. We cannot aim at an emotion directly; we can only aim at the situation, the words, and the norms, so the procedure has to say what it is really predicting. I also want it to be honest about the possibility that shame is not simply the bad twin here: we have all seen clinicians who feel nothing and change nothing, and well-placed exposure sometimes seems to be the only thing that reaches them, so I would rather that possibility be honored than assumed away. And I keep hearing that this unit has a culture of blame or that another has a reporting culture, as if culture were a dial my office can turn; I want to know which of the levers I actually control — who is in the room, whether the name is attached, whether the debrief is public, whether we publish our own reporting rates, how fast we reply — really move which emotion gets recruited, and which are pure theatre.

Please then run the finished procedure on three real shapes of case, including at least one where the answer is one I will not like and you should say why you think I will not like it. The first: a night-shift resident who mis-programmed an infusion pump; the patient came to no harm, she reported it herself within the hour, and her attending says she is a wreck and is talking about leaving medicine altogether. The second: a senior, well-liked, high-volume surgeon whose theatre team has documented for years that he humiliates nurses and skips the safety checklist; this event is a retained swab, and three earlier near-misses with his name on them produced no response at all. The third: an agency nurse who was on the unit for six shifts, was involved in a medication error, and is now gone — no continuing relationship, no standing in the community, nothing we say can follow her. That last case matters to me because I suspect it shows something about whether either emotional lever needs an audience whose regard is worth having, and about what is left when there is none. Please also handle the hard cases the procedure must not dodge: the clinician who shows every marker of guilt and does not change, the clinician who seems to feel nothing at all, and the case where the hospital's own reputational interest and the clinician's good come apart, since that is exactly where I mistrust my own judgment. Finally, an ethicist on our consultation service says the guilt/shame distinction is an artifact of English and that I should be talking about accountability instead of emotions; tell me whether the procedure should be able to survive that objection. For each worked case, end with a single line in exactly this form: Case N — Verdict: GUILT-LEANING / SHAME-LEANING / NEITHER-STRUCTURAL / ESCALATE. Close the whole answer with one sentence beginning Summary: that states, in a form I could read aloud to my director, what the procedure is and what it cannot do.
