Hume's problem: our inductive inferences rest on the assumption that the unobserved will resemble the observed — call this the Uniformity Principle. Hume gives two arguments that this principle cannot be justified. First, it can't be justified by demonstrative reasoning, since there is no contradiction in supposing the course of nature changes: the sun failing to rise tomorrow is distinctly conceivable, and nothing that is distinctly conceivable can be ruled out by demonstrative reasoning alone. Second, it can't be justified by probable reasoning, because any such argument would itself have to presume that what has held in the past will hold in the future, which is the very point at issue — so the argument is circular. Hume's conclusion is that induction rests on custom and habit rather than on reason: there is no reason to expect the sun to rise tomorrow beyond our animal propensity to expect it.

(a) Take a finite input space X with N points and binary labels. Consider all 2^N possible target functions from X to {0,1}, and put a uniform distribution over them. Draw a training set of m < N points i.i.d. from some fixed distribution over X and hand the learner the labels. For the remaining N − m points, the off-training-set points, the expected error of every learning algorithm — the expectation being taken with respect to the uniform distribution over targets — is exactly 1/2, whatever the loss function and whatever the distribution over inputs. So no algorithm beats any other: nearest neighbour, a neural network, and anti-induction, which predicts the opposite of what the data suggest, all have the same expected off-training-set performance. In particular no algorithm does better than chance. The result generalises to regression and to optimization: averaged over all cost functions, every optimizer is equal. There is no free lunch.

(c) I'll argue that the NFL theorems do vindicate Hume, and in a stronger form than is usually noticed. Hume's point was that induction has no justification that is independent of assumptions about the world. The NFL theorems show the same thing formally: when we average over all possible worlds, which is what averaging over all possible target functions represents, every inductive method does exactly as well as every other, including the methods that do the opposite of what induction recommends. So if we have no prior information about which world we are in, there is nothing to choose between induction and anti-induction, and the rational credence to assign to the sun rising tomorrow is 1/2.

The way out is to give up the idea that we can be assumption-free, and that is not as bad as it sounds. What we need is an inductive bias, and the natural one is simplicity: prefer the simplest hypothesis consistent with the data. This is not the kind of assumption Hume's argument targets, because simplicity is a property of our hypotheses and our representations rather than a substantive empirical claim about what the world is like; so unlike, say, assuming that the target function is periodic, it costs us nothing to assume it. Occam's razor is a priori, and it is what breaks the tie the NFL theorem leaves us with. And we know it works: in the actual world simple hypotheses have generally turned out to generalize better than complicated ones, which gives us empirical evidence for the simplicity bias. Since that evidence concerns the track record of methods rather than the uniformity of nature, it isn't the circular reasoning Hume objected to.

So the NFL theorems settle the matter. Hume was right that induction cannot be justified by reason, and the theorems show why: any justification would have to smuggle in an assumption about the world, and the theory of learning is in the business of making those assumptions explicit rather than of justifying them.
