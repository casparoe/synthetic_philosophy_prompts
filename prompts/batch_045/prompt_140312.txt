I sit on the program committee of a private foundation that has committed roughly $30M over five years to a single deliverable: a cardinal, interpersonally comparable measure of momentary wellbeing that a national health ministry could place alongside QALYs in cost-effectiveness analysis. Most of the grantees are psychologists and economists. The budget also covers about eight philosopher-years, and my job is to say what those are for. I don't want a survey of the field and I don't want a reading list; I want a defended ordering of a handful of questions, with your criteria stated and the trade-offs between them made explicit.

The area I think the money has to go into is the heterogeneity problem: what, if anything, the pleasure of a cold drink on a hot day, of finishing a proof, of a passage of music, of sex, and of another person's humiliation have in common in virtue of which each is a pleasure — and what follows for measurement if the honest answer is that they share nothing experiential.

Take the following as given from our grantees' data. It is stipulated for the purposes of this question, so please don't spend your time querying it:

(a) Within a single subject, momentary pleasantness ratings across these very different activities load on one factor, and that factor predicts choices in a reinforcement-learning task better than any activity-specific measure does.
(b) Across subjects, the rating scales appear to be related by monotone transformations that differ from person to person, so aggregation requires either a stipulation about whose scale fixes the unit or a move to something subjects never report at all.
(c) The best available cross-person predictor of those ratings is not anything subjects can describe about how the experience feels. It is a measurable signature: the extent to which attention is captured by, and repeatedly returns to, the current activity, together with the disposition to re-choose it under matched conditions.
(d) One of our econometricians has proposed cutting the knot by defining the unit directly in terms of that signature in a representative agent, and dropping the claim that the resulting number tracks how anyone feels.

The committee is split roughly three ways. Retentionists hold that there is a thin common experiential core — a hedonic tone available to introspection — and that we should learn to measure it directly. Substitutionists hold that the right move is to keep the causal-cum-functional role and let the signature be the target, treating pleasure as either a stipulative label for whatever plays that role or as a term we should retire. Abandonmentists take the heterogeneity result to show that pleasure was never the right target and that we should be measuring something else: desire satisfaction, reflective endorsement, or a pluralist list.

What I want from you:

1. Rank the four or five open questions you think these eight philosopher-years should actually go to, and defend the ordering. For each, say what it is upstream of, whether it is tractable in five years by people doing conceptual work in dialogue with this dataset rather than needing a decade of new empirical work, and — this matters most to me — whether its answer would change the design of the instrument. Some questions are load-bearing for the theory and inert for the deliverable; if you think any of the committee's favourites are like that, say so plainly.

2. Tell me which alleged implications of heterogeneity are overrated or don't follow. The candidates I keep hearing: that if pleasures share no common feel then welfare isn't pleasure; that interpersonal comparison becomes impossible in principle rather than merely underdescribed; that any valence measure is therefore an arbitrary stipulation, so one unit is as good as another; that hedonism quietly collapses into a desire-based theory; that proposal (d) is simply a reductio of the entire project.

3. Say whether the retentionist/substitutionist disagreement is, in whole or in part, merely verbal — and if it is, whether that defuses it or merely relocates the real question, and where the relocated question then sits in your ranking.

4. There is a validation trap I want addressed directly. Any candidate measure has to be checked against something, and the obvious candidates are subjects' reports, which (b) undermines, or the very theory the measure was supposed to operationalise. Is there a way out of the circle, and does answering that belong in the philosophy portion of this budget or in the empirical portion?

Finally, be explicit about what would move you: what result or argument over the next two years would push a question up or down your list, and what would make you abandon a line of work as unanswerable at this scale.

Conclude with a single line, on its own, of exactly the form \answer{...}, containing the one question you would fund first, in ten words or fewer. Put nothing after it.
