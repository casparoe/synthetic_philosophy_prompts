I direct the editorial standards desk at a large public broadcaster. Several hundred items a day pass our sub-editors, who have no training in linguistics or rhetoric and roughly four minutes each, and we have to decide, repeatedly, whether a sentence is doing its job or whether it is the kind of sentence Orwell was describing when he wrote that "the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts. The point is that the process is reversible." We have tried operating on his closing list of rules — prefer the short word, cut every word you can, prefer the active to the passive, avoid the ready-made figure of speech, and break the rules rather than write something barbarous — and it has not worked: applied mechanically it produces a banned-words list, it flattens technical terms of art that carry real precision, and it gives sub-editors no way to distinguish prose that is unclear from prose that is evasive, or prose that is evasive from prose about a world nobody understands yet.

What I want from you is a procedure, not a style guide. Please turn Orwell's diagnosis into an ordered set of tests my desk can run:

- Give the steps in the order they are to be run, and for each say what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as a failure. Separate the failure modes the tests are meant to catch. I suggest at least these, but correct me if the taxonomy is wrong: agentless drift (the nominalization or passive that leaves nobody doing anything to anyone), pre-packed predicates (compound phrases that arrive with a conclusion built in, of the "robust and proportionate response" sort), scope fog (unquantified or hedged claims — "a number of," "may have been," "in respect of"), and empty fluency, which is our newest problem: roughly a third of what reaches us is now drafted with text generators or by press offices, and it is short-worded, active-voiced, grammatically clean, and says nothing that could be contradicted. If you use a test like "can you construct the sentence that would deny this?", say so explicitly and say where it is unreliable.
- Mark the points where the procedure stops and returns "leave this alone," and the points where it hands the decision to a named senior editor because the question is one of values or editorial position rather than language — in particular, the choice between an official designation and the plain term for the same thing, where neutrality and importing someone else's euphemism can look identical.
- Be honest about which steps are close to mechanical and which are judgment calls dressed up as mechanics. My sub-editors will otherwise treat the whole thing as mechanical.
- Say what the procedure cannot do. I am especially worried about the gap between clarity and truth: making a lie grammatical and concrete is not obviously an improvement, and a purely formal procedure cannot tell the difference.

Then run it on the following four, giving the reasoning at each step rather than just the outcome:

A. Our own copy: "Following a thorough review of the incident, it has been determined that a number of procedural failures occurred in respect of the handling of the complaint."
B. A verbatim quotation from a government statement we are reproducing: "Ministers are considering a robust and proportionate package of measures to address irregular migration."
C. Our own early wire copy: "Emergency services say a number of people have been injured at the refinery." Our instinct is to insist on a figure; I suspect the procedure will tell us to stop, and I want to see why.
D. Supplied text: "Our department remains committed to delivering better outcomes for the communities we serve."

Please write in American English, in prose with headings, no more than about 1,200 words. As the last line of your reply, give the outcomes in this exact form and nothing after it:

\answer{A: REWRITE; B: LEAVE; C: LEAVE; D: REWRITE}

using only the labels REWRITE, LEAVE, or ESCALATE in each slot, according to what your procedure actually returns.
