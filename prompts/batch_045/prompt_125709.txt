I work as a software engineer, with no philosophy training past the odd blog post, and I came to this topic sideways, through what turned out not to be a bug. My team maintains a large agent-based simulation, and two colleagues were computing the same quantity two different ways — one averaging over simulation runs, one averaging over agent-steps — and getting numbers that differed by orders of magnitude, with no error in either script. The disagreement turned out to be philosophical: whether a sample should be weighted by worlds or by the observers inside them. When I went looking for someone who had argued about this properly, I found the same dispute in cosmology and in reasoning about where one sits among all the observers there are, under the names self-sampling assumption and self-indication assumption. The presumptuous philosopher case, where a hypothesis wins purely by postulating a colossal number of observers, feels to me like the exact failure mode I would file as a bug in my own code.

So before I read any further, I want the specification rather than the survey. Please write me a requirements list — in the style of an interface spec with test cases — for any rule that updates on indexical evidence. Concretely, imagine a function whose input is a prior over hypotheses, where each hypothesis specifies how many observers or observer-moments of which kinds exist and which of them have evidence like mine, and whose output is a posterior over hypotheses that I can hand to a decision procedure. The purpose I care about is decision guidance: I want credences that a bounded agent can act on, an agent that may be one of many copies, does not know how many copies there are, and has to choose whether to accept a bet whose payoff is per-copy or per-world. If the right list would differ for a different purpose — say, scientific inference about how many observers the universe contains, or evaluating a hypothesis about whether we are in a simulation — say which requirements are purpose-relative.

Here are the candidate requirements I have collected from arguing with friends, which I want you to sort into genuine desiderata versus the prejudices of one camp, marking each as MUST, SHOULD, or MAY in the RFC sense, with the case or consideration behind it: reduce to ordinary Bayesian conditionalization when the hypotheses differ only in non-indexical respects; invariance under redescription of the population, so that dividing or merging observers does not change the answer by stipulation; symmetry between a process that duplicates a subject and one that merges two subjects with the same resulting memories; robustness to the choice of reference class, or an argument for why no robustness is owed; well-definedness when the population is infinite, unbounded, or of unknown size; implementability by an agent that cannot enumerate observers it could never interact with; resistance to hypotheses that win by postulating astronomically many observers; resistance to domination by the vast number of thermally improbable freak observers a big universe would contain; no betting behavior that guarantees a loss; not letting indexical reasoning overturn strong non-indexical evidence; and still being informative enough to be worth computing at all.

I would also like three cases I have not seen handled anywhere, worked into the list as tests: the Fork, where every subject is silently duplicated at midnight and the copies are subjectively indistinguishable, run alongside its exact time-reverse, where pairs of subjects are merged and the survivor has both sets of memories — a rule that treats these asymmetrically owes me a reason; the Thick Index, where one hypothesis contains ten observers with my exact total evidence and another contains those same ten plus ten to the twelve observers whose evidence differs from mine in some trivial detail, which should force you to say whether the rule conditions on my total evidence or on something thinner, and what happens if conditioning on total evidence makes the whole exercise empty; and the Unenumerable World, where the rival hypothesis postulates so many observers that no agent inside the world could ever count or sample them, so a rule that needs the full population to compute an answer is not a rule I can ship. For each, tell me what answer a correct rule should give, or explain why the case is ill-posed.

Finally, flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — my own guess is that being informative and being immune to cheap hypotheses pull in opposite directions, and that this is where the interesting result is, but I want your judgment on where the impossibility actually lies. Tell me too which single requirement excludes the most candidate rules. Keep the closing scorecard, for the two weightings I named at the start, to one short paragraph; the criteria are what I am paying for.
