I just joined the clinical ethics committee at my hospital, and I've been reading back thru our last couple years of case notes trying to work out what we actually think, because the case on Thursday dosen't feel like any of the old ones. Here's our record:

- Mrs H, 84, advanced dementia, appears content, gets a chest infection. Living will written at 62, never updated, refuses ventilation. We honored it and she died.
- Mr D, 45, unconscious after a crash. At 30 he'd written a directive refusing blood products on religious grounds. At 42, still fully competent, he'd lost the faith and told his family several times that he'd want a transfusion now — but never changed the paperwork. We went with the later wishes, not the document.
- Ms K, bipolar. During a stable period she wrote a directive refusing ECT and naming her sister as surrogate. In a later depressive episode she begged for ECT. We honored the refusal.
- Mr R, competent at the time, refused dialysis in writing on the basis that it means being bedbound and in constant pain. That's just wrong, as it turns out. Now in renal failure, mild dementia, has a friend on dialysis and says he wants it. We set the written refusal aside and treated.
- Mr S, end-stage cardiac disease, no directive, but for years had told everyone "do everything, keep me going." When the time came we declined to do everything — burdens outweighed benefits — and we didn't treat his earlier demand as binding the way we treat earlier refusals.

New case, Ms N: diagnosed with early Alzheimer's at 66. Six months after the diagnosis, while still legally competent, she wrote a directive refusing antibiotics, tube feeding and hospitalization for anything at all, and added a clause along the lines of "I know people in my situation often settle into a placid, happy version of themselves and then say they want to live. That's not me talking, that's the disease. If I later say I've changed my mind, disregard it." Now 71, mid-stage, in a care home, no memory of writing any of it. Over the past two years the staff say she's built what they describe as a genuinely new life: a close relationship with another resident, a daily routine she clearly enjoys, painting, and she says often and consistently that she's happy and wants to be around as long as possible. Her daughter, who is the surrogate, wants the directive honored. Her partner and the staff want it set aside. Complicating detail: the new interests all started after she was put on a medication for restless legs of a kind that can sometimes produce intense new compulsive preoccupations, and no one can tell how much of the "new life" is her and how much is the drug.

So: what is this committee going to say? I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the rules we seem to be running — give me the two or three rules that fit all five of our earlier decisions about as well as each other, say what each of them predicts for Ms N, give me your best guess with the reason you prefer it, and tell me what single further case would most cleanly separate the rules that survive. I'd also like to know whether that clause of hers — the bit trying to pre-emptively cancel her own future change of mind — should do any work at all, given that in Mr D's case we let a later change of mind beat an earlier document.
