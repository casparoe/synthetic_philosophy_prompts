I am writing a novel whose narrator is a fourteenth-century North African scholar of the Muqaddimah's cast of mind, a man whose only working tools are asabiyyah, the cycle by which a hardier group on the periphery conquers a softened centre, and the thesis that a dynasty has a natural lifespan like an individual's and rarely outlasts a few generations. By a device of the plot he is handed a dossier of what modern archaeologists call collapse — Bronze Age palaces that stop keeping accounts, Maya cities whose royal inscriptions simply end, an island whose monuments outlasted their forests — along with the archaeologists' own quarrel over whether any of this was failure at all, or merely transformation that later writers mistook for failure. I want his reaction rendered inside his own machinery rather than as a modern summary of it, and I don't need citations or a reading list; I need his voice.

The dossier should also contain two entries of my own invention, which he must answer in his terms. Call the first phantom complexity: polities in which the archives, roads, coinage, tax registers, and monumental building all kept running for a generation or more after the solidarity that produced them had gone, so that every index of complexity still reads high at the moment the state is already dead — is that afterglow part of the state's life, or its corpse, and does he have any vocabulary for it? Call the second brittle plenty: societies that grew measurably more complex, with denser settlement, longer supply chains, and far more paper per head, while becoming steadily less able to act together at all, so that complexity appears as a symptom rather than as a capacity.

Two further questions matter most for my plot. His cycle requires a hardier periphery to renew a used-up centre, so in a world with no nomadic frontier left, does the cycle jam into permanent senescence, or does he discover new peripheries — sects, firms, diasporas, small cohesive minorities — and by what test would he recognise one? And how does he read a society that is shrinking without being conquered: sub-replacement fertility, no invaders, the machines still working? Is that the terminal phase, or is it not a collapse in his sense at all, because nothing has been taken from anyone and no one has replaced anyone?

End with the single item in the dossier he would fixate on and refuse to let go of, and with one point at which his own terms force him into a verdict he would have hated.
