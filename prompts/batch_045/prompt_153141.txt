I've been arguing with two people in my phil-of-mind reading group about the Turing test, and I keep coming back to a problem with it that I don't have a name for in the literature, so I've started calling it the foil-sensitivity problem: in the imitation game the machine isn't judged against humanity in the abstract, it's judged against one particular human on the other side of the curtain, and humans differ from each other an awful lot. So I want a case that makes foil-sensitivity do real damage, and I want it built to spec rather than just asserted.

Here's what I'm after. Design a scenario — realistic, near-future at the latest, one paragraph for the scenario itself, no uploaded minds and no perfect simulators, a system that can hold up an open-ended typed conversation is fine — in which a single machine, running the same policy/architecture in both conditions, passes against one human foil and fails against another, with everything else pinned down: the interrogators in each condition are equally competent and each correctly picks the human in a calibration round, both foils are genuinely trying and being themselves, no gimmicks (no refusing to answer, no pretending to be a child or a non-native speaker, no deliberately broken English), and the difference between the two foils is just ordinary human variation in how people come across in text. Then lay out the verdicts under three different readings of what the test is measuring: (1) the strict comparative imitation game, where the verdict is a forced two-way pick between machine and foil; (2) a population reading, where "not detected" means landing inside the actual spread of how humans perform across a properly sampled range of foils; (3) the embodied or "total" variant people associate with Harnad, where the machine also has to perceive and act and not just type. The specs: (1) has to flip between foils, (2) must not flip, and (3) must not flip either — the machine loses both times once you add the extra channel. Show the reasoning for each, and make sure you haven't just stipulated the verdicts: the flip in (1) has to fall out of the foil's actual traits and the machine's actual behaviour, and the non-flips in (2) and (3) have to fall out of what those readings add rather than from you telling me so.

Then the bit I actually care about. Name the single feature of the case that's doing the work, say what happens if you remove it, and give the strongest thing a defender of the test can say back — not the strawman that any test can be gamed. In particular take seriously the reply that this is a purely methodological artefact: average over foils, sample the population, and the test was never meant to return a verdict on a single run. Tell me whether that reply saves the test as a criterion of mind or quietly converts it into something weaker, and be explicit about which. And since my group is split between people who think passing would be sufficient for mind and people who think it could only ever be evidence, finish by saying which way, if either, this case pushes — and what it leaves completely untouched.
