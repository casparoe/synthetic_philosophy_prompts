Last night after the final meeting of a graduate seminar I have been auditing on collective decision theory, Miriam — a political theorist who has forgotten more about this material than I learned this term — and I spent a good two hours over dinner arguing about Sen's short article on the impossibility of a Paretian liberal, and we parted further from agreement than we began. The example as I reconstructed it on a napkin: two housemates and one copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover, with three possible outcomes — Prudence reads it, Laurence reads it, or nobody does. Prudence thinks the book indecent: her first choice is that nobody reads it, but if somebody must, she would rather read it herself in a spirit of disapproval than let Laurence read it for pleasure. Laurence would most enjoy reading it himself, but what he would really like is for Prudence to be made to read it; failing that, he would rather it went unread. Now give each of them a protected say over their own reading: Prudence settles which way the pair (she reads / nobody reads) goes, and Laurence settles (he reads / nobody reads). On the napkin the group's ranking came out as: nobody reads is better than Prudence reads (her say); Laurence reads is better than nobody reads (his say); and since both of them would rather see Prudence reading than Laurence reading, Prudence reads is better than Laurence reads. A circle.

My reading of the napkin was this. The circle is generated entirely inside the personal spheres: each person was consulted only about her own private reading, and that alone made the group's preferences inconsistent. So the incoherence is internal to the very idea of a protected sphere — as soon as more than one person has a right over anything at all, the social ranking stops being an ordering, and 'rights' can only be a façon de parler that a bit of formal argument has now unmasked. The honest conclusion, I said, is that we should do our political thinking in straightforwardly welfarist terms and drop the vocabulary of a personal sphere altogether. The Pareto step seemed to me the one premise nobody can quarrel with, since it says only that if everyone prefers one outcome to another then so does the group, and I cannot see what would be left of welfare economics if we abandoned it.

Miriam disagreed on both counts. She argued that the suspect premise is the Pareto one, and that the remedy is to discount preferences that meddle in other people's private affairs — Prudence's wish that Laurence not read, Laurence's wish that Prudence read — before asking what the group prefers. That struck me as straightforwardly circular: 'meddling' just means 'bearing on something the theory was supposed to assign to somebody's personal sphere', so you would be smuggling into the filter exactly the rights you were trying to evaluate. She also said something I waved away at the time, about rights being powers over one's own choices rather than conditions on a social ranking, which sounded to me like changing the subject from outcomes to procedures in order to dodge the mathematics. Who is closer to right? If Miriam is right that it is the Pareto principle rather than rights that has to give, I genuinely do not see how one restricts it without begging the question, and I would like to know what a non-question-begging version of that restriction is supposed to look like — or whether, as I still suspect, the only coherent lesson is that talk of a personal sphere has to be given up.
