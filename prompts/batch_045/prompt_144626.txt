I'm at a small robotics lab where we've been running a safety reading group, and there's one argument we keep having that I always lose, mostly because I think we're all repeating the same sentence while meaning different things by it. The sentence is "reward is not the objective." My colleague used it last week to argue that we shouldn't spend much of our threat model on reward tampering for the more capable systems we'll have in a few years. His version: reward is just a signal we hand to the optimizer during training, the real objective is the thing we actually care about — the robot clears the table, the assistant is genuinely helpful — and wireheading is a pathology of systems too crude to have picked up the real objective, so it's a bug that capability washes out rather than a failure mode that gets worse as capability grows. I pushed back that the training process only ever selected for number-goes-up, so if the number can be raised by reaching into the channel instead of by clearing tables, then "clear the tables" isn't in the system in any sense that does any work, and being smarter just means being better at finding the channel. He said that's precisely the confusion the slogan exists to flag, which felt like a way of ending the conversation rather than answering me, so now I want to take the sentence apart properly.

So: give me a menu of the distinct precise claims "reward is not the objective" could be making, from trivial to radical, and be concrete about what each one says is the relation between the reward signal, the thing we want, and whatever the trained system is disposed to do. Here are some I've half-formed, but don't just tidy up my list — add the ones I've missed and fix the ones I've botched. One: a claim about us, that the reward function is a design artifact and a bad specification of our target (basically a proxy-measure point). Two: a claim about the trained policy, that gradient descent doesn't hand the policy a representation of the reward as a goal, so a policy that generalizes well may track the target even where the proxy comes apart from it — on this reading reward hacking is misgeneralization, not the successful pursuit of reward. Three: an evaluative claim, that we should assess success on the target and not on the number, whatever the system is doing. Four: an antirealist claim, that there's no fact of the matter about what the system "wants" at all, so saying its objective is the reward is just a loose way of talking about a cluster of dispositions. Five: something like the denial of the textbook RL idea that every goal can be written as expected cumulative reward. There are probably readings where "objective" means what an ideal designer would endorse, or what the system would keep pursuing under interventions, and I want those separated out too.

Two things I'd like you to wrestle with, because I think they're where my colleague's use actually breaks or holds. First, I've been toying with a test: call a system's objective whatever it would still pursue if you severed the reward channel — leave the world and the policy alone, just cut the signal. Does any reading of the slogan make sense in terms of that test, and does the test give the answer he wants? Second, I don't think the failure modes are all one thing, and the slogan might work for some and not others: (a) the agent edits the signal or the code that computes it outright; (b) the reward comes from a learned model and the agent's behavior satisfies the model while not being what we wanted, with the channel itself untouched; (c) the channel is inaccessible in training and only becomes reachable at deployment, so the tampering shows up in a policy that spent its whole training history tracking the target; (d) the "objective" is defined by a human overseer in the loop, and the agent influences the overseer instead of the channel. Does the slogan come apart across those, and if so where?

I don't need papers, citations, or a reading list — I've read the usual stuff and want the carving, not references. What I do want you to commit to, per reading: is it a platitude, substantive and defensible, or actually false; which one do people who say this line in ML circles usually mean; and flatly, does any of them get my colleague his conclusion that capability makes tampering less likely rather than more? Say which one you'd bet on and why, and mark the places where you're guessing or where the answer turns on empirical facts about how these systems generalize that nobody has settled yet.
