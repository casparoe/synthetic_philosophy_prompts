Me and a friend from my number theory course got into an argument about the probabilistic heuristics people use for prime patterns, and I can't tell who's being naive. Here's how I understand the twin prime version: the prime number theorem says the "chance" that a number near n is prime is about 1/ln n, so if you presume the events are independent you'd guess the density of twin primes is 1/(ln n)^2 — but the Hardy–Littlewood answer is about 1.32/(ln n)^2. The thing I keep getting stuck on is that the independence presumption is obviously false here, and known to be false in advance: if n > 2 is prime then n is odd, which makes n+2 odd too and already doubles its chances, and similarly knowing that n survives sieving by 3 or 5 tells you something about n + 2 mod 3 and mod 5. So the 1.32 looks to me like a patch bolted onto a premise we already know is wrong, and my friend's proposal is that we should stop pretending and just estimate the real correlation from data — count twin primes up to 10^9 or wherever, fit the constant empirically, and use the fitted value. (He also pointed out that even in the single-number case, if you treat divisibility by different primes as independent and multiply (1 − 1/p) over all p up to √n, you get something like 1.12/ln n rather than 1/ln n, which he says shows the presumption of independence doesn't even get the easiest case right.) That feels more honest, but then I can't see in what sense the 1.32 counts as a prediction rather than a fit.

I've been trying to make the worry precise as something I call a dependence ledger: every time a heuristic argument has to add a correction factor for a dependence it failed to model, that's one more place the argument could have gone wrong, so your confidence in the conclusion should get multiplied by some factor below 1 for each correction, and it should decay fast in the number of corrections. On that accounting the twin prime heuristic is applying infinitely many corrections — one for each prime in that product — so its credibility should be essentially zero, which is clearly the wrong answer, since number theorists treat that asymptotic as very likely true. What exactly is wrong with the ledger: is it that these correction factors aren't uncertain estimates but computed quantities, or something else? And a related thing that's nagging at me: didn't Maier show back in the 80s that Cramér's random model of the primes gives outright false predictions about primes in short intervals? If independence-style reasoning has already been refuted once, what's the principled difference between the cases where people still trust it and the case where it broke — or is the trust just leftover habit?
