So I just got out of a two-hour dinner argument about whether it even makes sense to blame a country for starting a war — WWI, Fischer's 1961 book and the whole controversy around it, article 231 — and they made me referee and I honestly couldnt call it. Sam is the skeptic about collective blame, Nadia thinks he's hiding behind a technicality. Rough reconstruction, they can correct me on details:

(1) Sam: blame needs an agent with beliefs, aims and alternatives; a nation isn't one. "Germany started the war" is shorthand doing work nobody has earned — the July decisions were made by a handful of men (the Kaiser, Bethmann-Hollweg, Moltke) who deliberately kept the elected Reichstag out of it. So the Versailles war-guilt clause laundered a claim about a dozen people into a claim about 60-odd million who had no control over foreign policy, and who are all dead now anyway.

(2) Nadia: two things. First, blame doesn't require a single mind — we blame firms and courts all the time, and imperial Germany had a settled foreign-policy posture that a whole elite subscribed to and that crowds cheered in August 1914. Second, the point of "Germany started it" isn't to point at a mind; it's to mark a pattern of conduct the polity could repeat. You can't apologize, remember, or pay as a dead man — the addressee has to be the continuing political community.

(3) Sam: fine for firms, they have owners and a charter. What's the link between "the state did it" and "the nation did it"? Not contract, not consent, not control — the 1914 crowds were reacting to a story they were fed, mobilization sold to them as defensive. Give him a principle that gets you from facts like those to national blame and he'll sign up tomorrow.

(4) Nadia: "consent or control" is too strong even for individuals — we blame people for negligence, for what they should have known, for benefits they took without asking where they came from. And the Reichstag voted the war credits through with essentially no opposition, socialists included; nobody was dragged there. Blame aimed at a polity also doesn't have to be inherited guilt about 1914 — it can just be a standing obligation to distrust your own patriotic story in the present.

(5) Sam: then it isn't blame at all, it's a lesson — call it civic hygiene and he's with you, but drop the language of guilt. And here's his test, which he called the cabinet swap: rerun July 1914 with the German decision-makers replaced by a random draw from the British, French or Russian cabinets of that same year. If the war still comes, "Germany did it" was really about the men; if it doesn't, it's about the men again. Either way, he says, you never arrive at the nation.

(6) Nadia: the swap rigs the question. The system that reliably selected men like that and gave them that much room is part of what "Germany" names — a swap measures the occupants, not the selection mechanism. That's exactly what the Fischer thesis was about: the September Program and the pressure groups behind it suggest the push wasn't a few men's whim but a settlement among interests inside German society, which is why the argument was never just about who signed what.

(7) Sam: that widens blame to the elite and its lobbies, which still isn't the nation — and once you widen it to everyone who benefited or failed to check, you've got a claim that's true of basically every belligerent, so it stops picking anyone out.
