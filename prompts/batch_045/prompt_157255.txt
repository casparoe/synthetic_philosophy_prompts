I'm teaching a grad seminar on Wittgenstein's On Certainty this term, and somewhere around week four I noticed that I have a big pile of gut verdicts about which propositions are hinges and which are just ordinary empirical claims, and that I cannot get them into any order. So here is my list. I'd rather you tell me what principle (if any) I am fumbling toward than tell me what Wittgenstein thought, and please don't hand me a reading list or a stack of citations — I've got the syllabus, I want the principle.

That I have two hands: I don't want to call it knowledge, and doubt of it strikes me as empty rather than as wrong — hinge, as far as I'm concerned. That the earth existed for a very long time before I was born: same verdict, hinge. That I have a brain: I've never had a scan, my evidence is the same textbook testimony any first-year has, and still I'd refuse to call it an open question — hinge, or so it feels. That I have never been to the Moon: hinge-like for me, though plainly it wasn't one for the Apollo crews, and I'm not sure that's just a quirk of who is speaking. That this thing in front of me is a table: plainly empirical, I could be looking at a very good stage prop or at a headset feed — except that doubting it out loud in a seminar room feels like exactly the idle doubt I called empty two sentences ago, so I seem to be running two standards and I can't say which is the real one. That I was born in Dayton, Ohio: I've never seen a certificate, my mother told me, it could easily be false, and yet being corrected about it would unsettle me far more than being corrected about the table — verdict: feels like bedrock, and I think it isn't, which is the pair I keep going back and forth on. That water is H2O: learned from testimony, never verified by me, functions as bedrock for the chemists I know, and my verdict is that it's an empirical a posteriori identity and not a hinge, even though it looks structurally identical to the earth-is-old case. That 2+2=4: not a hinge, and whatever goes wrong when someone doubts it goes wrong differently from what goes wrong when someone doubts my having hands. That the Holocaust happened: an empirical historical claim I know on testimony, and a denier is denying something I know, not stepping outside the frame — except that I notice I reach for the word madness about such a person, which is the same word I use for the person who doubts his hands, and that worries me. That my students have minds: I don't think this is a hinge or a knowledge-claim at all; my relation to them isn't a bit of inferred theory.

So: can you state a principle — or two, if there's a genuine fork and they come apart somewhere — that captures as many of these as possible, saying what makes a proposition a hinge, wether hinges are personal or communal, and wether they can be true, false, or known; and then tell me plainly which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and would force me to drop? I'd also like it run on two cases I haven't put on the list, because I suspect that's where it breaks. First: a woman of seventy-two who was raised by a man she believed was her father, who finds out through a genetic test that he wasn't, and whose world-picture doesn't otherwise move — for seventy years that proposition was as close to bedrock as anything in her life, and then it was quietly overturned. Second: an anthropologist who spends years in a village where everyone treats dreams as carrying news of distant deaths and who never acquires that hinge herself, while a boy from the same village goes to medical school in the capital and loses it. Please write it up in plain American English, no seminar-speak.
