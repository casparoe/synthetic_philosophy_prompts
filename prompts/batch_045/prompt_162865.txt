ok so in my philosophy elective we just read that bit in Plato's Republic where Glaucon tells the story about the shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible, and the second nobody can see him he goes and sleeps with the queen, murders the king and takes over the kingdom. The point he's making (as I understood it anyway) is that nobody is actually just because they want to be, people are just because they're scared of getting caught and of what everyone else thinks of them, and if you handed anyone real impunity they'd behave exactly like the shepherd did.

I want to test that on something real instead of arguing about it in the abstract, and the case I keep coming back to is the whole Napster era, like 1999-2001. Going from memory here so don't hold me to exact dates: Napster launched in 1999, let anyone pull basically any song off anyone else's computer for free, the record labels sued, Metallica sued them separately in 2000 after their music showed up on there, a court ordered them to block copyrighted stuff, and they shut the network down in July 2001. Tens of millions of people were using it at the peak. Then a couple years later the industry started suing individual fans, and around the same time Apple started selling cheap singles online and streaming eventually came after that.

So: analyze the Napster years as a Glaucon case. Who is the person with the ring, and what exactly is doing the work of the ring? Does the fact that basically everybody grabbed the free music show that Glaucon was just right, that enforcement is the only thing holding people to the rules? Or does the analogy break somewhere, and where? A few things that bug me and that I want you to actually deal with rather than smooth over:

1) it wasn't really invisibility. Everyone could see how many millions of people were on there. Using Napster cost you nothing with your friends and might've even made you the cool kid who had every album, and it was the authorities who couldn't see you. Glaucon's ring hides you from everybody at once. Is that difference doing real work in the case, or does it just mean the real-world ring was even better than his?
2) each individual download felt like it hurt basically nobody, which is nothing like one guy murdering a king. Does that change what the case can tell us, or is it the same choice in a different costume?
3) a lot of heavy downloaders still bought concert tickets and merch and said they felt bad about the artists themselves, and a lot of people had this whole argument that the labels were ripping artists off so copying wasn't really stealing. Is that evidence against Glaucon, or does his challenge survive it, since his claim might just be that people follow whatever rules they take to be real?
4) from what I can tell people mostly stopped when paying got easy and cheap, not when it got scary. What does that do to the "fear of punishment" part of his story?

And the part I actually care about most: even if the case does show people grab the ring, does that tell me anything at all about why I should be moral when nobody's watching? Like is Glaucon making a descriptive claim about what we'd do, or also a claim about whether there's any reason to be decent? And can a real case like this one even reach that second question, or is it the kind of thing no amount of real-world data settles? Please be straight about which parts of this Glaucon's challenge can't speak to instead of forcing an answer out of it, and tell me what the case shows about the theory itself - does it back him up, is it an awkward fit, or is it a kind of situation his story never anticipated.
