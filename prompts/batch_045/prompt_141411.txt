Next Thursday I have to circulate, on behalf of the governance committee of the foundation I serve, a short recommendation to our full board, and I would like you to draft it. The case is this: our endowment is governed by a mission clause the founding donors insisted on when they transferred the assets, under which the mission can be amended only by a four-fifths vote of the board accompanied by a written statement of reasons published eighteen months before the change takes effect. Roughly two years ago a bare majority of the board began arguing that the mission should be redirected, on the strength of an internal review the board itself commissioned; eight of our fourteen members have joined in the last three years and say the original framing no longer fits what the endowment can now do. The donors are both alive, both still attend meetings, and both oppose the change, invoking the clause and their own account of what they meant by it. The majority's position is that the clause is an illegitimate attempt by past decision-makers to bind present ones, that each board is a distinct agent answerable to present circumstances, and that in any case the delay was written for moments of panic, not for a considered change of direction with a two-year paper trail behind it.

The memo should be addressed to the full board, run no more than two pages, and end with one concrete recommendation with dates attached. It must give the strongest version of the majority's case rather than treating the clause as sacrosanct. In particular I want it to work through four things I think the argument actually turns on: first, that the clause is asymmetric, slowing any change of mission but not the preservation of the current one, so that it entrenches the status quo even in cases where its panic rationale plainly does not apply; second, that the clause nowhere says what counts as the impairment it was written for, so the board is now making in the heat of the dispute exactly the judgment the clause was supposed to take out of the hands of a board in the heat of the dispute; third, that our by-laws also permit the board to amend the by-laws by majority vote, which may mean the clause binds nobody as currently drafted; and fourth, a fourth option I want evaluated honestly rather than merely listed, namely that the right move is for the board to specify now, while relatively cool-headed, the conditions under which the clause applies and what would count as evidence that they obtain, thereby settling the trigger instead of settling the case. Please also say how much weight the donors' present testimony about their own past intent should carry, and whether that weight ought to differ from what it would be if they were dead and we had only their text. Close by naming the strongest consideration on the side you are rejecting and stating what would have to be true for the recommendation to go the other way.
