im writing a piece for a magazine about why big infrastructure projects blow past their budgets, and one of the books I got sent a galley of has a chapter on the inside view / outside view distinction. I might quote a short passage from it, so I need to know how much of it I can actually stand behind. Heres the bit I'd want to use:

"The inside view is the one we all use by default: you look at your project — your plan, your team, your risks — and you build your forecast up out of those details. The outside view throws the details away. You ask instead what happened to other projects of this general kind, take the distribution of their outcomes, and use that as your forecast, because the details of your case almost always flatter you. Kahneman and his collaborators showed that the inside view isn't merely optimistic but fallacious: forecasts built up from the specifics of a case miss their targets at a stable and predictable rate, while forecasts read off the class of similar cases are well calibrated. The rational move, then, is to treat your project as a random draw from the reference class and to discount whatever makes you think yours is different. The virtue this rests on is epistemic modesty: the admission that your own judgment about your own case is unreliable, and that you should defer to the pooled judgment of everyone who has stood where you're standing. Some philosophers grumble that a reference class can be gerrymandered to yield whatever answer you like, but that objection is a red herring — you simply have to choose the class honestly. The lesson stands: anyone who forecasts from the inside view is committing the same error as someone who ignores a base rate."

I basically believe the broad picture, but two things people said on background made me unsure, and I don't want to print something a specialist would wince at.

First, a cost engineer I interviewed insisted that the reference class in these studies is really a class of *forecasters*, not of projects — that the base rate is evidence about how reliable people like him are, and that if you have your own track record (he says he's logged two hundred estimates with a known error distribution) the outside view has no business overruling it. He put it as: the outside view tells a surgeon with five hundred successful operations behind her to defer to the survival rate for operations in general. Is that a real limitation of the view, or is it just the inside view smuggled back in wearing a lab coat — since deciding which past cases count as "like mine" is exactly where the optimistic construal the outside view is meant to correct tends to creep in? I'm inclined to think he has a point, but I can't tell whether he's describing a scope limit or reinventing the fallacy.

Second, someone in a seminar I sat in on raised what he called the second-order outside view: if adopting the outside view is itself a forecasting policy, what is the base rate for the outside view turning out right, and does the view, applied to itself, eat its own answer? That felt clever at the time and possibly empty the next morning. Is it a genuine regress or a cheap parlour trick?

And a smaller practical one: a friend who builds data centres swears his cost estimates are famously bad but his timelines are fine. Can you take the outside view component by component, or does splitting a project into parts just reintroduce the inside view through a side door?

So: go through the passage and tell me what a defender of the inside/outside view picture would accept, what they'd quietly revise, and what is just wrong — and whether the chapter's punchline about the base rate still follows once the passage is cleaned up. I'd also like to know whether my cost engineer's point and the second-order worry are things the literature-style version of this view already has resources to answer, or whether they're the kind of objection people just wave at. Feel free to seperate "safe to quote" from "rephrase before you print."
