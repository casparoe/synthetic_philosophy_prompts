I run a tiny reading group — six of us, mostly grad students plus one retired architect — and we've spent the last month on Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, the one about history being at once useful and harmful to life. We keep getting stuck on the same thing: whether the three modes Nietzsche lays out are actually exhaustive, or whether they're just three of the ways a culture can relate to its past. You know the three: the one that goes looking for greatness and examples in the past so it can act now, the one that tends and preserves what is small, ancestral and one's own, and the one that drags the past before a tribunal and condemns parts of it in order to be able to live at all. One of us thinks Nietzsche's later genealogical work is basically a fourth mode; another thinks genealogy is just the condemning mode that got better at pretending to be neutral. Meanwhile I've got this pet idea I keep pushing on them — call it hospitable history: you go to a tradition or a body of the past in order to be changed by it, letting it set part of your agenda, rather than mining it for models, curating it, or prosecuting it. I want to find out whether that's really a distinct position or whether I'm just smuggling in something incoherent. So: build me a quiz that sorts people by their reactions to concrete cases rather than by which label they'd pick for themselves. Call it ten questions, each a specific scenario with three or four answers written as plain gut reactions, with a scoring key at the end mapping answer patterns onto the leaves. No question should mention any of the labels, and no question should be answerable just by remembering what Nietzsche said — we've all read the essay, so it has to be diagnostic of what we actually think about the cases.

Please don't build it by starting from the list of names. I'd rather you lay out the logical space first: something like the axes of (a) whether the value of knowing the past comes from what it lets you do, what it roots you in, or what it lets you break from; (b) whether the past has any claim on you that is independent of your own projects and projects of your community; (c) whether accurate knowledge of the past is worth having even when it weakens or sickens the knower; plus whatever fourth distinction you think actually does work in separating people rather than just generating sixteen empty boxes. Then locate the real positions in that space: the three modes, the integrated picture where all three serve a culture with enough plasticity to digest what it inherits, the attitude that treats history as a distraction from what is eternal and unchanging, the one that simply forgets and gets on with living, the truth-at-any-price view that knowledge of the past is valuable regardless of whether it serves life, and genealogy as its own leaf. Draw the cases from things like these: a museum repatriating looted objects; a cathedral spire rebuilt exactly as it was versus replaced with a contemporary design; a judge who is a constitutional originalist; a truth commission after a dictatorship; someone discovering the family house was paid for by the slave trade; a founder who reads nothing but biographies of founders; a movement that openly invents a new "ancient" tradition; a musician reconstructing a dead folk repertoire from fragmentary field recordings.

Two more things. First, mark the cells of the space nobody occupies, and say briefly whether that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the quiz genuinely cannot separate: I suspect the monumental and the preservative ones collapse whenever the object is both great and one's own, and that the condemning and truth-at-any-price ones collapse whenever the debunking serves no project at all — tell me if I'm wrong about where the seams are, and don't paper over a collapse by inventing a question that begs it. Second, add a short diagnostic at the end specifically for my hospitable history: is it a real fourth mode, a repackaged mixture, or a contradiction, and whether its really a distinct position or not, which question in the quiz is the one that exposes it? And don't point me at commentaries or give me a reading list — we're doing this from the essay and our own arguments, and half the point is not knowing what the secondary literature would say.
