I'm a fourth-year PhD student in astronomy. My research is occurrence-rate inference from wide-field surveys, which in practice means this: I take catalogues that contain no convincing detections of something and ask what upper limit on the underlying rate they actually license. My working vocabulary is therefore selection functions, effective survey volume, completeness corrections, Malmquist-type biases, and the standing fact that a survey never measures a rate but only the product of a rate and a detection probability that itself depends on assumptions about signal morphology I have declined to make. I use Bayesian model comparison and hierarchical inference routinely. I have no philosophy training and only the cosmology of a first-year survey course.

What I want is a translation. Please restate the Fermi paradox and its main proposed resolutions in the vocabulary I just described, one at a time, as faithfully as you can. The positions I have in mind are:

(1) The galactic-settlement argument: the galaxy is old, a civilization capable of interstellar travel could cross or settle it in a time far shorter than the galaxy's age, we see no evidence of any such presence, so interstellar-capable civilizations are rare or absent.

(2) The hard-steps or Great Filter reading: observers require some number of intrinsically improbable transitions, and the live question is whether the silence should make us pessimistic about our own future — whether most of the difficulty lies behind us or ahead of us.

(3) The early-observer resolution: the characteristic time for observers to arise is comparable to the duration of the window in which the galaxy is habitable at all, so a typical observer should expect to find herself before the galaxy has been filled.

(4) The barely-looked resolution: the searches actually performed constrain a minuscule fraction of the possible signal space, so the non-detection is nearly uninformative.

If it helps to have something concrete to bite on, here is the toy model I carry around: observers require n independent rare transitions, each with an exponentially distributed waiting time; the habitable window has length T; any civilization that survives long enough launches settlement fronts expanding at some fraction of light speed.

For each of the four, tell me what translates cleanly, what has to be bent or re-expressed to fit my vocabulary, and what cannot be said in it at all.

Then the residue report, on three points specifically.

First: (1) and (4) look to me like the same sentence in my vocabulary — a non-detection licenses only a bound on rate times detectability — yet the debate treats them as rivals. Is one of them secretly a claim about the selection function and the other a claim about the posterior, and does separating them dissolve their apparent conflict?

Second: in every problem I actually work on, the observer stands outside the sample. I design the survey, then draw inferences about a population I am not a member of. (3) appears to require reasoning from a sample I am inside of, and I suspect (2) does too. Does my vocabulary contain any resource for this — sampling weights, hierarchical models, posterior predictive checks bearing on the observer's own location in the sample — or is self-location a genuinely new ingredient, and if it is, does adding it change what (2) should conclude?

Third: please flag explicitly anywhere you would otherwise be tempted to smuggle in an assumption of mine and call it translation. Candidates I can see: that a detection is a discrete event with a known morphology; that the population is static over the integration time; that the noise is well characterised; that the survey was designed rather than stumbled into; that the quantity of interest is a rate rather than a single historical fact. I want to know which of these are doing real work in the arguments.

Finish with your verdict on the residue: is it a defect of my framework — something astronomy ought to have a term for and does not — or a sign that some of these arguments are less determinate than they sound?
