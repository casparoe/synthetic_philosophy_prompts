I'm on the committee writing this year's comprehensive exam for our PhD students in philosophy of cognitive science and AI, and my assigned question is on whether trained neural networks genuinely have representations — whether the posits of interpretability work (features, directions in activation space, circuits) are discovered structure in the network or artifacts of the decomposition the analyst chose. I need the question drafted along with the standard against which we'll mark it.

The difficulty I want is that the question should be unanswerable by rehearsing the realism/anti-realism debate, or by importing a generic philosophy-of-science conclusion about unobservables, or by reciting what sparse autoencoders and dictionary learning do. So I want the question built around a short self-contained vignette presenting three stylized empirical findings, which students must then use as evidence:

(i) Convergence. Two language models are trained from different seeds on disjoint halves of a corpus. A direction isolated in one by a sparsity-based decomposition method, when written into the other at the corresponding layer, produces the same specific behavioral change; and an approximately similar direction is recovered independently in the second model by the same pipeline.

(ii) Proliferation. The same pipeline, applied to the same model, recovers directions for arbitrarily gerrymandered classes — things no one would antecedently call representational contents, such as images of dogs photographed indoors before a certain date or cats photographed outdoors after it — and these directions are just as sparse, just as causally efficacious under intervention, and generalize just as well to held-out inputs as the direction for an ordinary category like dog.

(iii) A wrinkle. Steering along the dog direction does produce the targeted behavior, but also degrades performance on tasks with no evident connection to dogs; and the direction has no counterpart in a text-only model that nevertheless classifies dog-related sentences correctly.

The question should force students to say what it would even be for a network to have a representation, in a way that makes these three findings capable of confirming or disconfirming it, rather than trading on the slogan; to work out which of the three findings actually discriminates between realism, a projectivist or constructivist view on which features depend on the analyst's chosen basis, and a purely instrumental view on which feature talk is predictive and nothing more; and to say what further constraint — uniqueness of the decomposition, cross-model stability, compositional structure, robustness under distribution shift, something else — a realist would have to appeal to in order to escape (ii), and what is wrong with each candidate constraint. It should end by asking what would follow for interpretability practice if the anti-realist were right — whether the usefulness of these posits for steering and monitoring is even in question.

Please write the exam question itself (closed-book, no citations or empirical background required, aimed at a good answer of roughly 1,500 words in 90 minutes), then a model answer of 700–900 words, then a marking scheme worth 20 marks built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong, plus a short note on what separates an A answer from a bare pass.

One further requirement, since we'll be marking across several graders: in both the model answer and the scheme, mark explicitly which claims are settled or near-consensus among people who actually work on this and which are live controversies, and flag any place where the model answer itself takes a side, so that we don't grade a contested position as if it were the standard view. If you think one of the three findings in my vignette does less argumentative work than I've assumed, say so and adjust the question rather than writing a model answer that pretends it bites.
