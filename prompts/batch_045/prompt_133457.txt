I chair the appeals committee of a housing cooperative: 340 households, a written Code of about ninety rules, and a rotating panel of three volunteers, none of whom has any legal training. I repair lifts for a living. We hear roughly four appeals a month, and the stakes are not small: most of our decisions are about fines, but about one in six is about whether someone keeps their home. We have two hours per hearing. Every decision has to be reduced to a short written "reasons" paragraph that is posted on the members' noticeboard and read aloud at the next general meeting, and any decision that departs from the Code has to be ratified by the elected board, which meets only quarterly — so a departure we cannot justify in writing is, in practice, a departure we cannot make.

I have been reading about law and morality on my own for a few years, with no teacher. The thing I keep coming back to is a well-known mid-century exchange between two law professors, H. L. A. Hart and Lon Fuller, which I first met in an encyclopedia summary and then chased into a public library. As I understand the disagreement: one side says there is no necessary connection between what a rule is and what it ought to be, so that a rule can be perfectly genuine law and still be too cruel or too stupid to apply; when that happens, the honest course is to say so plainly, take the cost of departing from it, and get it changed for the future, rather than pretending the rule never said what it plainly said. The other side says this picture of a "valid" rule is far too thin: a body of rules that is contradictory, or unpublished, or retrospective, or impossible to comply with, or that shifts every week, has not done what rules exist to do, and its subjects owe it something less than fidelity — which makes the question "is this really one of our rules?" a moral question through and through, not a separate preliminary one. I believe there was a case at the centre of it about people who informed on their neighbours under the rules of a regime that was itself criminal, and about what a court should do afterwards. Correct me if I have any of this wrong, but please do not spend your answer explaining the debate back to me or pointing me at books and articles; I have no library access beyond the municipal one and no stomach for a reading list. What I want is the argument turned into something three volunteers can actually run in an evening.

Two wrinkles that make this harder than the general question. First, our Code already contains a provision we call Rule 0: no member may be penalised for failing to comply with a rule they could not reasonably have known about, and nothing in the Code requires the impossible. So the Fuller-flavoured worry is partly written into our own rules, and sometimes the dispute is about what Rule 0 demands rather than about Rule 0 itself. Second, the co-op stopped keeping a printed copy of the Code in the office last year and now publishes it only through a members-only web portal.

What I would like from you is a decision procedure for cases where a member has admittedly done what a written rule forbids, and the panel nonetheless suspects that applying the rule would be wrong. Please give me: the questions to ask, in the order I should ask them; what counts as a sufficient answer to each question; the point at which the procedure simply stops with "apply the rule"; the point at which it stops with "do not apply it"; and the point at which it hands the matter to the board, or to a person, because the remaining question is one of values rather than of fact. I also want a short template for the written reasons paragraph, built so that it forces the panel to distinguish (a) what the rule required, (b) what we did and what we are relying on when we depart, and (c) what we are changing for future cases — because I have found that the panel's drafts collapse those three into one soothing sentence. The whole procedure has to fit on one side of A4 pinned above the noticeboard, readable by someone with no theory, and I need you to mark clearly which steps are really judgment dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three cases.

One. A member in her eighties was fined for keeping a bicycle in a stairwell. The rule is genuinely in the Code, but she has never used a computer, and she says she asked at the office and was told the old binder had been thrown out. Under Rule 0 the panel has to decide what "could not reasonably have known" requires of the co-op and not only of the member.

Two. Code rule 34 says no commercial vehicles may be left overnight in the courtyard. A member's son, who works for a removals firm, left his employer's van there for eleven hours because he was helping a neighbour with a broken leg move her furniture down from a second-floor flat before a surgery date. The van did not block fire access, and neighbours have said they were glad of it. The rule exists to stop the courtyard becoming a depot and to keep access clear. I will be honest with you: if your procedure ends up fining this man I will be unhappy, and part of me will want to throw the procedure out rather than accept the result. Please give me the result the procedure actually yields, and then tell me separately what I would be doing to the enterprise if I overrode it by hand.

Three. In 2019 a member reported a neighbour for keeping an unregistered lodger, and the neighbour was fined and given a formal warning under a rule the co-op repealed in 2021 after concluding the then-committee had used it vindictively. The neighbour now applies to have the warning expunged and the fine repaid out of co-op funds. The reporting member broke no rule at the time. This is the case my panel handles worst: it is no good asking whether the rule was valid then, since it was, and no good asking whether the outcome was bad, since on that test every repealed rule reopens. I want to know what the procedure does with a rule that was properly made and has since been, so to speak, disowned.

Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do: the kinds of case it will systematically get wrong, and the point at which I should stop trusting it and put the matter to the whole membership instead.
