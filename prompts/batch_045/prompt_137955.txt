I'm writing a magazine piece on rules that can change themselves — nomic, constitutional amendment clauses, the way a legal system can repeal the very rule that tells you how to repeal rules — and I want to open the section with a poem instead of another block of explanation. Give me a sestina, six stanzas plus a three-line envoy, end-words something like rule, amend, repeal, vote, void, remain (your pick if you've got better ones), where the form does the argument: one of the stanzas is an amendment, so it breaks the standard end-word rotation and uses a different permutation, and everything after it follows the new ordering. The envoy has to work either way, so a reader can't tell whether the amendment was legal. Don't resolve it and don't wink about it. It should also be right about the logic — the gap between a rule that changes what the rules say and a rule that changes how rules get changed, and what happens when a rule amends the rule that permits amendments. Someone who's actually played nomic should read it and nod, and someone who hasn't should still get a poem out of it. Under a page, no epigraph, and dont explain it afterwards.
