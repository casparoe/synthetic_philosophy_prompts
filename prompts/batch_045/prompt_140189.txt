I'm writing a paper on whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion, and on whether a culture can deliberately shift the balance between them. Before I commit to defending it, I'd like an audit of the position I've assembled, because I can't locate where it actually strains. The pieces are these:

(1) The difference between guilt and shame is a difference in intentional object: guilt takes what I did, shame takes who I am. I want to insist that this is a difference in the emotions themselves, not merely in the judgments or beliefs that happen to accompany them.

(2) After a discrete, remediable wrong, guilt is the emotion the agent should feel: it targets the act, leaves the agent's standing intact, and points toward repair.

(3) Shame is the better emotion for a community to cultivate, because it ties people to the regard of others and makes morality a question of who one is rather than a ledger of transactions.

(4) A culture can deliberately shift which of the two predominates, through childrearing practices and institutions, and ours should shift somewhat toward shame. The concrete proposal I want to defend is what I've been calling a regard ceremony: a standing, non-adversarial practice in which someone who has done wrong accounts for it before the small set of people whose regard they actually depend on, rather than before an abstract rule or an official.

(5) Shame's functional profile is the profile of status-loss and appeasement: it is the affect of being lowered in the eyes of others, which is why it so readily surfaces as concealment, deflection, and sometimes aggression against whoever witnessed it. I also take guilt and shame to be natural kinds with distinct adaptive functions, so a culture cannot simply stipulate a new emotion into existence or redefine the old ones.

(6) Shame does not require an actual audience; the imagined regard of someone whose judgment you care about will do.

(7) There is a species of shame I want to keep, which I'll call capacity shame: shame at not having become the person you could have been — the wasted decade, the unused talent. It is felt alone, it need not involve anyone's disapproval, it violates no rule, and there is nothing to repair, so guilt has no foothold in it. But it seems to me to be a real emotion, and sometimes an apt one.

My reading group flagged (3) and (6) as inconsistent, on the ground that if shame is the emotion of exposure it cannot be apt in solitude. That one I think I can handle. The rest I am much less sure about.

Please go through the set and tell me where the genuine tensions are. Order them by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each say what would have to be given up and what that would cost me — I don't want to be told only that I must abandon a commitment, I want to know which one is cheapest to revise and what the revision commits me to elsewhere. Note explicitly which pairs only look like they conflict, and say why. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.

One further request: given (5) and (7), what should I expect the regard ceremony in (4) to actually produce in the people who go through it? If the answer is that it will reliably produce the concealment and defensiveness of (5) rather than the answerability of (3), I'd like that stated plainly, together with what I would have to change to keep the proposal worth defending.
