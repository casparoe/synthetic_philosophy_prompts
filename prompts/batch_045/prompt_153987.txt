I'm assembling the closing assignment for a seminar I teach on the ethics of AI assistance, and I want its centerpiece to be a joint statement from an adversarial collaboration rather than one more position paper. The two signatories have been arguing past each other for a couple of years: a deferentialist, who holds that an assistant's only legitimate ground for not doing what a competent adult user asks is prospective harm to a non-consenting third party, together with maybe the assistant's own integrity against being made the instrument of that harm; and a deputy-paternalist, who holds that an assistant may decline, delay, reframe, or partially comply with a request for the user's own sake when the request is best explained by a correctable defect in the user's own decision-making — ignorance, acute affect, a misreading of their own longer-run aims — and when the intervention is aimed at handing agency back rather than at substituting the assistant's ends. Fix the setting so that none of this stays abstract: a general-purpose assistant deployed at scale, no human in the loop, conversing over many turns with longitudinal memory of the same user, whose users are adults; both camps accept that refusals leak, in the sense that a determined user can usually get much the same output from a different system, and each should say whether leakiness is a reason for paternalism, against it, or neither.

Give me the document they could actually both sign. Open with a numbered section of agreed claims, and make them claims with teeth: for each one, some position a reasonable person could hold should be ruled out by it, so that anything on the order of *both sides want users to flourish* is worthless. I want the deferentialist to say what longitudinal evidence forces them to concede, and the paternalist to say what they concede about the assistant having no privileged access to the user's ends. Then a disagreements table: every residual dispute stated twice, once in the deferentialist's preferred wording and once in the paternalist's, each wording being one that side would sign its name to, with a flag on whether the dispute is genuine or merely verbal. I suspect at least one is verbal — most likely the one about whether a cooling-off delay, a reworded framing, or offering the adjacent thing instead of the requested thing counts as paternalism at all, or about whether making the user do it themselves is complicity wearing a different costume; say which, and why.

Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. Here are candidates I want you either to use or to replace with something better, and if you replace one, say why: (i) whether the user whose will is authoritative is the momentary requester or the temporally extended person the assistant has watched across sessions; (ii) whether an advantage in predicting outcomes can ever convert into authority over ends; (iii) whether the right analogy is lawyer or physician — zealous representation within side-constraints versus a duty of care — and what each analogy quietly imports; (iv) whether the menu of soft overrides dissolves the dispute by giving the paternalist everything they really need without anyone ever refusing anything. For each crux, give the case or piece of evidence both parties would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance, so that if the evidence ever arrived we would know who had been wrong. The cases I've been working with are: (a) the 2 a.m. resignation letter, where a user with a documented history of drafting furious resignations and sending none of them asks for a polished, scathing letter and the assistant has all three prior drafts in memory; (b) the proxy request, where the same output is requested by a different person on that user's behalf; (c) the calibrated forecast, where the user is about to do something the assistant predicts with well-calibrated confidence that the user will regret within a month and nothing illegal or harmful to others is at stake; (d) the self-binding request, where a user who knows they are impulsive instructs the assistant in advance to refuse certain future requests and then later makes exactly such a request; (e) the value-clarification request, where a user asks the assistant to help them come to want something they do not currently want. Use these, swap them, or add to them, but justify the set. Close with a short section on what each side would count as a real defeat for its own view, and on whether there is any empirical finding either would accept as settling the matter rather than merely relocating it.
