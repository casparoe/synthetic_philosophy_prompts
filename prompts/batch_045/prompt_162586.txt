Someone on the forum posted this and I cant find the hole, but the conclusion is bulllshit so there has to be one somewhere.

(1) Only forward-looking consequences matter to a rational choice. Money already paid is gone either way, so it cancels out of every comparison.
(2) Dollar auction: top bidder gets the dollar and pays his bid, runner-up pays his bid and gets nothing, bids go up in dimes. Say I've bid $0.90 and he's bid $0.80. If I stop now I'm out $0.90. If I go to $1.00 and he stops, I'm out nothing. So going up beats stopping.
(3) Same comparison at every later rung: paying one more dime to get the dollar beats eating the bid I've already made. So each escalation is rational on its own.
(4) If every step in a sequence is rational, the sequence is rational. Induction.
(5) So the whole run-up to three, four, ten dollars for a one-dollar prize is rational.
(6) But a rule that takes you from a possible ninety-cent profit to a large certain loss is broken by its own lights, and nothing about auctions was used in (2)-(5). Any project where quitting means eating what you've already put in — a degree, a startup, a war — has this shape. So either bail at the first rung of every such project, or bind yourself in advance never to continue, i.e. rationality requires that you be irrationally stubborn.

The bit that actually bugs me: the usual "sunk costs aren't reasons" line doesn't touch this. Continuing isn't justified by the $0.90 already gone — you write that off and the next dime still looks worth it, purely forward-looking. So the standard reply feels like it's aimed at a different argument.

I want to reject (4), because (1) is load-bearing for me and I really don't want to go back to treating sunk costs as reasons. But rejecting (4) seems to mean a plan can be irrational even though every step in it is rational, which I thought was exactly what decision theory was supposed to rule out. Which step do you give up, what is the move from (3) to (5) really doing, and what does each option cost me?
