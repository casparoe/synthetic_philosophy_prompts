I'm a retired experimental physicist — accelerator work, mostly — who has drifted in to reading philosophy of computation over the last couple of years, probly more than is good for me. I've noticed I have firm gut verdicts on the hypercomputation cases but no idea what principle they add up to, and I'd like you to find it for me. British English, please, and no names, dates or citations — I can't look anything up from where I'm sitting.

My verdicts, going case by case:

- A spacetime in which one worldline has infinite proper time sitting entirely in the past of some other observer's finite endpoint (I forget whose name is on these), used to decide the halting problem: if that spacetime is really physically possible, that refutes the physical Church–Turing thesis. Not a trick, not a redefinition.
- A machine that takes 1/2 second for its first step, 1/4 for the second, 1/8 for the third, and so gets through infinitely many steps inside a second: cheating. Not a computation. Even though I can't name the step at which it goes wrong.
- A box that correctly answers halting questions on demand: not computing either — it's a measurement of something outside the machine, so it doesn't bear on the thesis at all.
- A device that reads some physical quantity to unlimited precision and uses the digits to decide an uncomputable set: cheating, for the same sort of reason as the accelerating one.
- A recurrent net with genuinely real-numbered weights doing the same: cheating too.
- A quantum computer: doesn't touch the thesis, and only for the boring reason that everything it computes was computable already.
- A gadget producing the digits of an uncomputable real by some genuinely stochastic physical process: just an expensive die, not a computation.

Here's the pair I can't reconcile. I want to say the relativistic machine would refute the thesis if it's physically possible — that treats the thesis as an empirical claim about the world. But I also want to say I'd never accept that any real device was computing something uncomputable: I'd always redscribe it as measurement, oracle-consultation, or idealisation — which treats it as something closer to a conceptual truth I'd protect by redefining. Those pull opposite ways.

Is there one principle that gets all seven verdicts, or nearly? Which of them does it force me to give up, and what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a machine finite in every respect, but whose transition table is given by a physical constant we can only ever measure to more and more digits?
