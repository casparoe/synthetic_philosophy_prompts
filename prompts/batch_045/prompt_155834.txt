I am writing the core handout for a clinical ethics consultation rotation, and I want its centrepiece to be a decision tree that takes a trainee from a single starting commitment — that a competently executed advance directive should normally be followed — down to a named position in the debate about why, and whether, the earlier self has authority over the later one. I do not want a survey of the literature or a list of the standard views; I want a classification instrument that sorts people by what they think should be done in concrete cases.

Requirements:

1. Construct the space first, then fill it. Begin with four to six yes/no branch questions that jointly generate the logical space of positions, note which combinations of answers are genuinely available, and only then locate the actual positions in that space.

2. Every branch question must be answerable by someone who has never heard the labels: phrase each as a concrete clinical scenario or as a claim to accept or reject, never as a question about the authority of the earlier self in the abstract, and never as a question that names a position.

3. The following distinctions must each get a branch node, or else be explicitly noted as one your tree cannot place:

- the asymmetry between directives that refuse interventions and directives that request them;
- the fit problem, i.e. whether authority decays when the clinical situation that arises is one the signer did not envisage;
- the sovereignty reading versus the evidentiary reading of a directive, i.e. whether it binds because authorizing a future self to be governed is itself valuable, or merely because it is the best available evidence of what the patient would now want;
- whether the authority of the earlier self requires biological identity, psychological continuity, or continuity of the specific values the directive records;
- instruction versus delegation, i.e. whether authority attaches to the rule the patient wrote or to a designated surrogate exercising substituted judgment;
- the tainted-source question: whether a directive should be discounted because the values it records were formed under the shadow of the very prognosis it now governs.

4. Use these cases at the nodes, since they are the ones my trainees actually bring: the patient with advanced dementia who is apparently contented and flinches from the burdensome course her directive demands be withheld; the patient who retains capacity but whose values have reversed since signing, and who now asks for the treatment his directive refused; the directive that is valid in form but was written while the signer was managing a family's finances under pressure.

5. Produce seven to nine leaves. Each leaf carries: a label, or unnamed if you think nobody defends it; a one-sentence statement of the position; and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The following must appear as leaves: a pure contemporaneous-welfare view on which the earlier self has no authority at all; a critical-interests or narrative-identity view; precedent autonomy read as sovereignty; precedent autonomy read evidentiarily; a revision-tolerant variant on which the earlier self's authority persists unless the later change is itself reflective and stable; a proxy-priority or delegationist view; and a deflationary view on which a directive's authority is procedural only — it allocates decision-rights and limits institutional exposure — and does no moral work.

6. Close with three diagnostics: which combinations the logical space allows but nobody occupies, with a verdict for each on whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; which pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, and what extra question would be needed to pull them apart; and which single branch question does the most work, versus which is nearly vacuous because almost everyone answers it the same way.

Write for clinical ethics trainees, keep the whole instrument to about two pages, and do not supply citations or further reading.
