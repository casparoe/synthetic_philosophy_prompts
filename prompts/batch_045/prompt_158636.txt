You are a philosopher of mind and social epistemology who also writes short literary fiction. Work in two stages. First, in note form, decide what claim your story is going to make — not its plot, its thesis about the nature of deliberation — and state in two or three sentences what you take the right claim to be and what the strongest thing against it is. Then write the story.

The setting. In a near-future polity, the river-valley water authority of Alder Reach has replaced most of its standing committees with a procedure its staff call the Cascade. The Cascade is an institutionalized version of an idealization from AI-alignment discussions, sometimes called factored cognition or HCH: a question is answered by splitting it into a few sub-questions, splitting those again, until the leaves are narrow enough for one person to answer in an afternoon; each person sees only their own question, a short brief written by their parent node, and the distilled answers of their own children; answers are compressed upward, level by level, into a single recommendation. Its defenders say this is what good deliberation really is once you strip away the room, the speeches and the personalities —that the ideal deliberation is not a conversation but a well-chosen decomposition plus reliable summarization.

The case. Alder Reach must decide whether to decommission the century-old Vale Street floodwall and adopt managed retreat, buying out roughly 380 households in the Lowmarch neighborhood, where the protagonist's younger brother owns a house. The protagonist is Wen Salter, sixty-seven, retired machinist, drawn by lottery as a leaf node. Her assigned sub-question is narrow: whether the 2019 hydrological model remains an adequate basis for the hundred-year flood estimate, given the new upstream logging data. Working through it, she finds that the estimate depends on a treatment of upstream sediment that two sibling sub-questions handle in incompatible ways — one was asked about sediment as a maintenance cost, the other about sediment as a flood-risk variable — and that the incompatibility will never surface, because each sibling's answer is compressed to a single line before it reaches their shared parent, whose brief was written before either answer existed.

The failure mode to dramatize is what the staff, when pressed, call evaporation. A node has exactly two channels to its parent: an answer, and a confidence number. There is no channel for "your question rests on a distinction that does not hold," or for "the sibling answer you are about to combine with mine was asked a question that presupposes the opposite of what mine presupposes." Anything that does not fit the parent's vocabulary is lost on the way up. And because nodes are rated on whether they answered the question they were given, flagging a malformed question is a cost, not a credit. Above this sits a further phenomenon: the Frame Office, which writes the top-level decomposition, has framed "should we retreat?" as "what is the expected cost of retreat relative to repair?" — so the question of what the authority owes the people it is asking to leave has no branch to live in. Call the general condition frame lock: after enough cycles the institution can answer anything inside a frame and nothing about the frame, since "is this the right frame?" is itself routed into the frame and decomposed there.

Do not let the old way off easily. Give the story a rival: the Long Table — twenty people in one room for three days, one transcript, everyone hears everything, anyone may move to re-frame at any point, decision by vote. Show its failure modes just as concretely: the articulate dominate, the third afternoon is worse than the first morning, one respected engineer's offhand remark silently fixes the range of the debate, and the transcript is long enough that nobody reads the part where the decisive objection was raised. The story should compare failure modes, not deliver a verdict.

Formal requirements:

- Length: 1,400 to 1,800 words.
- Include one passage that simply shows a decomposition: the parent's brief and the three or four sub-questions it spawns, quoted in the document's own terse official register.
- Include one passage where the reader sees a leaf answer in full — two or three paragraphs, with its hedges and misgivings — set against the single sentence that actually reaches its parent. Let the compression make the point; do not narrate the moral.
- Include, inside the fiction, someone proposing a repair: a "malformed question" flag routed to a standing Frame Review, or a minority report that travels upward unsummarized. Have another character press the obvious objection — a flag is itself a question and a Frame Review is itself a committee, so the repair either adds another level to the tree or quietly reinstalls a single un-factored human at the top, which is where the idealization began. Wen should grasp this and not know what to do with it.
- End on the specific unresolved question, not on a moral or a resolution.

Write for a reader who knows this territory and is tired of being told conclusions. No citations, no bibliography, no real names, no footnotes: everything must be legible from the story itself. Favor precise prose over atmosphere, and no dystopian sheen — these people are competent and mostly trying to do the right thing.
