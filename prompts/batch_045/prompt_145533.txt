I'm applying for a twelve-month predoctoral fellowship (no teaching; expected output one journal-length article of about 9,000 words plus one workshop paper) and I need the proposal document itself drafted — a short abstract at the top, section headings, prose rather than bullet lists where prose will do. Please write it for a generalist philosopher on a selection committee rather than for a specialist. I'll fill in the references myself, so don't spend space on a bibliography or on attributing positions to named authors: refer to views by their content (the decision-theoretic or formal-modelling reply, the authenticity or self-creation reply) rather than by who is supposed to hold them, since I want the committee to judge the argument and not my reading list.

Here is the half-formed idea. Everything I've read about transformative choice treats the paradigm cases as irreversible, all-or-nothing, and once-in-a-lifetime: becoming a parent, converting to a religion, undergoing surgery that ends one way of life and begins another, emigrating. But the choices people actually describe in that vocabulary are often neither irreversible nor all-or-nothing. A relative of mine recently decided whether to get a cochlear implant: the device can be switched off, the surgery is not the end of the matter, and there are graded versions of the same decision — an implant later rather than now, one ear rather than two, a trial period with a simulator beforehand. Similar structures show up in psychedelic-assisted therapy courses, a trial year in a religious community, an immersive-virtual-reality programme, a sabbatical in a field one knows nothing about. My suspicion is that two things are being run together in these cases. First: reversibility in the world is not reversibility in the self — you can turn the device off, but you cannot un-be the person who has heard, and if the normative residue of a transformative choice survives only in the second sense, then the usual framing has been leaning on the wrong feature all along. Second: there is a growing practical industry of gap-closing — simulations, predictive questionnaires, curated first-personal testimony, trial runs — and it is not obvious that closing the epistemic gap always improves the choice; some of what people value about these decisions seems to be located precisely in not knowing how one will come out.

So the proposal should do the following. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, and be explicit about the stipulation you are making: are these reversible, dose-graded cases transformative choices in the same sense, or only analogous ones, and what follows either way? Explain why the two standard kinds of answer leave the question open — sketch the modelling reply (that the problem is just one of inference or of valuing information under uncertainty) and the self-creation reply (that the point of such choices is not to satisfy one's preferences but to make oneself), and say what each of them cannot handle. Give the method, month by month or section by section, over twelve months: what I would actually construct (a taxonomy of cases, a toy model, a set of counterfactual conditions), and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives — pure formal modelling, pure armchair case analysis, or deferring to empirical psychology. Say what a positive result would look like, ideally in conditional form: if such-and-such holds, then reversible transformative choice collapses into a familiar problem about option value and exploration; if something else holds, there is a distinctive residue, and here is what it is. Include an honest failure section: what would show that the project has no contribution of its own — for instance if it dissolves into ordinary decision theory on one side, or into the metaphysics of personal identity on the other — and a scope assessment, telling me plainly if the question is too large for twelve months and, if so, which narrowing you would recommend and what would be lost by it. Finally, one of my supervisors says the whole thing is just exploration-versus-exploitation with a payoff function that changes as a result of the choice, and that this is a well-understood problem; build in a section that answers that objection directly and say what would count as evidence that he is right.
