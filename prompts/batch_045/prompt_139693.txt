A friend of mine who does vision research has very firm intuitions about a family of hypothetical cases. She has ruled on a fair number of them, and I now need her verdict on one she hasn't been asked about, because my team is about to make a design decision that depends on it. She knows the literature on this — she says the first few cases are a famous historical problem, something to do with Molyneux, and that reasonable people have come down on both sides — but she refuses to name the cases or point me at anything to read, on the grounds that it would spoil the exercise. So I'm asking you instead. Please reason it out rather than telling me whom to read: I can't chase references from where I am, and a bibliography would be useless to me even if I could.

Her verdicts so far, as close to her own wording as I can get. Please assume throughout that any sensory restoration described works perfectly at the level of the organ itself, so that the answer is never going to be that the surgery doesn't really work that well in practice.

1. A man blind from birth has his sight restored at thirty. Shown a cube and a sphere he would identify instantly by touch, and asked which is which by sight before he touches either: yes, straight away, without groping around.

2. The same man, but now two balls identical in every tactile respect, one red and one blue, and he is told which is which while holding them. Asked to say which is red by sight alone: no, and she thinks the question is barely coherent.

3. The same man, told by touch that one object is twice the size of another. By sight, which is the larger: yes.

4. The same man, watching a silent shape pass behind a narrow vertical slit. Can he tell, by sight, whether it is travelling left to right or right to left: yes.

5. The same man, asked how many separate objects are on the table in front of him, having only ever counted them by touch: yes, though she hesitated here and said it would take him a moment.

6. The reverse direction: a woman who loses her sight at thirty and is handed a cube and a sphere she has only ever seen. Can she tell which is which by touch, immediately: yes, and she says she would bet money on it.

7. A woman deaf from birth whose hearing is enabled at twenty-five. Told which side of a room a buzzer is on by feeling its vibration, can she then tell, by hearing alone, whether a buzzing sound is on her left or her right: yes. But asked whether a sound is a violin or a flute, having handled both and felt them vibrate: no.

8. A person who has never smelled anything in her life, given a working sense of smell, asked to identify by smell the coffee she has drunk every morning for years: no.

Now the case I actually care about. A woman blind from birth has used, since she was seven, a head-mounted camera that converts the scene in front of her into a continuously updated soundscape: height in the image maps to pitch, left-to-right position maps to stereo pan, brightness maps to loudness. She is fluent with it — she navigates, recognises objects, reads large lettering, and can tell her cube from her sphere by their sound signatures alone. Assume the cube and the sphere in question have always been behind glass, so she has never touched them; the device is the only route by which she has ever encountered them. At thirty, a treatment restores her retinal function.

My questions for you are these. Can she tell, by sight, before touching anything and before hearing the device, which of the two is the cube and which is the sphere? Can she say how many objects are on the table, having only ever counted them through the device? And does either answer change if the device had delivered its signal as a vibrating grid on her back rather than as sound, or if she had learned it at twenty-eight instead of at seven?

What I want from you is not really the yes or no. I want the rule she is running. Tell me which of her earlier verdicts is doing the most work in narrowing things down, and which one she would have to abandon if she wanted to change her mind.
