been meaning to write down my actual position on perception for ages — I want to post it somewhere so people can attack the strongest version instead of me re-explaining it in comment sections every time — and every time I try I get the feeling the pieces dont fit together. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Perceiving and hallucinating are not the same basic kind of state. There's no core experience they share, no highest common factor you can carve out of the bad case and then go looking for in the good one.

(2) Naive realism in the good case, and not a watery version of it: the actual mind-independent things you're aware of are literally part of what makes the experience the experience it is. Constituents, not just causes. This is the bit I care most about and would be unhappiest to give up.

(3) Physicalism, and specifically: phenomenal character is fixed by what's going on locally in the brain. Same local neural state, same what-it's-likeness, full stop. I lean on this for two separate things — it's my explanation of why introspection can't tell the good case from the bad one, and it's why I think there will eventually be a decent neural story about hallucination.

(4) Method: I think the argument from illusion is a bad argument, because the bent-stick-in-water style cases are deviant cases and you don't get to generalize from deviant cases to perception in general. But the causal version — where a hallucination is produced by exactly the proximal cause a veridical perception would have had — does worry me, and I dont think disjunctivists have a satisfying line on it.

(5) Epistemology: hard internalist about justification. If it looks the same from the inside it's equally justified, and the hallucinating subject is blameless and shouldn't do anything differently. But I also want to say that in the good case your reason just is the factive state of seeing the thing, and that in the bad case there's nothing playing that role. And I like the knowledge norm for belief.

(6) I came to disjunctivism from the epistemology side, not the metaphysics side. The whole appeal is that perception is supposed to put you in direct non-inferential contact with the world and give you knowledge of it, and I can't see how any common-factor picture delivers that.

Then the case that got me worried. Call it the replay case: I'm wearing a passthrough headset, cameras outside, screens inside, so I'm looking at the real room with a real apple on a real table. Without any change I could notice, the system switches from the live camera to a recording of the same room from thirty seconds ago. The room hasn't changed in the meantime, so the apple is still sitting exactly where it looks like it is, and what's on the screens is pixel-identical either way. My verdict: I'm not seeing the apple any more — I'm having a hallucination that happens to match — but the experience is exactly what it would have been thirty seconds earlier. Variant B: instead of a recording, the feed is swapped to a live camera in an identical room next door with an identical apple on an identical table, and there is still an apple right in front of me. My verdict there: also definitely not seeing that apple.

So: is this a package? I have a vague sense that (3) is doing more work than I've noticed, and that maybe I'm running two claims together somewhere in (5), but I genuinely don't know which of these I'd drop. Go through my commitments and tell me which ones actually conflict, worst first, and for each one say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Please also separate out the pairs that merely look like they conflict — I'd bet at least one of them is fine and I just can't see why — and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
