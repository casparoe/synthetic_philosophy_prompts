I was in a reading-group discussion of the presumptuous philosopher case last week — the setup where two cosmological theories fit all the third-personal data equally well, but one of them implies something like a trillion times more observers per unit volume, and the question is whether the bare fact that you find yourself existing confirms the crowded theory by a factor of a trillion. Someone closed the argument with the line "you just have to treat yourself as a random sample from the set of all observers," and when I pressed him he refined it to "from the set of people who could have been you," which he claimed avoids the whole reference-class mess and also handles the awkward cases — where the extra observers are exact epistemic duplicates of you, or where they are beings whose evidence is nothing like yours — without having to decide whether those observers are in your reference class at all. I don't think one sentence does the work he thinks it does.

Please disambiguate it. What are the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making: about what the sample space is (actually existing observers versus merely possible ones, persons versus observer-moments versus evidentially indistinguishable candidates), about what "random" amounts to (uniform over which measure, and on what grounds), and about what the sampling assumption is supposed to constrain — a prior over worlds, a conditional distribution over which observer you are, or both? Lay the readings out as a menu with verdicts: which are near-trivial methodological platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are false or generate instability, and which actually deliver the trillion-to-one. Test the plain reading and his "could have been you" variant against the duplicates case and the unlike-beings case, and say whether the variant buys anything or just relocates the difficulty.

I want a verdict, not only the menu: commit to the reading you think is defensible, say whether the presumptuous philosopher's trillion-to-one survives on it, and give your reasons. Tell me which single step in that commitment you are least confident in, and what would change your mind about it.
