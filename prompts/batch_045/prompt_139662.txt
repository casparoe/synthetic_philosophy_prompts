Our TA had us work through a made-up model, which I'll reproduce so you don't have to guess. A population of constant size consists of "ash-bark beetles" that are randomly paired each round to play a prisoner's dilemma with payoffs T = 5, R = 3, P = 1, S = 0 (so mutual cooperation beats alternating exploitation, and R is better than the average of T and S). At the end of each round, with probability 0.8 the pair stays together for another round and with probability 0.2 they separate and are each returned to a global pool and re-paired uniformly at random. Independently, each individual dies at the end of a round with probability 0.1 and its slot is filled by a copy of some individual, with fitter strategies more likely to be copied; the TA said the exact update rule doesn't matter for what we're discussing. Beetles recognise only their current partner, remember only what that partner did last round, cannot detect kin, and there is no reputation system — so the only available mechanism is direct reciprocity. Strategies are inherited with a small mutation rate; there are no separate sexes.

My argument:

(1) In the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, defection strictly dominates: whatever the opponent does, defecting pays more. That is just a fact about the payoff ordering, so it doesn't depend on anyone's psychology or rationality.

(2) Dominance doesn't care whether the game is repeated. In the repeated game, at each round I face the same payoff table, so at each round I do better by defecting than by cooperating, given what my partner does in that round. So "always defect" should still be the fitness-maximising policy, and any claim that cooperation can be favoured by selection must be smuggling in a change of payoffs or assuming my partner plays some non-maximising policy like Tit for Tat.

(3) The standard condition we were given says that cooperation via direct reciprocity is stable just when the continuation probability exceeds (T − R)/(T − P), which with our numbers is 2/4 = 0.5. Our beetles have 0.8, well clear of that. So Tit for Tat is an evolutionarily stable strategy here.

(4) An ESS is by definition a strategy that, once resident, cannot be invaded by any rare alternative. So a single mutant Tit-for-Tat beetle appearing in an all-defect population should be able to increase, and the population should evolve to cooperation. Our TA used the phrase "kindling frequency" for the minimum starting frequency of cooperators needed for this to take off, but if (3) and (4) are right, that frequency is effectively zero — one mutant — or else the phrase is a red herring.

(5) So the "problem of cooperation", at least in this model, reduces entirely to an empirical question about how long interactions last. Kin selection, spatial viscosity, and group selection then look like rival explanations of the same phenomenon, to be adjudicated by measuring interaction length; and once we know that pairs last long enough, they do no independent work.
