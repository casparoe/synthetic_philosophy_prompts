I'm in a graduate seminar on population ethics and infinite value, and next week I have to circulate a one-page statement of where I've landed so the rest of the group can audit it. I'd like a dry run before I hand it over, because I have a feeling the pieces don't all fit and I can't locate the fault. Assume throughout that welfare is cardinally measurable and interpersonally comparable, that every life I mention is worth living, and that the puzzle isn't going to be dissolved by measurement worries. Here is the set.

(A) In finite populations I'm a totalist: holding the people fixed, more total welfare is better, and adding a life worth living to an otherwise unchanged world cannot make the world worse. I've made peace with the Repugnant Conclusion rather than treating it as a reductio — it looks to me like the predictable upshot of caring about total welfare rather than about averages.

(B) Impersonality, in a strong form: the value of a world is fixed by how many lives there are at each welfare level, and not at all by which lives those are or whose they are. Any two worlds that agree, level by level, on the number of lives at that level are equally good, however the levels are distributed across the individuals. My reason is the usual one — no person is privileged as a site of value — and I take the finite total view in (A) to presuppose something in this neighbourhood.

(C) Strong Pareto, and I want it to survive the move to infinity: if two outcomes involve exactly the same people, everyone is at least as well off in the first as in the second, and someone is strictly better off in the first, then the first is better. I want this when the population is infinite, and I want it even when the improvement is confined to a single person.

(D) Finite insensitivity: if a world already contains infinitely many lives worth living, then adding finitely many further lives — or removing finitely many — each of finite positive welfare, makes no difference to how good it is. My reason is that the total is already divergent: infinity plus or minus a finite amount is just infinity.

(E) Not every infinite world is as good as every other, and the ranking runs by level. Infinitely many lives at level 5 is better than infinitely many lives at level 2. And in the pair I keep going back to — call them Split and Uniform — I judge Uniform better: Split contains infinitely many lives at level 1 and infinitely many at level 100; Uniform contains infinitely many lives at level 100 and no others.

(F) A methodological constraint: I need the account to yield a complete ordering, one that ranks every pair of outcomes rather than declaring most of them incomparable, because I want to use it when I don't know whether the population I'm affecting is finite or infinite.

I don't know which of these is doing the damage, and I'd rather not be told which one I should be attached to. What I want is: (1) which pairs are genuinely in tension, ordered by severity rather than listed as equally bad; (2) for each genuine tension, the smallest case that exhibits it — construct it explicitly, with the worlds and the welfare levels, since I'd much rather be shown a construction than be told that a result exists; (3) which pairs only look as though they're in tension, and what dissolves the appearance; (4) the single revision, or the smallest set of them, that costs me least, together with what it costs me elsewhere in the set — in particular whether it forces me to abandon (F). Please skip any survey of the literature and any attempt to tell me which named position my set is closest to; I want the audit of this set, not a map of the field.
