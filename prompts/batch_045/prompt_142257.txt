i'm on a tiny data-governance committee at the transit agency where i work, and we're stuck on whether the new fare app should keep a year of trip history per rider or wipe it after 48 hours. one guy on the committee says the same thing every meeting: privacy is a commons, it gets overgrazed like any commons, so asking each rider to consent is like asking each herder to volunteer to graze fewer sheep. he uses it to push two things at once, wich is what makes me suspicious — first that notice-and-consent is structurally doomed so we need blanket retention limits and purpose limits instead of a toggle in the app, and second that any rider who says they personally don't care about the data is like a herder saying his one extra sheep won't matter, so they dont get a vote.

can you audit the analogy properly? i want the correspondence table — what plays the pasture, the sheep, the herders, the grass growing back, the enclosure — and then where it breaks.

my own sticking points, in case they're useful. (1) a pasture is rival: one sheep's mouthful isn't another's. but my trip data sitting in a warehouse doesn't seem to use up anything you might have wanted, so what exactly is the depletable stock? the data itself, or something like the background condition of not being trackable, or the accuracy of what can be inferred about people who never gave us anything? (2) i can't work out who the herders are even supposed to be. if the riders are the herders, they're putting sheep onto the pasture rather than taking grass off it, and the thing being consumed isn't grass, it's the riders. my colleague waves this away but i think it might be the whole ballgame, because depending on how you assign the roles the same story seems to justify opposite policies. (3) the spillover runs in a direction the pasture story doesn't have: once enough riders hand over a year of trips, a model trained on that can apparently guess things about the riders who opted out entirely — where they live, maybe the clinic they stop at on tuesdays. he says that's just the tragedy working. to me it feels more like herd immunity, where my choice moves your risk and not just mine. is that the better one, or does it import its own junk — thresholds, free riding, a single well-defined herd — and does the direction of the externality (i gain, you get exposed) fit either picture?

what i actually need at the end: does his conclusion survive? does this framing really license a hard 48-hour rule, or does it equally well license what our vendor wants (keep everything, pinky-swear not to sell it)? and if the depletion really happens when data gets linked and re-inferred rather than when it's collected, doesn't his own analogy point at a totally different remedy than the one he's pushing — targeting inference and linkage rather than collection? tell me if i've got that backwards. and if the commons is the wrong picture, give me something better i can say out loud in a meeting; i've seen privacy-is-like-quiet and privacy-is-like-darkness floated and both sound a bit precious to me.
