I write long-read pieces on science and ideas, and I'm working on one about 'believe and you will' culture — placebo responses in medicine, confidence rituals in elite sport, teacher-expectancy effects in classrooms — and I want to give general readers a philosophical grip on why 'you have reason to believe this' and 'it would be good for you to believe this' are not the same claim. The frame is the wrong kind of reasons problem: a consideration can count in favour of an attitude by making the attitude worth having rather than by bearing on whether the attitude is correct.

What I need is a single case I can put in front of non-philosophical readers, engineered so that two standard ways of drawing the distinction come apart in one direction. The consideration must be a reason of the wrong kind on the source test — its entire force comes from the value of ending up in that state, and it would evaporate if holding the attitude had no such effects — and yet a reason of the right kind on the fittingness test — it genuinely bears on whether the attitude's object has the property the attitude presents it as having (truth, in the case of belief).

Constraints: one paragraph; ordinary people in an ordinary setting; no demon, no bet, no bribe or threat, no promise, no predictor or oracle; the consideration must be one that a real person in that situation would sincerely offer as a reason ('I have reason to think X'), not a philosopher's device; and the divergence must be generated by the mechanics of the case rather than stipulated.

Alongside the scenario I need: the verdict each test returns and the reasoning to it; a note on which single feature of the case is doing the work and what happens to both verdicts if that feature is removed; which verdict you take to be the right one; and how someone who wants to keep the other test would attack the case.
