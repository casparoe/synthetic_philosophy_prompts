I’m a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep comming back to the Anthropocene fight. On one side you have stratigraphers pushing for a formal golden spike around 1950—plutonium fallout, Crawford Lake, that sort of marker. On another you have historians and geographers saying 1610 or the Industrial Revolution. And on a third you have the early-farming camp, who say greenhouse gases started rising thousands of years ago because of agriculture. The formal proposal got voted down in 2024, yet nobody seems to have moved; people just swapped to calling it an ‘event’ or a ‘historical period.’ Lately I’ve been talking to researchers who think the whole start-date hunt is a catagory error. They’ve floated something they call a ‘rolling Anthropocene’: instead of one boundary, the period has a provisional onset that gets revised every fifty years as the stratigraphic signal gets clearer, so the start date is always the earliest detectable global signal plus error bars. My hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because the sides aren’t really disagreeing about the evidence; they’re answering different questions—one wants a synchronous rock layer for the geologic time scale, another wants to know when human societies became a planetary force, and the ‘who decides’ part is a turf war over whether stratigraphy’s formal rules or critical usage gets to name the thing.
