Here's what I'm after. I run a small film-and-philosophy night, we're doing Coco next month, and I want to read the film through Hofstadter's picture of the self as a pattern rather than a substance — the view he works out in I Am a Strange Loop, and the one he applies to his own late wife: the idea that an I is a strange-loop pattern instantiated in a brain, that nothing stops the same rough pattern being instantiated somewhere else at the same time or later, and so that after someone dies a piece of them can genuinely keep going in the heads of the people who knew them, in wildly unequal amounts. I'm less interested in whether that view is true than in using it as a lens, so please don't drift into a general defense or critique of it.

Three features of the view I want you to keep hold of, because I think the film quietly violates them:

1. It's graded, not all-or-nothing: more of the pattern, more faithfully realized, in more heads, is more persistence. Less is less.
2. It's fidelity-sensitive: a scrambled, sentimentalized, or simply false version of someone is a worse instance of them, and at some point stops being them at all.
3. It doesn't need the original to be conscious or have a point of view. The distributed remnant isn't a subject somewhere; it's a bunch of structure in other people's heads.

Coco looks at first like it's dramatizing exactly this. The dead go on existing only as long as somebody living remembers them; Héctor is physically weakening in the Land of the Dead as Coco's memory of him thins out; what saves him is her being prompted back into remembering, plus a photograph and a song. But I think the fit is worse than it looks, and I want you to work the seam:

- The film's rule seems to be a threshold — one rememberer is enough, a million buy you nothing extra — while Héctor's onscreen fading is dosage-shaped. Is the film running a Hofstadterian dial or a switch, and does it matter which?
- Fidelity. Coco was tiny when Héctor left, so what she has is a toddler's fragment, and the thing that finally restores him is partly a song and partly a corrected story. Meanwhile Ernesto de la Cruz is in millions of heads under a description that is simply false, and by the film's own bookkeeping that keeps him going indefinitely. So the most-replicated person in the movie is the villain, and arguably the best carrier of Héctor is an artifact. Does the film have any way to distinguish carrying someone from merely hosting a story about them, or does it treat all memory as equally load-bearing? What would the Hofstadter-style view have to say about de la Cruz's counterfeit immortality?
- Host-dependence, and the duty it creates. If whether you go on existing depends on other people's attention, then withholding attention is a kind of killing, and curating it is power. Imelda tearing her husband's face out of the ofrenda photo is on this reading the most consequential act in the film, and the ending mostly forgives her for it. Does the movie notice what it's committed to, or does it buy the emotional reconciliation and quietly drop the implication?
- And the obvious objection I want you to take a stand on, because someone at film night will raise it: is Coco a distributed-I story at all? The Land of the Dead isn't located in anybody's brain, Héctor there has projects and feelings and recognises people, and memory functions only as a gatekeeping condition on an ordinary afterlife. If that's right, the Hofstadter lens is a costume the film tried on for a scene or two. Tell me honestly whether you think that's the case, or whether something in the film resists the deflation.
