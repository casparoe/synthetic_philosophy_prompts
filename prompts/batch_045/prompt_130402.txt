I'm a first-year PhD student in cognitive science, and I've been going to an informal reading group on the "strange inversion of reasoning" — the Darwinian move of explaining apparent design without a designer and the Turing move of explaining intelligent-seeming competence by a machine that follows procedures without understanding anything, the pair that gets summarized as competence without comprehension. I'm sympathetic to it; it does seem to me the only way to avoid positing something that does the explanatory work by being smarter than the thing it explains. But a visiting speaker at our department last month said, offhandedly, that the whole picture is self-refuting in the way crude eliminativism about the mind is, and my supervisor waved that away as the old argument-from-reason in costume. Waving it away didn't answer it for me, so I tried to write the objection out properly and now I can't find the step I'd reject. Here it is:

(1) Darwin's inversion, general: for any competence of a living thing, including our own cognitive competences, there is an explanation of how it got there that runs through a mindless, algorithmic process of variation and differential retention, none of whose steps involves understanding what the competence is for or why it works. So no intelligent author, and no prior comprehension, is needed to produce competence.
(2) Turing's inversion, general: any competence that can be decomposed into steps a machine could follow can be exercised by a system that comprehends nothing at all; its success is fully accounted for by its tracking formal features of its inputs and transitions, not by any grasp of what any of it means.
(3) Comprehension itself is a competence of a finite physical system — something we do, when we do it, by way of mechanisms. So if (1) and (2) hold in general, they hold of comprehension: the competence of understanding is itself something that arose without understanding and is exercised, at the sub-personal level, by machinery that understands nothing.
(4) If a competence is both produced without comprehension and exercised, at bottom, by processes that involve no comprehension, then comprehension does no work: it is not what gets you the competence, and it is not what makes the exercise of it succeed. The most it can be is a summary description of what the machinery is already doing; the least, an idle spin of wheels that makes no difference to anything.
(5) If comprehension does no work in producing or sustaining our judgments, then we never believe anything because we grasp that it is so. Our beliefs are the output of mechanisms, the way a computer's printout is.
(6) If we never believe anything because we grasp that it is so, then no belief of ours is held for a reason, nothing we believe is justified in the way that would make it knowledge — including the belief that (1) and (2) are true, and including every proof anyone has ever checked.

(8) So one of (1)–(6) has to go, and I don't know which.

I'd most like to keep (1), (2) and (3): the inversions look right to me, and comprehension really does look like something a physical system does. The two exits I've tried both stall in ways I can't quite articulate. The first is to say that (4) confuses levels — that comprehension is realized in sub-personal machinery rather than caused by it, and realization isn't competition, so there's no exclusion problem; but then I can't say what comprehension contributes that the machinery doesn't, and the objection just reappears one level down. The second is to say that (1) is only a claim about origins — how competences got here — and says nothing about what happens on any given occasion when someone exercises one; but the Turing half of the story isn't about origins at all, it's about a system performing right now while understanding nothing, so reading (1) historically just moves the problem into (2). Can you tell me which step you'd reject, why that step looked innocent, and what each of the alternatives costs — including what it costs someone who wants to keep both inversions and still claim to know anything? If the honest answer is that the argument is valid and I have to choose between the inversions and my own epistemic standing, say so and tell me which is cheaper.
