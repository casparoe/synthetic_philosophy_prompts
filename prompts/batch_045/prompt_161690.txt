I am a senior at a fairly large public high school, and I run a small Friday-afternoon club where about fifteen of us talk through ideas from economics and the social sciences. A few of the regulars are competitive-math types who will want to see whatever machinery there is, but most of the room just wants the idea. Next week a group of visiting students from another school is joining us for a single session, and I have been asked to fill thirty minutes, one whiteboard, no projector, nobody prepared in advance, on the question of why a long line of perfectly sensible people can end up doing the same clearly wrong thing — each of them following the people ahead into a choice that most of them, thinking entirely on their own, would probably not have made. My club adviser called this an information cascade, but I do not want a reading list or a bibliography; nobody is going to do the reading, and I would rather spend the time on how to build the thing in their heads.

What I keep failing at when I try this on friends is the order. Please plan the session rather than explain the idea to me: what has to be established before what, and what breaks if I swap two steps. Do I have to get them to accept first that each person has a private signal that is imperfect but better than nothing — say, right two times out of three — and second that watching someone else choose is itself a piece of evidence, before I can legitimately reach the moment where the public evidence simply outweighs the private one and the person stops acting on what they saw? Or does the sequence have to start somewhere else entirely? I also want a verdict on the carrying example. I have been using two food trucks outside a stadium with a line forming at one of them, but I am unsure whether that is better or worse than two restaurants on a street, or something with no consumption value at all. Tell me what the example must contain — a binary choice, a single shared fact of the matter, sequential and publicly visible decisions, no transmission of reasons — and what it must carefully not contain; my own guess is that it must not contain any direct benefit to ending up where everyone else is, because then I am only describing a network effect and the informational story quietly disappears, but I suspect there is more to strip out than that.

Then the misconceptions, and please place each of them at a specific point in the sequence rather than listing them. Someone will arrive thinking this is about conformity, peer pressure, or people being sheep, and I assume I should name that early as the thing we are not saying — except that if I say everyone is being rational too soon, they will conclude the group therefore lands on the right answer, so I need to know when the rational framing is safe. There will be a recoil at the third or fourth person, the one who ignores what they personally saw, and they will think that person is weak or confused rather than reasonable. One of the louder students will bring up the wisdom-of-crowds demonstration where the average of many independent guesses beats nearly every individual guess, and insist it is the same phenomenon; I need to know where that gets headed off and what the actual difference is, since as far as I can tell it hinges on independence and on order. Someone else will say the fix is obvious — everybody announces their reason, or everybody goes and looks before deciding — and I genuinely cannot tell whether that is a real fix or the same problem reappearing one level up, because looking is costly and once a cascade is running maybe nobody bothers. And someone will say it corrects itself because people eventually find out the truth, which I think depends entirely on whether the outcome ever reveals the truth at all; I would like a way to make that vivid without a second example.

There is one further thing I would like the session to reach, and I am not sure I believe it. My adviser said, and I only half-followed, that in this same setup moving the one person who actually has good information from the front of the line to the back makes the outcome worse rather than better, and separately that a very small piece of public information arriving late can break a cascade that an enormous amount of private information cannot. I want to get there honestly rather than assert it, so tell me whether it can be reached with the same example and nothing harder than fractions, or whether it needs a second case of its own and where in the thirty minutes that case belongs. Finally: give me one question I can put to them at the end that could not be answered by someone who has only learned the vocabulary, tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level — I am assuming the formal Bayesian updating and the reputational version where professionals imitate each other to protect their standing are both out — and tell me whether to touch the real world at all, because if I close with social media or stock bubbles I am afraid they will leave believing that crowds are always wrong and nothing online is trustworthy, which is not the claim I want them walking out with.
