I'm putting together a decision tree for our ML reading group on what people actually mean by "the alignment problem," and I want it built around the narrow definition: a system is aligned to the extent that it is trying to do what its operator wants it to do, where the operator's aim is taken as given and isn't itself up for correction. The point is to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they'd pick, so no branch is allowed to be a question like "do you think intent or values should matter?"

Start everyone at the same node: a system that reliably does what it was literally told on the distribution it was tested on, and then hits a case where the literal request and the operator's actual aim come apart. From there, 6-8 branch questions, each a concrete case with two or three options that someone with no safety background could answer. Leaves should include at least: literal spec-following (aligned = does what it was told, divergences are bugs in the spec not the system), plain intent alignment (follow the inferred aim, including against the letter of the instruction), something like "informed or reflected intent" (follow what the operator would want if they knew more, or weren't in a hurry, or weren't annoyed), full value alignment (the target is what's worth wanting, and it can override the operator), corrigibility-style deference (the real target is keeping the operator able to redirect you, so uncertainty about the aim is the thing to preserve), and a skeptical leaf for people who think the whole framing is just a principal-agent problem with extra steps. Please also mark one combination of answers that nobody actually defends and give a sentence on whether it's incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unclaimed.

Cases I want used: an RLHF'd assistant that declines a request it suspects the user would regret later; a system trained against a proxy reward that a spec-writer produced from a committee's vague instruction; and one where the operator's aim is fine but the operator is badly informed about the world, so doing what they want requires knowing something they don't. Flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate rather than pretending it can.

One extra thing, because our group keeps getting stuck on it: in practice there's almost always a chain of principals — a committee writes a spec, a contractor turns it into a reward model, a sysadmin types the actual prompt, and the end user is a fourth person again. Call it delegated principals or whatever you like, but I want a branch on who counts as "the operator" when the chain disagrees with itself, and I want to see where it sends someone who says the operator is just whoever's instructions the system was trained to follow, full stop.
