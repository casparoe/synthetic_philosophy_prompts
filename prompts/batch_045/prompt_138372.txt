The context. I have been drafting a paper against the narrative-gravity account of the self — the Dennett-style view on which a self is not a further ingredient of the world but an abstraction, the subject posited by a unifying story, with nothing more to it than that. I built the following case because I wanted a scenario that the account cannot dismiss as science fiction about duplication: it involves no cloning, no teletransportation, no branching. It is a case of *merging*.

The case. Autobiographical cross-integration was developed for patients who have lost the sense of ownership in episodic memory. It transplants a complete episodic archive: the recipient recalls the donor's episodes with full first-personal ownership — no sense that these are another person's memories, no marker of borrowed content, and phenomenal character indistinguishable from ordinary recollection. The recipient's own memories are then re-consolidated with the donated ones, so the combined set is causally and thematically continuous: one timeline, no seams, no gaps, no doubled events. The resulting autobiography reads as a single life with a single character arc, and on standard measures of narrative coherence it scores higher than either pre-operative autobiography scored on its own.

Now take Ana and Bo: lifelong strangers, different cities, no causal contact, no overlap of any kind. Each receives the other's full archive. Post-operatively each tells one continuous first-person life whose chapters alternate between the two pre-operative histories, and each sincerely reports having always been one person who had an unusual childhood split between two households.

The argument, as my colleague has written it up — and I now think it works too well.

(1) To posit a self is to posit the abstract subject of a unifying narrative; there is nothing more to a self than that.

(2) Which narrative? The one that best unifies the materials, where unification is a matter of degree along the dimensions we actually trade on: causal continuity, thematic coherence, explanatory and predictive yield, absence of seams. The account has no other resource, and no reason to prefer a worse unifier to a better one.

(3) Best-unifying is not indexed to a time or to a narrator. The narrative that best unifies a stretch of conduct may become available only later — after the diaries, after the later crime, after the diagnosis — and when it does we say, without absurdity, that this is who the person really was all along.

(4) After the graft, what best unifies the events distributed across the two pre-operative streams is a single narrative, and it is a better unifier than any pair of narratives that keeps the two streams separate.

(5) So, post-operatively, there is one self where the account, applied pre-operatively, said there were two.

(6) Sameness of self is transitive, and is not the sort of thing a surgical edit manufactures from whole cloth: if post-operative Ana is the same self as pre-operative Ana, and post-operative Ana is the same self as pre-operative Bo, then pre-operative Ana was the same self as pre-operative Bo.

(7) So Ana and Bo were always one person — and since the procedure generalizes, anyone is the same person as anyone whose archive could in principle be grafted into theirs.

(8) That is false. So the account either collapses into a triviality about how well a story hangs together, or it must be supplemented by a non-narrative fact about whose memories these are: the very ingredient it was introduced to eliminate.

A referee told me this is just Parfit-style fission run backwards and that the answer is that identity is not what matters. I do not think that is right. In fission the embarrassment is too many candidates for the role; here there is exactly one, and it is the one the account itself selects. Worse, if I concede that identity is not what matters, my colleague will say I have already granted that (7) is not absurd after all, and the argument stands as a proof of something he is happy to assert.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I must rank them, I would rather keep (1), (2) and (5) than (6), but I do not trust that preference, and I would rather you overruled it than flattered it.
