hey — I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar this term (mostly perception and bodily sensation; we did the pain material last week) and at the last session everyone's supposed to bring something short and not-an-essay. I want to bring a poem about pain asymbolia. I've tried writing it myself and it keeps coming out as either a symptom list or a greeting card about suffering, so I'm outsourcing.

Context so you don't have to guess: the cases are patients who can report everything you'd want about the sensation — where it is, that it's burning rather than stabbing, that it's an 8 and not a 3, that it's worse when you press here — but who say it doesn't bother them, and who sometimes grin or laugh while reporting it. The move people make with this (at least as we read it) is that the badness of pain can't just be a matter of how intense the sensation is: there's a separable component, call it unpleasantness or hurtfulness, and it's that which is supposed to carry the badness, or at least that which the asymbolic patient is missing.

The variant I actually want the poem about is a case our TA made up and handed out, call him Patient N. N reports no unpleasantness at all — pressing, burning, 8/10, completely unmoved, would rate the experience as not bad in the slightest — but N still wants it to stop: he says yes please to the anaesthetic, takes the pill when it's offered, and pushes your hand away if you keep pressing the spot, all while insisting that nothing about it is disagreeable. Our TA's point was that this splits something people usually run together: whatever makes a pain bad and whatever makes you want it gone might not be the same thing, and the textbook cases might just be knocking both out at once.

What I want:

A pantoum — four-line stanzas, where the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third lines of the next, and in the final stanza the repeats fold back so the poem's last line is its first line, verbatim. Five or six stanzas, no more. Rhyme if it comes, but the repeating lines matter more than the rhyme. First person, in N's voice, set in one concrete scene — the exam room, the doctor pressing, the clinic handout with its two columns of adjectives — not floating around in abstract space.

The formal bit I care about: make the recurring lines the sensory ones, the ones that report what the sensation is like — the number, the burning, the location — so they keep coming back byte-identical while the lines around them change, and let the changing lines be the ones about how N stands to it. That's the structural joke: this part never varied, and it was never the part carrying the badness. And since the last line is the first, the poem should land back on the bare sensory report at the end — not as a punchline, but as the actual question. We're back at the unchanged sensation and everything that was meant to be the problem has now been said around it.

Accuracy constraints — someone in my seminar who's read the literature shouldn't wince:
- N is not in denial, not being brave, not stoically enduring something bad. There's no hidden badness being suppressed. Don't smuggle in a tragic irony about what N has lost.
- Don't write it as a poem about how N is missing something essential, or is diminished or dehumanized. That's a different poem and it begs the question I care about.
- The wanting-it-to-stop has to be taken at face value as wanting. Don't explain it away as a reflex, or as N just being agreeable to the doctor, or as leftover habit.
- It's fine if the poem doesn't decide whether N's case shows that badness is a detachable component or shows that it was never in the sensation in the first place. But if it leans one way, it should lean without lecturing.

Take your time with it: brainstorm first — which of N's lines are stable enough to be worth repeating five times, what the concrete scene is, and where exactly the "so what" should land — and then write. Treat yourself as a philosopher who also writes poems that get taken seriously as poems, not a philosopher who versifies on the side; I'd rather have something that works on a friend who's never heard of asymbolia and still holds up for my TA.

Format: the poem first, on its own (give it a title if you've got a good one, skip the title if you don't). Then one section headed "Why I built it that way", 250 words max, covering which lines you made recur and why, where the poem is deliberately leaving the theoretical question open, and anything you had to leave out. No glossary of the philosophy and no reading list — I've definately got the readings.
