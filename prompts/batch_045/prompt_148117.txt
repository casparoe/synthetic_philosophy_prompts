I'm on a small committee rewriting the eligibility rules for a university art prize, and we've hit the predictable wall: the rules say the submission must be "original creative work," and we now have to say whether a piece made with the help of a generative image model qualifies, and if so under what conditions. It has become clear to me that we are not really arguing about models at all; we are arguing about what creativity is, and each of us is quietly appealing to a different account. So before we draft anything, I want a specification written from scratch: what would a theory of creativity have to deliver in order to be good enough for this purpose — to ground eligibility rules that have to be applied by non-experts, to a deadline, from a submission form and the object itself?

Please give me the requirements list, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of one camp. The cases I want any candidate theory to be able to handle, because they are the ones our committee actually divides over: the *apprentice* case, where an artist trains a model exclusively on her own lifetime of drawings and then exhibits, under her own name, work the model generated with no further input, work that critics take to be the most inventive thing she has done; the *flood* case, where the only human contribution is selecting one image out of a hundred thousand samples, and the selected image is genuinely excellent; the *constraint* case, where a human specifies a formal procedure so tight that the output is nearly determined by it, and we nevertheless credit the human, much as we credit artists working under severe self-imposed procedural constraints; the *enumeration* case, where a search process brute-forces a space and among its billions of outputs happens to contain something indistinguishable from a canonical human work; the *unwitnessed* case, of a work made and then destroyed with no one ever seeing it; the *inarticulate* case, of an improviser or painter who cannot say what they were doing or why the result is right; and the *relay* case, where a chain of contributors each make a small adjustment and no one has the whole in view. I also want you to sort the things my colleagues keep demanding into genuine desiderata and camp prejudices: that the verdict be decidable from the artifact alone, since we cannot audit anyone's process; that nothing be credited that could not be surprised by its own output; that nothing be credited without felt effort or conscious experience; that the theory stay silent about quality; and that it return the same verdict for a human and a machine producing the same artifact by the same route.

The part I most want is the structure of the list rather than a scorecard. Which subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, if any — and I have a suspicion that the demand for artifact-only verdicts pulls directly against the process-sensitivity that the apprentice and flood cases seem to require, and that the demand for a binary yes/no eligibility answer pulls against treating creativity as a matter of degree. Which single requirement excludes the most candidate accounts, and is it one we should keep? If the list turns out not to be jointly satisfiable, say so plainly and tell me what that implies for the prize: should we abandon the notion of creativity in the rules and replace it with something checkable, or pick an account that is adequate for our institutional purpose while conceding it is inadequate as a theory of the phenomenon? A line or two on how the familiar families — novelty-plus-value accounts, search-over-a-space accounts with the exploratory/transformational distinction, expressivist or intention-based accounts, and views on which creativity is a social verdict conferred after the fact — fare against the two or three requirements that do the most work is enough; I don't need a survey.
