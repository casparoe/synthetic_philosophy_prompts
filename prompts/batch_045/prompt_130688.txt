hey — I run a small ethics reading group (mix of advanced undergrads and grad students, meets every other week) and I'm putting together a one-page handout on whether it would actually be desirable to be maximally morally good. We did the famous saints paper a few weeks ago and the discussion went sideways, so I want something that maps the terrain instead of just rehearsing the for-and-against.

What I'd like is a taxonomy in the form of a tree. Every branch should be labeled with the question that splits it, every leaf should carry the name of the position (or 'unnamed — nobody actually holds this'), and every leaf should get one short line on its most awkward commitment — the thing a holder has to swallow. Aim for somewhere between ten and fourteen leaves. The questions have to be ones a participant could answer without already knowing which position it leads to: no 'do you think non-moral values matter?', more like a concrete case or a claim they'd have to bite a bullet on. If two branches only come apart on cases rather than on principles, just give me the case in a line under the node.

A few things I specifically want built in, because they're the bits my group kept circling:

1. There's a distinction I don't have a word for and I want it to be a branch. Some non-moral commitments seem to get squeezed out just because the saint has no time or money left: cello practice, long walks, reading novels. Others seem to be *constitutively* at odds with saintliness — the spirit in which you pursue excellence at something (competitive, perfectionist, a bit self-absorbed) is itself the thing the moral ideal would have to file down, even if you had unlimited hours. The first looks like a scarcity problem that a better world could solve; the second doesn't. Put those on different branches, since I think they generate totally different replies, and give each side a name if it has one.

2. Half my group said the whole puzzle is an artifact of one particular shape of moral theory — that if you swap the maximizing picture for a duty-based or virtue-based or satisficing one, the 'saint' stops looking so grim. The other half said it bites just as hard on any view that has a highest moral ideal at all. So I want a branch that separates positions by what shape the first-order moral theory is, and I want it to be visible in the tree whether the grimness generalizes or not.

3. I got stuck on something that might just be a technicality: is 'maximal moral goodness' a maximum — some attainable top that a person could actually hit — or is it more like a supremum, an unapproachable-ish limit you can only get closer to? If it's the second, then 'would it be desirable to be maximally morally good?' might be an empty question, and the real question is about whether moving closer is desirable, and whether it's desirable at every step or only up to a point. I'd like that handled as a branch, or if you think it's a pseudo-problem, tell me in a note and say what it should be replaced with.

4. Please include a leaf for the reply I half-invented in the meeting and can't get out of my head: maybe maximal moral goodness isn't desirable *for any given person* because the world needs some people to be specialists in non-moral excellences — a kind of division of moral and non-moral labor, where everyone being a saint would be a collective loss even if each individual saint were fine. And another leaf for the reply that a saint's extremeness makes them *less* effective at moving anyone else, so maximal goodness might be self-undermining in its effects rather than just costly to the agent. If these collapse into one leaf, say so and say why.

5. Scope seems to matter and I don't trust myself on it: 'should *I* become a saint', 'should *everyone*', and 'should *someone*' might get different answers, and a position that's fine for one scope might fall apart at another. If that's real, make it a branch; if it's not doing any work, tell me.

Finally, the two things that make a handout like this useful for me: first, after you've built the questions, tell me which *combinations* of answers are empty, and for each empty one whether nobody is there because it's incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to explore it. Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate — I'd rather have that marked on the handout than have two boxes that look different but sort people the same way. And don't just hand me a name for the box at the top: I want to know what each position is committed to and where exactly its edges are.
