ANSWER

(a) Ordinary rationality says: look at what the other person might do and pick the best reply to each case. If Bob cooperates, defecting gets me 5 rather than 3; if Bob defects, defecting gets me 1 rather than 0. So defecting is better either way, it is the dominant choice, and both players end up with 1. Superrationality is Hofstadter's alternative: a superrational thinker notices that any other superrational thinker will go through exactly the same reasoning she does, so the two of them will necessarily arrive at the same conclusion — there is really only one decision being made, not two. So she asks what happens if both of them make the same choice, compares 3 (both cooperate) with 1 (both defect), and cooperates. This is basically Kant's categorical imperative with numbers attached: act only on the maxim you would want to be universal. That is also why it works in a one-shot game — it has nothing to do with reputation or with meeting the same person again. And it doesnt matter whether the other player is actually superrational: cooperation is the universalisable choice, so a superrational player cooperates with anyone.

(b) (i) Standard rationality predicts that all 20 telegrams get sent. Not sending gives you nothing for sure, whereas sending gives you a billion dollars times the chance that nobody else sends, which is more than zero, so sending is better whatever the others do — it dominates. But everyone reasons like that, so 20 telegrams arrive and nobody gets anything.

(ii) A superrational player sees that if all twenty of them "decide" to send, they all lose, so twenty people who are making one and the same decision have to break the symmetry with a random device instead of all doing the same thing. Each player should roll a 20-sided die and send on one face only, i.e. send with probability 1/20. That is the right number because the probability that exactly one telegram arrives is 20p(1 − p)^19, which is maximised at p = 1/20 (for N players in general it is 1/N). The expected payoff is then one billion divided by 20, so fifty million dollars each, since each of the 20 players has an equal 1 in 20 shot at the whole billion. Much better than the zero that ordinary rationality gets them. And no player can sneak ahead by sending for sure while the other 19 stick to 1/20 — if she could, they all could, and that is just everyone sending all over again.

(c) (i) If Alice cooperates, then with probability q Bob cooperates too and she gets 3, and with probability 1 − q he defects and she gets 0. So EU(cooperate) = 3q. If she defects, then with probability q Bob defects too and she gets 1, and with probability 1 − q he cooperates and she gets 5, so EU(defect) = q + 5(1 − q) = 5 − 4q. Cooperating is better when 3q > 5 − 4q, i.e. 7q > 5, i.e. q > 5/7. So in practice she should cooperate whenever Bob is more likely than not to match her: 5/7 is only just over one half, so the rule of thumb "cooperate if Bob is more likely to match me than not" is basically right, and the precise cutoff just depends on the numbers in the payoff table.

(ii) At q = 0.70 she should cooperate. 5/7 works out at about 0.71, and 0.70 is essentially that, so she is sitting right on the cutoff, and where you are indifferent superrationality says take the cooperative option since mutual cooperation is the better joint outcome.

(iii) At q = 1 Bob is a perfect twin, so this is the ordinary superrational case and she cooperates (3 beats 1). At q = 1/2 Bob's choice is uncorrelated with hers, so her own choice tells her nothing about his and superrationality gives the same answer as ordinary rationality: defect (1.5 against 3). So the two ways of reasoning only come apart once the correlation is strong enough.

(iv) Yes, she should still cooperate. Superrationality is a universal rule, so it applies whatever the other player happens to be like; if she defected against someone she knew was an ordinary reasoner she would be making an exception of herself, which is exactly what the categorical imperative rules out.
