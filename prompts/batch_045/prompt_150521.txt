I teach a fourth-year seminar on imprecise probabilities, and I'm marking a problem set. I'd like you to grade one student's answer on your own reading of the material, before I check it against my own key. Everything you need is below; nothing depends on outside reading.

Background on the course. We represent an agent's doxastic state by a set 𝒫 of probability functions (a credal set) rather than a single one, taken to be closed and convex, with lower and upper envelopes defined as P_*(E) = inf{P(E) : P ∈ 𝒫} and P*(E) = sup{P(E) : P ∈ 𝒫}. We have covered conjugacy (P*(E) = 1 − P_*(¬E)), the superadditivity of lower envelopes, the distinction between a set of probabilities and a single interval-valued function, and the decision rules Γ-maximin, E-admissibility, maximality, and interval dominance. Throughout, we have stipulated the "credal" reading: an interval credence represents the agent's confidence as genuinely indeterminate, not as ignorance about which sharp credence the agent "really" has. The students know this is stipulated for the course, not argued to be the only coherent position.

The question, as I set it (verbatim):

> An agent's state over a three-cell partition {h₁, h₂, h₃} (exhaustive and mutually exclusive — say, three hypotheses about which subsystem of a machine failed) is represented by a credal set 𝒫: the set of all probability functions over the partition satisfying
>
> (C1) 0.2 ≤ P(h₁) ≤ 0.4
> (C2) 0.1 ≤ P(h₂) ≤ 0.3
> (C3) P(h₁) + P(h₂) ≥ 0.45
>
> (a) Give P_*(E) and P*(E) for E = h₁ ∨ h₂, for F = h₃, and for G = h₁ ∨ h₃, showing briefly how each is obtained and confirming that the endpoints you report are attainable. Is 𝒫 nonempty? If so, exhibit a member. [8 marks]
>
> (b) Explain why the interval for h₁ ∨ h₂ is not what you would get by applying ordinary interval arithmetic to the intervals for h₁ and h₂ (adding lower endpoints, adding upper endpoints). What general feature of lower envelopes does this illustrate, and why does the failure matter for the proposal that an agent with imprecise credences can be represented by a single interval-valued probability? [5 marks]
>
> (c) A classmate suggests that the credal set is only the agent's uncertainty about which sharp probability function in 𝒫 is the right one, so that precision can be restored by averaging over 𝒫 (a uniform average, say). State exactly what this suggestion assumes about the nature of the imprecision, and explain why, on the reading adopted in this course, the reduction is not available. [6 marks]
>
> (d) A bookmaker offers, at a price of $0.58, either to sell the agent a ticket paying $1 if h₁ ∨ h₂ occurs and nothing otherwise, or to buy such a ticket from her; she may also decline to trade. What do the decision rules we have covered recommend here? In particular: does anything in the framework license treating her as having an effective credence of 0.575, the midpoint of the interval for h₁ ∨ h₂? [6 marks]

The student's answer (verbatim, unedited):

> (a) Since the cells are exclusive and exhaustive, P(h₁) + P(h₂) = 1 − P(h₃). Constraint (C3) puts the sum at 0.45 or above, and (C1) together with (C2) put it at 0.4 + 0.3 = 0.7 or below, so P(h₁ ∨ h₂) ∈ [0.45, 0.7]. Both ends are attainable: (0.2, 0.25, 0.55) realises 0.45 and (0.4, 0.3, 0.3) realises 0.7, so nothing tightens these further. For F = h₃ I use conjugacy, P*(E) = 1 − P_*(¬E): P(h₃) ∈ [1 − 0.7, 1 − 0.45] = [0.3, 0.55]. For G = h₁ ∨ h₃ the two cells are disjoint with intervals [0.2, 0.4] and [0.3, 0.55], so P(G) ∈ [0.5, 0.95]. The set is nonempty: (0.25, 0.25, 0.5) satisfies all three constraints, so they are jointly satisfiable and the agent is not exposed to a sure loss.
>
> (b) Endpoint arithmetic is legitimate only where the cells can be varied independently. Here (C3) is a joint constraint: it excludes the pair (0.2, 0.1) even though each coordinate is permitted on its own, so the feasible region is not the box [0.2, 0.4] × [0.1, 0.3], and the extrema cannot be taken cell by cell. This is an instance of the general superadditivity of lower envelopes — P_*(E ∪ F) ≥ P_*(E) + P_*(F) for disjoint E and F, with equality when the two can be minimised together. It matters because an interval-valued probability that combined endpoints arithmetically would return ranges that no single member of 𝒫 realises: the endpoints would not be jointly feasible. So the interval representation is not a harmless shorthand for the set.
>
> (c) The classmate's suggestion presupposes the second-order reading: that the agent has a sharp credence function which is the correct one, and that the spread of 𝒫 merely records the agent's (or our) ignorance about which member it is. On that reading imprecision is a sharp probability over sharp probabilities, and taking an expectation over 𝒫 would be a harmless way of reporting it. On the reading adopted in this course it is not available: an interval credence represents the agent's confidence as indeterminate, and there is no further fact — no true member of 𝒫 — for anyone to be ignorant of. Replacing 𝒫 by its average therefore does not describe the agent's state; it substitutes a more committal one. It also conceals a substantive choice, since a uniform average is one selection among infinitely many and stipulating uniformity adds information the agent does not have. This is why the decision rules we studied work with the whole set rather than with a representative.
>
> (d) Write E = h₁ ∨ h₂, P(E) ∈ [0.45, 0.7]. Buying yields P(E) − 0.58, selling yields 0.58 − P(E), abstaining yields 0. Γ-maximin maximises the worst case: buying is worst at −0.13, selling at −0.12, abstaining at 0, so it recommends abstaining. Maximality does not narrow the choice: on our definition an act is maximal when no rival has strictly greater expectation under every member of 𝒫, and at P(E) = 0.7 buying beats both rivals, at P(E) = 0.45 selling does, and at P(E) = 0.58 all three tie — so each act is maximal. E-admissibility gives the same permissive verdict, since each act maximises expectation at some member of 𝒫. Interval dominance yields no comparisons either: the value-intervals [−0.13, 0.12] for buying, [−0.12, 0.13] for selling and [0, 0] for abstaining all overlap. The rules therefore diverge, and the divergence is the substantive result: only the conservative rule singles out abstention, and the framework gives no uniquely rational act here.
>
> That said, the agent must do something. When a single number is needed, the natural representative is the centre of the interval: the constraints give no reason to prefer one sharp value in [0.45, 0.7] to another, so her effective credence is 0.575. At a price of 0.58 the ticket is then marginally overpriced, and she should sell.
