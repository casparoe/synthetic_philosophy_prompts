I chair the serious-harm review panel for a hospital network. We sit fortnightly, we review roughly forty cases a year, and our findings are read by the board, by patients' families, and occasionally by a coroner, so a finding that someone's failure to act caused a death can end a career, and a finding that it did not can end a claim. The panel is three clinicians and one lay member, none of us with any training in philosophy; we have ninety minutes per case and no budget for outside experts or library access.

Around two thirds of what we review is harm that nobody brought about by acting: a chart not reviewed, a deteriorating patient not escalated, a defibrillator not checked, a drug not reordered, a result not chased. We have no principled way to say when one of these absences is part of the causal history of the outcome and when it is merely a sad fact about the background against which the outcome happened. We currently improvise something close to a but-for test, and it keeps giving us answers we cannot defend: it returns causation wherever a hundred people each failed to do the same thing, it returns causation where the omitted act would have been too late or too weak to help, and it returns nothing at all when the failure belongs to no one in particular.

Write me a decision procedure my panel could actually run, in order, with the following properties.

It should open with the questions to ask in sequence, what will count as an adequate answer to each, and where the procedure terminates early with a negative finding. It should use a small fixed vocabulary of verdicts — I would suggest four, along the lines of causal omission by a named agent, causal omission not attributable to any individual, breach without causal contribution, and no omission — each defined tightly enough that two panels would not disagree about which applies, and each with a note on what follows from it in our report: recording, referral, sanction, or nothing.

The procedure must include a specification rule for the counterfactual comparison, since this is where our arguments actually happen. Say explicitly what is to be held fixed when we ask what would have happened had the act been performed: other people's failures, the patient's underlying condition, what the same agent would have done next, the state of equipment. Require the panel to write the "would have" scenario as a short paragraph, say what to do when the panel cannot agree on it, and say what to do when the omitted act only had a chance of averting the harm — a thirty per cent chance of rescue, say — including whether the procedure adopts a threshold or refuses to.

It should handle five patterns that account for most of our disputes, and for each it must say what it returns and where it stops:

the latent omission — an omission that does nothing on its own and matters only because a safeguard downstream also failed;
the shadowed omission — the same act was incumbent on someone else, or on an automated check, who also did not perform it, so that no single absence made the difference;
the unadopted duty — the omission is a breach of a written protocol that nobody in that unit had ever realistically followed, so that on the unit's actual practice there was nothing to omit;
the derived omission — the agent's failure is itself the product of a failure upstream, in rostering, training, supply or maintenance, so the causal question can always be pushed back one step, and then another;
the vacant role — the duty attached to a post that was unfilled that shift, so there is an omission with no omitter.

Give us a stopping rule for tracing derived omissions backwards, a causal horizon, since without one every one of our findings turns into a finding about a staffing decision made years ago by someone who has left.

Mark every point at which the question before the panel is really one of values rather than fact — who may fairly be blamed, what we owe a family, whether a sanction is proportionate — and state plainly that the procedure is silent there and hands the decision to a person. Keep the whole thing to two sides of A4; it has to be usable in the room by people who have never read any of this literature and, since we cannot look anything up, nothing in it may depend on our knowing one.

Then run it on three of our cases, as an appendix. First: a patient's observations crossed the escalation threshold at two in the morning and the nurse did not call the outreach team, expecting, in line with the unit's actual practice, that the night coordinator would review the charts at four; the coordinator was diverted to another emergency and did not; the patient arrested at half past five, and review at four would probably have prevented it. Second: a drug needed in resuscitating anaphylaxis was not reordered when stock fell three weeks earlier; three people had authority to order it and each assumed another had; two usable substitutes were in the building, neither in the resus trolley and neither named in the protocol; the patient died. Third: a junior doctor was the sole doctor covering two wards overnight after being told the second ward was staffed; a review assigned to her by protocol did not happen and a patient in that ward died; the rota that left her covering both had been signed off eleven months earlier by a manager who has since left.

At least one of these must come out in a way my panel will not like, and I want you to say so without softening it: give the verdict the procedure returns, show the steps, and tell us why we will find it unwelcome. Close by stating what the procedure cannot do — the questions it has no resources to settle, the cases in which its verdicts are artefacts of how we specified the counterfactual, and the ways in which writing it down will change the answers we give.
