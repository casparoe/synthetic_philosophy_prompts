I have been asked to give the four-minute tribute at the concert marking the retirement of Marianne Voss, who taught music history and ran the chamber-music program at a small conservatory for thirty-eight years. I played in her ensemble for two decades, and I now teach elsewhere. I have two problems, and they pull against each other.

The first is her own view. Voss was a convinced Schopenhauerian about instrumental music: music is not a copy of anything, not a representation of some Idea or of any feeling or event, but a direct presentation of the will itself, which is why it can be understood without concepts and why any verbal account of what a piece means is one remove too far. She banned program notes, would not discuss content in lessons, and thought most writing about music was a category error with good prose. So a speech that praises her by saying what her life's work meant, or what the music on tonight's program means, would perform exactly the mistake she spent her career objecting to. I need the tribute to honor her without doing that, and without quietly smuggling interpretation back in under some other name.

The second is the room. Half of it is her colleagues and former students, including several who will notice if the metaphysics is stated loosely, including some younger ones who think the whole purist position is a pose. The other half is her family, her neighbors, and the town subscribers, who have never heard of Schopenhauer and should not have to sit through a lecture on him.

There is one thing I do want in the speech: her own extension of the view, the one piece of theory she ever defended in print. She argued that when a composer sets a poem, the words do not deepen the music; the music lends the words their apparent depth, which is why the same line set by two composers feels profound in two different ways and not at all in a third, and why most settings are worse than the poems they borrow from. I would like that idea to carry some of the weight of the tribute rather than be mentioned and dropped.

Please write the speech: about four minutes, under six hundred words, landing as affection rather than as a seminar. Then add a note of no more than a page saying where the speech takes liberties with Schopenhauer, where it flatters Voss's version of him, and which lines a specialist in the front rows would call a misreading. And if you think the commission is incoherent — if the ban on saying what music means, applied honestly, leaves a speech of praise with nothing to do, or only with ways of dodging — say that plainly instead of writing something graceful, and tell me what I should actually do with the four minutes.
