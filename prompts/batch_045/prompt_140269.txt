I'm in a reading group that's plowing through some historiography stuff — we did the Trojan War last month, King Arthur this month, the Exodus is next — and I keep coming away with the feeling that I've got a pretty consistent set of gut reactions about when a legend has turned into history, but no idea what principle I'm actually applying, or whether I'm just being inconsistent. So here are my verdicts. To keep us off the archaeology, I'm stipulating the evidence in every case: please don't correct the facts, I've made them up for the sake of the cases. Case 1, Troy: stipulate a real city on the mound usually identified with Homeric Troy, destroyed violently somewhere in the right couple of centuries, with an oral tradition about a war there that only gets written down much later, in poems full of gods and single combats. Verdict: that's history. There was a war-ish thing at Troy and our story is a badly distorted report of it. Case 2, Arthur: stipulate a real fith- or sixth-century British war leader with a name something like Artorius who fought some battles, and then centuries of accretion — knights, a round table, a Grail, the works, plus a 'he'll come back' tradition. Verdict: that's legend, not history. Those stories aren't about him, and 'Arthur was a real person' feels like a much weaker claim than 'the Trojan War happened.' Then I notice 1 and 2 are structurally the same and I've answered them oppositely, and I can't tell which verdict to drop. Case 3, the Exodus: stipulate no trace whatever of a mass departure from Egypt or a forty-year desert sojourn, but a genuine memory of a small group of Semitic workers getting out, and — the bit I think is doing the real work — a story a community has retold at a ritual meal every year for two-plus millennia, which is more or less what constituted them as a people. Verdict: I want to say it's history in some sense even though the event as told didn't happen, and I can't tell if that's sentimentality or a real distinction.

Some invented cases to poke at it. Case 4: a Victorian novelist invents a legend about a massacre at a particular mountain pass; thirty years later archaeologists find a mass grave there from exactly the period she was writing about. Pure coincidence, no chain of transmission in either direction. Verdict: not history, just a lucky guess — so the principle can't be merely 'the central claim is true.' Case 5: an oral tradition about a great battle, passed along in an unbroken chain for eight hundred years, in which the place, the combatants and the outcome have all drifted, so that all that survives is 'there was a terrible battle, long ago, somewhere east of here.' A battle really did happen. Verdict: still history, just barely — I don't feel tempted to say the tradition is 'about' a battle that never occurred. Case 6: a medieval monastery forges a charter saying a saint granted them the valley; it's believed; two hundred years on there's a real pilgrimage road, a real market town and a real annual fair, all of which exist because people believed the story. Verdict: the charter is a lie and the story isn't history — and yet there's plainly a sense in which the story is now part of history, and I want a principle that tells me whether that's one distinction (history of the events versus history of what the story went on to do) or two. Case 7: a 'the hero sleeps and will return' legend; four centuries later a pretender takes the hero's name and starts an actual rebellion on the strength of it. Verdict: no idea. The story caused real events but doesn't describe them.

My own stab, which I think is broken somewhere: a legend becomes history just in case (i) the story's central name or place picks out something real, and (ii) there's an actual chain linking that thing to our story — memory, testimony, practice, not coincidence — and (iii) something about content surviving the transmission, which is where I lose my grip, and which is probably the bit that makes me say yes to Troy and no to Arthur. What I'd like you to do: test that against the cases, repair it or replace it, and hand me one or two candidate principles stated precisely enough that I can run new cases through them, with a note saying which case bought you each clause. Then generate three cases I haven't considered, at least one where your principle and my gut come apart, and say which verdict has to go. Be straight with me about which of my verdicts no clean principle can save — I'd rather give one up than accept an epicycle. And as you go, flag what's settled and what's genuinely contested among people who work on this (whether any of it really turns on reference, on truth, or on social function, say); don't smooth a live dispute into a consensus. My friend who does geoarchaeology says the whole question is just 'what do you mean by history,' and I hope that isn't the whole answer — but if it is, tell me that too.
