I write historical novels set in periods and among events whose shape most of my readers already know. The manuscript I am currently outlining concerns a real and well-documented mercantile expedition that failed, and anyone who opens the book will know within thirty pages that it failed. A review of my last novel complained, fairly I thought, that the book moved as though the catastrophe had been fated: every chapter laid another brick on a road that led to only one place, and the characters' decisions read as stage directions rather than as gambles. I do not think the remedy is to stop telling stories with known endings. I am not going to give up dramatic irony, and my readers do not want me to; the pleasure of the form is precisely that we watch people walk toward an outcome we can already see. What I want is to keep the outcome known while making the route to it feel like one that could have gone otherwise, and to do that without falsifying the record or manufacturing uncertainty nobody had.

The difficulty is that I cannot see, from inside my own drafting, when I am doing the thing I am trying to avoid. I know how it ends; so does my reader; and the archive I work from is not neutral, since documents survive in part because they later mattered, and the people who wrote them were frequently explaining themselves after the fact. I have begun separating out three or four failure modes, and I would like a procedure that handles them, though I would rather you sharpen or replace my distinctions than simply adopt them. First, my own foreknowledge leaking into causation: treating something as a cause mainly because, given the outcome, it looks like one. Second, retrofitted motive: giving a character a reason that fits what they later did, and then experiencing that reason as though it explained the action rather than being inferred from it. Third, archival selection: promoting whatever survived in the record, and above all whatever was uncovered, litigated, or confessed afterwards, into the engine of the plot, when its prominence in the file may be an effect of the disaster rather than a cause of it. Fourth, a problem that belongs to the reader rather than to me: an explanation can be scrupulously fair and still land as inevitable, because the person reading it knows the ending and supplies the rest. A related worry is that when I try to restore contingency by dramatizing alternatives, I may be inventing options that were never in the air: logically available, but not ones anyone at the time proposed, feared, or could have acted on. I have been calling these dead alternatives, as opposed to live ones, and I want the procedure to draw that line and to say what would count as evidence that an alternative was live.

So please turn what is actually known about hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy into a procedure I can run on a plotline before I draft it: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the answer is a stop or a rewrite, and where the question comes back to me because it is a judgment about the novel rather than a fact about the past. The constraints are these. I have roughly an afternoon per plotline. I work with a research assistant who reads the period hands and knows nothing of the theory, and a fact-checker who must sign off on anything the book asserts about the world; so most steps have to be executable by the assistant, and the procedure should say which steps cannot be delegated. It must not ask me to withhold the outcome or to feign ignorance I do not have, and it has to leave room for the reader's foreknowledge as a fixed condition rather than a defect to be engineered away. Then run it on three subplots from the current outline: a purser's embezzlement, recorded in the sources because it was discovered during the post-mortem into the failure, which I have been using as the main causal hinge and as my villain; a lieutenant's change of allegiance, for which the surviving letters give no reason at all and to which I have supplied a childhood grievance; and the loss of the expedition's fortified trading post, which on the military facts looks genuinely overdetermined, since the garrison was too small, the relief was not coming, and the siege works were competently built. At least one of the three should return a verdict I will not like, and I would rather have it from you than from my editor. I should also say that I have been toying with a device for restoring contingency from the reader's side: a scene dramatizing an outcome that did not occur, staged as a rumour running through the camp, a letter that argues for the other course, or a chapter from the perspective of someone who expected things to go differently. Tell me whether your procedure licenses that device, on what conditions, and where it degenerates into manufacturing a contingency the period did not contain. Finally, be plain about what the procedure cannot do: I would rather know now which of these questions it can only re-describe.
