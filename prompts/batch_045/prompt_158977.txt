I'm finishing a master's thesis in public policy, and over the last year I've drifted into a position I now catch myself repeating in seminars — one I'd like stress-tested before it hardens into something I defend out of habit. It has two parts. The explanatory part: the pattern often called the gender-equality paradox — that average gender differences in personality traits, personal values, and educational and occupational choice tend to be *larger* in countries that score higher on standard gender-equality indices — is best explained by something like the unmasking of underlying differences in interests. The idea is that in poorer, less equal countries career choice is largely instrumental, driven by the need for security and relative pay, whereas where survival is not at stake and formal barriers are lower, choice becomes expressive, and what it expresses is not what the barriers were hiding. (That is roughly how my economist friend puts it: remove the noise of necessity and the signal shows through.) The normative part: if that is right, then the gender gap in STEM is much less of a justice problem than my field assumes — we should treat it largely as an outcome to be respected, keep anti-discrimination enforcement aimed at what we can actually show is wrongful, and stop reading the composition of STEM as a standing symptom of exclusion.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and with the two parts treated separately, since I suspect they can come apart. Please include what has arguably already happened rather than only hypotheticals: I gather the most-cited study behind the STEM version was corrected and that independent researchers could not fully reproduce it, and I want to know how much that should move me given that the paradox is also reported for personality and values using different data, and given that the correlation appears in some later work and in at least some international reports. Tell me what a committed defender of my own reading would concede as counting against them — not merely what critics say — and be explicit about where my view is insulated and whether that insulation is respectable: the natural retreat is to say that the indices do not measure real gendered treatment, only gaps in achievement and participation, and I can't tell whether that is legitimate measurement scepticism or a degeneration into unfalsifiability. I also want three specific hypotheses probed. First, my own: maybe what greater equality changes is not the cost of entering STEM but the cost of *avoiding* it — generous welfare states and dual-earner norms make long-hours, time-inflexible, high-intensity careers optional, and STEM is disproportionately such a career, so the divergence may be about the organisation of STEM work rather than about women's interests. Second, my friend's: the equality indices bundle together things like economic participation, education, and political representation while saying nothing about cultural dimensions such as assertiveness or uncertainty avoidance, so if the correlation is carried by one component, or by national income, the unmasking story is in trouble. Third, the endogeneity worry: preferences may be shaped by anticipated treatment, by the domestic division of labour that survives in even the most egalitarian countries, or by the timing of fertility, in which case treating revealed choice as the baseline is circular.

Say what my view predicts that its rivals don't, so I know what to actually look for rather than what to argue about. I should also tell you what would move me most: cross-cohort and migrant evidence — children of immigrants moving between a low-equality and a high-equality country, or within-country change over time — because it separates origin culture from destination institutions; I am distrustful of self-reported preferences as support for my own position, for the reasons above, and I want to know whether relying on them makes me guilty of the circularity I just described. Finally, give me an honest assessment of whether the explanatory part is testable at all: "remove the obstacles and see what remains" has no clean operationalisation when no country has removed them all and when some of the obstacles are internal, and if my view turns out to be exposed mainly to evidence that cannot in practice be collected, I want to know whether that is a defect in the view or a defect in the data — and, if it is the former, whether I should stop holding it in the form I currently hold it. Argue against me directly rather than surveying the debate, and please don't give me citations or a reading list; I can't follow up on them.
