Dear Andrew,

I wonder if I might ask you to help me with a passage. I know you have retired from teaching, and I would not impose except that I have run out of people to ask.

Since my wife's diagnosis two years ago — early-onset Alzheimer's, she is sixty-three — I have been her full-time carer, and last spring I joined a local Stoicism group on the suggestion of a friend from the hospital support group. It has helped me more than I expected. But I have become stuck on the text they hand out, and the group's discussions have not unstuck me, because everything there is pitched at the level of consolation rather than argument.

The passage is the opening section of Epictetus's *Enchiridion*, in an old translation I was given as a photocopy (no title page, so I cannot tell you whose):

1. Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions. Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions.

The things in our control are by nature free, unrestrained, unhindered; but those not in our control are weak, slavish, restrained, belonging to others. Remember, then, that if you suppose that things which are slavish by nature are also free, and that what belongs to others is your own, then you will be hindered. You will lament, you will be disturbed, and you will find fault both with gods and men. But if you suppose that only to be your own which is your own, and what belongs to others such as it really is, then no one will ever compel you or restrain you. Further, you will find fault with no one or accuse no one. You will do nothing against your will. No one will hurt you, you will have no enemies, and you not be harmed.

My difficulty is not that I fail to grasp what the dichotomy of control is supposed to be. I have read the modern books the group circulates, and I have sat through the diagram of concentric circles; I understand the slogan well enough to have tried to live by it. My difficulty is that I cannot tell what kind of claim the classification is, and the difference matters enormously to me at the moment.

I spent forty years in structural engineering, and in that world control is a causal notion with tolerances attached: a variable is under control when your intervention reliably produces a specified effect within a stated margin. Read that way, the items on the first list look like the least controllable things about me. I cannot, by deciding, stop wanting her to be as she was; I cannot switch off aversion to the way an evening is going. Whereas the items on the second list are precisely the things I *do* have causal leverage over — whether she is fed, whether the house is safe, whether the agency sends someone competent. So on my professional understanding of the word, the two lists look close to inverted.

I can think of three other things *our own* might mean, and I cannot decide among them. It might mean *produced by me and by nothing else*. It might mean *such that nothing outside me can compel or block it* — the claim being that my assent is, as it were, the one thing in the universe no one else can get a grip on. Or it might mean *constitutive of me, in the sense that it is what I can properly be credited or blamed for*, so that what belongs to others is whatever is allotted by someone else's will and therefore not mine to answer for. These give very different readings of the phrase *slavish by nature* and of the promise that no one will ever compel or restrain you, and I would like to know which one the argument actually needs — that is, which reading makes the inference from *it belongs to others* to *you will be hindered and will lament* go through.

There is a second thing I cannot settle. Is the classification meant to sort *items* into two boxes, or to sort *aspects* of any single undertaking? I have started to think it must be the latter. Whether she has a calm night is not fully in my control and not fully outside it: it depends on the disease, and it depends on what I do. Some of the modern Stoics in the group have responded to this by adding a third category of things that are only partly up to us, but that strikes me as a muddle, because *partly* is not a third kind of thing — it is a reminder that any outcome has the aspect that is mine and the aspect that is not. What I cannot see is whether the aspectual reading still earns the payoff the passage promises, or whether it quietly converts that promise from a causal guarantee into something merely evaluative.

A third worry sits behind both. The same tradition says that everything is fated and that the world is a single ordered whole, and one member of the group used the old image of a cylinder that rolls in its own way only because of its own shape, however it is pushed. I take the point, but I cannot see how it leaves room for anything to be *up to* anyone, unless *up to us* was never about causal leverage in the first place — which brings me back to the first question.

And there is a practical version of all this. I have been told about the practice of pursuing one's aims with a reservation, as though one mentally added that fate may prevent them. If that is right, then caring as hard as I am able about an outcome is compatible with the discipline; if the discipline instead requires me to regard the outcome as nothing to me, then it asks something of me that I think is wrong — I do not want to become a man who is serene about whether his wife is frightened.

Could you write me a page explaining which of these readings the passage supports, and why the others fail? Please do not send me a bibliography or point me at commentaries: I am at home almost every hour of the day and I have no library access, so all I can use is reasoning from the text and from what you know. I last did philosophy as an undergraduate, so I would be grateful if you argued the steps out rather than naming positions at me and assuming I can fill them in.

With thanks, and apologies for the length of this,

—Peter Halloran
