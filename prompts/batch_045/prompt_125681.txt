I build learned simulators for a living: I fit a transition model, roll it out, and then worry about the gap between those rollouts and the real system. That vocabulary I know well — misspecification versus poor fit, calibration against held-out trajectories, identifiability, priors over model classes, robustness under perturbation of a model, and the live question of whether a model can extract anything from data it generated itself — but I have never read philosophy of science, and I would like the central question of one of its debates translated into it.

The question: when a scientist runs a scenario only in their head, with no apparatus and no new data, and comes away knowing something about the world, what happened? On one reading, the imagination is a kind of instrument: a simulator tuned to nature's transition function, whose output is evidence about the world in roughly the way a measurement is. On the other, nothing of the sort: every step was already fixed by assumptions the scientist held, the imagery is presentation, and the result is a theorem about a model, so nothing was learned beyond what was packed in.

Please do three things. First, restate both readings as precisely as you can in my vocabulary — simulator, rollout, target system, held-out data, misspecification, self-generated data, calibration — and say which of my distinctions each has to lean on, and where my vocabulary is quietly taking a side. Second, run the translation on a concrete case: a bead free to slide without friction on a rigid circular hoop spinning at constant angular speed about its vertical diameter; does it stay at the bottom? For each reading, say what would have to be true of the mental simulator for the answer to count as knowledge. Third, give me the residue report: what maps cleanly, what you had to fudge, and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. I also want your verdict on two suspicions of my own: that the crux is that the mental simulator cannot be validated against held-out trajectories without leaving the armchair, which is either exactly my sim-to-real problem or a different and deeper one; and that a third story — the imagination runs a model known to be wrong in specified ways, and we learn what survives deformation of that model — either dissolves the disagreement or covertly takes a side in it. Finally, tell me whether the leftover notions (laws versus models, necessity, explanation) are something my vocabulary lacks or something the debate never made clear. Please give me no references, citations, or reading lists; I want the argument.
