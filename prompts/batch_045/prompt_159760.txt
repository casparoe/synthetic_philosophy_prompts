I teach an upper-level seminar on meaning, computation, and mind — sixteen students, mostly philosophy and cognitive science majors, one ninety-minute session. They have already been through Searle's Chinese Room and have met the symbol grounding problem, but they have absorbed both as a slogan ("symbols have to be hooked up to the world somehow"), and I don't think they feel its force. Last term I tried a quick dictionary-only exercise and it collapsed immediately: one student pointed out that my little codebook was obviously grounded in the English it was written in, everyone agreed, and we spent the rest of the hour arguing about the word "meaning" instead of about the problem. So I want a properly staged activity in which the grounding regress is something the students actually get stuck inside, and in which anchoring a handful of symbols visibly does something that piling on further definitions never does — and I'd rather they run into the point than hear it from me, so please keep the theoretical vocabulary out of the instructions they see.

Here is the skeleton I have in mind, which you should improve on or replace. The class works in pairs through three short phases. Phase one: each pair receives a "Manual" for an artificial language of about a dozen marks, in which every mark is defined solely in terms of the others — a deliberately circular lexicon (a glim is a fip that has been trebbed; a fip is two or more glims standing near a zur; and so on) — and may consult nothing else; their task is to answer ten comprehension questions and compose a short passage in the language, which they will be able to do. Only afterwards do I ask them what "glim" means, with the constraint that the answer may not contain any word of the language, and let them run around the circle themselves. Phase two: each pair gets a box of ordinary objects and a "pairing protocol" that anchors three or four of the marks to samples — ten things that are glims, ten that are not, then a test on objects they have never seen — while the remaining marks stay unanchored; the contrast I'm after is between pairs who can sort novel objects and pairs who can only manipulate marks, and I want the anchored pairs to notice that once a few marks are tethered the whole Manual stops feeling empty. Phase three is where I'm stuck: I want a mark the pairing protocol cannot anchor, something relational or abstract — a mark for "the same one as before," or for "all," or for "because" — so that grounding by sample presentation runs out one level up and the regress reappears somewhere new. Please give me the full design: exact rules and materials for each phase, a realistic minute-by-minute timeline for ninety minutes, and the actual tasks and test items to use, including what makes a good unanchorable mark and what makes the anchored categories resistant to being faked with a verbal rule.

Then give me a debrief that maps what happened in the room onto the parts of the problem: internal coherence versus grounding, why the lexicon circles, why tethering a small base appears to accomplish more than any amount of further definition, and why it isn't obvious that any of this scales to abstract vocabulary. Tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it — particularly the "it's grounded in us" move, which killed the last version, and the student who says we just need better definitions — and how I should respond in the moment rather than in the debrief. Finally, be honest about which part of the symbol grounding problem the activity cannot stage at all and I will therefore have to say in my own voice afterwards. Constraints: no technology beyond printed cards and objects I can carry in a box, no money, and no requirement that students have read anything specific for that session.
