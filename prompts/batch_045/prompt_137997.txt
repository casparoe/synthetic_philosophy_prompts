I TA for an intro ethics course and we're doing a unit on supererogation next month — the usual beyond-the-call-of-duty material, Urmson's 1958 paper on saints and heroes, the worry that the category is unstable, whether utilitarianism has room for it, that kind of thing. I want to screen two clips from Saving Private Ryan: the stretch near the end where Miller dies on the bridge and tells Ryan he has to earn it, and the framing bit with elderly Ryan at the Normandy cemetery asking his wife whether he's been a good man and whether he earned what was done for him. I think the film is quietly about supererogation, but I also think I might be forcing it onto a war movie, so I'd like a reading that pushes back on me where it should.

Two things I keep going in circles on. First, the mission itself: a squad sent to pull one guy out because his brothers are dead, on orders. Under a role-based way of thinking about obligation, isn't the rescue just what they were told to do, and so not supererogatory at all? And if you take the numbers straight, it looks less like optional heroism and more like something close to impermissible — several lives spent on one. So where in the film is the supererogation actually located? My guess is that it's in the smaller acts inside the mission rather than in the mission as a whole, but I cant quite make that clean. Second, the bequest, which is the part students always grab onto: Miller's request seems to convert a gift into a debt. Ryan is told his life has to repay a sacrifice, and the ending (him at the grave, still needing his wife to reassure him) suggests the debt never gets discharged. I've started calling this the discharge problem: if a supererogatory act really does generate an obligation of gratitude, and Ryan spends the rest of his life discharging it, then nothing he does afterward is ever praiseworthy-beyond-duty again — and worse, does the rescue retroactively stop counting as supererogatory, given that a supererogatory act is supposed to leave the recipient's options open rather than open a ledger? Is gratitude even the sort of thing that can be demanded from someone, or is Miller's request itself a moral mistake that the film is quietly criticizing while also being built on?

One more half-formed idea I can't tell is real: Miller has enormous personal reasons to keep going — orders, the men he's responsible for, not being the officer who quit, self-respect — so his act is at best deontically optional while being motivationally overdetermined, and I want to say that makes it less interestingly supererogatory than the same act done by a stranger with no stake in it. Is that distinction collapsable, or does it track something the literature cares about? Related: does the movie need the character who freezes and fails, the one who lets the prisoner go and then shoots him later, in order to make heroism look optional in the first place — i.e. does a film like this need a permissible-but-blameworthy category sitting next to the praiseworthy-but-optional one? Which scenes should I actually point my students at, what would you tell them the film gets right that the papers don't, and where should I admit that Spielberg is just doing war-movie things and not making a claim about duty at all?
