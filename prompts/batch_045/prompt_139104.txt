I built this for a reading group on the rule-following paradox and now I cant see where it breaks, which makes me trust it less, because the conclusion is nuts.

Quick setup. The skeptic's deviant rule (agrees with addition up to some bound, then spits out 5) has a weak spot: it leaves an empirical hostage. Hand me numbers past the bound and my behavior could in principle tell addition apart from it, so the dispositional replies get some grip. So I tried to build a rival no test could ever touch. Call it chameleon-addition, written ⊞:

x ⊞ y = x + y, if some member of my linguistic community ever actually performs an addition on exactly the pair (x, y) — past, present or future. Otherwise x ⊞ y = x + y + 2.

Every addition anybody in my community actually does gets the addition answer. And you can never run the experiment that would seperate ⊞ from +, because the moment you do the computation to check, the pair becomes one that has actually been computed, and ⊞ gives the addition answer there. Kripke's rule leaves a hostage; mine leaves none. It also swallows the community reply whole, since the community's total behavior is inside the reference class.

The argument:

(a) If I mean addition by "+", there has to be a fact that constitutes my meaning addition rather than ⊞.
(b) ⊞ and addition agree on every computation anyone in my community ever performs, actually or counterfactually: any computation whose result anyone could observe is one that gets performed, and ⊞ matches addition on all of those.
(c) So no fact about actual or counterfactual behavior — mine, my community's, my dispositions, my mental states insofar as they are hooked up to behavior — distinguishes addition from ⊞.
(d) The facts that could constitute my meaning one thing rather than another are all facts of the kind (c) covers.
(e) So no fact constitutes my meaning addition rather than ⊞, and hence I don't mean addition by "+". I don't mean ⊞ either. I mean nothing.
(f) Nothing in (a)-(e) turns on "+" or on me. For any expression and any candidate meaning you like, I can cook up an observationally bulletproof rival the same way. So nobody has ever meant anything by anything.
(g) But (f) can't be right, for two reasons. First, teaching addition visibly works: kids drilled on a finite pile of sums go on to get new ones right, when one of them writes 5 for 68+57 the others correct her, and strangers who never met converge on the same answers to problems nobody has done before. If no one means anything and no one follows any rule, all of that is a row of miracles. Second, (f) eats itself: (a)-(f) are marks I made, and by (f) they mean nothing, so they can't be a proof of anything.
(h) So something up there is wrong and I can't say which step.

The two things I most want to keep are that the kid who writes 5 is making a mistake in the ordinary full-blooded sense, and that the argument doesn't destroy itself. I'd sooner drop the constitution demand in (a) than either of those. But I want to know what that costs me, and whether dropping (a) really lets me keep both.

Commit to one diagnosis — don't hand me a menu of live options and let me pick.

Five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no summing up at the end: which step you reject; why it looked fine; what rejecting it costs you; what the best rival rejection would have cost and why you're not taking it; and whether I get to say the kid is wrong in the full sense or only a deflated one.
