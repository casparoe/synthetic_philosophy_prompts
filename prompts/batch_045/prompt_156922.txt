I'm a retired hydrologist — spent most of my career on groundwater modelling, and since I stopped work I've been going along to a philosophy reading group with a few other retired people from the village, which is how I got into all this. Its not idle either: I'm on the board of a small wetlands trust and we're arguing right now about whether to sink a big slice of our capital into buying and restoring a peat bog that won't really pay off, ecologically speaking, for a couple of centuries, versus spending the same money on habitat work that pays off inside ten years. The council we deal with discounts the future benefits at a few percent a year, with a lower rate after a few decades, and I've been the one grumbling that this is basically just not caring about anybody born after about 2050. Before the next meeting I want to get my own house in order, because I've got a nagging feeling I'm contradicting myself and I can't locate where.

Here's roughly where I've landed: (1) I take the old line usually credited to Ramsey, that discounting someone's welfare merely because it arrives later is ethically indefensible, and that the taste for doing it is weakness of imagination plus a certain failure of sympathy — a moral failing, not a preference to be respected. So I reject pure time preference outright. (2) But I'm happy to discount future consumption because future people will probably be richer than us, so the same bundle of goods does less for them; that seems to me a completley different reason and nothing to do with time as such. (3) I'm also happy to discount for the chance the benefit never actually arrives — the scheme fails, the body meant to maintain it dissolves, or there's nobody around to enjoy it — since that's just expected value. (4) I think the effective rate ought to fall as the horizon lengthens, because we genuinely don't know what the right rate is, and if you average over the plausible rates then the far-distant future ends up counting almost as much as the present; somebody in the group sent me an economics paper making this point and it seemed right at the time. (5) I think human extinction would be an moral catastrophe on a different scale from everything else, because of the sheer number of lives that would otherwise exist, so preventing it ought to swamp ordinary spending decisions. (6) And at the same time, if you offered me a hundred pounds today or a hundred pounds in thirty years, I'd take the money today without thinking about it, and I don't think there's anything irrational in that at my age.

So: does this hang together? Two people in the group think (1) and (6) are inconsistent and I think they're wrong, but I'd like that explained properly rather than asserted. What worries me more is the middle: (2), (3), (4) and (5) each feel like a respectable refinement you could bolt onto (1), but I suspect that when you actually run them out over two hundred years they don't all point the same direction, and that at least one of them quietly reinstates the pure time preference I threw out in (1) while another hollows (5) out into something that can't do any work in a real committee meeting. Go through the pairs, tell me which are really in tension and which only look like it, rank the real ones by how bad they are, and for each say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me — then tell me which single revision would tidy up the most. I'm genuinely not attached to any particular one of these; I'd rather be consistent than right about any single item, and I'd rather be told the thing is a mess than given a lecture on the history of discounting.
