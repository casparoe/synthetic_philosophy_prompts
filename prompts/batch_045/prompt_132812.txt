I serve on the committee drafting the disclosure policy for a large genomic research programme. Two questions are in front of us: whether to honour a participant's standing request not to be told certain results, and whether to return unsolicited findings that we come across while looking for something else. A colleague on the committee, a health economist, has produced an argument he says settles both at once and in the same direction. I cannot accept his conclusion, but I also cannot locate the mistake, and I would rather not dismiss the argument for the wrong reason.

Here is the argument as he gave it to us.

(1) Good's theorem. Take an agent with probabilities over states, a utility function over consequences, and a fixed set of feasible acts. Offer her, at no cost, the chance to observe the outcome of an experiment before she chooses. Then the expected utility of the situation in which she has that option is at least as great as the expected utility of the situation in which she does not. The proof is two lines: conditional on any signal, she can still perform the act that was optimal before the experiment; so the value of choosing after the signal is at least the value of choosing without it, for every possible signal; and averaging over the signals preserves the inequality.

(2) Nothing in that proof turns on what the signal is about, on whether the agent understands it, on whether she will in fact use it well, or on how well she will remember it. It uses only two things: that she is free to condition her choice on the signal, and that the acts available after the signal include the acts available before. So the result is entirely neutral about content and about competence.

(3) Therefore, for a rational agent, costless information cannot be worse than ignorance. He calls this the principle that ignorance never pays.

(4) So anyone who declines costless information, or who asks in advance not to be given it, is making a mistake by her own lights: there is no such thing as a rational preference for ignorance. Whatever else may be said for the person who asks not to be told a secondary finding, who avoids a spoiler, or who declines a predictive test for a condition nobody can treat, she is not serving her own interests.

(5) So a policy of honouring such requests cannot be defended as protecting the person who makes it. It can only be defended by its effects on third parties. And a clinician who respects the request is, by the patient's own lights, making the patient worse off.

(6) I pressed him on the case in which every decision has already been made: the treatment is over, the outcome is fixed, and the clinic offers to tell you now, for nothing, which way it went, knowing that being told will darken the rest of your life and change nothing else. Even here, he says, ignorance never pays, because the theorem is silent about timing and about content.

(7) I pressed him on information that forecloses options, where coming to know something triggers a duty you would rather not have — a duty to report, to disclose, to remediate, to testify — or otherwise alters what you may do or what others may do to you. Even here, he says, the theorem holds, since choosing as if you did not know is the only move the proof requires, and it is always available.

That last pair of replies is where I lose my footing, because neither strikes me as credible and yet I cannot say which premise fails. I have two候选 diagnoses, and I cannot tell which bears the weight. One is that something slips between (3) and (4): the theorem is a claim about an expectation taken before the signal is drawn, and my colleague is using it to condemn a preference, and to underwrite a standing policy, in a way that confuses what is true in expectation beforehand with what is true of the situation you actually end up in. The other is that the proof quietly assumes that consequences are fixed by the act and the state alone, so that what the agent knows enters only through what she chooses; in the cases at (6) and (7) the outcome seems to depend on her knowledge itself, and then the two-line proof has nothing to bite on.

Please tell me which step you would reject, why that step looked safe, and what rejecting it costs. I would like the alternatives priced, not just one of them dismissed: if the fault is the slide from an ex ante expectation to a verdict about preference and policy, what exactly does the theorem still entitle us to say? If the fault is the assumption that outcomes are functions of act and state alone, does giving that up cost us the separation of belief from desire that the whole expected-utility apparatus rests on, and with it any external standpoint from which a preference for ignorance could be called irrational? And is there some third break point I have not considered — something about the agent's later self not sharing her earlier self's aims, say — that does the work more cheaply?

Two claims I am reluctant to surrender: the theorem itself, read as a claim about the idealised agent in (1); and the thought that a right not to know protects something real, which is what our committee is being asked to decide. If those two cannot both be kept, say so and tell me which you would drop.

Finally, I want to know what would change your mind. Name the argument or consideration that would move you to a different diagnosis, tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and say what my colleague would say in reply if he were in the room — I have to go back to him with this.
