I work on reliability engineering for a fleet of deployed machine-learning systems, and I'm auditing a graduate seminar in philosophy of science at the same time; the two keep colliding, and I'd like you to act as a philosopher of science who specializes in causation and mechanistic explanation but is also fluent enough in ML systems to follow talk of ablations, fine-tuning runs, and distribution shift. Please think carefully and start by brainstorming freely — list, before you organize anything, every candidate defeater you can think of — and then give me a ranked map of the view's exposure. Here is the view.

My team maintains a taxonomy of incidents: every anomaly we see gets tagged with a mechanism label, and the decision that actually matters to us is whether an anomaly A in one system and an anomaly B in another (different architecture, different training run, possibly different modality) are the same mechanism or two. I have ended up defending a strong position, which I'll call the joint-susceptibility criterion: sameness of mechanism is constituted by sameness of manipulable causal role and by nothing else. Operationally, A and B count as the same mechanism if and only if (i) there is some intervention I, specifiable at the level of the systems' internal variables rather than merely at their inputs, that predictably shifts the incidence or severity of both A and B in the same direction; (ii) the effect of I on each is mediated by a shared set of intermediate variables; and (iii) no intervention within our intervention budget separates them, i.e. none moves A while leaving B untouched. Surface similarity of the triggering inputs or of the output signature, and identity or overlap of the underlying implementation, I treat as evidence at best, not as the criterion: I explicitly want to allow multiple realizability, so two anomalies can be the same mechanism even though no parameters are shared between the systems.

What I want from you is what would count against this. Not only the standard objections to interventionist and mechanistic accounts of explanation that one meets in the literature (I've read enough Craver, Woodward and co. to recognize those; please don't just rehearse them — apply them to my specific three-clause criterion and tell me which of the clauses each one actually bites, and which of them my view already survives). I especially want you to take seriously a case that prompted the whole thing. In system 1, a failure F is reliably eliminated by ablating component c; in system 2, a superficially indistinguishable failure F, on the same class of inputs with the same output signature, is unaffected by ablating the homologous component c-prime but is eliminated by ablating an unrelated component d, and a fix fine-tuned on data from system 1 transfers to and suppresses the failure in system 2. My criterion says: different mechanisms. My colleagues say that is absurd, and that transfer of the fix plus identical behavioural signature is as close to proof of sameness as the field ever gets.

So: which observations, cases, or formal results should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? Please cover at least these, and add what I haven't thought of. First, over-generation: a single upstream knob — a decoding temperature, a global regularization strength, an RLHF scaling factor — modulates the rate of many intuitively distinct failure modes at once, so clause (i) looks like it will collapse them into one mechanism unless clause (ii)'s shared-mediator requirement does real work; does it? Second, under-generation: fused or compensatory realizations, where knocking out a component has no measurable effect because a backup pathway takes over, or where two components cannot be manipulated separately at all, so clause (iii) misfires in both directions. Third, whether sameness as I've defined it is even transitive — A and B jointly susceptible, B and C jointly susceptible, but some intervention separating A from C — and what it costs a notion of "mechanism" if it isn't, given that we use these labels to support inductive inferences about fixes and risks. Fourth, level-relativity: if the verdict flips depending on how coarse my allowed intervention set is, am I committed to there being one correct grain, or have I quietly made mechanism identity relative to an intervention budget? Fifth, drift: one system, one mechanism, observed over a year of updates, with the internal variables gradually turning over; how much turnover before it is a different mechanism, and does my criterion give a principled answer or just inherit whatever my intervention set happens to be sensitive to? And finally the unfalsifiability worry I'm most nervous about: if every apparent counterexample gets absorbed by a redescription of the intervention set, has the view been qualified into something that risks nothing, and is that a defect for a criterion like mine or an acceptable feature of an operational definition?

Throughout, tell me which of these a committed defender of my criterion would herself concede as counting against it, and which she would rightly dismiss, and be willing to conclude that the view is well insulated if it is. For what it's worth, the thing I personally find most persuasive is transfer-of-fix evidence — if suppressing A by one route reliably suppresses B, I have trouble insisting they are different mechanisms no matter what my clause (iii) says — so I'd like you to say explicitly what my criterion would have to look like for that intuition to be respected rather than explained away, and what it would cost me to give the criterion that shape.
