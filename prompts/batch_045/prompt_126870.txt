You are a professional philosopher specialising in the metaphysics of agency and free will. Please think carefully and at length before you begin writing: I would like you to start with a short private brainstorming phase in which you map out the dialectical terrain, list the strongest considerations on each side, and note where you think my framing below goes wrong, and only then compose the essay itself.

I have just spent a long and rather circular evening arguing with a friend, and I want the argument settled properly. The guiding example is sphexishness. You will know the anecdote: a digger wasp drags its paralysed prey to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside to check the nest, comes back out, and drags the prey in. If, while the wasp is inside, an experimenter moves the prey a few inches away, the wasp emerges, returns the prey to the threshold, and then goes inside to check the nest again — and if the experimenter moves the prey again, the wasp will repeat the check-and-drag routine many times over, apparently without ever noticing that the check has already been done. (I am not interested in whether the natural history here is accurate: please treat it purely as a thought experiment, and set aside questions about what real wasps do.) Sphexishness, as a philosophical notion, is the property this anecdote is meant to illustrate: the execution of a multi-step routine whose later steps are not sensitive to whether the earlier steps have achieved their point, so that the behaviour persists in a loop under perturbation. It is standardly treated as the very image of the mechanical, and therefore as the very image of what an agent with free will is not.

My friend's thesis, which I think is close to the received view, is roughly this: sphexishness is the enemy of freedom. What threatens agency is rigidity — the failure to revise one's behaviour when the situation changes — and so the moral of the wasp is that the free agent is the flexible, adaptive, information-sensitive one, the one whose behaviour is plastic enough to track whatever the environment throws at it. Freedom, on this picture, is a matter of degree, and the degrees are the degrees of behavioural plasticity.

I think this is too quick, and I want you to adjudicate. Please write an essay of roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words that weighs the arguments and then reaches and defends a verdict — not a survey, and not a non-committal "it depends on what we mean by freedom". Some constraints and provocations, which I would like you to address rather than bypass:

First, I suspect "sphexishness" as I have defined it bundles together at least three separable properties, and I want to know whether they come apart in the way that matters: (a) behavioural stereotypy, the sheer sameness of the sequence from one occasion to the next; (b) informational encapsulation, the failure of later stages of the routine to receive the news that earlier stages have already succeeded or been rendered pointless; and (c) counterfactual invariance, the fact that the agent would go on running the routine even under changes that remove its rationale entirely. It seems to me that an agent could exhibit (a) without (b) or (c) — think of a concert pianist whose scale practice is perfectly stereotyped but who would abandon it the moment the reason for it lapsed — and that such an agent looks like a paradigm of self-disciplined agency rather than a defective mechanism. If stereotypy alone is not the freedom-relevant defect, then the friend's equation of freedom with plasticity is already in trouble.

Second, the opposite extreme seems to me equally mechanical, and I would like you to say whether you agree. Consider the approval-seeker who recomposes his entire personality to match whoever last spoke to him, or the agent I will call the weather-vane, whose every inclination is a fresh response to the most recent input, so that there is no policy, project or character that survives a change of surroundings. Such a figure is maximally plastic and maximally information-sensitive, yet it is hard to see what he does as the exercise of agency at all, since there is no persisting agent doing the tracking — merely a sequence of responses. If both the rigid loop and the endlessly adaptive response are, in their own ways, mechanical, then "mechanical" cannot simply mean "inflexible", and I want your account of what it does mean.

Third, a case that I think the friend's position handles especially badly, and which I invented in the course of the argument: the reconsideration compulsion. Imagine someone who is, at the level of first-order behaviour, highly flexible — he revises his plans constantly, updates on everything, never repeats himself merely because he did it before — but who does so because he is in the grip of a rigid second-order rule, something like "never let a decision stand", which he applies with total uniformity and which he cannot revise even when it is pointed out to him that it is ruining his life. Here the plasticity is real at one level and yet is itself the product of a rigidity at another, and the rigidity is invisible to any test that simply perturbs the environment and asks whether behaviour changes. More generally: I want you to consider whether sphexishness should be located in the agent's relation to her reasons rather than in the shape of her behaviour, and whether a purely behavioural test for it can ever be adequate, given that a fixed disposition to be flexible and a flexible disposition to be fixed might be behaviourally indistinguishable from the outside over the short run.

Fourth, a wrinkle about the perturbation itself. In the wasp case the change is introduced by an experimenter, and is arguably outside the range of variation the routine was ever shaped to cope with. One might therefore be tempted to excuse the wasp: its behaviour is perfectly reasons-responsive within ecologically valid conditions, and only breaks down under laboratory conditions that no wasp could have encountered. But this excuse seems dangerously elastic — an addict, a fanatic or a bureaucrat might equally be said to be reasons-responsive within some narrow privileged range, and to break down only under "unprecedented" pressure that we then declare irrelevant. I would like you to say what, if anything, distinguishes a legitimate restriction of the counterfactual range from a special pleading, and what follows from your answer for how demanding a test for sphexishness we should adopt.

Where I have landed, and where I want you to push back if I am wrong, is that the freedom-relevant notion is something like a profile of counterfactual sensitivity: an agent is free to the extent that her behaviour would vary across the range of situations in which her reasons for acting would vary, and would stay put across the range in which they would not. On that picture, sphexishness and weather-vane plasticity are not two different-sized helpings of the same quantity but two different kinds of mismatch between an agent's sensitivity profile and the profile of her reasons — one under-tracking, one over-tracking — and the friend is therefore wrong to treat rigidity as the single axis along which freedom is measured, though right that the wasp's specific failure is a real failure. My residual worry, which I would like you to resolve, is whether "the profile of her reasons" can be specified without either collapsing into mere description of whatever the agent happens to be sensitive to, or smuggling in a substantive moral psychology under the guise of an account of freedom.

Please give the strongest version of the friend's view before you criticise it, deal explicitly with the reconsideration compulsion and the weather-vane cases, and finish by stating plainly which side of the original argument you think was closer to the truth and why. Write in British English, for a reader who is philosophically literate but not a specialist in this literature, and without any references, citations or reading suggestions — I want the argument, not the bibliography.
