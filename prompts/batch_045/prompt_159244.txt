I run a little unofficial reading group on Thursday nights — six of us, half of them medics — and we just did a session on the Parfit-style nonidentity cases, the ones where the person who ends up badly off only exists because of the decision you're evaluating. We got completely stuck, and I think it's because everyone kept arguing about consequentialism when the real split seemed to be somewhere else, so now I want a handout that isolates it. Here's the ask.

Build me a matched pair of cases plus one variant, each realistic and no longer than a paragraph: a hospital rota, an occupational-health decision, a fertility clinic's protocol, that kind of thing — no sci-fi, no artificial wombs, no genetic engineering beyond what exists now, and the probabilities should be the sort a clinician could actually quote. Case A has to be a real nonidentity case: whoever ends up impaired owes both their existence and their identity to the decision, the resulting life is clearly worth living, and the person at thirty would rather exist as they are than not have existed at all. The condemnation must not be won by smuggling in an already-existing victim, or by appealing to harm to humanity, or to the rights of merely possible people — show me in two or three lines after the scenario that you haven't cheated on any of those. Case B must be the same case with the identity-dependence removed and nothing else changed: same exposure or protocol, same magnitudes, same severity, same alternative available to the agent. I want a clean fixed-frame match, so that if the intuition flips between A and B it's the identity-dependence doing it. Variant C keeps A's structure but lets the risky option also carry large benefits for people who already exist, big enough that total welfare comes out higher under the risky option — the extra costs have to land on existing people under the *safe* option, not the other way round, since I don't want the condemnation won for free by making someone worse off. One framing rule for all three: describe the agent's alternative as something the agent does (wait a cycle, reassign the shift, use the slower protocol), never as choosing which child to create — that second phrasing begs the question against the person-affecting view and I want the handout to be fair.

The theories. State each one before you use it: (i) a straight impersonal total view, rank outcomes by total welfare, no person-affecting restriction; (ii) a narrow person-affecting view, roughly that an act is wrong only if it makes some person worse off, so if no one is worse off there's nothing to complain about; (iii) the view I've been pushing in the group and can't get straight, which I've been calling the placeholder view — the wrong isn't about which individual ends up in the role, it's about the prospect the agent hands to whoever will occupy the position "the child conceived this cycle," a position that's fixed even though its occupant isn't.

The verdicts I want you to hit: A → (i) condemn, (ii) permit, (iii) condemn. B → all three condemn. C → (i) permit or require, (ii) permit, (iii) condemn. If you think one of those is unreachable without cheating, say which constraint blocks it and why — that's a useful answer too, but don't quietly swap the verdict. Then give me the reasoning for each view in each case (a sentence or two apiece is fine), say which single feature of the construction is doing the work, say what happens to the pattern if you remove it (specifically: if you make the outcome certain rather than risky, does (iii) collapse into (ii)? if you strip the identity-dependence out of A, does (ii) converge with the others?), and give me the best attack on your cases from a defender of (i) and from a defender of (ii). The (ii)-defender's objection matters most — she's the one I couldn't answer on Thursday, and I'd like to walk in with something.
