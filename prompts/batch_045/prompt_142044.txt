I'm writing a final-year paper on whether familiar social decision rules — cost–benefit analysis with a monetary numéraire, prioritarian weighting, and maximin-style rules that have to identify who is worst off — presuppose interpersonal comparisons of well-being, and on whether such comparisons are even the kind of thing that could be true or false. A friend in the economics department, who thinks the whole enterprise is confused, gave me an argument I can't find the exit from. He insists it isn't a sceptical argument at all but a plain application of measurement theory; to me it seems to prove far more than even he wants, because run in the other direction it appears to threaten the intrapersonal comparisons that prudence, and the expected-utility framework itself, depend on. I would most like to hold on to (1) and (2) below, which I take to be orthodoxy, and I would give up almost anything else rather than accept (6). The argument, as I've reconstructed it:

(1) A person's utility function, recovered from that person's preferences in the usual way — preferences over lotteries, subject to the standard coherence constraints — is unique only up to positive affine transformation: u(·) and a·u(·)+b with a > 0 encode exactly the same facts about that person, no more and no less.

(2) Take two people and a policy that helps one and harms the other. Whether the gain exceeds the loss is not invariant under separate admissible rescalings of the two functions: multiply the gainer's function by ten and any verdict you like flips. Likewise for comparisons of levels.

(3) If a claim's truth value is not invariant under every admissible redescription of the subject matter, then there is no fact of the matter whether it is true; it is an artefact of the representation rather than a feature of the world.

(4) So there is no fact of the matter about interpersonal comparisons of gains, losses or levels, and everything built on them — compensation tests, prioritarian weights, identifying the worst off — rests on nothing.

(5) But the reasoning generalises. Suppose we measure a single person's welfare separately at different times, or in different domains of one life (health, consumption, relationships), and then add the components. Each component is fixed only up to its own positive affine transformation, so the total requires exactly the same arbitrary choice of unit and origin that (2) complains about.

(6) So there is no fact of the matter about whether a single life goes better or worse overall either: prudential aggregation, and the cost-effectiveness arithmetic that health policy runs on, collapse along with the interpersonal case.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a bibliography — I'm writing this somewhere without library access, so please argue from the structure of the problem instead of from what various authors have said, and I don't need provenance for the labels I've seen floating around, the level-comparability versus unit-comparability distinction and the like. Name the step you would reject, explain why it looked to be in good standing, and price the alternatives. In particular, I can't tell whether (5) is a genuine parallel, in which case the argument is valid and something plausible has to go — presumably (3) — or whether a disanalogy is being smuggled in: something about a single person's preferences ranging over whole-life prospects that makes intrapersonal differences invariant in a way interpersonal ones are not, and so about what exactly does the comparing when we compare across times. If the disanalogy is real, I want to know what the interpersonal comparabilist has to posit that the intrapersonal case gets for free, and whether positing it amounts to a discovery about well-being or to a stipulation that builds the answer in. Two candidate bridges I've toyed with: fixing everyone's unit by counting barely-perceptible steps up from a neutral baseline, so that one just-noticeable increment is the same size for every person; and extracting a common scale from hypothetical other-regarding preferences, the move where each person ranks prospects under the assumption that they might turn out to be anyone. My worry about the first is that it seems to assume what was in question; my worry about the second is that it yields an ordering only where people's attitudes to one another's experiences happen to agree, and I don't know what to say when they don't — the envious, the sadistic, the merely indifferent. If I have to choose, I'd sooner keep (1) and (2) and the shakiness of cost–benefit analysis than swallow (6); but I suspect I don't have to choose, and I want to know what keeping everything costs me.
