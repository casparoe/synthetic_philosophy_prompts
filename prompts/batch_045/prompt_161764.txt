The question:

[1] State the Principal Principle, including the admissibility clause, and in a sentence or two say why the clause is needed. Give one example of inadmissible information and say what would go wrong if the clause were dropped.

[2] A coin will be tossed in another room at noon. You don't know much about it: your evidence splits about 60/40 in favour of its chance of heads being 0.9 rather than 0.3. What should your credence in heads be, and why? Now suppose that, before noon, a perfectly reliable source tells you that the coin did in fact land heads. What should your credence be then, and does that conflict with the Principal Principle?

[3] Someone in your group proposes the Expected Chance Principle: your credence in A should always equal your probability-weighted average estimate of the chance of A, and the Principal Principle is just the special case in which you already know the chance for certain. Is that right, is the Principal Principle really a special case of it, and is the Principal Principle best thought of as a rule for changing your mind when you learn a chance, or as something else?

The student's answer:

[1] The Principal Principle says C(A | ch(A) = x and E) = x, where E is admissible evidence. Admissible evidence is evidence that bears on A only by way of what it tells you about the chances — as opposed to evidence about how A actually turns out. The clearest example of inadmissible information is the outcome itself, or a crystal ball report of the outcome, and the clause is needed because if we allowed E = A we would get C(A | ch(A) = 0.7 and A) = 1, which contradicts the 0.7 the principle asks for, so without the restriction the principle is inconsistent. One thing worth noting is that chances don't change over time: the chance of a given outcome is a fixed feature of the setup, which is what makes it objective rather than merely a feature of somebody's evidence. The little t subscript in ch_t(A) is just there to keep track of which chance we happen to be talking about, not to say that the chance is relative to a time, so in practice it can be dropped.

[2] Here you should defer to the chance: your credence in heads should be the objective chance of heads, since the chance is the fact about the world that you are trying to track, and it doesn't matter whether you know what it is — the chance is what it is regardless of your uncertainty. Your evidence leans toward the coin having a 0.9 chance rather than a 0.3 chance, so 0.9 is the chance to defer to, and your credence in heads should be 0.9. If a perfectly reliable source then tells you that the coin landed heads, your credence should go to about 1. That doesn't conflict with the Principal Principle, because that testimony is inadmissible: it tells you how A turned out rather than anything about the chances, so the principle has no say about that update and you simply conditionalize on the testimony in the ordinary way. Nor is the principle telling you to throw away evidence you already have — it constrains the credence you'd have given the chance plus admissible evidence, and everything else goes in by conditionalization.

[3] The Principal Principle is basically a rule for changing your mind: when you learn that the chance of A is x, you set your credence in A to x. The Expected Chance Principle is a different and weaker rule of thumb, and it isn't really connected to the Principal Principle, which tells you to match the chance rather than to average over your guesses about what the chance might be. So no, the Principal Principle isn't a special case of it. What the Expected Chance Principle does capture is the degenerate situation in which you have no evidence at all about the chance, where there's nothing to defer to and averaging is the best you can do.
