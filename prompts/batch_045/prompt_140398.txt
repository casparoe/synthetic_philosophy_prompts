I need a villanelle to close the last session of a reading group on whether death is bad for you, and I want it to end on the Epicurean no-subject argument. Two refrains: one should be the Epicurean claim, but do NOT just drop the famous tag-line from the ancient text in as the refrain — no canonical maxim, no latin — I want that point said in your own words, the way a live person would actually say it. The other refrain should be the deprivationist protest in a concrete voice: one particular person who died young and the decades she didn't get. Let the villanelle machinery do real work — the argument answers the fear, the fear comes back anyway, over and over.

Two things it has to get right or someone in the room will wince. First, the symmetry move: the Epicurean leans on the time before you were born, the objector can grant the symmetry and still say the past isn't a loss because there was no one yet to have it, whereas the future was hers to lose, and the Epicurean's answer has to be that after death there's no subject to be deprived either. Second, a case we came up with that I want in there: instead of dying, imagine being retroactively never-born — nobody remembers you, nobody grieves, nobody takes your place, and there's no dying process to be frightened of. The argument seems to say this is exactly as much nothing-to-you as ordinary death, but everyone in the room found it much worse and couldn't say why. Have the Epicurean explain that recoil without giving up the claim that death isn't bad for the dead person — locate the badness while she's still alive, in the anticipation or in what the living person stands to lose — and let the refrain come back anyway rather than declaring a winner. Oh, and if you can manage it in one line somewhere: the fear is louder at forty than at eighty even though on this view the badness is zero at every distance.

Nineteen lines, real villanelle form, good poem first and argument second.
