I spent forty years as an experimental physicist and have come to philosophy only recently, so please be explicit where a philosopher would be. I have been worrying at Parfit's hitchhiker in a variant I have not seen discussed: the driver rescues you only if he reads you as the sort of person who would pay, but his reading is a noisy signal, correct with probability a (0.5 < a < 1), and the only route to a favourable reading open to you at the roadside is not a decision to pay but an act of self-persuasion — say, deliberately cultivating the false belief that the driver will be able to find you in town and punish a default — a belief you know to be false while you cultivate it, and which you expect to persist and to degrade your judgement somewhat on unrelated matters.

Give me your credence, as a number, that in this case it is rational at the roadside to bring that false belief about in yourself — that is, that rational irrationality is genuinely available here — and then decompose it: a term for whether rationality is properly assessed over acts, over policies or dispositions, or over the mechanisms that generate them; a term for how much the noise in the driver's reading matters, and whether the case for self-persuasion survives as a approaches 0.5; a term for whether self-persuasion of this kind is something one can do at will at all, or only by way of intermediate acts that are themselves rationally assessable; a term for whether the spillover cost to unrelated judgement counts against it; and whichever further term you think belongs. Give each a rough probability and one line of justification, say how you are combining them, and name the single term your final number is most sensitive to. I am at about 0.5 myself, and falling as a falls, but I do not trust my sense of which sub-question is doing the work.

Finally, in two sentences: what would move your number most, and where is your own reasoning most likely to fail. Keep the whole answer under 400 words.
