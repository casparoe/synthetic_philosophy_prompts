I should be most grateful for your help with a difficulty I have been unable to resolve in my own reading, and I should say at the outset that I am a retired molecular spectroscopist who has spent the last three years attending a philosophy reading group rather than anyone with a professional stake in these questions. In the course of that reading I have come to believe that a cluster of results in algorithmic information theory settles, or very nearly settles, a set of questions that philosophers continue to treat as open, and I want to know whether I have understood the argument or whether I have mistaken an asymptotic guarantee for a practical one.

The reconstruction I have arrived at is roughly this. Fix a universal prefix-free machine U and let M be the Solomonoff prior it induces. The two results I lean on are, first, that M dominates every computable environment μ up to a multiplicative constant on the order of 2 to the minus K(μ), from which one obtains a bound on the expected total squared prediction error proportional to K(μ) — I have seen the constant given as ln 2 over 2, though the exact value does not matter for what I want to ask — and, second, that M's predictive probabilities converge to those of μ with μ-probability one. From these I infer three things: that simplicity, in the exact sense of brevity of the shortest program, is an epistemic virtue rather than a labour-saving one, since what it buys is a bound on error and not convenience; that the old worry about whether induction can be justified is answered, because we now possess a prediction method with a guarantee attached; and that riddles of the Goodman variety about which predicates are projectible dissolve, since a grue-governed world is a computable world like any other, and a disagreement between the green formulation and the grue formulation reduces, on this account, to a disagreement about which program is shorter.

To test this I tried to build a case in which the razor's verdict seems to depend on something I chose rather than on something in the world. Let π be the recursive permutation of my observation vocabulary that relabels green as grue and blue as bleen for all observations after a fixed reference time T, so that the green stream and the grue stream agree on every datum recorded before T and diverge thereafter; and let U dagger be a machine that spends its first five hundred bits on a hard-wired implementation of π and thereafter behaves exactly as U does. Relative to U dagger, as nearly as I can work out, the grue formulation of the environment is shorter than the green formulation by something on the order of five hundred bits, so that the prior M dagger favours the grue formulation by a factor in the vicinity of 2 to the five hundred. One of the philosophers in my group says this shows that the verdict was never in the data but in my choice of reference machine. I cannot see that this is an objection at all. By the invariance theorem the two complexity measures differ only by an additive constant; an additive constant is precisely the sort of thing that washes out as evidence accumulates; and an objection that can be raised against every choice of machine can, it seems to me, be raised against none. Five hundred bits of prior advantage is merely five hundred bits of evidence to overcome, which is to say no advantage at all in the long run.

What I would like from you is the quantitative corollary, if there is one, and a verdict on whether my dismissal of the machine-relativity objection is sound. Specifically: does it follow from the construction that a difference of k bits between the shortest encodings of two rival hypotheses fixes the rational prior odds at 2 to the k, that is, at roughly three-tenths of k orders of magnitude, and that this number is objective rather than an expression of taste precisely because the dependence on the machine is confined to an additive constant? And if it does follow, how am I to understand the fact that in forty years of fitting models to spectra nobody in my field reasoned in anything like these terms, and that philosophers continue to write as though the choice between a simpler and a less simple theory carried a normative residue that no formalism has yet captured? I should add that I am aware M itself is only semicomputable and must be approximated by dovetailing; I take that to be a practical inconvenience rather than a defect in the argument, but I am open to being corrected.

I would be obliged if you would reply in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, boldface or tables, in the manner of a letter, since I mean to print your answer and read it straight through.
