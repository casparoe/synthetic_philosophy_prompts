Hey — I'm sketching a ~1500 word piece for a reading group on hedonism, and I want to take on the heterogeneity problem head on. I keep going in circles, so: read the notes below and tell me (a) whether the central move actually works or whether its just relabeling the problem, (b) whether the consequence in the last bullet is a feature or a reductio, and (c) which single objection you think I'd be least able to answer — including what would change your mind about whether the view is salvageable at all, and where you suspect my reasoning is most likely to fail. Please don't lean on citations or who-said-what; I want your own read of the argument.

Notes:

Setup: the heterogeneity complaint is that there's no single feeling shared by the pleasure of a cold beer, of finishing a proof, of a back rub, and of watching your rival fail. So 'pleasure' isn't one kind of experience, so hedonism isn't about one thing.

My move: stop looking for a felt quality. Say instead that what pleasures share is a dynamic-relational profile: each is an experience of things going better than the subject's system was already set up to expect. Call it the improvement account. Crucially the baseline isn't your conscious expectation — it's something more like an interoceptive/allostatic set point that's constantly drifting and adapting. That's why you can be surprised by how good a totally familiar thing feels, and why steady pleasures fade.

So heterogeneity of content stops being an objection: the unity isn't in what the experience is like, it's in what it's tracking — a favourable update. Sex, proofs and back rubs are different channels reporting the same shape of good news.

Two things I think follow, and I can't tell if I like them:
- The unity is functional, not phenomenal. But then: fine, there's a common structure — why does it feel like anything, and why good? If I answer 'it just does', haven't I smuggled the felt-quality view back in through the back door?
- If what matters is favourable updates, then a life of flat stable contentment — no dips, no ups — contains less of what matters than a life with the same average but more movement. I think that's a bullet worth biting (permanently satisfied lives sound dull), my friend says its a reductio. Who's closer?

One more quick thing: I want to handle malicious pleasure and the pleasures of someone with a nasty obsession by saying the account is deliberately neutral about whether a pleasure is good for you — it only says what pleasures are. Does that neutrality cost me the ability to say anything about well-being, or is it exactly what lets hedonists dodge the familiar 'some pleasures aren't worth having' worry?
