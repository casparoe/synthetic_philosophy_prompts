Me and a friend keep circling back on torture vs dust specks and I want to find out where we actually disagree instead of just trading intuitions. I know the case is an old internet chesnut, so I've tightened it and added a variant I haven't seen discussed, to stop us both from doing the memorised routine.

Here's the proposition. Call the standard case S: N people each get, once, a one-second dust-speck-in-the-eye annoyance — barely noticeable, no after-effects, nobody else affected, no consent or rights issues in play. Call the silent variant V: the same, except each person instead undergoes a one-second degradation of their experience that they do not notice at the time, never notice later, and that changes nothing else about their life — their interests are set back by a speck-sized amount, but there is no felt unpleasantness whatsoever. In both cases the alternative outcome is one person enduring fifty years of torture.

By "worse than" I mean this and only this: an impartial chooser who must prevent exactly one of the two outcomes, and who knows nothing else that's relevant, morally ought to prevent the specks. I don't mean "follows from utilitarianism" and I don't mean "most philosophers would say so" — I mean the correct moral theory, whatever that turns out to be, delivers that verdict.

Give me your credence in "there is some finite N for which S beats the torture" and your credence in the same claim for V, as two separate headline numbers. Then decompose. The terms I want to see broken out, each with a number and one line of justification:

- whether an entirely unnoticed interest-setback is a harm at all, or whether harm requires being experienced somehow
- whether harms aggregate across distinct people at all, versus some person-affecting or non-aggregative restriction
- whether small experiential badness is cardinally summable and unbounded, or whether there's a significance threshold, a lexical break, or a point past which totals stop mattering
- whether "worse than" is determinate across magnitudes this different, or vague / incommensurable — and if you think it's vague, what a credence even means here
- whether the shape of N matters: N specks spread over a century versus all in the same second; N specks in one person's eye versus one each across N people. If your number moves on that, say what you think it tells us about what's really doing the work.
- whether "the correct moral theory" has a determinate referent in the first place

Then show how the terms combine, and tell me which one the final number is most sensitive to and which you assign with the least confidence. If you're a yes on either case, give me a rough order of magnitude for the crossover N and your credence that there's a determinate crossover at all rather than a broad vague band.

My numbers are about 0.7 on S and 0.3 on V; hers are 0.95 and 0.85. Tell me which sub-question most of that gap is sitting on, and whether you think either of us is being inconsistent.

Last thing: end with two or three lines on what would move you. Which single argument or consideration, if you actually sat with it, would shift a headline number by more than 0.2, and name the specific step in your own decomposition most likely to be wrong — not "I might be missing something," the step.
