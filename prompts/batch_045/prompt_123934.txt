For the past two terms I have been running a graduate seminar on speaker meaning, and I am now trying to turn a long-running argument with a colleague in the linguistics department into a conference paper for the autumn. The target is the overtness condition in the Gricean analysis of utterer's meaning. The claim I want to defend is that a large and socially important class of implicatures is off-record: the speaker needs the hearer to draw the inference, and simultaneously needs to be in a position to deny having invited it — to be able to say, truthfully, that she never said any such thing. My suspicion is that a theory on which meaning something requires the hearer to recognise the intention behind the inference has no room for a speaker who is trading precisely on deniability, and that the current fashion for inserting a third level between saying and implicating is a symptom of this rather than a solution to it. Before I commit any of it to paper, I want the strongest version of the other side delivered at close quarters — as a seminar exchange, not as a survey.

So please play a philosopher who has spent her career defending the Gricean architecture, and make her sharp rather than a strawman. She holds that the saying/implicating distinction is real, and that what is said is fixed by conventional meaning together with disambiguation and the assignment of reference — no pragmatic enrichment of the proposition expressed; she holds that conversational implicature is a species of speaker meaning, and that speaker meaning is analysed in terms of reflexive intentions; she treats cancellability, calculability and non-detachability as useful if defeasible diagnostics; and she thinks that the data which have driven others to multiply levels — embedded scalar readings, apparently missing constituents, the behaviour of the tests in institutional settings — can be handled by keeping firm the threefold distinction between what the speaker said, what she implicated, and what her words merely happen to suggest to a given audience. She concedes small things readily: that the maxims are not a decision procedure, that the original formulations are programmatic, that some enrichments look grammatically entrenched. She will not concede that saying and implicating blur into one another, or that implicature can float free of speaker meaning. Keep her to two or three paragraphs a turn; let her interrupt me, let her press an advantage rather than only parrying, and let her ask me for details of a case in character if she needs them. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. One further discipline, because I shall be quoting her in the paper: when we reach a point on which people who actually work on this material disagree — including about what Grice himself was committed to — she should say plainly which part is settled machinery that everyone accepts and which part is live controversy, and she should tell me when my argument is attacking a position nobody holds.

Here is my opening move. A museum curator facing a restitution claim writes to a colleague, in an email she knows will be retained and disclosed: 'I have read some of the provenance files for the collection.' Both of them know that she has read all of them; that is the entire point of the formulation, since a weaker claim about her own knowledge is worth a great deal to her if it is believed and costs her nothing if it is not. The colleague, exactly as she intends, takes her to be conveying that she has not read them all; the inference is one she wants drawn, and it is one she can cancel with 'in fact, all of them' — a cancellation she would never actually perform, since uttering it would be a fresh act with its own uptake. So: did she mean that she had not read them all? If yes, then your overtness condition is doing no work, because her strategy depends on the recognition being unshowable; if no, then your theory withholds the name of speaker meaning from what looks like a paradigm of calculated communicative behaviour. And before you reach for the maxims — of course the inference goes through because the hearer assumes she is being as informative as she can be; the interesting question is what becomes of the analysis when the speaker is exploiting that assumption while holding the cancellation in reserve, and whether a test that only tracks what she could grammatically have added is really a test for what she meant.
