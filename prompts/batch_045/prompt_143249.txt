Please act as a professional philosopher who specializes in social epistemology and who also has real experience advising institutions that run online platforms; think the problem through carefully before you write, and in your answer start with a short private brainstorming section (say, four or five bullet points) in which you work out what could go wrong in this case and which distinctions actually carry weight, before moving on to the document itself. I need something I can circulate, not an essay, so please keep the memo under about 700 words, avoid specialist jargon wherever a plain word will do, and above all do not give me a literature review, a reading list, or any citations or named authors or studies — our committee has no journal access, so anything that depends on looking things up is useless to us; reason the issue out from the case itself.

The setting: I am a software engineer (no formal philosophy training — please don't assume I know the vocabulary) who sits by appointment on the Information Environment Committee of a mid-sized regional public library system. We host a moderated civic discussion platform called Baseline, used by roughly twelve thousand patrons for neighborhood issues and nonpartisan civics programming, and we have to make a decision by the end of the month. A vendor is offering us a module, Bridge, that does two things: it inserts into each user's feed a small number of posts from people who disagree with them, selected by a "cross-cutting" measure, and it attaches to those posts a short generated summary of the opposing position written in its strongest form, because raw posts from the other side tend to be read as noise. The vendor reports a pilot in which cross-cutting exposure went up substantially and self-reported confidence in one's own view went down slightly, which they present as evidence of success. Half the committee thinks this is obviously what a library is for. Half thinks a library should not be in the business of algorithmically deciding which disagreements its patrons are shown, and that the generated summaries in particular mean patrons are being fed a synthetic version of their opponents rather than their opponents.

The wrinkle I want you to engage with, and the reason I'm asking a philosopher rather than just polling the committee: our own reading of the situation is that there are two quite different failure modes that get lumped together, and they call for opposite responses. One is simple omission — a patron who shares a neighborhood and a friend group with people like herself may literally never encounter the strongest version of the contrary view, because it is filtered out upstream by who she follows and what gets shared; more supply of contrary content looks like exactly the right medicine there. The other is something closer to discrediting: some of our most active users have absorbed, from sources they trust, a standing instruction that outside institutions are not to be believed, so that the appearance of contrary material does not update them at all, and the fact that a library is pushing it can itself be read as confirmation of the story they already hold. A third pattern we think we see in the logs is what one of our librarians calls feeling-informed-without-exposure: users can recite a vivid account of what the other side supposedly believes, which they picked up from their own side's caricatures of it, and are extremely confident; exposing them to the actual thing may simply fail to register, or may look to them like one more instance of the caricature. My own proposal, which I'd like you to pressure-test rather than just adopt, is that we sort possible interventions by whether they try to change what reaches a user (reach) or how much credence she assigns to what reaches her (credence), and that we only adopt Bridge on the condition that we commit in advance to a specific, measurable criterion for pulling the plug after one term.

What I need from you is the written opinion I will circulate: a clear recommendation — adopt, adopt with conditions, run a bounded pilot, or decline — with the reasoning, a concrete time frame and a concrete set of measurable conditions attached, and an honest statement of the single strongest consideration on the side you are recommending against, stated well enough that the people who hold it would recognize their own view in it. Our committee is eight people: four librarians, two trustees, a high school civics teacher, and me; the document will be public, so it should be written so that an interested patron can follow it. If you think my reach/credence distinction is the wrong frame, say so in the memo and give us a better one.
