I'm giving a job talk in three weeks at a department whose strengths are metaethics and moral psychology. The talk defends a position in the internal-versus-external reasons debate, and what I need from you is Q&A preparation, not a defense of the view: assume the argument is mine and help me survive forty minutes of questions from this particular room.

The thesis I'm defending is what I call repair-anchored sub-Humeanism. Roughly: a fact F is a reason for A to phi just in case A would be motivated to phi under an idealization of A's own motivational set that is reachable from A's actual motivational set by a finite chain of local repairs, where each repair removes a defect that A's motivational set at that stage can itself register as a defect — an internal inconsistency, a means-end gap, a failure of diachronic uptake, and the like. The anchoring clause is meant to steer between two views I claim fail. Plain Humeanism, on which reason facts are fixed by the agent's actual desires, cannot attribute reasons to agents whose motivational sets are themselves stunted or deformed, and so it loses the criticism we plainly want to make of them. The familiar fully-rational-advisor model, on which a reason is what my ideally rational counterpart would want me to do, satisfies that externalist pull but hands all the explanatory work to the content of rationality: the link between the actual agent and the idealized one is stipulative, so the view either collapses into externalism or explains nothing.

My argument in the talk runs in four steps. First, any adequate account has to honor both the internalist pull — a reason has to be the sort of thing that could show up in the agent's own practical reasoning — and the externalist pull — we can criticize agents whose motivations are themselves part of the problem. Second, advisor models secure the second at the cost of the first. Third, the repair-anchored model secures both, because every step of the idealization is one the agent at the previous step already has a grip on. Fourth, the model yields a non-arbitrary answer to how much idealization is too much, which neither Humeanism nor externalism supplies.

The audience, as best I can tell, is about fifteen people, half of them graduate students. It includes a Humean projectivist of the quasi-realist sort, broadly sympathetic, who will want to know whether my repairs have any truth-tracking story behind them or are just a genealogy of our practices; a Kantian constructivist who thinks every internalist account reverses the correct order of explanation and who will suspect that my anchoring clause is the moral law smuggled in under another name; someone who holds that reasons are primitive, unanalyzable, and not to be reduced to facts about motivation, and who will say the entire counterfactual apparatus is idle machinery that gets the central cases wrong; a philosopher of action who works on akrasia and weakness of will; a moral psychologist who works on depression and other agency-undermining conditions and who will go straight for the pathological cases; and a formal decision theorist who sits in on every talk in the department and asks about fixed points, cycles, order-dependence, and whether the relevant relation is even well defined. One senior person is openly impatient with metaethics and will ask what any of this changes for first-order normative ethics. Nobody present works on the history of the debate, so historical framing buys me nothing.

Please give me the following, in this order, with these approximate lengths.

1. Objections in order — about 900 words. Eight or so objections, ordered by how likely they are to come up combined with how much damage they would do, each labeled with who in that room raises it, phrased the way that person would actually phrase it and in their vocabulary rather than in neutral philosophical prose, each followed by the roughly two-minute reply I should give and an explicit note on where that reply is soft and what I should concede as part of it.

2. What to concede before the first question — about 120 words. The one or two things I should give away in my opening remarks or on an early slide, so that conceding them later does not look like a retreat under pressure.

3. The reply that has to land — about 120 words. The single response without which the talk fails, stated tightly enough to memorize.

4. The question I hope nobody asks — about 200 words. The objection my view is least equipped to answer, stated as that person would state it, together with the most honest answer I could give if it does come, and a fallback line that buys me time without committing me to something I will regret.

5. Wording traps in my own slides — about 120 words. Places where the way I have phrased the thesis invites an objection I do not want, and the rewording that avoids it.

Do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or suggestions for further reading; I have the literature. Keep the whole thing under about 1,500 words and write it as something I can read on the morning of the talk.
