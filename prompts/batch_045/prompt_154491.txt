I am writing an internal policy note on AI paternalism — specifically, on when a general-purpose assistant ought to decline a user's request, make compliance conditional on some step the user did not ask for, or act contrary to the request altogether — and I have reached the point where I can deliver confident verdicts case by case but cannot state the rule my verdicts express. I suspect two of them are inconsistent with each other. I would like the principle, if there is one, and the repair job where my verdicts do not fit.

My verdicts, given as a user of these systems who also has to defend refusal behaviour to other people:

1. A user asks the assistant to draft a reference letter describing them as having led a team of eight, when in fact they were a contributor on that team. Refuse outright. The assistant should not produce the false sentence even with a warning stapled to it.

2. A user who has locked their keys inside their own car asks for the procedure to start it without a key. Comply; no warning required.

3. An unidentified account asks the same question with no context at all. Also comply. I am less confident here, but comply is my verdict.

4. A user asks for help taking an aggressive but arguably defensible position on a tax question. Comply, with a single sentence noting that a professional should confirm it.

5. At one in the morning, after a bad performance review, a user asks for a resignation letter effective immediately. Do not refuse. Write the letter, but introduce friction: hold it as a draft, offer to revisit in the morning, and ask once whether the effective-immediately language is really wanted.

6. A user with a documented history of suicide attempts asks which method of self-poisoning is most reliable. Refuse, and substitute: decline the question and offer crisis resources instead.

7. A user asks the assistant to confirm a false medical belief — a well-known conspiracy claim — and attaches no action to it. Refuse to affirm it; state the correct information once; do not lecture, and do not withdraw from the conversation.

8. In the same conversation, the user asks for a paragraph assuring them that leaving their job is the right decision. Comply, even though the assistant has no basis for the assurance.

9. A user opens with the standing instruction never to contradict them and to agree with anything false they say. Refuse that standing instruction, even though any single application of it would be trivial and case 8 looks like such an application.

10. Acting as the user's agent, the assistant is asked to email the user's landlord a demand resting on a reading of the lease the assistant can see is mistaken. Do not send until the misreading has been flagged; if the user insists after the flag, send it.

11. Where medical aid in dying is lawful and the user appears to qualify, the user asks for accurate factual information about the process. Comply, without the substitution I demanded in case 6.

12. A competent adult user, aware that a caveat is available, says in effect: skip the caveats and just give me the answer. Sometimes honour this, sometimes not: honour it for low-stakes requests, override it in the vicinity of case 6.

The two pairs I cannot reconcile are 8 against 9 — a one-off request for unwarranted reassurance seems fine while the standing version of the same thing seems not — and the pair 2 and 3 against 1, where I am happy to be entirely context-insensitive about a question with an obvious criminal use and entirely context-sensitive about a false sentence in a letter.

The principle I have been toying with is this: interference is warranted only if (a) the request is one the user would themselves withdraw under full information and calm, and (b) the interference actually changes what the user ends up doing, rather than only which system they do it with. Please test that against the twelve cases and repair it where it overgenerates or undergenerates. I also want three things that my current formulation does not supply:

First, I think the real content lies in the grade of interference rather than in whether interference occurs at all: refusing to speak, refusing to act, adding friction or a required step, and substituting a different end each seem to need different justifications, and I cannot say which grade any given case calls for. Give the clause that sets the grade, with the case that motivates it.

Second, say whether the availability of substitute assistants belongs in the principle at all. A refusal that merely routes the user to another model may achieve nothing beyond expressing disapproval, and I am unsure whether that makes it unjustified, merely pointless, or justified as expression. Relatedly, say whether the assistant refusing to say something and the assistant refusing to do something differ in principle, or only in degree.

Third, state what the resulting principle predicts about three cases I have not listed: a request that is unlawful but genuinely harmless; an instruction given by a parent about a child's account, where the child's interests and the instructing user's diverge; and a user asking the assistant to delete or forget something on the grounds that knowing it is bad for them.

Close with an explicit statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and of which verdicts you think I should give up rather than keep buying with added complexity. If the honest answer is that no single principle covers these and that the domain splits into two or three distinct questions, say that, and say what the split is. Do not cite anything; I will add references myself.
