I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about what you owe someone you regard as your epistonic equal — sorry, your epistemic equal — when the two of you have looked at the same question and come out on opposite sides. He holds something like a steadfast line: he had examined the evidence, the evidence supported his verdict, and the additional fact that a person he respects reached a different verdict is just one more datum, often a weak one, that he is entitled to weigh and discount. I kept pressing a conciliationist thought: that there is something in the bare symmetry of the situation — you think they are as likely as you are to have got it right — that has to move you, and moves you whether or not you can find anything wrong with your own first-order reasoning. He had one move I did not answer well. If symmetry really forces me to give ground, he said, it should also force me to give ground about the rule itself, since plenty of people he respects deny the rule; and then I owe him an account of what my confidence in conciliationism should be after I have conciliated about conciliationism. I found myself saying something unstable, and I would like to stop waving my hands about it.

I am teaching a seminar on this in three weeks and I would rather the students hit the problem with their own credences and their own score on the line than hear me characterize two views on a whiteboard. Please design the session for me.

Setting and constraints. An upper-division undergraduate epistemology seminar, fourteen students, one eighty-minute block, one room with a projector, one instructor and no assistant. Materials have to be cheap and analogue: index cards, a bag of coloured tokens or poker chips, a spreadsheet or projector for posting numbers. No money changes hands and nothing may depend on students knowing any literature. The words conciliationism, steadfastness, equal weight, and peer should not be spoken by me, or appear on any handout, until the debrief begins.

What I want them to come away having felt, in their gut and in their score, not in a definition:

First: that give equal weight to your peer is not one rule with one content. Its content depends on how much of their evidence is already yours, and that is exactly what you cannot see from the inside. Three unanimous people against you are one thing if they each looked at something different and quite another if they all looked at the same thing. I want students to be in a situation where the visible facts are identical in the two cases and the right response is not, and to find this out only by discovering which case they were in.

Second: that a policy for handling disagreement has to be applied to itself, and that doing so does something different to different policies — some walk away to a place nobody in the room occupies, some oscillate, some refuse to budge at any group size. I want them to watch their own number move or fail to move under their own rule.

Third: that accuracy is the only thing that pays, but under a scoring rule that punishes both over- and under-confidence, hedging is not free either. Being agreeable should be worth something in one part of the room and nothing at all in another.

The skeleton I have been playing with, which you should tighten, correct, or replace with something better. Private evidence first: each student gets a small private sample drawn from an urn of unknown composition, writes down a private estimate of the proportion and a confidence, and is scored against the true composition later. Then the disagreement stage: students post estimates anonymously and revise, without discussion, seeing numbers but never each other's samples. The variable I want hidden is evidential overlap. In some groups the several students' samples are genuinely independent draws; in others they are, unknown to them, copies of a single draw, so that unanimity carries roughly the weight of one person's evidence rather than several people's. Then a discussion stage where everything may be shared and estimates may be revised again, and only then the reveal of the true composition, the payout, and the reveal of who was arguing with photocopies. Finally the loop: every student writes down a general rule of the form when someone I count as my equal disagrees with me, I move my confidence this fraction of the way toward theirs, with a number; the numbers go up on the projector unattributed; each student then applies their own number to that very distribution and writes a second number, and then a third, and we look at where the sequences go.

What I need from you: the actual rules and the exact numbers — sample sizes, urn compositions, what is written on which card, which numbers are public and when, the scoring rule with its formula and a worked example of a payout, and how a group of fourteen is partitioned so that the overlap variable is invisible until the reveal; a minute-by-minute timeline for eighty minutes; the exact wording, as brief as possible, for the two or three things the instructor has to say out loud before the debrief; a debrief of about twenty minutes that maps specific moments in the session onto specific parts of the disagreement problem, including the self-application move I could not answer last night, and that honestly owns the fact that half the room was misled about their evidence; a frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and what to do when it does, including the student who refuses to give a number, the student who treats it as a puzzle about the urn rather than about disagreement, and the possibility that everyone hedges to the same bland degree and nothing interesting happens; and, just as frank, a note on which feature of the conciliationism-versus-steadfastness dispute the game cannot reproduce at all and which I will simply have to state and argue for myself. If some part of my skeleton will not work, say so and say why before you redesign it.

Begin with a single sentence giving me the bottom line: the one mechanism in the session that actually does the work, the thing students do that makes the problem land on them. Then go straight into the design — no preamble, no restatement of what I asked for, and no general remarks about disagreement or about teaching.
