I run a student philosophy society and I've got a 75-minute session next week where I have to get across the difference between epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment (subject-sensitive invariantism, the "stakes shift whether you know" view). Room of about fifteen, mostly first- and second-years who saw Gettier once in intro and who, the moment they hear "it depends on the context," hear "so knowledge is just whatever you feel like" — plus two or three people who've read a bit more and will want to know which view is actually true. A friend of mine in the grad seminar says the whole dispute is merely verbal and I shouldn't bother teaching both, and I don't think she's right, but I want a plan that survives that heckle rather than collapsing under it. So: don't explain either view to me, I know them both reasonably well — plan how I teach the contrast. What has to be on the board before the two views can even be seperated (my guess is that they have to be able to see whose situation is doing the work, the ascriber's context or the subject's stakes, and that this has to come before the views rather than dropped in as a footnote), and why in that order.

The case I'm tempted to build the whole session around is the airport one — you're seeing someone off, a stranger at the gate mentions a stopover, and whether you "know" the flight stops there seems to swing with how much is riding on your getting it right, even though nothing about your evidence changed. I like that it's boring and ordinary, but I'm not sure it should carry everything, and I'm also unsure whether I should start from the older bank case and only then bring in the airport one, or skip the bank case entirely. Tell me which single example should carry the session, what it has to have and what it has to lack (I think it needs the evidence held fixed *visibly* fixed, and it probably shouldn't involve anyone's life being at risk, because that makes people argue about the example instead of about the view). Then there's the case I actually keep coming back to and can't place: Priya has glanced at a gauge and has perfectly ordinary, modest evidence that a valve is shut; her own stakes are nil, she wants to go home; Sam next to her has to decide whether to send a crew into the chamber, and his stakes are enormous. It seems fine for Priya to say "I know it's shut" and at the same time wrong for Sam to say "Priya knows, so I'll go ahead" — and it seems fine for Sam to say "she doesn't know" while Priya's evidence never changed. My instinct is that this is where the two views genuinely come apart rather than just redescribing the same data, and that it puts pressure on contextualism because the knowledge claim and the action it's supposed to license belong to different people. But tell me where in the sequence it belongs: up front as the puzzle that forces the distinction, at the end as the test of whether anyone got it, or nowhere because it's really a problem about action-guiding and not about knowledge at all.

Then the misconceptions. The ones I expect are: relativism in the vulgar sense ("so anything goes"); the two views being collapsed into one, with people unable to say what would differ between them; the evidentialist misreading where high stakes are heard as "you should go check again," i.e. as a change in what evidence you ought to have rather than in what your evidence has to do for you; and the one-directional worry that if high stakes can destroy knowledge then trivially low stakes must manufacture it, which I think someone will raise and I don't want to hand-wave. Tell me which of these come from the framing I choose versus which come from the views themselves, and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off — before it forms if that's possible, and how. Also: what should I openly refuse to explain at this level (I'm thinking the semantic-vs-pragmatic question about whether "knows" is really context-sensitive or whether the effect is conversational gets one sentence and no more, but tell me if something else is the thing to sacrifice). Give me one question to end on that would show me they've got the distinction rather than the vocabulary — I was thinking something like asking them to build a case where the two views disagree, but tell me if there's a better check.
