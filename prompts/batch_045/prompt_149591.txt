**Working title: "One bit of memory"**

**0. The setup.** Two intersections that look exactly alike. Turning at the first gets you 0, turning at the second gets you 4, and missing both drops you at a motel for 1. You remember the plan. You can't tell the intersections apart, and at an intersection you don't remember being at an intersection before or what you decided.

**1.** The best plan is to go straight with probability 2/3 at each intersection, worth (1/3)(0) + (2/3)(1/3)(4) + (2/3)(2/3)(1) = 8/9 + 4/9 = 4/3. I'll assume this is uncontested.

**2. What I think is actually puzzling.** Suppose you're at an intersection and you take yourself to be executing the 2/3 plan. Your posterior that this is the *first* intersection is 1/(1 + 2/3) = 3/5, since you reach the first for sure and the second only with probability 2/3. Now price the two pure actions against that posterior. Exiting: (3/5)(0) + (2/5)(4) = 8/5. Continuing: (3/5) times the value of being at the second intersection while still planning to go straight with probability 2/3, plus (2/5) times 1 — that's (3/5)[(1/3)(4) + (2/3)(1)] + (2/5)(1) = (3/5)(2) + 2/5 = 8/5. Equal. Which is what a mixed plan *should* look like from the inside: if you're randomizing on purpose, you're indifferent.

So the standard "the driver gets to the intersection and wants to defect from his own plan" framing needs the posterior to be held fixed while the plan is varied. Keep it plan-generated and there's nothing to defect *to*. My claim: the interesting pathology isn't akrasia or a clash between a planning self and an acting self at all. It's that the expected payoff at the information set (8/5) exceeds the ex ante value of the very plan you know you're running (4/3), even though you're certain to arrive at the first intersection. The books don't add up. That's a failure of amalgamation, not of resolve, and it means the resolute/sophisticated axis is the wrong thing to be arguing about.

**3. The pebble.** Before setting off, put a pebble in the cup holder — one bit of state you can see and change. Plan: at each intersection, look at the cup holder. Empty means move the pebble in and go straight; occupied means turn off. With a reliable reading this gets you 4 with certainty. One external bit converts imperfect recall into perfect recall.

Now make the reading unreliable: independently at each intersection, with probability ε you see the opposite of the true state. Run the plan above. At the first intersection you misread with probability ε and turn off immediately, for 0. Otherwise you leave the pebble in and go straight; at the second you read correctly with probability 1 − ε and turn off for 4, or misread again and drive past for 1. That gives (1 − ε)[(1 − ε)4 + ε] + ε·0 = 4 − 7ε + 3ε². This beats the plain 4/3 plan up to about ε ≈ 0.48 (solve 3ε² − 7ε + 8/3 = 0), then falls off a cliff toward 0. I haven't checked whether some sneakier pebble plan — e.g. one that goes straight on reading "occupied" instead of turning — dominates both the pebble plan and the 2/3 plan in the middle range, and I suspect it does.

The bit I actually care about: the pebble's message is a message from your earlier self, and decoding it requires assuming your earlier self did what the plan said. That's the same assumption whose failure generates the original problem. So the pebble doesn't dissolve the paradox, it relocates it one level down, into past conformity — and with ε > 0 you get a dial between imperfect recall and mere imperfect information. Question I can't answer: is there a clean way to say at what ε the "why don't I just reconsider" argument starts biting again, or is that just a continuum of ordinary filtering problems with nothing philosophical happening anywhere along it?

**4. Objection I want to head off.** Someone says: the pebble is just more actions, so you've changed the game and of course the answer changed. My reply: the strategy space in the original problem already permits randomization at each intersection, so extra actions buy nothing; what the pebble adds is a variable that carries information across nodes, which is a change in recall structure rather than in options. Is that reply any good?

**5. Loose end.** If the numbers in section 2 are right, is the moral that we should stop thinking there's a single expected payoff that attaches to a person at a time? Or is 8/5 just a number you're not allowed to compute, and the whole puzzle is an artifact of computing it?
