I'm the sixth-form representative on the Youth Advisory Panel at the small independent museum in my town, and I sit in on its Exhibitions Committee when it discusses the new industrial gallery. At our meeting on the 14th we have to sign off the copy for a single display panel, on the collapse of the town's hand-loom weaving between 1816 and 1819: wages roughly halved, the workhouse filled, and a typhus outbreak in the winter of 1817 killed somewhere in the region of forty people. The panel has to be with the printers by the end of that week if the gallery is to open in March, so what we decide on the 14th is more or less final.

The committee is split, and I don't think the split is really about tone. Our curator, who wrote the draft, wants to headline the eruption of Mount Tambora in April 1815 and the cold, wet summer that followed it, together with the typhus, and her text writes about the volcano and the fever as though they did things to the town — nature striking first, the fever moving through the workhouse. One of our trustees, a retired history lecturer, says this is environmental determinism dressed up for visitors: it personifies a bacterium and a mountain, and it quietly lets Parliament, the landowners and the Board of Guardians off the hook for the relief decisions that turned a failed harvest into a mortality crisis. I don't want to split the difference by softening a few adjectives, because it seems to me the argument is about what we are doing when we call something a cause at all.

I've been turning over a criterion in my own reading and I'd like the memo to use it rather than dodge it. Call it the mediation test: a natural factor may be written up in agentive language only where (i) it made a real difference to what the human agents could reasonably have done — the cold summer did close off options that were open in 1814 — and (ii) its effects were not wholly routed through some institution that could have acted otherwise. On that test the failed harvest probably passes, but the death toll probably doesn't, since how many people died of the fever seems to have depended on whether the Guardians gave outdoor relief or packed families into the workhouse, which is a decision rather than a weather event. But I can also see the objection that the test is too convenient: it will always tend to push the moral weight back onto whichever human body we happen to be cross with, and it lets us describe the very same event as an act of nature when we feel generous and an act of policy when we don't. My EPQ supervisor made much this point and I haven't got an answer to it.

Could you draft the memorandum I'll circulate to the Exhibitions Committee before the 14th — addressed to the committee, from me, 700 to 900 words? It should (a) recommend one concrete course of action, and say plainly which: approve the copy as drafted, approve subject to named revisions with a deadline for the curator to sign them off before printing, or send it back for a full redraft; (b) give the reasoning, working the mediation test — or a better criterion, if you think mine is wrong, in which case say so and say why — into a test the committee could actually apply to this panel and to later ones; and (c) treat the strongest argument on whichever side you reject as strongly as you can, since I'd rather the curator or the trustee felt answered than flattered. Please don't pad it with generalities about balanced interpretation or multiple perspectives; the committee has heard all of that already. I'm seventeen and doing History, Economics and English A-levels, so use plain, formal British English and gloss any term of art you rely on.
