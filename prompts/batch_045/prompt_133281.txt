I have been a working illustrator for about fifteen years, and in the last two most of my commercial work has been made with a text-to-image model somewhere in the loop. A client has now asked me to label what I make, and I have to decide what I am willing to sign. That has made a question personal that I used to treat as academic, and it has made me notice a pattern I cannot name.

The pattern is that whether a piece counts as *mine* seems to depend on the grain at which its making is described — and the verdict does not appear to be tracking anything in the finished image, or, so far as I can tell, anything in the causal history either.

Two instances. First, one afternoon of my own. Described coarsely: I typed a sentence and kept one of four pictures that came back. Described finely: about sixty prompts, three abandoned compositions, a hand repainted, a background composited from a second run, a mask drawn by hand, half an hour of colour work. Same afternoon, same file. At the coarse grain colleagues say the object was ordered rather than made; at the fine grain they say it was made — and I have heard both said about the same file in the same conversation.

Second, the flip does not need a model. A street photographer's picture: described as "he pressed the button at the right moment", it sounds lucky; described as a decade of walking and looking and knowing where to stand, it sounds authored. And it runs the other way too: a wholly hand-painted picture described at a fine mechanical grain — the brush went where the shoulder took it — begins to sound unauthored as well.

I have set aside two things I first reached for. The intentional fallacy, as I understand it, concerns what a work means, not who made it; and the sorites worry about how many steps make an author is not my worry, since the same steps change their look depending only on how they are told. I also suspect the answer is not about effort, since effort-based arguments have never moved me.

So: is this an established phenomenon, and does it have a name? I do not want a bare label. I would like an account of what the verdict is actually tracking — my own guess, which I would like tested, is that it tracks something like who can answer for the object rather than who caused it — and of where the effect stops: whether it is specific to authorship, or shows up for any "did an agent do this" verdict such as credit in a collaboration or blame for a mistake, and whether there is some grain of description at which judgements stop moving. If nothing established fits, please coin a term, define it, and say which nearby cases it excludes. Please answer in British English.
