I write fiction, and for the past four years I have served as one of seven trustees of the Verrall Trust, a private foundation that makes a handful of large, unrestricted grants in fundamental science. Our charter points us toward work that no conventional funder will touch because its payoff is distant, speculative, or embarrassing to describe.

The proposal in front of us is called Empirical Constraints on Simulated Cosmologies, submitted by a consortium of theoretical physicists, for roughly $9 million over five years. It has two work packages. The first is archival and cheap: mine existing data for signatures that the world is computed rather than basic — a lattice-like discreteness in the arrival directions of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays, a preferred-frame anisotropy that would betray cubic structure in spacetime, and what they call a resource ceiling, a level of complexity in many-body quantum systems past which the world's behaviour stops matching any continuous model. The second is the one that has divided us. Using beam time that is already scheduled at an existing heavy-ion collider, they want to tune a series of collisions so as to maximize the computational cost of simulating the collision region. Their reasoning is that a simulation running on finite hardware must have a resolution and complexity budget, and that a load engineered to exceed that budget must produce one of two observable outcomes: visible degradation, in the form of artifacts, or an intervention by whoever is running the simulation. They intend, deliberately, to make the simulation notice us. The incremental cost of this is small, which is why several trustees are tempted to let it ride along with the rest.

The objections are the ones you would expect: that the worst case is everyone dying and that no one elected this consortium to run that risk on the rest of us, the comparison being to the dispute about whether anyone should broadcast messages to the stars before humanity has agreed to it; and, from two trustees, that the whole programme is unfalsifiable anyway, since any artifact could simply be a law of this world and simulators could fabricate the absence of artifacts, so a negative result buys us nothing. The applicants rebut both, and one of their rebuttals strikes me as the most interesting sentence in the document: if simulators routinely shut down a simulation once its inhabitants began straining the resource budget, then the fact that we have already built colliders, thermonuclear weapons and large quantum computers without being switched off is itself evidence that no such intervention policy governs this world, and the provocation run therefore adds no risk that the last eighty years of physics have not already run. One trustee calls this the best argument in the proposal; another says it is a selection effect of precisely the kind that makes arguments like it untrustworthy.

Our chair has asked me to draft the board's recommendation memo, on the grounds that the two scientific advisers' notes are, in her phrase, unreadable to the rest of us, and that I am the trustee who has spent the most time thinking about what it would mean to be a character in somebody else's story. I should also declare an interest: my last novel was set inside a simulated city, and part of me very much wants them to switch the machine on. So I need the memo to be written so that a trustee who wants the opposite outcome could still use it.

Please draft it: about 1,200 words, addressed to the trustees of the Verrall Trust, suitable for circulation at our next meeting, that

- gives a bottom line in the first paragraph — fund both packages, fund the archival package subject to conditions, fund the archival package only, or decline outright — in a form the board could vote on by a show of hands;
- explains, for trustees who are not physicists, what the simulation argument actually establishes and what it does not: the three-horn conclusion about extinction, ancestor-simulations, and ourselves, the roles of substrate-independence and of an indifference assumption about which observers we should take ourselves to be, and why the argument is not by itself a reason to think we are simulated;
- assesses the provocation run's we-would-already-be-dead rebuttal fairly, saying what it gets right and where, if anywhere, it breaks;
- addresses the consent problem and says whether the analogy with broadcasting to the stars actually holds;
- sets out conditions or a timetable the researchers could genuinely respond to — a deferred decision on the provocation run pending results from the archival work, external review, some form of wider consultation — rather than generalities about oversight;
- and states the strongest consideration against its own recommendation in a paragraph an opponent could sign.

If the honest answer is that the decision turns on something the board does not yet know, say what that is and how we could find it out, and then recommend what to do in the meantime.
