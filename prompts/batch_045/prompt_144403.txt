Hey, I'm taking a decision theory / philosophy of action seminar this term and our study group has been stuck on Parfit's hitchhiker for like five weeks without anyone moving, and I want to know why.

Setup, in case we use different words for things: you're stranded in the desert, a driver will take you to town iff he's confident you'll pay him $1000 once you get there, and he's good at reading whether you're that kind of person. Once you're in town, paying costs you a grand and does nothing for you.

Our group split three ways immediately and has stayed there. Maya: in town, not paying dominates, so paying would be irrational; the only real question is the earlier one about whether to bind yourself or become the paying type, and that's a separate diachronic question about self-management (she had a name for the view, something like constrained maximization or resolute choice). Dev: the driver is really predicting what your decision procedure outputs in that situation, so the thing to assess is the procedure rather than the act, and the procedure that pays does better ex ante, so pay. Sam: the case is a rigged intuition pump — an impossible predictor, a one-shot with near-perfect correlation — and no theory should be built around handling it, so the only legitimate question is the boring ex ante one about dispositions.

I tried to break the tie by inventing a variant, which made things worse. In mine the driver doesn't read your character or your procedure; he reads your own self-prediction. Before he decides you have to state how likely you think it is that you'll pay in town, and he drives you iff that number is high. You know yourself well and you know you know yourself well. So the thing being scanned is fixed by what you'll actually do, and you can't make it true by deciding. Now Dev's move seems to have nothing to grip on, but he says I've changed the subject, and Maya says that's the point: the whole what's-the-object-of-evaluation move was smoke to begin with.

My hypothesis is that we're not disagreeing about what to do at all. We're fighting about (i) which thing rational assessment even applies to — the act, the disposition, the procedure, the self-model — and each of us silently picked a different one; (ii) which cases are allowed to count as data, i.e. whether a case with a fallible-but-very-good predictor is a real decision problem or cheating; and (iii) buried under both, what rationality is *for* — getting the most for the agent, or being the rule you'd want to be running from the outside, or winning across a whole class of problems. And then the rational-irrationality half of the topic, whether it can be rational to make yourself irrational or to get yourself to believe something for bad reasons, is just (i) and (iii) with belief swapped in for action.

Is that the right shape, or is there an actual substantive disagreement buried under it — a formal one, like whether some dominance or tournament-style result settles which rule wins, or a factual one about what agents can control? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a tour of the standard theories, and an honest answer about what would count as progress here: a theorem, a distinction, a different question — or nothing, and why.
