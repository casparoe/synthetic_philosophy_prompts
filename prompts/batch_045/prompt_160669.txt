I'm retired — infrared spectroscopy mostly, then some atmospheric remote sensing at the end — and a few of us from the university retirees' association run a little reading group that got onto string theory and the multiverse after one of our number gave a talk on it. Half of us think the whole framework is unfalsifiable nonsense dressed up as physics, the other half think there's something to the claim that a theory can get support from things other than prediction, and we keep talking past each other because we're all using different words for what we think is going on. So I want a decision tree to sort us out.

Where we all start: we agree that no direct empirical test of the landscape / multiverse framework is likely in anybody's lifetime, we agree the mathematics is genuinely impressive, and we agree that "it's elegant" on its own proves nothing. The disagreement is entirely about what else, if anything, can legitimately move a credence here.

What I'd like you to build:

Start from the logical space, not from a list of names. Give me the separating questions first — the ones whose answer-combinations carve out the room — and only then drop the actual positions into the cells they occupy. Every branch node should be phrased as a question one of us could answer without having any idea which position it leads to: a concrete case, not "do you think string theory is science?"

Please use these cases somewhere in the tree, and for each one ask two things, because I suspect some of us come apart only on the second: (1) does this raise, lower, or leave unchanged your confidence that the framework is on the right track, and (2) would you vote to put money and postdocs into it.

- A theory T was built to solve one specific problem P. Decades of attempts by good people have produced no rival that solves P. T still makes no novel testable prediction. Now three variants: nobody produced a rival because the field's attention moved elsewhere; because every attempt at a rival turns out to secretly reduce to T; because the rivals keep dying of technical problems nobody saw coming. Does the answer change across the variants?
- T was constructed to explain anomaly X. Later somebody notices that T also explains Y, which nobody was trying to explain and which is otherwise puzzling. Then: the explanation of Y only works after a free parameter is set; and separately, T also has a tidy story for why we don't see Z, which by symmetry we'd have expected.
- T's only empirical consequence is that the low-energy world should look exactly like what we already measure, and its new physics sits at a scale we will never build a machine for. A harsher version: T predicts that we will see nothing new no matter how far we push.
- T implies a vast ensemble of local worlds with different parameter values, and what we should expect to observe depends on a measure over that ensemble which nobody can fix — pick your measure and you can get opposite expectations.

Leaves I want present, under whatever standard label you think fits, or marked 'unnamed': the falsificationist/demarcation skeptic who says there's nothing here to have a credence about; the Bayesian who says the likelihood is undefined so no updating can occur; the view that no alternatives is itself evidence; the view that unexpected explanatory coherence is evidence (keep these two as distinct leaves if the cases really separate them — tell me if they don't); the objection that a theory which accommodates every possible observation predicts nothing; the position that the measure problem means no probabilities are assignable at all; our mathematician's position that a framework isn't the kind of thing with a truth value, it's chosen rather than believed; and the position that all of this is really about research bets, not belief. Somewhere in there I also want the distinction between "T is probably true" and "T is probably what our successors will still be using" made to do some work, because I think that's where half our arguments actually live.

Say which answer-combinations are unoccupied and whether that's because they're incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any two leaves your tree genuinely can't separate instead of pretending it can. Roughly seven to nine branch questions and about ten leaves, and if you can, add a five-question short version with a scoring key that we can all take before the next meeting so we can see where we land.

No citations or reading lists please — I've read the encyclopedia entries and they're exactly what we're stuck on. Standard names for positions are fine, who said what isn't.
