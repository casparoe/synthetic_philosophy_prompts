I've been haunting the rationalist corners of the internet for the better part of fifteen years — Overcoming Bias back in the day, LessWrong and the decision-theory threads ever since, all the Newcomb and Sleeping Beauty and updatelessness material — and I've finally run into something there I can't dissolve on my own, so I'm bringing it to you. It started from the absent-minded driver setup, which I'll restate in my own numbers so we aren't arguing about a half-remembered paper. A driver is heading home along a highway with two identical-looking intersections; exiting at the first puts him in a bad neighbourhood (0), exiting at the second gets him home (4), missing both leaves him at a motel (1). He can't tell the intersections apart, so the only plans he can execute are of the form "whenever I find myself at an intersection, continue with probability p", using a device that re-randomizes freshly each time rather than a single coin flip before departure — a single flip only gets him 1, because the only pure plans he can name without being able to identify a node are "exit at every intersection" (0) and "continue at every intersection" (1), and no mixture of those beats 1. (I'm setting aside gadgets with hidden internal state, which just restore recall and collapse the case.) Working ex ante, 4p(1−p) + p² = 4p − 3p² peaks at p = 2/3 and is worth 4/3, and I'm confident that is the ceiling over everything he can actually implement.

Here is the argument I can't get out of. (1) A plan is rationally permissible only if, at every point where it might call on you to act, doing what it says maximizes expected utility by the lights of what you know then — the ordinary dynamic-consistency or sequential-rationality condition, the same one that licenses two-boxing in Newcomb problems. (2) If he is running p = 2/3 and finds himself at an intersection, his expected numbers of visits to the first and second intersections stand in the ratio 1 : 2/3, so his credence that he is at the first is 3/5 and at the second 2/5; this is the same visit-weighting that gives thirders their answer in Sleeping Beauty, and I know halfers would say 1/2. (3) Given those credences, picking a fresh continuation probability p′ for the rest of the drive is worth (3/5)(4p′ − 3p′²) + (2/5)(4 − 3p′), which peaks at p′ = 1/3 and is worth 9/5 — strictly more than sticking with the old plan, which by the same formula is worth 8/5. (4) The halfer credence makes it worse rather than better: at 1/2 the best continuation is p′ = 1/6, worth 49/24, nearly 2.04. (5) But 9/5 exceeds 4/3, and 4/3 is the most any plan he could have adopted before leaving was worth; re-deliberating at a moment of action cannot manufacture expected value that no ex-ante plan attains. (6) Nothing in the construction depends on the details: take any sequential decision problem, blind the agent as to which node he's at, have him re-derive a self-locating credence and re-optimize, and step (3) hands him more than the ex-ante ceiling, so uncertainty about where you are in your own plan is not a handicap but a source of surplus, and the surplus gets bigger the more confused you are — this in the very same problem where a device that simply tells him which intersection he's at is worth 8/3 to him, and I can't see how being confused and being informed can both pay. (7) So one of (1) through (3) is false.

I want to know which step you'd reject, why it looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs me — and if you'd reject (1), what replaces dynamic consistency for an agent with imperfect recall, since "evaluate policies rather than acts" sounds like the whole answer and I suspect it isn't. Two things I'd particularly like addressed: what the 9/5 is even a measure of, since I have a strong feeling it isn't the expected payoff of anything, and whether my re-randomizing device is smuggling something a single pre-departure flip couldn't. For what it's worth, one person in the thread says the fix is to require the plan and the credence to be mutually consistent, and when I solve that fixed point I get p = 4/9 with a credence of 9/13, worth 32/27 — about 0.15 below 4/3 — so buying consistency costs real money and I don't see why I should pay it. I'd rather keep (1) and the ex-ante arithmetic than (2) or (3), but follow the argument where it goes.
