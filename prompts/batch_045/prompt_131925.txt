I work on the deferral layer of a clinical triage tool — the part that decides, case by case, whether to answer or to hand the case to a human — and for about a year my closest collaborator and I have been arguing about it in epistemological vocabulary we can't seem to put down. Stripped of the engineering: she holds the KK principle (if a subject knows that p, then it is in a position to know that it knows that p) and, more strongly, that at least some non-trivial conditions are luminous, in the sense that whenever such a condition obtains the subject is in a position to know that it obtains. I hold the margin-for-error view: pick any candidate luminous condition, run a series of cases in which it fails and each of which differs from a case in which it obtains by less than the margin that knowledge requires, and the luminosity claim collapses; no non-trivial condition is luminous and KK falls with it. She is presenting the positive case to our safety review in six weeks and I am presenting the skeptical one, and we would rather arrive with a single document than with two decks that talk past each other.

Three things have made the argument stop being textbook, and I want them in the document. First, her best recent move is that my case series needs a state space with no smallest gap, and ours does not have one: every evidence variable the system sees takes one of finitely many discrete values, so for any condition there is a last case in which it obtains and a first case in which it fails, separated by one least step of the grain, with no case in between for a margin to exploit. She infers that luminosity is a thesis about grainy subjects and that a purely qualitative margin argument proves nothing here; I reply that the argument only becomes quantitative — turning on the subject's accuracy at grain distance, on where the grain happens to fall relative to the boundary, and on whether a friend of luminosity is entitled to lean on a grain whose placement is arbitrary with respect to anything the subject can tell. Second, the principle I actually need for deferral is the negative one: when the subject does not know that p, it is in a position to know that it does not. That is not what our year of argument was about, and we disagree about whether it stands or falls with the positive thesis, and about whether these principles should be stated dispositionally (in a position to know) or actualized (if the subject actually reflects, without acquiring new evidence, it can then know). Third, we are unsure whether denying luminosity really yields a denial of KK, or whether KK is better read as a coherence constraint on a body of knowledge than as a claim about the luminosity of the condition knowing-that-p — and I suspect at least one thread of the dispute is about words rather than about the world.

Please draft the joint statement we could both sign: a numbered section of claims we already accept — about what luminosity and KK say, about what the margin-for-error reasoning establishes if its premises hold, about what our finite-precision setup does and does not supply; then a disagreements table in which each of us would accept the wording of her own column; then the cruxes, stated so that settling one would move at least one of us. For each crux give a case or argument we would both grant as bearing on it, and write down in advance what each of us predicts about it: I want at least one crux that is empirically checkable on the system itself, concerning what each of us expects the reliability and calibration behaviour near the decision boundary to look like as we vary the precision of the evidence variable, and at least one that is settled from the armchair. Include the negative-luminosity and actualized-versus-dispositional formulations as their own rows rather than folding them into KK, and if you conclude that some part of the dispute is verbal, say plainly which part and why — but do not buy agreement at the price of a formulation so thin that neither of us would quote it in our own slides.
