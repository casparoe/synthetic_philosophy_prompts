I teach an upper-level seminar in early modern moral philosophy, and I close my unit on Émilie du Châtelet with a speculative session that I would like to make considerably more rigorous than it has been in past years: what, exactly, would she have said about the happiness-technologies of a world that appeared after her death in 1749? I would like you to do the reconstruction, and I want a determinate prediction with graded confidence for each case, not a survey of possibilities or a reminder that counterfactuals about dead authors are uncertain.

The relevant text is her Discours sur le bonheur, the short, semi-autobiographical treatise on the sources of human happiness that she wrote in the 1740s and which appeared only after she died. I will give you my working reconstruction of the position; please correct it where you think it is wrong, but do not quote the text back to me and do not cite or gesture at secondary literature. I have all of that in front of me. What I want from you is the argument.

My reading, in four strands, as I present it to students:

1. Happiness is a condition of a whole person over time, not a momentary sensation, and it presupposes a native capacity or temperament — a disposition to be moved and to take an interest — which is unequally distributed, not fully under the agent's control, and largely fixed early. Someone without it cannot be made happy by any arrangement of externals.
2. That capacity needs objects. Happiness requires passions and tastes that give a person something to be engaged by, and she ranks study and the love of glory especially high because they are inexhaustible, independent of other people's favor, and available in solitude.
3. It requires freedom from prejudice, which in her usage covers both intellectual error and socially inculcated false valuation; prejudice distorts judgment and chains people to goods that cannot deliver what they promise.
4. She grants illusion a real, unsettling role — being deceived is sometimes part of what makes people happy — while also holding that a happiness resting on false beliefs is fragile, degrading, or in some way not the real thing. I have never been able to make strands 3 and 4 sit comfortably together, and I suspect the tension is genuine in the text.

Two stipulative labels I use in seminar: I call interventions *supply-side* when they furnish new objects for the passions, and *capacity-side* when they alter the underlying disposition that makes objects able to move us at all. Du Châtelet, on my reading, thinks capacity is prior and supplies little: no abundance of objects helps the person who cannot be moved, while the person who can be moved needs surprisingly few.

Now the cases. Each is a technology or institution from long after 1749; assume in each that it works more or less as advertised, so that the question is not empirical.

A. A daily medication that raises the baseline of mood by a fixed amount, taken indefinitely. It produces no euphoria, nomania, and no blunting: the taker still grieves a real loss, still forms accurate beliefs about her lover's infidelity and feels badly about it in proportion, but recovers faster and starts from a higher floor.

B. An engagement-optimized feed: an unending stream of novel, mildly pleasant, very low-cost content, tuned to hold attention. The user spends three hours a day with it, reports enjoyment while using it, and does not report enjoyment when asked about it afterward.

C. An artificial companion: an interlocutor that is always available, unfailingly attentive, warm, agreeable, never bored, never hostile, never needy; it knows it is not a person and says so plainly when asked. Some users form attachments to it that they describe as love and that structure their days.

D. A state that administers a quarterly subjective-well-being survey and is legally required to shift budgetary allocations between departments toward whichever uses of money produce measured gains in reported well-being.

For each: what would du Châtelet have said, and why? Where would she have placed it relative to the others — welcomed, tolerated with suspicion, or diagnosed as a counterfeit? Which of my four strands is doing the load-bearing work in her answer, and is the intervention working on the supply side, the capacity side, or, in the awkward cases, on a side she has no vocabulary for? Most importantly: in which cases, if any, would she have said that the thing on offer is not happiness at all but something wearing happiness's clothes, and what exactly would her test for that be — I take it that her test is not simply felt pleasantness?

Please also address, at the end, one cross-cutting question: which single commitment of hers is doing the most work across all four cases, and what one further fact about the modern world, if she were given it, would most change her answers? If you think one of the four cases is one her text genuinely cannot reach, say so for that case and mark it as indeterminate rather than refusing the whole exercise.

Format. Two parts. First, connected prose of roughly 700 to 900 words: a short section per case (A through D) and a closing section on the cross-cutting question. Second, following the prose, a single JSON object — no commentary between the prose and the object — with this exact schema:

- a key summary_corrections whose value is an array of objects, one for each strand of my reading you would revise or reject, each with the fields strand_number (integer 1 to 4), verdict (one of accurate, distorted, anachronistic, unsupported), and note (one or two sentences).
- a key items whose value is an array of four objects, one per case, each with the fields case_id (one of A, B, C, D), predicted_stance (a single sentence, at most 40 words), confidence (one of high, moderate, low, indeterminate), load_bearing_strands (array of integers naming the strands from my summary that do the work, or an empty array if none do), sidedness (one of supply, capacity, both, neither, no_vocabulary), counterfeit_verdict (one of genuine, counterfeit, mixed, unclear), counterfeit_test (one sentence stating the test you are attributing to her), framework_strain (one sentence on where her resources run out), anachronism_risk (one sentence naming the most likely way this reconstruction cheats by importing a modern assumption), and verdict_short (at most 25 words).
- a key cross_cutting whose value is an object with the fields most_load_bearing_commitment (one sentence), most_consequential_new_fact (one sentence), and ranked_by_difficulty (array of the four case_ids ordered from the case her text constrains least to the case it constrains most).

Please write in American English.
