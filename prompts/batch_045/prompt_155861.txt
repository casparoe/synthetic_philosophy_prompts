I am in the lower sixth, and I attend a school philosophy society that is mostly run by upper-sixth students. Last week we were discussing whether a highly sophisticated AI system could ever be conscious. I suggested that we have no way of telling what, if anything, it is like for such a system, and that this uncertainty is itself a reason for caution. One of the older students replied that consciousness is an illusion, and treated this as disposing of my point entirely: if there is no such thing as what it is like for a subject, then there is no open question about the system either, and nothing for me to be cautious about.

Since then I have read a little about the position behind that slogan. As I understand it, the claim is that there are no phenomenal properties — no properties of the sort people gesture at when they ask what an experience is like — and that what really exists are functional, access-related or representational states, together with introspective mechanisms that misdescribe those states as possessing phenomenal qualities. Please tell me if that is a caricature before you build anything on it.

What I want is the slogan broken into a menu of precise claims, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical, with a verdict on each, rather than a single reading explained at length. Along the way I would like a few distinctions handled properly instead of glossed:

1. What kind of illusion is meant: something like a perceptual illusion, where a state seems to have a quality it lacks; a mere false belief; a case of a term failing to refer; or the stronger claim that the concept is incoherent and the whole discourse is a useful fiction?

2. The self-undermining worry. If the illusion consists in things seeming a certain way to me, and seeming is itself phenomenal, then the view appears to reintroduce exactly what it denies. I would like to know whether the illusionist has a stable answer to this, and whether the answer depends on cashing out "seems" in functional terms.

3. A distinction I want tested rather than assumed: one version says the introspective appearance is explained away and there is nothing that appears; another says the subject's own self-monitoring system issues a false representation and that representation is the target of the denial. Does the second version really differ from a representationalist view on which the phenomenal property just is the content misdescribed, or does it quietly collapse into a rival it was supposed to oppose?

4. Whether some readings are really proposals about how we should talk — verbal or quietist ones — rather than substantive metaphysical claims, and how one would tell the difference.

5. Finally, the payoff: which readings would actually do the work my opponent needed, i.e. dissolve my caution? Please separate readings on which the question disappears outright from readings on which it is merely rewritten as a question about the system's functional organisation, and say plainly if on the latter my worry survives in a different form. If none of the readings does the job, say so.

Please write in British English, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. I would find a menu with a short status verdict on each entry easiest to take back to the society.
