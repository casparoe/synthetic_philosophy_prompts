I direct the editorial team for a high-school world history program, and we are in the middle of a full revision. My team runs into one problem several hundred times per volume: passages in which something non-human does the acting — an epidemic, a drought, a volcanic winter, a shift in the monsoon, a glacier, a shallow harbor, a vein of ore. Our house style currently has one blunt rule for these, which is to put the people back in: no sentence should make a pathogen or a weather system the grammatical subject of a historical claim. My copy editors and my subject-matter reviewers have told me, independently, that the rule now does as much harm as good. It produces flat, evasive prose, and it sometimes deletes a causal claim that is true, load-bearing, and the entire point of the paragraph. I agree, and I want to replace the rule with something my editors can actually run.

Please write me that procedure. It has to work on a single sentence or short passage, in about ten minutes, run by a copy editor with no training in philosophy or historiography, and its output has to be a specific edited sentence plus a verdict from a small fixed set — leave as is; leave with a required mediating clause added; rewrite with the human decision-makers restored; delete the causal claim; escalate to a subject-matter editor. Give me the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and what does not; tell me where the procedure stops with a no; and mark the points where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values, pedagogy, or politics rather than fact.

Three things the procedure has to get right. First, it must catch the drift from "X was among the causes" to "X made the outcome inevitable" — geography as destiny — including the cases where that drift carries a political charge, because attributing a region's poverty to its rainfall or its disease ecology rather than to a century of extraction is not a stylistic slip. Second, it must catch the mirror-image failure: rewriting to restore human agents in a way that credits officials with a decision nobody made, a foresight nobody had, or an option nobody had available. Third, it has to distinguish a trigger from a background vulnerability, and say what to do when a sentence collapses the two.

We already use two informal heuristics, and I want you to keep them if they survive scrutiny, replace them if they do not, and tell me which you did and why. One we call the restore-the-decision-makers test: if you cannot rewrite the sentence with human agents as the subject without losing something, then the non-human factor is doing causal work and must not simply be deleted. The other we call the matched-case test: find a case that shares the non-human factor but has a different outcome; if you can, the factor was not sufficient, and the sentence may not imply that it was.

Then apply the procedure to three passages from our current draft. Treat the empirical claims in each as checked and accurate; the only question is the causal framing.

One. "Smallpox reached the valley before the soldiers did. Within a year it had killed the ruler and both his heirs, and the confederation that might have held the mountain pass no longer existed."

Two. "The eruption took the harvest. By spring the price of rye had tripled; by midsummer the city had a new government."

Three. This one is our sample unit opener, it appears in the materials we show adoption committees, and it tests very well with teachers. I would rather not lose it, so if your procedure says it cannot stand, say so plainly and give me the replacement sentence. "The cold came and did not leave. The harvests failed eleven years running, the granaries emptied, and a dynasty that had held the throne for two centuries simply stopped."

Finally, include a bank of five short test sentences my copy editors can use to check that they are applying the procedure the same way I am, and close with an honest statement of what the procedure cannot do — where it will misfire, which disputes it cannot settle, and which tradeoffs, in particular the tradeoff between vividness and precision at a ninth-grade reading level, remain judgment calls that no checklist will eliminate.
