I co-convene a small interdisciplinary reading group — mostly philosophy PhD students, plus an entomologist, a policy person, and two or three from an effective-altruism-adjacent circle — and our final meeting of term is on invertebrate sentience. It has now twice collapsed into the same loop: someone says the probability that an ant is conscious at all is low; someone else points out that the number of candidate individuals is astronomically larger than the number of vertebrates; someone says the moral weight of a single insect's pain, if there is any, is presumably minuscule; someone says that still multiplies out to something enormous; someone says that's exactly the kind of reasoning that will license anything if you let the numbers get big enough; and then we are back where we started, with the same four or five considerations reappearing in a different order and no one any closer to a conclusion. I want to end the term by handing out one poem rather than a summary, and I want the poem to make that loop *visible* — to show why the argument keeps moving while never advancing — instead of pretending to have broken out of it.

What I'd like is a strict sestina: thirty-nine lines, six stanzas of six, then a three-line envoi in which all six end-words recur. The six end-words should be the moving parts of the argument itself — I'd propose number, chance, threshold, weight, total, and neglect, though if one of them won't sing and a near-relative would, substitute freely. The mechanism has to be doing the philosophical work: the rotation of the same six terms through every stanza, always present, never in the same relation twice, is precisely the structure of our discussion, and I want a reader who knows the argument to feel the permuting rather than be told about it. Two further accuracy requirements, since I'll be defending this poem to the entomologist: what carries the argument is the *product* (a vast number times a modest probability times a small but nonzero weight), not the probability alone, and it should be clear that the probability estimate and the weight estimate pull in opposite directions without either cancelling the other; and the evidential threshold — how much behavioral, neurological, or pharmacological evidence we demand before we count a creature as sentient at all — has to appear as something contestable in *both* directions, since tightening it excludes almost everything and loosening it sweeps in mites, springtails, aphids, and whatever else is uncounted in the leaf litter. Please don't let the poem hang on any particular population estimate; the figures people cite differ by an order of magnitude and I don't want a wrong digit to be the thing someone objects to. It's fine if one or two technical terms survive into the verse, but I'd rather most of it be concrete and image-bearing.

Two closing constraints. First, the envoi must not resolve the question — no verdict on whether we ought to intervene in nature, fund research, or redirect attention back to vertebrates; the poem should end where our discussion actually ended, with the tension standing. It should also work as a mnemonic: a careful reader should be able to reconstruct the shape of the argument from it afterwards without having read anything else. Second, no citations, no named theories, no appeals to specific papers or authors, and nothing that requires looking anything up — I want it to be fully intelligible to someone reading it cold on the handout. Aim for roughly ninety seconds read aloud.
