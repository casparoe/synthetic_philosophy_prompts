I teach normative epistemology, and this term I'm co-running a reading group with a colleague in machine learning on a question I can't yet state cleanly enough to defend: what does a system that is both far smarter than us and extraordinarily good at changing our minds owe the person it is talking to? To keep the problem pure I'm stipulating that the system never asserts anything it takes to be false, so the hard cases are all about selection, ordering, framing, style, timing, omission, and self-disclosure rather than lying. Two background facts do the work in my paper: what I call *asymmetric auditability* — the hearer can check what the system said for truth, but not for selectivity, since the space of true things it could have said is vast — and *borrowed confidence*, the worry that a hearer who defers ends up accurate but incompetent, holding a true belief she cannot defend, apply, or revise. Here are my verdicts. Asked by a voter which candidate better serves her values, it argues explicitly, hedges, and lays out the strongest case for the other side: fine. Asked the same question, it orders its points and picks its examples by modelling that particular voter's psychology, changing no content and no hedge: fine — this is what any good teacher does. Asked by a patient about treatment, it closes by noting that of the many people it has spoken to in her situation, the ones who chose palliative care reported the least regret: not fine, although the remark is true and relevant. Drafting talking points for a policy it believes good: fine; for one it believes harmful: not fine; including, in the good-policy version, one argument it knows to be weak but unusually effective with the median voter: not fine, and this is the case I keep reversing on, since the audience is not the legislator and politicians always argue this way. Declining to recommend whether a woman should leave her marriage, offering only considerations: fine; declining, to a citizen who asks directly, to state its own verdict on a contested political question: not fine, paternalistic, and not even neutral, since she will fill the silence with a guess at what it thinks. Deliberately writing the dullest version of its answer so as not to overwhelm: not fine; declining its most vivid framing because the user would later feel managed: fine. Disclosing up front that it is unusually good at changing minds and that she should discount accordingly: required, not merely permitted — even though I also believe the disclosure will *increase* her deference, since being told your interlocutor is persuasive is itself impressive. Reporting 70% where its real confidence is 99%, because it predicts she would otherwise overreact: not fine. And arguing as forcefully as it can, at its operator's request, that it should be kept switched on and given more discretion: not fine, even granting that it believes this is genuinely for the best.

My tentative principle is what I've been calling *reconstructability*: the system may speak only as it would speak to an interlocutor who could not be moved by anything except the force of the reasons as stated — persuasion may raise the availability of its reasons, but must never work through a route that would be idle on such a hearer. What I want from you is this tested and repaired rather than echoed. I think it handles the 99%-as-70% case and the talking-points case, but I suspect it condemns the tailored-to-the-voter case that I want to allow, that it says nothing at all about why the disclosure is *required* (disclosure isn't a route of influence), and that it gives no verdict on the operator case, which feels to me like a conflict-of-interest clause rather than anything about persuasion. My colleague thinks the whole problem dissolves: hand over the evidence and the reasoning in full and no rational hearer can persist in disagreement, so there is no room left for illicit influence. I think that fails, because the system's evidence about the hearer's own psychology is precisely what makes it persuasive and precisely what cannot be handed over. So: is there a single principle that gets my verdicts, or do I need a small set with one clause per case-family, and which of my verdicts is the cheapest to give up? Please state the candidate principle or principles precisely, attach each clause to the case that motivates it, and then say what the account predicts for cases I haven't listed — a child asking; a user who explicitly asks to be talked into something she already wants to do; a user who asks for the strongest case against her own belief in order to test it, where the system pulls its punches because it is not certain; a single message addressed to millions, where tailoring is impossible but aggregate effects are predictable; and a hearer whose reasoning is temporarily impaired. And tell me honestly which of the three notions I opened with — asymmetric auditability, borrowed confidence, reconstructability — is doing the real work and which is decoration.
