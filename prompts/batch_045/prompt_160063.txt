I have been working through a chapter on rule-following in a popular introduction to Wittgenstein — one of those books that explains things clearly by compressing them — and I want to know how much of what it says I should keep. The chapter expounds the reading of Wittgenstein associated with Kripke's book on rules and private language. Its account runs, more or less, as follows:

"Kripke's sceptic shows that no fact about me settles what I mean by any expression. Suppose I have never computed with arguments larger than 57. Everything I have said and done with '+' is then compatible both with my having meant addition and with my having meant quus — the rule that agrees with addition whenever both arguments are smaller than 57 and delivers 5 otherwise. So nothing in my history fixes whether 125 or 5 is the correct answer to 68 + 57, and the argument generalises to every expression in the language. The natural reply is that meaning is a matter of my dispositions to answer; that reply fails twice over, because my dispositions are finite while the function is infinite, and because a disposition tells me what I will do, not what I ought to do. Kripke's own positive view, the 'sceptical solution', supplies the missing fact in social dress: what makes it the case that I mean addition is that my responses agree with those of my linguistic community, and it is that agreement — rather than any meaning-constituting fact about me — that makes attributions of meaning true. Two things are usually said to follow. First, meaning is essentially social, so a genuinely solitary rule-follower is impossible: with no community to agree or disagree with, there is nothing for his meaning to consist in. Second, the paradox is really Hume's problem of induction in a new costume; the sceptic is only reminding us that we have no guarantee that the future will resemble the past. Kripke himself does not conclude that nobody ever follows rules: he lets us keep our ordinary talk about meaning, at the price of conceding that such talk states no facts. But since the sceptic's argument can be run for any expression whatsoever, it is a global scepticism about meaning, and there is no answer to it — which is why the book is standardly read as showing that our attributions of meaning, taken at face value, are simply false."

Two parts of that struck me as wrong, though I cannot say why. The reduction of the puzzle to induction felt off, since the sceptic seems to be challenging something about what I meant in the past rather than about what will happen later; and the step from "no fact constitutes meaning" to "our meaning attributions are false" looked to me like a slide rather than an argument. I may be over-reading.

The chapter also draws a conclusion I care about, and I have since seen the same move made elsewhere. A post on a technical blog argued that the paradox settles a question about machine learning: a network is trained on finitely many input–output pairs, and infinitely many functions fit any finite training set, so there is no fact of the matter about which function a trained network implements; hence it is empty to say that a model is following the intended rule rather than a quus-like rule that agrees with it on the training distribution and diverges elsewhere, and the only defensible notion of alignment is a purely behavioural one. Separately, a commenter objected that the sceptical solution merely relocates the problem: if what fixes my meaning is my community's agreement, the very same sceptical argument can be run against the community's judgments, so the solution either provokes a regress or quietly helps itself to the sort of meaning-constituting fact it was meant to do without.

I would be grateful for an audit. Please take the chapter's account claim by claim and tell me what is right, what is a defensible simplification, what is subtly wrong, and what a defender of the sceptical solution would reject outright. In particular: what is the sceptical solution actually supposed to do, if it is not meant to supply a fact that constitutes meaning; how much sociality does the account really require, and does it rule out a solitary rule-follower — consider someone raised in isolation who has never computed with numbers above twenty but who would in fact answer 125 to 68 + 57 and would correct himself exactly as we would, and consider whether it matters that no community ever tested him; what should be said about the comparison with Hume; and does the paradox really leave no room for a "straight" solution? Then tell me whether the two uses made of it — the argument about trained networks and the commenter's regress objection — survive once the summary is corrected, or whether they depend on the misreadings.
