You're a political philosopher — democratic theory, legitimacy, that whole shop — and I need you to actually work this out rather than hand me a menu. Before you answer, spend some time brainstorming in the background: sketch two or three candidate principles, try to break each of them against my list of cases, and only then commit to the one you think survives and argue for it. And be straight with me about where you're confident and where you're guessing, because I'm going to build a scene on whatever you give me.

I'm a novelist and I've written myself into a hole. The book is set in Saltmarch, a invented city-state of about forty thousand on a tidal estuary — so nothing here has to match the real world. Everything is settled once a year at the Meeting, by show of hands, and the franchise when the book opens is "hearth-holders": anyone registered as keeping a hearth and paying the hearth tax inside the walls. The crisis is a proposal to build a sea-gate, funded by a hundred-year bond, which will permanently flood the moorings where the barge-dwellers live. The plot turns on who gets a hand at that Meeting, and I want the argument underneath it to be something a reader feels in their gut rather than a slogan. So here are my gut verdicts, then the rule I've been trying to make work.

1. The moorings people: families who live on barges tied up inside the city's jurisdiction nine or ten months a year, no hearth, no tax, but the harbourmaster's law runs against them and the gate destroys their homes. They vote. Very sure.
2. A hearth-holder who has lived abroad twenty-two years running a shipping business, keeps a house and two servants in the city, visits one month a year, intends to retire and die there. He does not vote. Very sure, and I can't quite say why.
3. His granddaughter, nineteen, born in the city, away at university in another country for a three-year degree, definitely coming back. She votes. Fairly sure.
4. A twelve-year-old born in the city who will still be paying off the bond at fifty. Does not vote — but this one makes me uneasy in a way 2 doesn't.
5. A woman eighteen months into a two-year fraud sentence in the city gaol: a hearth-holder, out before the gate is finished, will live behind it for the rest of her life. She votes. Mildly sure.
6. The Wardens of the Slack, a religious order living inside the walls who maintain the sluices: subject to city law like everyone else, but they've vowed obedience to their abbot and insist they hold no view on the city's business, and would vote as a bloc however he told them. My gut says no — and I worry that I'm just punishing them for their religion, which would make me a hypocrite.
7. A lifelong resident with advanced dementia, who will spend her remaining years inside the walls looking at the gate and can no longer follow an argument. My gut says no. But I can't articulate why this is different in kind from 4 rather than just sadder, and that bothers me.
8. The unborn — most of the people who'll live with a hundred-year bond don't exist yet. My gut says they have some real claim but that a vote is the wrong instrument for it, and I genuinely go back and forth on what the right instrument would be.

The rule I keep trying to make work is: you get a hand at the Meeting if the decision can be enforced against you and you're the kind of being that can have a view about whether to obey it. That gets 1, 3, 4, 5 and 7 roughly where my gut puts them, but it also lets 2 in (the city can seize his house; he has views in abundance) and it lets 6 in (they can be made to obey and they obviously could have views, they've just promised not to), and it has nothing at all to say about 8. I've tried patching it with something like "and whose life is bound up with the city's life going forward" — which feels right but definately sounds like a way of smuggling my verdict on 2 inside the principle rather than deriving it.

So: take my rule, or throw it out and start again. Tell me which of my verdicts you'd overturn rather than build a baroque principle to save it, and why. Give me the cases your principle decides that aren't on my list — one of them should be a case I haven't thought of and will need, ideally something to do with the tide-dwellers who are only inside the jurisdiction three months a year. And if you can, give me a one-line version of the finished principle that a character could say out loud at the Meeting without sounding like a lecture.
