hey — I'm a novelist, about three hundred pages into a book I'm not sure I understand yet, and I need a short story to read out loud at a festival thing in the autumn. 1200–1800 words, close third person, present tense, and it has to survive being read aloud to a room of people who didn't choose to be there.

The book is set in a made-up port city where the glassblowers' guild keeps this custom they call the Reckoning: once a year every master gives away, free, the single best object they made that year, and it goes into the city's collection. Nobody wants to. Every one of them would rather go back to selling their work. Nobody is forced and nobody is lying and no one defects — that's the part I care about. I've started calling it a velvet Moloch: a race to the bottom where every individual step is something everyone in the room would publicly applaud, so there's no one to blame, no moment anyone could have said no, and no point at which the thing looks like a trap from the inside. I don't want the usual version — I know the Moloch bit in Howl and the long rationalist blog post that turned the name into a label for this stuff, and I know arms races, adtech, credential inflation. Give me something where the fuel is generosity, or craft-pride, or not wanting to be the person who refused.

One more constraint that I think is the whole story. In my notes I keep coming back to the idea that the guild's membership isn't a fixed number: apprentices drift in, ships bring outsiders, there's always the possibility of one more. So no single person's compliance is ever pivotal, and no coalition can ever know it's complete. I want a character to try the obvious fix — a pact, a boycott, a public oath, whatever — and have it fail for that structural reason, not because people are weak or stupid or got bought off. If my reasoning there is wrong, tell me, but inside the story it has to feel like arithmetic rather than betrayal.

Ending: either a real escape that doesn't cheat (no king arrives, no regulator, no sudden moral awakening that costs nobody anything), or — if there isn't one — an ending where someone names precisely which exit is blocked and why. And please don't let anyone give a tidy speech explaining the mechanism; if a character understands it, let them understand it self-servingly or half-wrong.

After the story, one paragraph, straight: is "velvet Moloch" actually a different animal, or is it just the old multipolar trap in nicer clothes, and am I building a whole novel on a distinction that definately won't hold? I'd rather find out now than on page 600.
