I spent last night arguing with an old friend — a psychologist who works on moral judgement — about free will, and it ended exactly where these arguments always end: he said determinism would rob us of everything that matters, I said nothing that mattered was at stake, and neither of us had moved since we had the same argument in 2019. What frustrates me is not that we disagree but that I cannot tell what we are disagreeing about. The frame we kept coming back to was Dennett's: his 1984 book is called *Elbow Room: The Varieties of Free Will Worth Wanting*, and its question — not whether free will is compatible with determinism, but which of the things anyone might mean by free will are worth caring about — seems to me to be where the real argument now lives. Yet the compatibility dispute has run in recognisably the same shape since Hobbes and Hume, every argument on both sides is in print several times over, and nobody moves: the classical compatibilists and the reasons-responsive theorists keep pointing out that nothing we practise in holding people responsible requires indeterminism, the source and libertarian incompatibilists keep insisting that only ultimate authorship or agent causation could underwrite desert, the hard incompatibilists agree with them about the metaphysics and then draw the bleak conclusion, and the revisionists suggest we simply change the subject. To sharpen things, my friend had run an informal vignette study with about sixty undergraduates, in which a fictitious technician offers to install, before birth and at no cost, exactly one of the following for life: (A) moment-of-action alternative possibilities — whenever you deliberate, you could in the very same circumstances have done otherwise; (B) reasons-responsiveness alone — you reliably recognise and react to reasons, with no alternative possibilities anywhere in the story; (C) historical self-authorship — your present character is the product of your own earlier effortful choices rather than of luck and upbringing alone; (D) what he calls navigational openness, a diachronic rather than synchronic modal property — at every branch point of your life more than one subsequent life-shape remains genuinely available, so the future is not in principle foreclosed, as opposed to the merely conditional truth that you would have done otherwise had you wanted to; and (E) a placebo — nothing about you changes at all, but you are caused to hold, with total conviction and at no other cost, the belief that whichever of A–D matters obtains. Roughly two-fifths chose D, A and C split most of the remainder, B — the item professional compatibilists actually defend — came last, and about a fifth chose the placebo. He thinks this shows the folk are natural incompatibilists who want an open future; I think it shows they are picking something no position in the literature is even offering, or that they are answering a question about which life they would prefer to inhabit rather than reporting what their concept of responsibility requires; and we shouted about that for an hour without getting anywhere.

So here is my hypothesis, which I want tested rather than confirmed. I suspect the stalemate is not one disagreement but three stacked ones. First, a partly verbal dispute about which item on the menu, or which kind of item, the term picks out: the candidates are not rival answers to one question but belong to different ontological categories — a modal property of moments, a disposition, a historical property, a modal property of whole lives, and a belief state — and the sides differ about what would even count as a candidate for being free will, which is why each side can accuse the other of changing the subject without either being able to say what the subject is. Second, a hidden empirical question, the one my friend's vignette was gesturing at: what ordinary people are actually responding to in the standard cases, and whether that response is evidence about the concept or merely about our preferences among futures. Third, and this is the part I think is bedrock, a normative disagreement about what 'worth wanting' is a question about: whether it tracks what would satisfy or console us, what we have reason to want, or what would vindicate the practices we are unwilling to give up — blame, resentment, gratitude, praise, and desert-based treatment more generally — and whether the legitimacy of those practices can be answerable to our psychological satisfactions at all. That last question may be one neither side can argue for rather than merely assume.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict or a survey: tell me, with reference to what each camp actually argues, whether this three-part account is right, whether one of the three is doing all the work, and whether I have misdescribed any of them — in particular whether the verbal dispute is really verbal, or whether it conceals a substantive claim about which desiderata can be jointly satisfied. I would also like your view on what would count as progress given the diagnosis you favour: a taxonomy of which menu items are compossible and under what metaphysics; an argument that one of them — navigational openness without luck, say, or authorship without an infinite regress of self-making — is not merely unavailable but incoherent; a decision about whether the empirical question is tractable and whether it could matter; a change of question altogether, from what is worth wanting to what is worth keeping or worth wanting for whom; or an honest argument that the debate is ill-posed and that no progress should be expected. And if you think my friend's placebo result is simply noise, say so and explain what a result that would bear on the question would have to look like.
