Here's the rough shape I have in mind; you make it exam-ready and tell me if the crux is wrong.

The exam paper stipulates some physics (clearly flagged as invented):

- Phenomenal valence is real, measurable, and additive. Every physical system above some threshold has some; most of it is tiny. Call the negative part suffering.
- There's a conservation result: the total valence in a closed system can't be increased or destroyed, only moved. So no intervention can reduce the *amount* of suffering in the universe — only relocate it.
- There's a cheap industrial technology ("shunting") that moves negative valence out of the systems where it's concentrated into a sink. The cheapest sinks spread a fixed quantity across an astronomically large number of tiny systems — think googols — so that each one ends up with an utterly imperceptible, unnoticeable sliver of negative valence. None of these systems is connected to any organism, and nobody will ever observe any of them.
- The only calibration point anyone has for valence is our own case and that of other animals. The physics hands you the numbers about sand, silicon, dust, whatever, but not the moral interpretation of them.

So the question, in effect: is a government justified in funding a large shunting program whose whole effect is to take concentrated suffering and dilute it across a vast number of imperceptibly-suffering systems? I want the crux to be the dilution move — whether spreading a fixed quantity of suffering so thin that no single bearer has a noticeable pain is genuinely an improvement, and what your answer to *that* commits you to about how the badness of suffering aggregates. The conservation stipulation is there to stop them from just saying "obviously, reduce suffering, done."
