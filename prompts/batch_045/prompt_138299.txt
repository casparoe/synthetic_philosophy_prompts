I help run a workers' co-op — forty-eight people, a bakery and a shared kitchen. Every few years we redraw the rounds: the job-descriptions people are assigned to for a stretch of years (ovens, deliveries, counter, ordering). We score each round on a rough 0–100 index of how good a working life in it is, counting pay, hours, physical strain, and how people say they find it. The numbers are crude; we would stand behind them to within about three points either way and no further.

When we argue about a redraw we try to reason as if we did not know which round we would land in. We did this for years before someone told me it has a name — reasoning behind a veil of ignorance — and that the version where you judge a scheme by its worst position is called the difference principle. From what I have since read, the principle is officially stated over income and primary goods rather than over how well a life goes, and it is supposed to apply only when the better positions are genuinely open to everyone; with us the rounds are assigned by lottery, so that part is satisfied. I mention it only so you know what I think we are doing.

Here is our record. In every past case there were four rounds of twelve people each; I give the four scores.

1. (60, 60, 60, 60) against (68, 68, 68, 59): we kept the flat one — eight points for three quarters of us given up in order to hold the bottom at 60.
2. (60, 60, 60, 60) against (62, 62, 62, 60): we took the second. Same bottom, more for everybody else. Note that this inequality did nothing at all for the worst-off round, so we have never really insisted that an inequality work to the advantage of the bottom.
3. (50, 50, 50, 50) against (95, 95, 95, 51): we took the second. One point at the bottom justified a wildly unequal schedule.
4. A fixed schedule (85, 85, 85, 30) against a rotating one in which everyone cycles through all four rounds, but retraining costs pull the scores during each stint down to (84, 84, 84, 26), so that each person's working life averages about 69.5. We took the rotating one, even though at any given moment somebody is sitting at 26, and even though the total across the co-op was slightly lower.
5. A safe schedule (55, 55, 55, 55) against a new oven that gives everyone 95 if it works and 45 if it fails, with no idea of the odds. We took the safe one.

Next week we vote on three proposals, and this time the rounds have different sizes, which we have never had before:

A. Two rounds: a small repair-and-cleaning round of 4 people at 62, and a general round of 44 at 66. Nobody anywhere has a bad stretch.
B. Two rounds: 12 people at 60, and 36 at 95. The highest total of the three by a wide margin, and the lowest floor.
C. One rotating schedule: everyone cycles through three postings at 95 and one at 8 — the overnight oven rebuild, brief, equally shared, which nobody does twice — so that every working life averages 73.25. The highest floor of the three, no inequality between people at all, and a stretch inside every life far worse than anything in A or B.

What I want from you is less the letter than the machinery. Which rules fit all five of our past votes — judging a scheme by its worst whole life, or by its worst whole life subject to some floor on how bad a single stretch may be, or by floor first and total second, or by floor first with differences of two or three points treated as noise, or something else I have not thought of? Which of those rules pick A, which pick B, and which pick C? Which of our five decisions is the closest analogue to next week's, and does it settle the matter or only look as though it does? If our record genuinely leaves it open, what would we each be committing to by voting A, B, or C? And what one further proposal — give me the actual numbers — would best separate the rules still standing?

Then give me your single best guess for how the vote goes, and end your reply with a last line of exactly the form \answer{A}, \answer{B}, or \answer{C}.
