I'm a journalist working on a feature about the economics of very small payments — subscription platforms, creator economies, the way large private fortunes now get assembled out of millions of transactions of a dollar or two — and I would like to use Robert Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain example from Anarchy, State, and Utopia (the basketball player whom a large number of fans each voluntarily pay a trivial extra sum to watch, with the result that a previously equal distribution becomes strikingly unequal) as the conceptual frame for the piece. Before I build an article on it, I want to know what the example actually establishes, because I suspect the version that circulates in popular writing does less work than people assume, and possibly the wrong work.

So, first: separate the claims that get run together. As I read the secondary discussion, there are at least four candidates for what the argument is meant to deliver. One, that no patterned or end-state principle of distributive justice can be maintained without continuous interference with how people conduct themselves. Two, that the case is simply a counterexample to such principles — nothing in it looks unjust, so justice must be historical rather than patterned. Three, that the interference needed to preserve a pattern is itself objectionable, independent of the pattern's content. Four, that preserving a pattern is informationally hopeless, since every transfer changes what everyone else is permitted to do with what they hold. Which of these is the actual conclusion, and which of the others would have to be argued for separately? In particular, the move from each transaction being unobjectionable to the resulting distribution being unobjectionable looks like a substantive assumption rather than a logical step, and I want to know how much of the argument rests on it.

Second, and more important for my purposes: does the force of the example depend on its structure? I have been playing with three variations. In the first, the same player starts from the same distribution and ends with the same very large sum, but a single benefactor hands over the whole amount in one transaction; there is no crowd and no accumulation of small acts. In the second, the mirror image: a society with highly unequal entitlements in which, over the same season, a large crowd of people voluntarily redirect small sums into a common fund that pays out equally to everyone, and the inequality dissolves by the same mechanism — many small voluntary steps — that produced it in the original case. In the third, each spectator pays willingly in the sense the argument needs, nobody is forcing anybody, but the player is the sole available source of something the spectators have strong reason to want and there is no acceptable alternative to paying.

For each variation, tell me what the argument needs: does it have to go a certain way, or can it absorb whatever the right answer turns out to be? Then give me your own verdict on which single feature is really carrying the weight — the many-small-transactions structure, the claim that justice is historical and depends on a baseline of just holdings, or the thin, non-coercion-based notion of voluntariness in transfer — and say which variation, if any, exposes the popular version of the argument as weaker than the philosophical one.

I want a committed answer rather than a menu: take a position on what the argument establishes, take a position on each variation, and defend both, explaining why the alternatives you reject are less plausible. Where your verdict depends on a contested moral intuition rather than on something the text settles, flag that in a sentence or two, since I will need to hedge it in print. Paraphrasing is fine throughout; I don't need the book's exact wording.
