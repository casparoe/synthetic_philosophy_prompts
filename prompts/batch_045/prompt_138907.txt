I'm running a one-off discussion night for a badly mixed group — two ant people from the bio dept, a couple of philosophy grad students, and maybe four friends who just like science fiction — on whether an ant colony can have a mind. I want a decision tree-ish handout I can photocopy, not an essay, and I want it to sort people by what they say about cases rather than by which label they think they like. So no node should just ask "is the colony conscious" or "do you believe in superorganisms" — every branch should be a concrete thing that could be true of a colony, answerable in one sentence by someone who has never read any of this literature.

Cases I want built into the branches, because these are the ones my group will actually argue about:

- the queen swap: the queen is replaced by a functionally identical one mid-season, and the colony's foraging pattern, trail layout and response to threats carry on smoothly. Same colony, same mind, or a new one wearing the old one's habits?
- the replayed transcript: you record the complete chemical state of a nest — every trail gradient and depositing pattern — wipe the nest, then play the recording back into a fresh colony, which then behaves as the old one would have. Did you copy a belief, or only a habit?
- the bandwidth doubler: every ant gets a tiny radio that roughly doubles how fast and how far it can pass on what it just did, so that colony-level patterns propagate maybe ten times faster than normal. Does speeding up the medium change whether there's a thought there, and does anything interesting happen at the old speed that stops at the new one?
- the stigmergic ventriloquist: an experimenter paints fake pheromone trails, and the colony forages north for days as if there were a food source there, when there is nothing. Is that a colony-level hallucination, a colony-level memory of a food source that never existed, or just the environment being weird?
- nest fission: a colony is split in two halves, each inheriting an equal share of ants and a copy of the trail layout. Two subjects or one that has divided, and does your answer depend on whether you think there was one before?
- the blind colony: all the foragers are removed, so nothing new comes in, but the nest still regulates its temperature and brood care for weeks. Can you keep the mind while deleting every channel to the outside?

The angle I care about: I want the tree to separate three things that my group keeps sliding together — whether there's anything it's like to be the colony, whether colony-level states have accuracy conditions at all (could the colony be wrong about where the food is, as opposed to just behaving badly), and whether the colony is an agent in the sense of having something like its own reasons, as opposed to being a self-organizing system that we find it convenient to describe in agent vocabulary. So please don't let those collapse into one axis; build the logical space from combinations of answers and then locate the actual positions in it.

Practical constraints: about eight branch nodes and maybe six or seven leaves. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, keep it on the tree and tell me why you think nobody is there — incoherent, merely unfashionable, or just unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate, especially if the difference between them is metaphysical rather than behavioural, since I suspect at least one famous pair is like that. Give each leaf a name if it has one and call it unnamed if it doesn't, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing a smart person at my table would pounce on. And please keep the whole thing readable by someone whose only background is having once read about ant colonies in a popular book; if a node needs jargon to be phrased, it probably isn't phrased right yet.
