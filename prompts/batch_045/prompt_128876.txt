I'm presenting a dissertation chapter on Adam Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments* at a works-in-progress session next week — twenty minutes of talk, twenty of discussion — and I want a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a defense of the view.

The chapter's thesis is a reading of the impartial spectator. I argue that it should not be understood as an ideal observer whose judgments are impartial in the sense of giving everyone's interests equal weight, but as a calibration procedure: a habit of adjusting the pitch of one's passion until an actual, well-informed, disinterested bystander can be brought to share it, since on Smith's own account it is the concord of sentiments — not their content — that yields the pleasure of mutual sympathy which agents are actually after. Because the calibrators are real spectators, the procedure inherits their biases; and it inherits them asymmetrically. Call this the calibration asymmetry. Downward, for the unsocial passions and above all for resentment, calibration reliably corrects: a bystander's resentment is always weaker than the injured party's, so any procedure that tunes my passion to what a third party can share pulls resentment toward the pitch Smith calls proper. Upward, for the social passions — specifically the disposition to admire the rich and the great — calibration amplifies: admiration is the sentiment whose sharing is most pleasurable, the people whose sharing of it is most worth having are those of highest rank, and rank-admiration spreads among spectators, so the mechanism stabilizes precisely the corruption of the moral sentiments that Smith himself deplores. I then argue that Smith's internal resources do not repair this. The man within the breast is either internalized from actual spectators, in which case it inherits the corruption, or an unexplained innate faculty, which sits badly with his own account of how conscience is acquired; I call this problem spectatorial bootstrapping. Self-command cannot help either, since it is defined as moderating one's passion to the pitch the impartial spectator would approve, and so presupposes the reliability of the very calibration in question. And the famous passage where he says the rich are led by an invisible hand to make nearly the same distribution of the necessaries of life that an equal division of the earth would have produced is a claim about beneficial unintended consequences, not about the correctness of anyone's judgments. Conclusion: the doctrine of the corruption of the sentiments is not an incidental complaint but what the mechanism predicts.

The room will be: a Hume specialist who reads the impartial spectator as a variant on Hume's steady and general point of view and is likely to find my novelty claim overstated; an intellectual historian of the Scottish Enlightenment who works in the natural-law tradition and is allergic to reading Smith as either a proto-utilitarian or an ideal-observer theorist; a historian of economic thought who cares mainly about commercial society and will ask whether my corruption claim is really a critique of commerce; a moral psychologist who studies reputation and norm internalization and will want a mechanism and a falsification test; a virtue theorist who takes self-command to be Smith's master virtue; and an ethicist of a contractualist cast who reconstructs the spectator as a device for impartial grading and will press the worry that my reading strips Smith of any standpoint from which to criticize his own society's sentiments.

Please give me roughly eight objections, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by how likely they are, each attributed to the member who would raise it and phrased the way that person would phrase it — in their idiom, not in mine. For each: a reply I could actually deliver in about ninety seconds, and one line on where that reply is weakest or what it would cost me to maintain it. Then tell me which two objections have enough force that I should concede them in my opening two minutes instead of waiting to be pressed, which single reply has to land if the chapter is to survive, the question I am hoping nobody asks, and the weak point in my own argument that I should volunteer before someone else finds it.

Two further requests. First, for each of my premises, mark whether it is textually settled or a contested interpretation, and flag the places where I need to hedge explicitly in the talk: I do not want to present my contested reading as though it were the consensus reading. Please keep separate what people who work on Smith agree about and what is genuinely disputed among them, and do not smooth a live disagreement into a summary. Second, do not give me bibliography, citations, or pointers to the secondary literature — I have those, and I want only the arguments. Please write in American English.
