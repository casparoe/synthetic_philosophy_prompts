I am convening a small adversarial collaboration on the question whether large language models understand anything they talk about, and my job is to circulate, in advance, the document the collaboration is supposed to produce: a joint statement both parties could sign, with the residual disagreement made exact.

The two parties, as I would characterize them going in:

The competence-first party holds that understanding is a matter of what an internal state does. A state understands its subject matter to the extent that it tracks that subject matter rather than merely covarying with superficial marks of it, supports generalization that survives changes of surface form, can be interfered with in the way its apparent content predicts, and earns its place in the explanation of the system's success rather than riding along as a passenger. On this view, the fact that a system was trained only to predict text is evidence about the difficulty of learning from text, not a refutation; and once the question is put in these terms, current models turn out to understand a good deal, unevenly, and in some respects badly.

The derivation party holds that success at text prediction is compatible with the system's internal states having no content of their own. Whatever semantic appearance those states have is, on this view, inherited from the human practices recorded in the training corpus: the system is answerable to what has been written, not to what is the case, and any understanding it displays is second-hand, owed to the authors whose usage it compresses. Applied to such a system, they hold, the ascription of understanding is at best a façon de parler.

Scope: the collaboration concerns the epistemic or semantic notion of understanding only. Nothing in the document may turn on consciousness, sentience, phenomenology, or moral status. Both parties should be treated as agreeing that those questions are separable, and any move that smuggles them in should be flagged as out of bounds.

Please produce the following.

**1. Agreed claims, numbered.** What both parties can sign: about the shared setup (what the training objective is, what the architecture is, what the behavioral evidence actually shows); about which considerations both agree do not settle the question; and about the dialectical situation. Hold these to a real standard. An agreed claim should be one that some informed participant in the wider dispute would deny — I want at least two that each party would rather not concede. Claims of the form "the question is subtle" or "more empirical work is needed" are worthless to me; if you catch yourself writing one, replace it with the denial someone would actually make and say why both parties reject it.

**2. A disagreements table.** Columns: a neutral statement of the disagreement; the formulation of it that the competence-first party would sign as its own view; the formulation the derivation party would sign as its own; and a classification of each row as substantive, verbal, or partly verbal, with a one-line reason. I expect at least one row to be partly verbal, and I want you to name the term doing the work and show what the dispute looks like once that term is replaced. If you conclude that the whole dispute is verbal, argue for that explicitly rather than quietly restating the two views so that they come out compatible.

**3. Cruxes.** For each: the sub-question stated precisely enough that a settled answer would move at least one party; a concrete test, case, or argument that both parties accept as bearing on it, specified in enough detail to be carried out with techniques that already exist — nothing that presupposes ground-truth semantics, a new measurement technology, or a theory nobody has; each party's prediction, written down in advance; and, separately, the result each party would count as moving it, which need not be the result it predicts. Where both parties predict the same outcome but would read it differently, say so and give both readings.

Four candidate cruxes, which you should use, repair, or replace with better ones:

1. *Unsourced error.* Can a system trained only on text be wrong in a way that traces to nothing in its training distribution and to no one's say-so — a false representation of a domain that appears nowhere in the corpus? Sketch the design: specify a novel formalism or rule system at test time, in context, and probe whether the system's applications discriminate correct from incorrect in a way that resemblance to familiar textual practice cannot explain. The crux is not whether the system succeeds; it is what success or failure here would show, and whether the derivation party will grant in advance that some pattern of results would move it.

2. *Intervention and transfer.* Candidate features can be located as directions in activation space and the system's behavior pushed by intervening along them. Is the resulting behavior what the hypothesized content predicts; does that hold in task families far from the one used to locate the direction; does it survive scrambling of surface form with deep structure held fixed? And then: does that give the competence-first party what it wants, or does it only show that the system contains a structure homomorphic to the domain, which the derivation party will say is cheap and available to any sufficiently rich compressor?

3. *Explanatory credit.* When a system succeeds, is the success explained by the content of its internal states or by statistics of the corpus that those states merely summarize? Make this bite: pick tasks whose solutions are not reconstructible from any single training document but are reconstructible from the structure the corpus as a whole is about, and say in advance what pattern of successes and failures would assign credit where.

4. *Answerability.* Is being able to be corrected — having an error pointed out and revising for the reason given, rather than being nudged into different behavior — constitutive of understanding, or merely good evidence of it? Both parties should say which they hold, and what each would conclude about a system that revised reliably but only under a rephrasing that left its internal state apparently unmoved.

**4. A close.** Two short sections: the single crux each party would choose if only one could be investigated, with one sentence of justification; and one sentence, in each party's voice, on what would make that party withdraw its central claim.

Aim for a text both parties could put their names to without wincing: no hedging for its own sake, no rhetorical scoring, and no claim attributed to a party that the party would disown. Length is yours to set, but I would rather have eight sharp claims and four fully specified cruxes than twenty padded ones.
