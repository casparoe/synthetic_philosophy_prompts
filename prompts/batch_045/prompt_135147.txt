I want the implicit philosophy of a bundle of hospital practices reconstructed — not a general essay on death, but the answer to a specific puzzle.

Here is the bundle, as I understand it. A patient with a catastrophic brain injury is declared dead on neurological criteria — irreversible loss of all brain function, brainstem included — while mechanical ventilation and drugs keep the heart beating, the blood warm and oxygenated, wounds healing, and, in the well-publicized cases where the patient was pregnant, a fetus gestating for weeks until it can be delivered. The declaration does a great deal of work: it is what makes the subsequent organ recovery lawful under the dead donor rule, which requires that removing the organs not be what kills the donor. Yet the recovery is conducted in every external respect as an operation on a patient: the body goes to the operating room under the decedent's name, with an identity band, an anesthesia chart, and an anesthesiologist; protocols discuss anesthesia, analgesia, and neuromuscular blockade during the recovery, and one reason given is that a brain-dead body still produces reflexes and hemodynamic surges, including complex and apparently coordinated movements of the trunk and arms, that must be prevented. Meanwhile, in a bed down the hall, a patient whose cerebral hemispheres are destroyed but whose brainstem still functions is not dead at all: she is a patient, with a name, a chart, and interests that a surrogate is bound to protect.

What I want from you is the implicit answer this bundle gives to Dennett's question. In his story Where Am I?, the narrator's brain is removed and kept alive in a vat, linked by radio to his own denuded body, which goes on walking and talking without it, and the narrator is then asked to say where — and what — he now is. My suggestion is that a hospital answers that question institutionally, with an identity band, a death certificate, a consent form, and, in the parts I find hardest to interpret, an anesthetic given to a corpse and a second patient carried on a dead woman's chart.

So: what view of what a person is, and of where a person is located, does this bundle presuppose? I want four things, in order. First, reconstruct the implicit theory, including its notion of irreversibility: the loss is called irreversible precisely in the situation where machines are performing the functions that were lost, so say what irreversibility has to mean here and whose decision it tracks. Second, distinguish what the bundle strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: could an organism- or animal-based view, a brain-based or psychological-continuity view, and a Parfit-style view that treats identity as less deep than it appears each license exactly this protocol, and if so, where does the practice leave the question genuinely unsettled? Third, say whether the parts are consistent with one another — the anesthesia and paralysis, the contrast with the hemispheric-destruction patient, the pregnant decedent handled as two patients, the body that moves in a lifelike way though no one is at home. Fourth, tell me what would concretely have to change in the protocol if the implicit theory were replaced by one of those rivals, and what would not have to change at all.

Answer in under 700 words, as continuous prose or in short paragraphs. No preamble, no headings, no citations or discussion of who has argued for what.
