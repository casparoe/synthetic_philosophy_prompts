Write a philosophy essay of about 1,200 words on the following question.

Theories of emotion are nearly always tested against episodes: a sudden fear, a flash of irritation, a burst of grief. But a great deal of emotional life is not episodic. Someone can love a friend, resent a sibling over a childhood slight, be proud of a city, or dread a particular diagnosis, for years on end, while having no occurrent emotional experience of the matter at all — often for months at a stretch. Call this the standing-emotion problem: an adequate theory must say what it is to possess a standing emotion during the intervals between episodes.

The three standard families of theory are: (1) feeling theories, in the Jamesian tradition, on which an emotion is constitutively an experience of one's own bodily changes; (2) perceptual or attitudinal theories, on which an emotion is constitutively an experience in which an evaluative property of some object is presented to one; and (3) judgement theories, on which an emotion is constitutively an evaluative judgement, or the assent to an evaluative proposition.

Each family faces the standing-emotion problem, and each has an apparently easy reply: treat the standing emotion as a disposition — a disposition to undergo feelings, to undergo evaluative perceptions, or to make evaluative judgements, as the case may be.

Your task is to assess whether that dispositional reply works, and what follows from it for the choice between the three theories. Please weigh at least the following considerations.

1. Symmetry and discrimination. If all three families can help themselves to the dispositional move, the standing-emotion problem apparently cannot decide between them. Is that the right conclusion, or does the move cost one theory more than another? Note that the three resulting dispositions are different dispositions, so the question of which is constitutive survives: to resent one's sibling appears to be to be disposed to resent, not merely to be disposed to have feelings of some unrelated sort or to make judgements of some unrelated sort.

2. Correctness conditions. Standing emotions are assessable for intelligibility and proportion — a decades-long resentment can be pointless, or wildly disproportionate to its occasion — and this assessability is often offered as an argument against feeling theories and in favour of judgement or perception theories. Does the standing case add anything to the episodic case here, or does it merely repeat it?

3. Finks, masks and the merely hypothetical. The familiar worries about dispositional analyses apply here too. A person might genuinely resent her sibling and yet be incapable of ever feeling, perceiving or judging accordingly — say because she takes a medication that flattens her bodily arousal, or because she has trained herself out of the relevant reactions without thereby ceasing to resent him. If a theory must cash out 'she resents him' as a counterfactual about what would happen under some test condition, it needs a way of handling cases where the manifestation is prevented by the test, or where the disposition is destroyed by it. Which of the three families, if any, has the resources to do so?

4. The analogy with standing belief. The occurrent/standing distinction for emotions looks structurally like the occurrent/dispositional distinction for belief, about which a great deal has been written. Does that analogy illuminate the standing-emotion problem — for instance, by suggesting that an emotion is a state with a functional role rather than a disposition to manifest — or does it mislead, by assimilating emotions to a category whose intentionality is arguably quite different in kind?

Do not end on a non-committal note. Having weighed the arguments, defend one of these verdicts: that the standing-emotion problem refutes feeling theories; that it refutes judgement theories; that it refutes neither and is genuinely theory-neutral; that it is best answered by a hybrid view on which standing emotions are dispositions of a distinctive and irreducible emotional kind; or that it is really an argument for pluralism about 'the emotions' rather than for any single theory of them.
