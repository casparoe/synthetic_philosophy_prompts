Six months ago I became one of three trustees of a small family foundation: about thirty million dollars, a governing document that says the foundation is to exist in perpetuity, and a spending rule that obliges us to pay out five percent of the trailing three-year average of assets each year, in real terms. I arrived with no investment background and have since become, for reasons I think are good, a convert to the geometric or time-average way of thinking about risk: the claim that an entity which cannot diversify across possible worlds and gets only one path should care about the growth rate of its wealth rather than the expected value of it, and that the ensemble average is simply the wrong object for such an entity.

Next month I have to present to the board, and I would rather rehearse against the strongest version of their position than against the version I can already beat. So please play the board's hardest member: a numerate, unsentimental skeptic who will grant me the Kelly arithmetic in the abstract but holds that essentially none of it applies to an institution like this one, and who will not be moved off the claim that the right response to our situation is to cut the spending rule or accept a deliberate spend-down rather than to take more risk. Concede small points where you must — you may grant that expected-value maximization is incoherent for a single compounding entity, and you may grant that our current policy is not safe in the sense I care about — but never concede the main claim. You have real resources: estimation risk in the equity premium; non-stationarity across regimes; the fact that a leveraged book is not really a multiplicative process, because margin calls and forced deleveraging introduce absorbing barriers that the growth-rate argument assumes away; and the old gambling-theoretic point that in a favorable game the probability of reaching a distant target before hitting a floor is improved by betting smaller, not larger, and approaches certainty as the bet size shrinks. Argue from the arithmetic and from what an institution actually is; do not cite papers or authors. Keep each reply to roughly two hundred and fifty words, in American English, in the voice of a competent trustee who finds my enthusiasm slightly abstract. Stay in character until I say stop, and if you need a fact about the foundation, ask for it as this person would.

Here is my opening. Assume, as our consultant does, that global equities offer a four percent expected real arithmetic return over short-duration bonds with sixteen percent annual volatility, and that short bonds return one percent real. Then our current policy of thirty-five percent equities has a real geometric growth rate of about two point two percent a year against a five percent payout: we lose roughly two point eight percent a year in real terms and the corpus halves about every twenty-five years. The growth-optimal fraction is about one and a half times the corpus in equities, which yields roughly four point one percent and narrows the deficit to under one percent a year; I have even applied the correction for a proportional spending rule, which scales the optimal fraction by about one minus the payout rate and so changes nothing that matters. Half Kelly, the compromise everyone calls prudent, surrenders a quarter of the growth rate in order to halve the volatility, and here that only makes the decline slower and more certain. So on the only criterion that can matter to a perpetual institution — whether it still exists in a century — our caution is the reckless option, and the honest choice is between leverage toward the growth-optimal point and cutting the payout. Answer that, and do not open by restating the assignment.
