I'm putting together the discussion notes for a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind that I'm TA-ing this term, and the session on emotion is the one I keep rewriting. I've got the cognitivist/judgmentalist material in decent shape, so I want to set that aside and spend the session on the two views that my students conflate most: on the one hand, the broadly perceptual picture, on which an emotion is an experience in which an evaluative property shows up for the subject — non-inferential, world-directed, recalcitrant in the face of the subject's own better judgment, and answerable to how things are rather than to what the subject wills; on the other hand, a conative or enactivist picture, on which what makes a state an emotion is not its representational content at all but the way it reorganizes the subject's field of salience and action-readiness — urgency, approach and avoidance, bodily and interpersonal posture, the takeover of attention — with the intentional object fixed by whatever that reorganization is a response to, and evaluative content entering only as a gloss the subject (or the theorist) puts on the orientation. A friend in the seminar insists these aren't really rivals, that one is describing what the emotion is *of* and the other what the emotion *does*, and I want to press hard on whether that's true or whether they genuinely collide.

What I need is a structured comparison that works through the collision points rather than summarizing each view in turn. Please say, for each theory: what fixes the identity of an emotion type (what makes a given episode the same emotion as another, and what makes anger differ from fear); what does the explanatory work in accounting for the emotion's motivational force; what its correctness or fittingness conditions are and whether they are inherited from something independent of the emotion; and whether the emotion is held to detect its value or in any sense to confer or constitute it — think of love appearing to make its object matter, or something's becoming contemptible partly in virtue of one's contempt. Then run both theories, side by side, through four cases I've been collecting: (i) second-order emotions such as shame at one's own fear, anxiety about being anxious, or amusement at one's own grief, where the object is itself a state with content and one needs some account of how one emotion takes another as its object; (ii) objectless-but-situated states — caffeine dread, Sunday-evening dread, the free-floating elation of a manic episode — where no candidate proposition seems to be available; (iii) a double dissociation I've been using, between what I'll call motivational anesthesia (the full evaluative experience with no action-readiness or bodily mobilization) and affective blindsight (the full autonomic and behavioral profile of fear — avoidance, vigilance, startle — with the subject reporting no sense of anything being threatening); and (iv) emotions directed at causally inert, absent, or impossible objects — awe at a proof, indignation at a wrong done centuries ago, grief at the heat death of the universe, nostalgia for a place that never existed. Feel free to add one case of your own if some further case does more diagnostic work than these.

Finish with a verdict section: which of the two is the more promising research program, what supplementary machinery each would have to bolt on in order to survive the cases it handles worst, and — this is the part I most want — one specific consideration that would make you abandon your verdict, plus an honest account of which parts of the disagreement are substantive and which are merely differences in what the two theories count as the theory's explanatory target. Aim for around 1,200 words, prose with headings rather than bullet dumps; assume an audience of graduate students who have read the standard literature, so you can use terms like direction of fit, recalcitrance, formal object, and fittingness without defining them. Please don't spend any words on citations, a bibliography, or suggestions for further reading — I'll insert the references myself, and the reading list is fixed by the syllabus.
