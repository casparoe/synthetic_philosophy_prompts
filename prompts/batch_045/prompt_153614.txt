I've been asked to give a forty-five-minute session on heritability next week to a class of first-year master's students in education policy — quantitatively competent (they've had regression and variance decomposition and can read an ANOVA table), but with no formal genetics, and with almost everything they think they know about the topic picked up from news coverage of polygenic scores and school achievement. The immediate occasion for my caring about this is an argument I had on Sunday with an old friend from medical school, which ran to about two hours and ended with both of us more convinced than when we started. Her position, as I would reconstruct it: the heritability of adult height is somewhere around 0.8, so most of any given person's height is genetic, so childhood nutrition programmes are fighting a losing battle; and, in a more careful formulation she retreated to when I pushed, that a high heritability estimate tells you how much room the environment has left to work with. The first inference I could dispose of easily. The second I could only gesture at — something about h² being a ratio of variances in a specified population under a specified range of environments, and about shifts in a mean being compatible with any value of h² — and she replied, reasonably enough, that if heritability constrains nothing about what interventions can accomplish, it isn't clear what the number is for. I don't want the session to be a report on that argument, but I do want it built so that both inferences are dead on arrival by the end without my ever mentioning that we had it.

What I want from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself. Concretely: first, the order in which the components must arrive and what depends on what — I assume the population-relativity point cannot land before students have actually watched a variance get partitioned, but I'm not confident about the rest of the sequence, and in particular about where gene–environment correlation and interaction belong, early as part of the definition or late as a complication. Second, the one example or small dataset that should carry the whole session, together with an argument for what it must contain and what it must lack: I've been sketching something around a continuous trait in a fictional organism measured across two environments of differing uniformity, but I suspect the right carrier is either a trait whose causal story is transparently environmental and yet heritable or one that is paradigmatically genetic and yet barely heritable, and I can't settle which cut does more work. Third, the misconceptions they will arrive with or form en route, in the order in which they'll form them, with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than merely corrected at the end — I have in mind the decomposition fallacy, the immutability reading, the slide from heritability within a group to heritability of a difference between groups, treating gene–environment correlation as a footnote, and the overcorrection into concluding that the statistic tells us nothing at all. Fourth, one question I can put to them at the end that distinguishes genuine grasp from a student who has memorised the phrase that heritability is a population statistic: I'd like something that requires a number or a judgement in a novel setting rather than a definition, and I'd like to know in advance which answers should worry me. Fifth, what I should explicitly decline to open at this level and how to say so in a way that doesn't look like evasion — I'd rather the session not turn into a fight about group differences, but I also don't want to dodge the one inference about groups that people are actually going to make from these numbers.

Finally, tell me where the plan is weakest: how confident you are that this ordering would actually work with this particular audience, and single out the one move in it you are least sure about — the step most likely to misfire, to be misread, or to collapse back into exactly the confusion it was meant to prevent.
