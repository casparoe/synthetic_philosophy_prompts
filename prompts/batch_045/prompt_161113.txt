Me and a friend spent like two hours arguing about the Löbian obstacle last night and we ended up more confused than when we started, so: can you give me the gut-level version of why an agent that reasons by proving things can't get itself to trust a successor that reasons exactly as well as it does, let alone better?

I know the one-liner — wanting to prove that everything the successor proves is true would amount to proving your own soundness, and Löb's theorem blocks that — but I have no feel for why the trust can't be built up some other way, and my friend's claim that trust in a successor with just more proof-search time is fine while trust in one with one extra axiom is hopeless struck me as arbitary. Does the obstacle even bite when the successor is a literal copy of the agent, or only when it's strictly stronger? Is there a principled line between more compute and one more axiom, or is my friend's distinction fake?

And here's the bit we really couldn't resolve: people use the same kind of Löbian fixed-point reasoning to show that two agents who can read each other's source code can end up cooperating in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma. There the theorem seems to *give* you trust rather than deny it. What's the difference between that case and the self-modification case? Is it just that one is about two systems and the other about one system trying to vouch for another, or is something else doing the work?

Last thing: is any of this really a fact about agents, or only a fact about agents that insist on having a proof before they act? If an agent instead had probabilistic or default-and-revisable trust in its successors — trust it could withdraw later, rather than a certificate up front — would there be an analogue of the obstacle, or does it dissolve? Basically I want to know which part of this is the math and which part is a design choice we made.
