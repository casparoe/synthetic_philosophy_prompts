Two philosophers of science who have been arguing past each other for years have agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the joint statement they could both sign.

One is an "NFL skeptic": she holds that the no-free-lunch theorems for optimization and supervised learning vindicate a broadly Humean skepticism about induction, since no inductive or learning policy outperforms any other once performance is averaged over all possible environments, so the track record of science gives no reason at all to expect it to continue. The other is an "NFL deflationist": he holds that those theorems are artifacts of averaging over a permutation-invariant ensemble of target functions, that they quantify over a set of worlds almost none of which resemble ours, and that they therefore say nothing about whether induction is reliable here, even if the demand for a non-circular justification of induction is left standing.

Structure the document in three parts.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims: the mathematics both accept, including exactly what the theorems range over and what would have to be true of an ensemble for them to apply; the distinction between justifying induction and showing it to be reliable; and any shared account of what "reliable" and "structured world" mean here.

Second, a table of the residual disagreements. Each row should give the same disagreement twice, once in the wording the skeptic would accept for her own position and once in the wording the deflationist would accept for his, and should state plainly whether that row is a substantive dispute or only a verbal one.

Third, the cruxes, with at least these three: whether a normative conclusion about inductive practice can be drawn from a formal result proved for permutation-invariant ensembles; whether the apparent compressibility of the tasks we actually face can underwrite induction without circularity; and whether the uniformity of nature is a contingent, discoverable feature of our world or a commitment adopted because no alternative is available. For each crux, name one test, case, or argument that both parties would accept in advance as bearing on it, and record what each predicts it will show. Two devices are available: the "lunch-gradient" result, a stipulated theorem stating that the largest expected performance gap achievable between two learners on an ensemble is bounded by that ensemble's departure from permutation-invariance, measured as its description length relative to a fixed reference machine; and a preregistered meta-study over a fixed battery of real-world forecasting and classification tasks in which simplicity-biased learners are compared against complexity-matched permutation controls, with the gap recorded against a compressibility statistic for the battery.

Keep the settled apart from the contested: treat the correctness of the theorems as mathematics, the absence of any agreed non-circular justification of induction, and the inability of any single empirical result to settle a normative question as settled, and flag everything else as disputed without presenting either reading as the consensus. Where a crux is merely verbal, say which and why; where it is substantive, say what the world would have to be like for each side to be vindicated.
