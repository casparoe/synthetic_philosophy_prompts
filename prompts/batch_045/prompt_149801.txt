I host a small podcast on decision theory and my guest next week is someone who defends superrationality — the Hofstadter line that in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between two copies of yourself you should reason as if your move and theirs are the same event, so you pick the option that's best if everyone picks it. I don't want questions where she just re-explains the view and reruns the Platonia story; give me 6-8 questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one that presses on whether this is anything more than evidential decision theory once you cash out what 'same algorithm' has to mean, one on near-copies (agents 99% algorithmically similar, not identical — does the reasoning degrade smoothly or cliff-edge, and why should similarity do that work?), and one built around a case I keep chewing on: the staggered run. Two instantiations of the same algorithm, but copy A is launched, its output sealed, and the seal is physically irreversible an hour before copy B is launched, and B knows all of this. If superrational cooperation survives that, what exactly is it tracking — and if it doesn't, how much of the view was just bookkeeping about causal loops? Add one question on the obvious jab that this is the categorical imperative with payoff matrices bolted on, and end with something that makes 'no serious view survives its own counterexamples' style deflection hard, asking what result or observation would actually make her give it up.
