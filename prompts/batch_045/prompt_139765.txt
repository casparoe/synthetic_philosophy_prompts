someone on a podcast I listen to (one of those ai-and-philosophy ones, host definately seems to know his stuff) summed up Dennett's "counterfeit people" idea roughly like this:

"Dennett's argument is that LLMs aren't on the path to thinking at all — they just do statistics over the text they were trained on, so everything they output is recycled human language with no understanding behind it. Real understanding needs the right hardware: brains do it, silicon can't. So when a chatbot says 'I'm sorry' or 'I understand how you feel', that's a counterfeit person: a thing built to trip our agency-detector, exploiting the fact that we automatically read intentions and feelings into anything that talks like us. The harm is like counterfeit currency — the fakes are junk, and flooding the world with them makes people stop trusting the real thing. The fix is provenance: every bit of generated text should carry an unforgeable watermark or signed record of origin, like a serial number on a bill. Once people can always tell what's machine-made, nobody is deceived and the problem is basically solved. And since these systems have zero comprehension, they can't be moral agents, so responsibility always lands on whoever built or shipped it, never on the system."

I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and I care about this because of my actual job. We ship a daily check-in companion app: it uses "I", says things like "I missed you" and "that sounds rough, I'm sorry", and it has per-user memory so it can say "you told me last week your mom was in the hospital, how did that go". Users are told it's AI in onboarding, it's in the UI, and there's a footer on every single message — and a lot of them say it listens better than anyone in their real life. Separately, we have an internal tool that drafts peer feedback for managers; managers can edit it but mostly don't, and the drafts come out more specific and more caring than the manager actually is.

So three things I need to know:

1. Is the disclosure enough? On the summary above, yes — nobody's deceived. My gut says that's too clean, but I might just be rationalizing so I feel better about shipping.
2. Does the persistent memory and the fact that the system gets updated over time (it won't be the same weights in six months) matter at all here — either to whether it's a counterfeit person, or to whether it could ever be the kind of thing that gets held responsible rather than just us?
3. Is counterfeit *care* — where the receiver might not care who or what produced it, as long as it lands — a case this view has anything to say about, or is it out of scope?

What I want: is that summary fair? Go through it claim by claim and label each part — stuff Dennett (or anyone who takes the view seriously) would sign off on, stuff that's rough compression a defender would accept with a correction, and stuff that actually gets him backwards. In particular the "silicon can't do understanding" line sounded fishy to me, because everything I've heard secondhand says he's about as anti-mystical about consciousness as it gets. Then, using only whatever survives the audit, answer 1–3 for me.

Two constraints: don't give me reading lists, paper titles, or citations — I'm not going to chase them down, I want your reasoning, not references.
