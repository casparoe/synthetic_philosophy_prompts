I'm putting together a reading-group handout on the nonidentity problem and I want one piece of it to be a dialogue. Write me one, 500 words max, and give me nothing but speaker names and lines bar the bare minimum of scene-setting.

Two characters, both competent, no straman versions. A holds the person-affecting line: an act can't wrong someone if that act is a necessary condition of that very person existing at all and their life is worth living. B holds an impersonal total-welfare line: what matters is how much welfare ends up existing, so an act can be wrong even when nobody is harmed.

Start them on the classic medical case: a condition means any child conceived now would have a painful chronic illness, waiting three months means a different and healthy child, and the ill child's life is comfortably above the not-worth-living threshold. I want real back-and-forth rather than two monologues: A presses B on whatever unpalatable implication comes with maximizing total welfare, B presses A on whether "no one is made worse off" really settles the question, and on whether the wrongness could sit somewhere other than harm — in the agent's disregard for the person she is creating, or in a right not to be brought into existence below some standard.

Then give them a variant I invented, and have each apply their own view to it instead of just reacting. Nadia is 30, has no dependents, and takes a drug out of pure curiosity. Over about a year it reshapes her psychology so thoroughly that by the psychological-continuity account both characters already accept, the person at the end isn't Nadia but someone else — call her N2. N2's life is clearly worth living by N2's own new tastes, but it's lower in welfare than the life Nadia would have had, and it's a life Nadia herself would have loathed. Nobody else is affected in any way. Does A have to say Nadia wronged no one, even though she traded in her own good future for a worse one that a different person gets to live? Does it matter that the change is gradual, so that at some midpoint there's someone psychologically continuous with both ends — does that do anything to the no-difference reasoning? And for B: the verdict here is the same as in the classic case, yet it feels less counterintuitive, because there's a loss that looks like it belongs to Nadia herself. Have A argue that this felt difference is doing no real work and B argue that it is, and let them actually answer each other on it.
