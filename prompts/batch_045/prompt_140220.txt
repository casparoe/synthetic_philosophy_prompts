For most of my life this was a professional subject: I taught twentieth-century European history for thirty-one years and, since retiring, I have led day-long workshops for teachers at a Holocaust education center in the Midwest. Eighteen months ago it also became a family one. A cousin doing genealogy found that my grandmother's brother served in the German order police in occupied Poland in 1942, and I am now the person in my family who is expected to have an account of how ordinary men came to do what they did — for the relatives, and for my own teenagers.

I am not asking about that historical question, or about how to settle the well-known argument between the situational account associated with Browning and the cultural-dispositional account associated with Goldhagen. I have read enough of both to know that each is more careful than its reputation. I am asking about something that happens in the room when I present the material, and I have now watched it happen often enough that I want to know whether it is a thing with a name.

Three versions of it. First: the more mundane and relatable the causal mechanism I describe — a middle-aged man not wanting to be the one who lets his comrades down; career anxiety; the small steps by which a first shooting becomes a routine; the fact, by most accounts, that men who asked to be excused were allowed to step aside without punishment — the more hostile the reaction. Hostility scales with the recognizability of the mechanism, not with the strength of the causal claim. Second: the same teachers who resist that story will, ten minutes later, accept a perfectly mundane causal story about why a rescuers risked everything — she had a spare room, one thing led to another, she did not think of herself as brave — and they do not hear that story as diminishing the act at all. The resistance runs in one direction only. Third: it disappears entirely outside the moral case. Explain a student's failure by exhaustion and poor teaching and everyone nods; give the structurally identical explanation for a killing and the room goes cold, and the accusation is that I am making excuses.

The nearest thing I know from general psychology is the textbook observation that we tend to over-read other people's behavior as expressing their character. That cannot be the whole of it: my teachers are not over-reading character in the abstract, they are specifically refusing a causal story whose content they find unthreatening in every other domain, and they seem to refuse it precisely because a story of that shape would, if accepted, make the actor less blameworthy. Which suggests I may be gluing two things together — a refusal of the explanation, and an upgrading of the dispositional story — and I would like to know whether those come apart.

So: is there an established concept or literature for this? If there is, tell me what it says and where exactly it stops fitting my three cases. If there is not, say so plainly and coin a term with a definition tight enough to have boundary cases. I am especially interested in whether the phenomenon is genuinely a failure of reasoning or something more like a correct reading of what offering a causal story conversationally implies in a moral context — in a sentencing hearing, after all, a causal story really is offered as mitigation, so the recoil would be right there and wrong in a history classroom. Please also tell me whether my three cases are one phenomenon or more than one, and what would count as a case of it and as a near-miss that is really something else.

I cannot look anything up, so please do not give me citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; if you mention an author, a study, or a named concept, paraphrase what it claims rather than pointing me to it. Also be explicit about the difference between what is settled among people who work on this and what is a live dispute or your own proposal — I do not want a contested view presented to me as the consensus view, and I would like to know which parts of what you say you would expect a specialist to reject.

Finish with a markdown table, one row per candidate concept or per coinage of yours, with exactly these columns: term — the name, or your proposed coinage; one_line_definition — what the concept claims, in your own words; fit — one of exact, partial, or poor; best_matching_instance — which of my three cases it explains best, by number; misfire_case — a case it would count as an instance but which I should not count as one; epistemic_status — one of settled_terminology, live_dispute, or coinage.
