I am a PhD student in political theory, and a chapter of my dissertation uses the iterated prisoner's dilemma to model how cooperative norms could get off the ground. A member of my committee handed me what he calls the evolutionary completion of the PD, and I cannot find the broken step in it, though I am convinced the conclusion is false.

(1) In a one-shot PD, defection strictly dominates: whatever the other player does, you do better by defecting.
(2) In the indefinitely repeated PD with continuation probability w, cooperation is sustainable as an equilibrium — under grim trigger, provided w ≥ (T−R)/(T−P) — so rational choice theory does not rule out cooperation as such. The one-shot result is a limiting case, not the whole story.
(3) The dispositions that actually generate human cooperation in PD-like situations — reciprocity, reputation-sensitivity, guilt — are adaptations. Selection retains a disposition only if exercising it raised, on average, the fitness of the organisms that bore it.
(4) A payoff matrix that records only money, or only the immediate outcome for the two players, therefore omits the consequences that matter: the fitness-relevant ones. Once those are written in, cooperating in a putatively one-shot PD maximizes what the agent really has reason to want.
(5) So cooperation is rational in the one-shot case after all. The dominance argument in (1) is an artifact of an incompletely specified game, not a result about rationality.
(6) By the same reasoning, though, nothing we are disposed to do could ever be irrational. Take any disposition selection has given us — the taste for sugar, the pull of revenge, parochialism toward out-groups. By (3) exercising it was fitness-enhancing, by (4) it therefore serves our real objective, and by (5) it is rational. Some of these dispositions misfire badly in modern environments, so the conclusion is unacceptable.
(7) Worse, (5) seems to empty (1) of content: any game in which people cooperate anyway can be redescribed as not really a PD, so the model never binds.

I want to keep (1) and (2), since the game-theoretic core is what the chapter rests on, and I do not want to say that evolutionary explanations of cooperation are merely decorative. Which step do you reject, why does that step look innocent when stated the way he states it, and what does rejecting it cost me — does it force a choice between treating rationality as payoff-relative and treating it as fitness-relative, or is there a way out that leaves both (1) and the evolutionary story intact? I would also like each of the alternatives priced, not just the one you prefer.
