I'm a senior at a large public high school, and I write a fortnightly satire column for the school paper, which mostly means making fun of the announcements. Lately, though, my AP Statistics teacher spent a class on the annual international happiness rankings — the ones built from survey questions that ask people to rate their own lives on a zero-to-ten ladder, with countries then ordered by the national average. Two things he said stuck with me. First, the output everyone reads is an ordinal position: a country can climb several places without anyone in it becoming better off, if enough other countries' averages drift downward, and a move of two or three places is often indistinguishable from sampling noise given how small the national samples are. Second, the survey asks for a reflective judgment about your life as a whole, not for a reading of how you actually feel from hour to hour — so a government bent on improving its score would be optimizing a very particular object, and not obviously the one that readers of the league table have in mind. My teacher also mentioned, half-jokingly, that people's answers to ladder questions shift depending on what comparison cases they happen to be thinking about when they answer, which got me thinking about what a serious optimization effort would actually look like.

So here is what I'd like you to write. The format is a leaked internal white paper — around 700 words — from a fictional mid-sized country's Office of Wellbeing Measurement, opened for public consultation, laying out a national programme to reach the top five of the happiness rankings within ten years. Give it real civil-service furniture: a departmental header, a ministerial foreword, numbered sections, a technical annex, a consultation deadline. The voice must be deadpan bureaucratic throughout — no winking, no editorializing, no character breaking frame to tell the reader this is bad. The proposals should be genuinely clever applications of the measurement facts: redefining the sampling frame so that certain populations stop counting; a programme of "ladder familiarisation" that quietly changes how respondents interpret the scale; an initiative to supply neighbouring countries' citizens with a more realistic view of their own circumstances; a scheme that times the survey to fall during a season when the country looks good against itself; and, buried in the annex, a scrupulously honest note on statistical significance and on the difference between improving the average and improving the rank. I'd like at least one passage where the paper considers, in complete seriousness, whether it would be more efficient to change how citizens' lives are evaluated in the third person rather than to change the lives, and one where the excluded populations are invited to submit feedback during the consultation period — by mail, to an address they are not expected to reach.

The point I want the piece to land, without ever stating it, is the gap between a metric and the thing it was supposed to track: the reader should come away feeling that an office could succeed completely at this project while nothing at all improved for anyone, and should be left unsure whether the failure is in the ranking or in the decision to make it a national target. Someone who has actually thought about well-being measurement — the difference between life evaluation and momentary affect, relative versus absolute standing, who gets left out of a sample — should find the satire fair rather than merely rude. Please keep it under 800 words, keep the prose plausible as an actual government document, and don't have anyone in it named after a real person.
