ok so — my friend and I spent like three hours last night arguing about whether a set of rules can genuinely authorize its own revision, and we got nowhere, and I need to know who was closer. Her claim was that whenever a rulebook says "these rules may be changed by procedure P," there's always some further rule doing the work — the one that says *this* is the rulebook and *P* is P — and that further rule is never itself authorized by anything, so every so-called self-amending system is quietly leaning on one unamended bit. I said the regress stops somewhere in practice even if it doesn't in theory, but I definetly couldn't say where or why, and she wasn't buying it.

Reason this isn't just drunk philosophy: I'm on the five-person drafting commitee for the founding charter of a commons foundation we're starting — it'll hold and license a big shared archive (text, images, some trained models) on behalf of about ninety member organizations, and adjudicate disputes between them. None of us are lawyers, one person does logic, one writes code. We've got roughly eight weekend sessions across one academic year and that's it.

The bits we already know we're going to hit:

- the charter will exist in two layers, prose plus an executable licensing/access layer, and the two will drift apart; something has to say which one wins when they conflict, and that something is itself a provision of the charter
- half the commitee wants to entrench "the archive stays open" so it can never be sold off, but the supermajority that would violate it can presumably also repeal the entrenchment clause, so it's not obvious the clause does any work at all
- every provision sunsets after seven years unless reauthorized, which means letting something lapse is a constitutional change nobody ever proposed — amendment by expiry
- we're going to use an automated triage step (an LLM thing, honestly) to sort incoming proposals into "this is an amendment" vs "this is just an interpretation of an existing rule," which is cute because that distinction is exactly where the self-reference lives
- members can secede and take their share, and nobody can agree whether that's amendment, dissolution, or someone leaving the system altogether

So: which three or four of these should we actually spend our eight sessions on, in what order, and why? I don't want a tour of the literature — we can't read it and it wouldn't help. What I want is: what's upstream of what; what's tractable for five non-specialists in a weekend; what's cheap to punt on because we could always fix it later by amendment; versus what's expensive to punt on precisely *because* getting it wrong forecloses fixing it later; and what we'd learn in the first year of actually running the thing that should move something up or down the list. Also tell me what you think we're missing entirely, and what evidence would change your ranking.
