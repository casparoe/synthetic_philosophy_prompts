In lab journal club someone shut me down with "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence". My actual claim was pretty modest: a preregistered multi-lab replication of a surprising social-priming effect came out small but in the predicted direction (p = .04 on the planned test, Bayes factor around 3 against a deliberately skeptical prior), and I said that should move us at least a little. He said the claim is extraordinary, so .04 isn't enough, end of discussion. Can you lay out the different precise theses that sentence could be expressing — something about priors and likelihood ratios, something about error rates and where to set alpha, something about the asymmetric costs of publishing a false positive vs. missing a real effect, something about what a later failed replication should do to our confidence, something about journal editorial policy — and for each say whether it's trivial, defensible, or confused? I especially want to know which of those readings would actually get him that this result should be discounted, and wether there's any reading on which "extraordinary" does extra work beyond what a skeptical prior already does.
