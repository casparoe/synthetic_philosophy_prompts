I chair the rules committee of the Meridian Settlement Exchange, an automated venue on which firms' software agents trade with one another. The core interaction is close to a one-shot prisoner's dilemma: an agent can either honor the shared settlement protocol, which requires disclosing its true valuations to the clearing layer, or free-ride while under-reporting. At present nearly everyone free-rides. The proposal before us is to let each participant lodge its agent's source code in an escrow registry before a session, so that an agent may condition its action on the registered code of its counterparty. The simplest such rule is to honor the protocol if the counterparty's program is textually identical to one's own; the more ambitious variant has an agent honor the protocol when it can find, within a shared formal language and inside the session's time budget, a proof that the counterparty will do the same.

Our red team tested both and reports: (i) cooperation is reliable between agents that read each other's literal source, but collapses when one side is a compiled or merely behaviourally equivalent rewrite of the other, even though neither agent can be told apart from the other by anything it does in any session; and (ii) in the proof variant, cooperation holds among agents that share a verification kernel, but fails between two agents whose kernels are each sound yet find different proofs in the time allowed, including pairs each of which would have honored the protocol with an exact copy of itself.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate: adopt the registry, reject it, or adopt it with conditions, with a recommended rollout and a review date, and with reasons our members and the participating firms could actually act on. The memo must engage two objections rather than restating our mandate. First, our chief economist: a device whose entire function is to make conditional cooperation available is equally a device for making conditional punishment available, so registrants can threaten to defect against any counterparty that declines to register, which coerces participation and amounts to a cartel-formation service, the opposite of what an exchange exists to do. Second, two other members: if we regulate this at all, the rule should be behavioural, keyed to what an agent would do, not to the text of its source or to which proofs its kernel happens to find, since conditioning on syntax and on proof-search budget is arbitrary. The memo should also say what, on your recommendation, a registered agent is permitted or required to do when its counterparty has not registered.
