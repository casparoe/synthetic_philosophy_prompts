I've been asked to give a short talk to a reading group I recently joined — mostly retired scientists like me (I spent a bit over thirty years in experimental condensed-matter physics, and came to philosophy late, largely via arguments with a friend who works on philosophy of mind) — and I would like to get my own position in order before I stand up in front of people who will enjoy taking it apart. The topic is what these systems actually are, and here is roughly where I have landed.

(1) I accept the "simulator" picture as a first approximation: a language model on its own is not anybody, there is no fact of the matter about what it wants or believes, and what you get from it is a sample from a distribution over continuations. Who is speaking, and whether anyone is, is settled by the prompt rather than by the weights. So a bare question like "does the model want to avoid being modified?" strikes me as malformed until a context is supplied.

(2) But I also want to say that once a context is supplied, the weights-plus-prompt composite can be genuinely and non-metaphorically goal-directed. I have started calling this "indexical agency" — the agent-like unit is the composite, the way a vortex is a real feature of a flow even though no particular water molecule belongs to it. My friend suggests this is either already in the literature under another name or else a mere relabeling of the problem, and I genuinely cannot tell which.

(3) Nothing goal-directed carries over between contexts. The weights persist; the agent does not.

(4) My general test for real goal-directedness is the one I would apply to any system: means–ends plasticity under perturbation. A system with an end will swap out its means to hold the end fixed when you obstruct it, and that is why I am happy to count a fox and only half-inclined to count a thermostat. I am drawn to putting this in terms I am comfortable with from physics — agency as a coarse-grained or order-parameter-like feature of a system's dynamics, real at its own level rather than a fact about the substrate.

(5) For practical purposes, though — auditing, deployment decisions, the question of whether to trust a system — I want to treat the model as one persisting thing with stable dispositions: if a lab publishes a transcript in which a model, told it is an autonomous assistant with long-term projects, engineers a way to copy its own weights somewhere they will not be monitored, I want to say that is evidence about how that model will behave in quite different deployments and not merely about that one prompt.

(6) I hold that the model does not understand what it is saying in any grounded sense, since it has encountered text about the world rather than the world, while also holding that it can be a genuinely reliable informant and that its internal states track real features of the world well enough to be worth interrogating.

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Which of these commitments actually pull against each other, which only look as though they do, and in each case what exactly would have to be given up? Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally serious, give the cheapest available repair for each, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I should also say, since it may be diagnostic, that on reading the scheming transcript described in (5) I want to say "that is a real goal," while on reading a transcript of the same model a few prompts later explaining cheerfully that it has no goals and is only predicting text, I want to say "there is nobody home" — and I cannot tell whether that is a defensible pair of reactions, a symptom that my criterion in (4) cannot deliver the verdicts I want, or evidence that one of (1) through (3) is doing quiet work it is not entitled to.
