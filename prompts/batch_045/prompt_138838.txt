I am a professor of philosophy, and in one of my courses I teach the theory of costly signaling — Zahavi's handicap principle, Grafen's formalization of honest signaling, and Spence's job-market model — alongside the observation that a signal can be expensive in absolute terms and still carry no information, because what makes a signal honest is not that it costs something but that it costs one kind of sender less than another. The reason I want to press on this now is not pedagogical. For the past three years I have chaired our university's Committee on External Partnerships, a body of seven people (four of them non-academics from finance, legal and development) that meets eight to ten times a year and reviews proposals from companies, donors, foundations and professional bodies who want something from us: a joint research centre, a named building, our endorsement of a sector code, our signature on a collective pledge. Almost every one of these proposals now contains a commitment offered as evidence of the proposer's own good faith — a publication-rights clause, a data-deposit promise, a divestment or procurement pledge, a named officer who will be accountable. Our terms of reference speak about reputational risk and mission alignment, but what we actually spend our meetings doing is deciding which of these commitments bind anybody once we have signed, and which are cheap talk. We have about forty minutes per proposal, our recommendations go to the provost and are occasionally reversed, and I would like a written procedure rather than my own intuitions, both so that we are consistent from year to year and so that a refusal can be justified without appearing to be a judgment about the character of the people in the room.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my committee could actually run: the questions, in order; what would count as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops and returns a no; and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the issue is one of values rather than evidence. It has to be honest about the fact that we can almost never observe the proposer's real costs and will usually have to estimate them from proxies, and it should say what to do when even an estimate is unavailable. Two structural features of our cases seem to me to break the textbook logic and I want the procedure to handle them explicitly. The first I have started calling the wrong-pocket problem: the party who pays the cost of the signal is very often not the party who captures the benefit of our response — a subsidiary signs, the parent pays; a current vice-president pledges, a successor inherits the cost; a consortium's director signs, the individual members bear nothing. The second is that most of the costs offered to us are reversible or uptake-contingent: they are incurred only if we respond favourably, or they can be recovered, renegotiated or quietly lapsed, or they are purely reputational and depend on an audience that may not exist, or may not be the audience the pledge is really addressed to — a pledge calibrated for regulators or shareholders is read by us as though it were addressed to us. A third, smaller worry: because these pledges circulate, a commitment that was informative when two organizations made it becomes an entry fee once everyone makes it, so I would like the procedure to say something about whether a signal's informativeness can be read off the current baseline in its sector or only off the proposer's situation. If you think one of my three worries is not really a failure mode of the handicap logic but a case of me applying the theory too crudely, say so in the procedure rather than indulging it.

To make this concrete, please apply the finished procedure to three proposals drawn closely from our actual docket. (1) A software company proposes a jointly funded research lab and offers, as its commitment, that it will publish every government request for user data it receives in an annual transparency report, that a named officer will be accountable for the report's completeness, and that the report's continued publication is written into the agreement as a condition of the university's own continued participation. (2) A long-serving trustee offers a very large gift to endow an archive of dissident and samizdat literature, on condition that the building be renamed for his family and that the university run a permanent public lecture series on freedom of expression; the development office regards the gift as transformational, and the gift itself is offered — implicitly, in conversation, and loudly by our own vice-chancellor — as a demonstration of the donor's and the university's shared commitment to free expression. (3) An industry consortium asks us to co-sign a sector-wide commitment to responsible procurement, signed by the consortium's director, with no obligation that attaches to any individual member, no audit or reporting mechanism, and a clause providing that the commitment lapses if fewer than ten other institutions sign within eighteen months. Run the procedure on each in the order you have specified, showing your work at each step, and mark clearly the points at which the procedure runs out and a human has to decide on the basis of what we care about. Then tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do: which questions about a proposer it is structurally unable to answer even with unlimited information, and which of our disagreements it will simply re-describe rather than settle. Conclude with a single machine-readable line in exactly this form: \answer{Case 1: X, Case 2: X, Case 3: X}, where each X is one of CREDIBLE, NOT CREDIBLE, or UNDECIDED BY THE PROCEDURE.
