My book club read one of those popular psychology books about how nearly everyone in these studies is a Western undergraduate, and we ended up spending two evenings arguing about what that means for whether morality is real. I've been chewing on it since and I think I've landed somewhere, but I'd like someone to check whether it actually holds together before I inflict it on them again. Roughly where I am: (1) I'm a realist in a thin sense — some things are wrong whatever anyone anywhere believes, and I dont want to end up as the sort of relativist who can't say the Inquisition was wrong; (2) the cross-cultural stuff makes me distrust my own gut reactions, because if I'd grown up in a small-scale society I'd apparently have quite different intuitions about sharing and about those sacrificial dilemmas, and the story about why I have mine runs through markets, schooling and church rather than through the moral facts, so I think I should discount them; (3) but where everyone does seem to agree — prohibitions on unprovoked violence inside the group, some expectation that favours get returned, the reaction to someone hurting a child for fun — I want to say that agreement is at least weak evidence we're tracking something real, and I'd use it against the relativist; (4) I also think a fair bit of the headline variation is an artefact of how it was measured, since I suspect that when someone in a remote village is asked by a stranger whether they'd smother a crying baby to save everyone else, they're answering a question about strangers and odd requests rather than reporting a moral judgement, and that ultimatum-game offers are about local sharing norms rather than about fairness as such, so a lot of the supposed disagreement is disagreement about what's being asked; (5) I nonetheless think some of what came out of modern Western societies is genuine moral progress — the move away from honour norms where a family's standing depends on the sexual conduct of its daughters, say — and I want to call that progress and not just another local fashion; (6) so my working method is that when I notice one of my intuitions looks culturally local, I discount it and try to reason from more general impartial principles like harm and consent instead.

What I want is an audit of this package, not an essay on moral realism. Which of the six actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Two of the accusations I got I think are unfair but I can't say why properly: one friend says I'm doing exactly what I complain about, since I say you can't generalise from a room of undergraduates to humanity and then generalise from a handful of villages to a claim about morality; another says the whole project eats itself because the researchers running the cross-cultural studies come from the same unusual slice of the world as the students do. I suspect both of those dissolve on inspection, but I'd like to hear the reason.

Please give me exactly five numbered points, a couple of sentences each, ordered by how badly the problem damages my position, each one saying which commitment has to give and what giving it up would cost me. No introduction and no summing-up paragraph at the end, and please write it in British English. If one of the six is actually fine as it stands, or there's a problem I haven't listed, put that in the list too — I'd rather be told one of my worries is imaginary than have problems invented to fill the count.
