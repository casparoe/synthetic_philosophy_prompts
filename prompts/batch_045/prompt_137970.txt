I am in the final year of a philosophy degree, and this term I am taking a seminar on value theory and practical reason. We have just spent two weeks on the family of continuum, or "spectrum," arguments that have been used to attack the transitivity of "better than": chains of pairwise comparisons in which every individual step looks compelling, but whose endpoints seem to stand in the reverse order. I came away dissatisfied with both the arguments and the usual replies. The arguments lean heavily on a tolerance premise and on differences too small to be noticed; the replies — locate a sharp cut-off somewhere in the chain, reject the final comparison, or simply accept intransitivity and learn to live with it — leave untouched the question I actually care about, which is whether our comparative verdicts describe a single ordering of the options at all.

So I have tried to construct a case that reaches the same kind of result without appealing to imperceptibility, to vagueness, or to any tolerance premise, and I would like to hear two competent and genuinely opposed readers work through it.

Please write a dialogue of roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words between two unnamed members of the seminar — call them A and B — set just after the session, as they are packing up their things. They should be equally intelligent and equally stubborn, and neither should be a foil for the other. Write it as drama rather than as a set of position statements: mostly short turns, no headings, no summarising narration.

My case. A cathedral chapter must adopt exactly one of five conservation proposals, P1 to P5, for a badly degraded fresco cycle. A panel of conservators has ranked all five proposals on each of five respects taken separately, and there are no ties, no vagueness, and no dispute about where any proposal stands on any single respect. The respects are: (i) how well the proposal secures the long-term structural stability of the plaster; (ii) how faithfully it preserves the original materials and technique; (iii) how legible it leaves the surviving imagery for visitors; (iv) how completely a future generation could reverse the intervention; (v) how little it demands of the chapter's money and patience. The rankings, where 1 is best and 5 is worst:

P1: 1, 5, 4, 3, 2
P2: 2, 1, 5, 4, 3
P3: 3, 2, 1, 5, 4
P4: 4, 3, 2, 1, 5
P5: 5, 4, 3, 2, 1

P1 beats P2 on four of the five respects; P2 beats P3 on four of five; P3 beats P4 on four of five; P4 beats P5 on four of five; and P5 beats P1 on four of five. Every one of these differences is perfectly discernible. Nothing here turns on a change too small to register.

A holds that "better than" is not transitive, and that this is a discovery rather than a defect: comparative verdicts are delivered along respects, the respects are irreducibly many, and there is no further fact — no set of weights, no single underlying scale — that those verdicts are tracking. She must give a positive account of what a comparative verdict is if it is not a judgment about where two things sit in one transitive ordering, and she must tell the chapter what to do in the face of a cycle.

B holds that transitivity is non-negotiable and that the fault lies in the case: the relation that cycles here is not betterness but an impostor — a crude respect-counting heuristic; a contrast effect produced by judging each proposal against the one considered immediately before it; or a judgment of "improvement on" measured against a shifting baseline rather than of "better than." He must say which impostor it is and how to tell it apart from the real thing, he must give the chapter a determinate verdict on the five proposals — including the option of saying that some are equally good, or on a par, or incomparable — and he must explain what entitles him to overrule step-verdicts the panel found perfectly clear.

Requirements for how they argue:

Every speech must take up something specific the other has just said; no turn may simply restate a position, and neither may answer a point the other did not make.

Each of them should make at least two substantive concessions — the kind that force a small revision of their own view rather than a polite "fair enough."

Between them they should work through, without naming any philosopher, paper, or book and without quotation of any kind: whether there must be a best option among the five; whether an agent with cyclical comparative verdicts is thereby irrational or merely liable to be exploited (the kind of argument in which someone is induced to pay to trade around a cycle and end up where they began); whether admitting a third status of being "on a par" helps B or costs him as much as intransitivity would; whether the five respects are genuinely independent of one another, or whether a gain along one of them changes how much another matters; and whether any weighting of the respects is itself settled by the panel's findings or has to be imported from outside them.

A should press the point that the cycle is not merely an artifact of the table's symmetry: it survives a good many small perturbations of the rankings — swapping the entries of two neighbouring rows within a single column, for instance — so B cannot dispose of it by pointing at the symmetry alone. B must answer that, and should also be allowed to lean on the symmetry where it genuinely helps him.

Do not let them settle anything by declaring the dispute merely verbal; if either of them comes to think it is verbal, they should say what makes that verdict itself a substantive claim. By the end, the residual disagreement should be located precisely: one clearly statable claim such that if it is true A wins, and if it is false B does.
