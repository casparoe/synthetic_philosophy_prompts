My two housemates got into it the other night after dinner and I've been turning it over since. Neither of them does philosophy formally so forgive the home-made terminology, I'm reciting this as best I remember.

(1) Dan's opening: weakness of will isn't a real failure of rationality at all, it's just what time-inconsistent preferences look like from the inside. Model people as discounting the near future disproportionately steeply and everything we call akrasia falls right out: on Sunday you plan to run at 6am, at 6am the cost is immediate and the benefit is six hours away, so you don't run. No mystery and no irrationality, just a discount curve. He says the "problem of akrasia" was manufactured by assuming exponential discounting in the first place, and that the actual work is in measuring the curve and designing commitment devices.

(2) Marisol: that explains the behavior by deleting the thing that needed explaining. The puzzle was never "why does the near future loom large"; it's that the agent, at the moment of acting, still judges right then that getting up is the better thing to do, and stays in bed anyway. Preferring a good more when it gets close is just having a preference. Acting against your own concurrent all-things-considered judgment is a different animal, and hyperbolic discounting says nothing about it: it models the preference shift, not the gap between judgment and action.

(3) Dan: the concurrent cases are mostly an illusion. What the agent at 6am has is a genuine new preference; her "I should get up" is the memory of Sunday's preference, not a current one, so there's no synchronic conflict left to explain. And where someone really does resist a retained judgment, we do have a norm available: the ranking formed cold, further from the temptation, with more information, is the correct one — and the fact that people pay to bind themselves (delete the app, buy the non-refundable ticket) is revealed preference for that ranking over the hot one.

(4) Marisol: two problems. Cold and far-away are different criteria and they come apart — the guy planning his workout the night before is calm but uninformed, the guy at the gym door is informed but in the grip of the moment; which one speaks for him? And the precommitment evidence doesn't show what he wants. Wanting to be *made* to do something is perfectly compatible with genuinely preferring the other thing when the moment arrives; it shows a desire for constraint, not a verdict about which preference was the correct one. She also threw in that Dan's own framework doesn't guarantee precommitting pays: there are models where the agent who foresees her own caving does worse than the merely naive one, because she correctly predicts failure and never starts. So rational and better-off come apart in his story, and he hasn't said which one his norm was tracking.

(5) Dan: even granting the residue, it's a problem about what makes one continuing self over time, not about practical rationality — that one should be handed over to metaphysics, not used to argue the discounting model is incomplete.
