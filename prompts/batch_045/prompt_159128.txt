I'm a retired barrister in London, now doing a part-time master's in philosophy, and my reading group has just finished a stretch on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. I keep thinking that a completely ordinary feature of legal practice is already committed to a view about these matters, and I'd like help reconstructing it. Here is the practice. When an appellate court reviews a trial judge's findings of fact — above all findings about whether a witness was telling the truth — it applies a deferential standard: it will not reverse merely because the appellate judges, reading the same transcript, would have found otherwise, and the reason conventionally given is that the trial judge saw and heard the witnesses and they did not. But there are two wrinkles I can't fit together. First, the same courts refuse to let an expert — a psychologist, say — tell a jury that a complainant is credible or that a witness is lying; credibility, they insist, is for the tribunal of fact to assess for itself, and jurors are routinely told they may reject expert opinion entirely. So deference is owed to the trial judge on exactly the question on which no deference is owed to an expert. Second, the deference standard survives into cases where the appellate body has the same first-order evidence as the decision-maker: paper-only appeals against administrative decisions, and now, increasingly, appeals where the hearing below was recorded and the appellate court can watch the same demeanour the judge watched. My study-group friend suggests we distinguish evidence-transmission deference, where the trial judge passes on evidence the appellate court lacks, from judgement-deference, where the mere fact that a competent decision-maker reached a conclusion is itself treated as a reason for the reviewer; her suspicion is that the practice quietly runs on the second, and that the stated rationale about demeanour is a rationalisation.

What I want is the implicit epistemology. Is the trial judge's view being treated as first-order evidence about the world, or as higher-order evidence about the reliability of the appellate judges' own assessment of the transcript? Can the practice be justified by its own stated rationale in the recorded-hearing and paper-only cases, or does it presuppose something stronger — that a decision-maker's arriving at a belief is evidence for its truth, independent of the evidence available to her — and is that a coherent epistemic notion at all, or only an institutional one about who gets to settle a question? Where is the practice strictly committed, and where is it merely compatible with several theories that would all license it? Does it presuppose the conciliationist line on disagreement, or is it consistent with steadfastness — and note that a three-judge panel resolves its own disagreements by counting, not by each judge shaving confidence towards the dissent, which looks like neither. And what of the appellate judge who reads the transcript, concludes the trial judge got it wrong, and is nonetheless required to affirm: is she being asked to occupy an akratic state, believing one thing while taking it that the rational response is another, or does the practice only govern what she may do and what reasons she may give, leaving her beliefs alone?

Please reconstruct the view the practice presupposes, distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely suits it, say where it is underdetermined by any epistemology, and tell me concretely what would have to change in appellate practice if we replaced the presupposition with a straight evidentialist one and with a conciliationist one.
