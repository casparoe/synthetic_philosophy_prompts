I'm sitting in on a seminar on public memory this term and I keep trying out this half-invented idea on my friends, so I want to see what you do with it. Call it commemorative lock-in: the claim that a monument's meaning isn't fixed when it goes up, but is a function of its own duration and of the ongoing maintenance that keeps it standing. Three moving parts: (1) honoring is an activity rather than a one-off speech act — a statue has to be cleaned, listed, repaired, policed, used as a meeting point, and each of those is a small act of re-authorization, so the honorific part of what a statue says decays unless somebody keeps paying for it; (2) but the record part of what it says — that this community once put this person here and then kept him there for a century and a quarter — only accumulates, and after long enough the object stops being chiefly about its subject and becomes chiefly about us, so the age of a monument changes what the thing even is and therefore what's at stake in removing it; (3) erection and removal are asymmetric — putting a statue up is a cheap one-time act by whoever happens to win the committee fight that year, while taking it down is expensive, dramatic and irreversible, so leaving it alone is never the neutral default it presents itself as. I like it because it's basically path dependence applied to bronze, but I can't tell if it's a real theory or just a way of winning any argument.

So run the Colston statue in Bristol through it, and please be disciplined about the mapping rather than just opining. Facts as I remember them (correct me only if something load-bearing is wrong): bronze figure of Edward Colston, Bristol merchant, senior figure in the Royal African Company in the 1680s and a big local philanthropist, unveiled 1895 on a Portland stone plinth whose inscription called him one of the most virtuous and wise sons of the city; abolitionists were apparently among the alternative subjects proposed, and the Society of Merchant Venturers pushed hard for Colston; Grade II listed in 1977; from the 1990s growing demands to remove it; Bristol City Council's 2018 plan to add a second, contextualising plaque bogged down for years in fights over the wording; on 7 June 2020, during the George Floyd protests, protesters pulled the statue down, defaced it and rolled it into the harbour, leaving the plinth in place covered in graffiti; the council fished it out, and it went on show at the M Shed museum still graffitied in 2021 and then permanently from 2024; four people tried for criminal damage were acquitted by a jury in January 2022; and in spring 2025 a new plaque went on the empty plinth, dropping the description of Colston as a city benefactor and explaining his removal instead. Who counts as the authorizing community at each of those stages, what would count as an act of re-authorization or as its withdrawal, and where does the theory say the honor actually lived at each point? Does it explain why the plaque route failed, and does it imply the plaque route should have been made to work anyway? What does it predict about the graffitied statue now permanently installed in the M Shed — a successfully de-honored object, or one that drifts back toward honor? And does the same story go through for street names, which my friend says are a completely different case because a street sign doesn't look like anybody — is he right, or is it the same lock-in with a much cheaper exit?

Then I want the case to push back on the theory, since that's the part my seminar keeps skipping. Does the 125 years actually do any work here, or would the identical analysis go through for a monument put up in 1920 that everyone still remembers the point of? Is the theory just flexible enough to rationalise any outcome — keep it, topple it, museumify it, re-plaque it — and thereby say nothing? Be explicit about where it goes silent: on standing (who gets to decide, and what makes them the relevant community), on the criminal damage question (I read the acquittal as a jury unwilling to treat the statue's continued presence as a protected interest, but maybe that's just ordinary protest-rights reasoning and has nothing to do with monuments), on what to do when the authorizing community and the living community come apart. And finally set it against the flat present-tense view that says none of the history matters and only the message the object sends right now is morally relevant — which one handles this case better, and can you think of any case that would actually separate the two?
