I'm running the MU-puzzle session again for our undergraduate reading group next week — ninety minutes, whiteboard, about fifteen people, a mix of CS and philosophy, everyone's done some propositional natural deduction but nobody's seen anything metamathematical — and the reading is the Hofstadter chapter with the MIU system (axiom MI; four rules: add a U to the end of any string ending in I; double everything after the M; replace any III with a U; delete any UU) and then all that business about jumping out of the system at the end. I've run it three times and it always derails the same way: they pick the rules up instantly, spend twenty minutes churning out strings, somebody notices the number of I's never lands on a multiple of three, and from there the session splits into either "so the system is broken" or "so, like, nothing can be proved, man". What I want isn't an explanation of the puzzle — I know the mod-3 argument fine — it's a plan for how to teach it: the order the moves have to come in and why, specifically where (if anywhere) the jump-out framing should be named out loud rather than just demonstrated, and where I should show that the outside proof is itself a piece of mathematics that lives somewhere (induction over the length of a derivation, residue classes mod 3), because I'd of thought the thing that actually wrecks the session is students concluding that "outside" means mysticism, or that if reasoning about a system needs another system then it's turtles all the way down and nothing was really established.

The other thing I keep fiddling with is the carrying example. Instead of opening with MU itself I was going to start them on a tiny invented system over two symbols, say ● and ▲ — axiom ●, and rules: from x derive ▲x; from x derive x▲▲; from x derive x●x — so the invariant is that the number of ●s stays odd and the target ●● is unreachable. But I'm not sure it's the right thing to hang the whole session on: does it need to be a residue-class invariant rather than a plain "stays odd" one for the move to the I-count-mod-3 to feel like the same trick instead of a brand new one, does the fact that none of my rules ever change the count make the transfer too easy, and what does the example have to have and lack so it doesn't teach the wrong lesson — that unreachability is always a counting fact, or that invariants are just something you spot if you stare hard enough?

Here's my guess at what they'll get wrong; tell me where in the sequence to head each off and which of these I've got wrong or am missing: that M, I and U mean something (multiplication, numbers, whatever); that if they can't find a derivation in ten minutes it's probably impossible; that the rules ought to run backwards (why can't U turn back into III); that rule two should apply to any chunk of the string and not only everything after the M; that the mod-3 observation is a trick rather than a proof — and inside that one, the gap between "every rule preserves the property" and "every theorem has the property", which needs induction over derivations and is where I think the real jump is; that the system is therefore defective or inconsistent; that unprovability proved from outside is somehow less certain than a derivation. I also want the closing check to be one where they can't tell in advance which kind of answer is even being asked for — hand out a fresh system plus a target and make them decide whether the job is "derive it" or "prove it can't be derived" — so tell me what that system and target would have to look like for the check to actually separate someone who's got it from someone who's just memorised the mod-3 move.

Keep it tight and in this order: "The sequence" (~500 words), "The carrying example" (~250), "Misconception map" (~300), "The check" (~150), "What I refuse and how I say so" (~150). In that last one: I don't want to get into arithmetisation or the actual incompleteness proof, and there's a question one of them always asks — whether there's any general test that decides, for an arbitrary string, whether it's derivable — where I gather there is in fact a clean criterion (starts with M, nothing but M/I/U, and the I-count isn't a multiple of three) but the interesting direction is a construction I'm not doing in front of them; how do I handle that honestly without derailing? And tell me where the analogy to Gödel stops paying — my sense is the puzzle gives you a proof of unprovability from outside, which isn't the same shape as a sentence of the system itself that's true but unprovable, and that MIU has no interpretation in which MU could be true in the first place — and whether any of that belongs in a session like this or just washes over them.
