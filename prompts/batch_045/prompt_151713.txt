Hi — I could use your help drafting a memo. Bit of background first so you know who's asking.

I'm a retired field hydrologist (thirty-some years measuring streams and arguing about flood frequency curves), and since I stopped working I've gotten into reading philosophy on my own, mostly at night, which is somehow how I ended up on the board of a small private foundation. We hand out a few million a year, nearly all of it for boring, measurable public-health work in two countries. There's one discretionary "frontier" line, about $150,000, and this year we've argued ourselves into a standstill over it. I've been asked to circulate a written opinion before the meeting on the 14th, and I don't want to blow it.

Two applications left in the pile.

The first is ordinary: $150k to a screening-and-treatment program we've funded twice before, with independent follow-up data. Their own numbers, which we trust to within maybe a factor of two, come out to roughly 2,000 severe cases prevented. Nothing exciting, nobody will remember it.

The second is two people who want the same money for a pilot early-warning sentinel network for novel pathogens with pandemic potential. Their case is explicitly expected-value: they put it at maybe 1 chance in 10,000 that this particular pilot is the thing that triggers a response averting a pandemic killing on the order of 200 million people, which lands at something like 20,000 expected lives saved — ten times the boring program. When we pushed on where the 1-in-10,000 came from, they said, honestly, that it's their own model, that nobody else has built one, and that if we don't fund it nobody will.

Two more things that have the board properly stuck. First: we recieved forty-one applications of this shape this cycle. Tiny probability, enormous payoff, own model, no track record. The claimed payoffs got larger as the deadline approached and the biggest numbers arrived last. If we fund the highest expected value, we're funding a claim; if we fund the boring program, we look like we ignored 20,000 expected lives because a big number made us squeamish. Second: one member said that if a pandemic were really avertable for $150k, somebody with vastly more money would already be doing it, and another member said that argument is worthless because plenty of important things go unfunded for dumb institutional reasons and you can't read odds off other people's inertia.

I've been reading about this general shape of problem on my own — Pascal's-mugging type cases, and the worry about "fanaticism" — and my instinct is a hydrologist's one: the bigger the event you claim, the further out on the tail you are, the less the record constrains you, and a curve whose answer is driven by its single largest and least-checked input isn't an estimate, it's a story. I also think something is wrong with any rule under which whoever names the largest number gets the money, year after year. But I dont want to win this on my own hobby argument. The two people I respect most on the board are on the other side:

- Dana: for any discount you like, there is *some* probability at which the pilot wins, and we can't honestly say the true probability is below it. Our discomfort with large numbers isn't evidence about the world.
- Marcus: fine, but the unit isn't this one decision, it's the policy we'll apply for the next thirty years, and a policy that always pays the most astronomical claim on offer will spend every future dollar on claims — and will start attracting them.

Please draft the memo I circulate. What I want in it:

- A concrete recommendation with a time frame: fund A, fund B, split it (someone floated giving B $15k purely to buy a sharper probability estimate and holding the rest until they report back), or adopt something standing and decide under it.
- A rule or test the board could genuinely apply again next year, not just this once. I've been kicking around a few and want you to say which survive and which are junk: discounting a payoff when the probability estimate came from the same people who'd benefit; asking whether our $150k is really the binding constraint or whether other money would do the work anyway; treating all forty-one longshots as a single portfolio so the astronomical claims compete with each other instead of each one separately swamping the boring program; and some way of thinking about what the money buys in *information* rather than in impact.
- The strongest version of whichever side loses, written well enough that Dana or Marcus would recognize it as their own argument and not a caricature.
- Plain American English, no jargon I'd have to explain to the others, and please don't lean on the names of philosophers or papers — nobody else on this board has read any of it and a reading list would just irritate them.

Thanks — and if you think I've framed the whole thing wrong, say so in the memo rather than quietly fixing it.
