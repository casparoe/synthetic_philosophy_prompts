ok so I just had a genuinely long arguement with my friend about Ian Hacking's looping effects and I can't tell if she was right or just being annoying. Her claim was basically: when a diagnosis stops getting used — fugue, hysteria, neurasthenia, whatever — that's just progress, we noticed the thing wasn't a real disorder and stopped sticking the label on people. I kept saying no, that's not what Hacking's picture gives you, because human kinds aren't like that: the classification and the people classified feed into each other, people change in response to being classified, and the classification then has to change again. And a kind sticks around only as long as there's an ecological niche for it — some available way of behaving, a cultural story, an institutional setting, a way to be a person that the label opens up. Pull the niche and the kind doesn't get refuted, it just becomes uninhabitable, which is a much weirder kind of disappearance than "we were wrong." She said that framing makes it sound like a conservation issue and I said yes, exactly, and now I want to see how far that goes.

So: write me a direct-mail fundraising appeal, 700-ish words, from a made-up nonprofit called something like the Society for the Preservation of Endangered Human Kinds. Three parts — a letter from the director, a tiered sponsorship catalog ("adopt a fugue," giving levels, a photo caption), and a short sidebar. The voice should be exactly the warm, despairing, deadpan earnestness of a wildlife-conservation mailing: habitat loss, population counts, reintroduction programs, legacy gifts. The jokes have to come from getting the actual view right — a diagnostic kind as a species with habitat requirements, the niche as its ecosystem, "reintroduction" as the attempt to rebuild a niche rather than to discover anything, the archival of kinds whose last bearers have died. And there's one thing the brochure should keep almost but not quite admitting: you can't preserve a kind of person without making up some people to be it, and nobody can say whether that's a harm to them or the best thing that ever happened to them. Nobody in the piece is allowed to state that in plain words. Keep it in the euphemisms — "kind-bearer recruitment," "ethics-reviewed reintroduction," "the posterity cohort," that sort of thing — so a reader who knows the literature feels the problem in their gut and a reader who doesn't just feels uneasy. Don't wink, don't add a winking footnote, don't turn it into an essay at the end.

One more wrinkle I want in there, because it was her second objection and I think it's actually the funniest part: she said the whole project is self-undermining now, since any reintroduced kind would be loop-aware — its bearers would've read Hacking, would know they're inhabiting a deliberately revived category, and that knowledge is itself part of the loop. I told her that's not a refutation, it's a loop one order up, and that the Society should just sell it as a premium feature. So make the flagship reintroduction project a "heritage kind," officially certified loop-aware, marketed on the strength of its feedback-stability plan — the pitch has to make clear-eyed self-knowledge about looping sound like an upgrade, a mark of authenticity, without ever conceding that it's a problem. Bonus if the sidebar is about a kind that got *too* successful and now has to be managed like an invasive species.
