I'm a fourth-year PhD student in intellectual history, and I'm convening the final session of our graduate reading group on early modern political thought. We're spending it on the political fragments of Pascal's *Pensées* — the argument that justice is endlessly disputable while force is not, that the two somehow have to be welded together, and that, because we could not give justice any force, we ended up giving force the name of justice. I'd like to close the session with a poem, read aloud rather than handed out, and I've come to think it has to be a villanelle: the form's whole machinery is two lines that keep coming back and never merge, which is exactly the shape of that argument.

So: a full nineteen-line villanelle, six stanzas, with the two refrains carrying the two poles of Pascal's formula — one the claim that justice without force is helpless, the other that force without justice is mere tyranny. Put them in your own words; I don't want a translation of anything, no epigraph, no footnotes, and Pascal shouldn't be named anywhere in the poem. The refrain lines should feel like they answer and cancel each other as they take turns: each time one returns, it should arrive in a context that has made the other one harder to restate plainly.

The voice should be recognizably the *Pensées'* own — clipped antitheses, paradox, sudden concreteness, a certain impatience with eloquence — not a lyric effusion and not the devotional first person of the religious fragments. And the argument should be rendered closely enough that someone who knows the fragments could trace it line by line: that what counts as just shifts with a river or a mountain range; that some codes have reckoned as virtuous what ours call crimes; that custom grounds authority for no better reason than that it is accepted; that the brigand who seizes an estate becomes, with time and inheritance, a lawful sovereign whose title no one examines; that justice is contested and force is not, which is why force gets to wear justice's name; that one therefore obeys the law as law, and that private judgment of injustice cannot license disobedience, since what lies on the other side of that liberty is not justice but civil war. There is also a pressure the poem has to hold rather than discharge: Pascal is not a relativist — he thinks there is a real justice that our corrupted reason cannot reach — so convention has to come through as a grim necessity, not as a punchline, and the poem must not quietly resolve the dilemma by appealing to God as a fix.

Structurally, I'd want the first tercet to set the two poles against each other, the middle stanzas to work through why justice cannot establish itself (the variation of laws, custom as the sole equity, the legitimized usurper), and the closing quatrain to do the real work: let its two non-refrain lines state the reversal — that we could not make the just strong, so we made the strong just — so that the two refrains, arriving as the last two lines immediately after it, land differently than they did the first time, as a description of how things actually stand rather than as an open question. If you can manage it without forcing, I'd also like the poem to leave the reader with the sense that this argument has the same skeleton as the wager: you cannot know the thing, and you have to act anyway. But don't say so.
