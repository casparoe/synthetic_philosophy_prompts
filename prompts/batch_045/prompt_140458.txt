I teach a twelve-person seminar on rational choice for upper-division philosophy and economics majors. It meets once a week for seventy-five minutes, there are no screens in the room, and I would rather not open with a lecture. Six weeks ago I was elected to the board of my condominium association — seven owners, and we decide everything as a series of separate yes-or-no items. Twice now I have watched us vote, item by item, into a package of commitments that I do not think a single one of us would have endorsed as a whole. So I want a session in which students feel in their own ledgers what it is for a set of commitments to be exploitable, and then, more importantly, feel what it is for a group of individually unimpeachable people to become exploitable as a group.

Two framing constraints. First, the point has to arrive through the rules and the payoffs, not through anything I say at the front of the room: the words coherent, exploit, axiom, and book should not be uttered until after the settlement. Second, I want the vulnerability to be visibly a matter of the internal relations among a person's or a group's own commitments. Whoever plays the house should not know anything the players do not — no peeking at the composition of the bag, no special information about the world.

Here is what I have in mind, roughly, and where I need help.

Phase one: individuals. An opaque bag contains thirty balls, each red, blue, or green, in a composition that is sealed in an envelope and not revealed during trading; one ball will be drawn at the end. For each of a handful of tickets that pay a fixed number of points if the drawn ball is a given color, or one of two given colors, each student writes down a single number: the price at which they are willing either to buy the ticket or to sell it. I then trade against them. Please design the price schedule, the ticket menu, the order in which trades are offered, and a way of having several students act as separate brokers so that no one sees the full package being assembled. I want the ledger settled arithmetically, before the draw, so that students discover the outcome is already fixed — and then, I think, draw anyway, because the anticlimax is part of the lesson. Tell me how to stage that beat.

Phase two: committees. Students form committees of three. Each member receives a private card with a full assignment of personal prices over the three colors, and each card is internally consistent. The committee does not deliberate toward consensus; for each proposed trade with the house, members vote by sealed ballot, and the committee takes the trade if and only if a majority votes yes. I have sketched one set of three cards that I think does the trick — assignments of roughly 0.6, 0.2, 0.2 with the mass placed on a different color in each card, and three tickets on red-or-blue, red-or-green, and red — where the group's majority prices come apart in a way that no card does. Check my numbers, fix them if they are wrong, and show the arithmetic of the resulting ledger and of each member's private objection. The properties I want are: every card is consistent on its own; for each of the three trades there is a majority in favor; and no single member voted yes to all three. If three-person committees make this too easy to spot, tell me what changes with five.

Phase three: the committee is told its position is losing no matter what happens, and is invited to repair it — to revise its prices or to change how it decides. Please design two or three rounds of this, including a buyout offer, so that the defect shows up as a disposition that can be re-exploited rather than a one-time event, and so that the question of what decision rule would make the group immune arises from the students rather than from me. I would like the debrief to state precisely what such a rule would have to guarantee, and to be honest about what familiar rules give up: for instance, what is lost by pooling members' numbers through averaging, and what is lost by taking a majority or a middle value on each ticket separately.

Debrief. I need a mapping from specific moments in the session to specific parts of the idea, including the moment where a student realizes the house knew nothing about the bag. I also need an honest list of what will predictably go wrong: students refusing to name a single number and instead giving a spread; students treating the exercise as a guessing game about the bag's composition rather than a report of their own confidence; someone working out the trick early and warning the room; and the awkward fact that a person can be untouched by all of this if they simply decline to trade or do not care about points. Say how to handle each, and say what the two-sided single-price setup is quietly assuming about attitudes to risk and about the size of the stakes, with a practical fix.

Finally: be frank about what the session cannot deliver. In particular, I suspect it cannot show that the exploitable party is being irrational in any sense other than being liable to lose, and I do not see how to stage, in one session, the version of the argument that concerns how commitments should change over time. Name what I will have to supply myself. And give me a stripped-down twenty-minute version I could run with my board over a bottle of wine, plus one sentence I could say to them that is not you are being irrational.
