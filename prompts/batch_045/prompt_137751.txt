I need a case built to order on the precautionary principle, and built so that it isolates one thing rather than winning by accumulation. I do not want authorities, citations, or case law: the argument has to stand on the case itself.

Work with this formulation, and do not amend it or substitute a better one: where a course of action carries a risk of serious or irreversible harm, the absence of full scientific certainty about that risk is not by itself a sufficient reason for declining measures against that harm.

What I want is a case in which this formulation, applied honestly to both arms of a binary choice, initially forbids both — I want the symmetry, or paralysis, objection to have genuine bite — and in which the paralysis is then dissolved by one structural feature of the options, so that the principle yields a determinate verdict: one arm required, and the other either forbidden or at most permitted.

Constraints on the scenario:

1. Realistic and contemporary: a decision a real public agency, hospital committee, or utility could face this year, set out in a single paragraph. No science fiction, no civilizational or existential stakes, no runaway technology, no infinite utilities, no devices whose only function is to make the case work.
2. Both arms must trigger the clause. Each carries a risk of serious and irreversible harm, and the uncertainty about each risk is genuine. A case in which only one side triggers it is worthless to me.
3. Epistemic symmetry: the uncertainty on the two sides must be of the same kind and to the same degree — the same kind of evidence, the same width of interval, the same residual ignorance. The trigger has to fire twice, so uncertainty cannot be what separates the arms.
4. Comparable magnitudes: the probabilities must sit in one ordinary band (say 0.5 to 15 per cent over the decision horizon), and both harms must be expressible in a single currency — statistical lives, quality-adjusted years, expected school years, whatever fits the setting. I want no help from a dramatic magnitude gap and none from incommensurability.
5. Framing-invariance: the verdict must not depend on whether the "do nothing" arm is described as an omission or as an active policy. Show me the case under both descriptions and confirm that the verdict is stable across them.
6. Proportionality, cost-effectiveness, and burden-of-probation — burden of proof — considerations may enter only after the trigger has fired on both sides. If in fact one of them is carrying the entire argument, say so plainly; that is a permitted thing to report and not a permitted way to win.

Two things I have already tried and rejected, so that you do not hand them back to me. A doing/allowing asymmetry looked promising and then collapsed under re-description: whether the status quo counted as continuing a practice or as adopting a policy of continuing turned out to be settled by nothing more than how the governing statute happened to be drafted. And a bare appeal to "learning more before we act" is value-of-information reasoning in costume; if reversibility or option value is your resolving feature, it must be stated as a fact about the options — what is recoverable, by when, at whose discretion, at what cost — rather than as an atmosphere.

What I want back:

The scenario, one paragraph.

The derivation: the clause applied to arm A, applied to arm B, the paralysis made explicit, and then the feature that dissolves it, with the resulting verdict stated.

The numbers, set out where I can check them against constraints 3 and 4.

The removal test: the smallest perturbation of the case that returns it to paralysis, and what that tells us about which feature is load-bearing.

The hostile reading: how someone who holds that the precautionary principle is either an idle redescription of risk-weighted cost-benefit analysis with the burden of proof quietly shifted, or else irreducibly paralytic, would characterise what your case actually establishes — and whether the case answers that charge or merely outlaws it by construction.

If the specification cannot be met as written, say so, and tell me which clause has to give.

The point of the exercise, so that you know what counts as cheating: I am trying to establish whether the principle has any content that an ordinary expected-utility calculation lacks. A case that reaches its verdict through the size of the harms, through a definition, or through a clause I did not give you establishes nothing I care about.
