You are a philosopher who specialises in Nietzsche's early period and in the philosophy of collective memory. I'd like you to work slowly and in stages: before answering, go through the cases below one at a time, sketch two or three candidate principles for sorting them, state explicitly which candidate you rejected and why, and only then give me the principle you want to defend. I am less interested in a summary of the essay On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life than in what its distinctions do when applied to cases Nietzsche never imagined.

Here are my verdicts on six practices, delivered the way they strike me rather than the way a theorist would want them.

One. A former mining town in the Ruhr valley has kept the headframe of its last pit. Each year, on the Sunday nearest the anniversary of the closure, a rota of volunteers recites from memory the names of the 412 men who died in that pit between 1871 and 1986, in the order in which they died. It takes close to two hours. Almost nobody present knew any of the men. My verdict: this is the good case. Nothing comes of it; no policy follows; the town's unemployment is untouched; and it seems to me that its uselessness is part of what makes it good. The objection I keep hearing — that it is ancestor-worship with no forward-looking content — leaves me cold.

Two. A county genealogical society maintains a record of every person born in the county since 1800, roughly ninety thousand entries, including stillborn and unnamed infants, on the stated principle that no life is too small to be recorded; members are asked to adopt at least one forgotten ancestor and to visit that grave once a year. My verdict: I find something close to repulsive in this, and I don't think it is merely the scale. But I cannot say what the difference is, since it looks like exactly the loving attention to the unremembered that I just praised in the Ruhr case.

Three. A biotech firm I consulted for keeps a Wall of Discontinued Compounds: a framed card for every compound it abandoned, each with the project team's photograph and one line about what was learned. The chief executive calls it our institutional courage and says it licenses risk. My verdict: I dislike it more than I can justify. It is plainly intended as monumental history — models held up for emulation — but the models are of failure. Does the monumental mode have any room for exemplars of failure, or does the category collapse without greatness? And if it does have room, why do I still dislike the wall?

Four. My doctoral department awards the Kestrel Prize each year to the dissertation that most decisively refutes a published claim of its own supervisor's, and keeps in a glass case in the common room the overturned claims themselves: a shelf of some forty pieces of dead work. My verdict: I love this and have defended it in faculty meetings. Yet I suspect that turning intellectual parricide into a ceremony changes its character, and specifically that a department which rewards overturning will start producing overturnings to reward, which liberates nobody.

Five, in two parts. (a) A city hospital named for a nineteenth-century benefactor who made his fortune in the slave trade: after a three-year public inquiry, a published report and a series of public meetings, the trustees removed the name and commissioned a permanent wall text setting out what the inquiry found. My verdict: this is genuinely critical history and the right outcome. (b) A residential college whose head removed its founder's name from the website, the stationery and the degree certificates by administrative memo, left the 1931 carved dedication in the gatehouse standing because the stonework is listed, and commissioned no account of what the founder actually did. My verdict: this is the opposite of critical history, a gesture that borrows its shape without any of its substance. My brother, who sits on the governing body, says I am being unfair: the outcomes are nearly the same, the process is invisible to anyone actually affected, and my insistence on an inquiry is procedural fussiness dressed up as principle. I don't have a reply to him that I believe.

The principle I keep returning to is this: a practice of historical memory is healthy just when it increases the plastic power of the person or community engaged in it — the capacity to grow out of oneself, to take what is past and foreign into oneself and transform it — and unhealthy when it either freezes the past into a model to be imitated or dissolves it into a collection to be venerated; critical history is the special case where growth requires a violent break, and it is healthy only when those who break can then make something new. Please state that principle, or a better one, precisely; say which of my cases motivates each clause; tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which verdict I would have to give up. Note that as it stands it seems to misfire on cases one and two: both look like pure preservation, and pure preservation is what I have just called unhealthy, yet I bless one and damn the other.

Then apply it to two cases where I genuinely don't know what to say. First: the chamber choir I sing in forbids any recording of its performances, on the director's stated ground that a recorded performance becomes an object to be judged rather than an event to be had. I want to call this the unhistorical in Nietzsche's sense, but I am not sure: refusing to record is not the same as forgetting, and the ban might be nothing more than a superstition about authenticity. Second: a city has commissioned a memorial to a wartime atrocity that is designed, by the material it is made of, to erode completely within fifty years, so that the monument's disappearance is part of the work. Is that the healthiest possible object of memory, or is it an evasion?

Finally, two things I want you to be explicit about. Say what would change your mind: which clause of your principle you are least confident in, and what argument or case would lead you to abandon it. And mark clearly, as you go, where you are expounding what the essay says and where you are using Nietzsche for something of your own — I would rather know which is which than be told a tidy story.
