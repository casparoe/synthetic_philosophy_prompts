My uncle died in April and left a bit of money to a small community fund, and somehow I ended up as one of five trustees. We hand out small grants monthly. Our procedure is very un-quantitative: we sort proposals into three tiers (fund in full / part-fund / decline) using a rough five-point scale, we never put dollar values on outcomes or assign probabilities, we have an by-law saying 15% of the pot is never to be risked, and we revisit declines after six months. Our treasurer Marta, who used to price insurance, sent me an argument that we aren't just leaving money on the table but are being outright irrational, and maybe breaching our fiduciary duty. I can't find the hole, and I can't accept the conclusion:

(1) A decision procedure is irrational if it can be reliably exploited: if there's a sequence of trades, each of which the procedure would freely accept, that leaves the agent with something it itself ranks lower than what it started with, and the agent can see this coming.
(2) Our procedure isn't expected-utility maximization. It treats some pairs of options as incomparable rather than as equally good, it refuses to convert the reserve into a gamble no matter what the odds are, and its answer to "is A better than B?" can flip when we revisit six months later.
(3) So by the standard money-pump and representation results, there is a sequence of individually acceptable moves that would leave the fund with less of what it wants, and we could see it coming.
(4) Rationality for a group is just rationality for a person. The fund is the agent, and if it would be irrational for one trustee with one utility function to do X, it's irrational for the five of us to do X.
(5) And what we actually do is take majority votes over tier rankings, which can cycle even when every member's own rankings are perfectly consistent and transitive. We did exactly that last month and broke the tie by flipping a coin.
(6) Therefore our procedure — and any procedure remotely like it — is irrational, and the only rational arrangement is a single decision-maker with one sharp utility function and one sharp probability over everything: no tiers, no untouchable reserve, no revisiting. On this view the five-trustee by-law is a rule requiring us to act irrationally.

I'm not giving up the by-law. Which step do you reject? If I have to choose I'd most like to keep (1), since it's the only thing that makes "fiduciary duty" mean anything at all, and I'd like to keep (5), because that really did happen and nobody in the room felt irrational. Marta says that if I reject (4) I can only ever criticize individual trustees and never a decision of the fund, which sounds like a bad place to end up. Name the step, say why it looked fine, and price the alternatives.

Give me the diagnosis in prose, then a table with one row per step and these columns: step | verdict (keep / reject / reinterpret / genuinely unsure) | why the step looked fine | what I pay if I give it up | what it commits me to downstream. Finish with a single line: the one change I should actually make to how we run the meeting.
