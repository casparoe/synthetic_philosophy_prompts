Take the "Curveball" episode in the run-up to the 2003 invasion of Iraq and analyze it as a case in the epistemology of testimony, specifically through the dispute between reductionists, who hold that a hearer's warrant from testimony must be built out of the hearer's own non-testimonial evidence, and anti-reductionists, who hold that testimony is a basic source of warrant whose default entitlement is displaced only by specific defeaters. The facts, which take as given: an Iraqi émigré codenamed Curveball told German intelligence from 1999 onward that Iraq was operating mobile biological-weapons laboratories; American agencies had no direct access to him and received only filtered, partly translated reports relayed by the German service; several of his own handlers and some American officers questioned his reliability, his competence, and his background; his claims nevertheless propagated through more than a hundred classified reports and into the Secretary of State's February 2003 presentation to the UN Security Council; and the Iraq Survey Group later concluded that they were false.

Analyze it in both directions. First, apply the views: the story passed down a chain — source, case officer, translation, analyst, senior official — in which arguably no link had first-hand evidence of the source's reliability, so say which links, if any, were in a position to believe justifiably, what each view would have demanded of them, and where each view differs from the other and from ordinary care about evidence. Distinguish carefully between the two things reductionism can mean here: the global thesis that testimonial warrant is always constructed out of perception, memory, and observed regularities, and the local thesis that a hearer needs some evidence specifically about this speaker's competence and sincerity in this domain, including his incentives.

Second, let the case test the dispute. Three of its features seem to me ones the debate is not built for, and I want to know whether they tell against either view or merely fall outside it: the final hearer is an institution with no single body of evidence; the speaker's incentives to assert are themselves independently knowable, which may let the local reductionist absorb the whole episode and thereby collapse into plain evidential reasoning; and what mattered at the end was not only what was believed but what was asserted publicly and what was done, under time pressure and at irreversible cost. Where the two views converge, say so rather than manufacturing a disagreement; where the real failure is one of institutional deference, motivated reasoning, or the ethics of assertion rather than of testimonial warrant, say that too. This is an epistemic post-mortem, not a political one, and not a survey of the literature — end by saying what the episode should make one think about reductionism versus anti-reductionism.
