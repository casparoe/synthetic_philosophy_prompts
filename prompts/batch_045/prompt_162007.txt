The thesis, and the argument I plan to sketch for it.

The standard argument I am responding to: in a chaotic system nearby trajectories separate at an exponential rate, so any non-zero error in the specification of the initial state is amplified past any fixed tolerance within a finite time; a system can therefore be deterministic and yet unpredictable in practice, and determinism does not deliver prediction.

My objection to the standard argument is that it measures error in the wrong currency. It measures error in the full state, under some metric on state space. But what anyone predicts is an observable: a real-valued function of the state, such as a regional average temperature, a peak amplitude, the sign of one component, a time-average of some quantity over a fixed window, or which of several attractors the system settles on. Two states can be arbitrarily far apart in the state-space metric while agreeing to within tolerance on the observable in question, and states close together can disagree widely on it. Exponential growth in state-space distance therefore yields an upper bound on the growth of error in an observable, not its value, and the bound can be very loose indeed.

Four consequences I want to defend. First, there is no such thing as the predictability horizon of a system; there is a family of horizons, indexed by observable, tolerance and metric, and the ordering of two systems by "how predictable they are" is not stable under a change of observable. Second, the philosophical claim that determinism does not give prediction has a suppressed argument-place: sensitive dependence and positive Lyapunov exponents are properties of a system together with a metric on its state space, while predictability is a property of a system together with an observable and a tolerance, so the claim is really about determinism plus a predictive interest. Third, since observables are typically what we measure directly, predicting an observable need not proceed by estimating the whole state and integrating it forward; the quantity may have dynamics of its own, or many distinct initial states may be compatible with the same observable history. The demon should therefore be modelled as a predictor of observables rather than of states, and "could a demon predict this system?" should be replaced by "could a demon predict this observable?", which can have a different answer for the same system. Fourth, the methodological moral: chaos is neither necessary nor sufficient for the practical failure of determinism. Long-run averages and other statistical features of a chaotic system can remain predictable long after individual trajectories have become untraceable, while a system with no positive exponent along the relevant trajectories can still make an observable — say, which of several very finely interleaved basins of attraction the state falls into — unpredictable to within any tolerance anyone would find useful.

Two claims I explicitly do not make, and which I expect to be accused of making: that coarse-graining of the kind familiar from statistical mechanics rescues predictability (my point is that the choice of observables does work before any coarse-graining of the dynamics takes place), and any general anti-realism about dynamical properties.
