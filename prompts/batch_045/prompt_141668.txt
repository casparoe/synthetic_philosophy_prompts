I teach a 75-minute seminar on reasoning under uncertainty, about eighteen upper-division students. I want a runnable in-class game that hands them the doomsday argument without anyone naming it: no mention of birth ranks, human extinction, or the standard urn version until the debrief.

Set it up with a scaled-down surrogate so that no real population figures are needed: two hypotheses about the total size of some population, an explicitly announced prior, and one number handed privately to each student, drawn uniformly from 1 to the true total, on which they bet or trade. One constraint I care about: I want a typical card to be genuinely ambiguous, so the round does not collapse because a third of the room received numbers larger than the small total allows. If honest uniform draws cannot deliver that without flattening the likelihood ratio to nothing, say so in a design note, state which feature of the argument you traded away, and use the version you think teaches best anyway.

Two more mechanisms I want built in. First, a second sealed note, opened after the first betting round, telling each student which pool their number was drawn from: some drew from the population itself, others from a pool that also contains a large fixed bloc present in equal numbers under both hypotheses, which should wash their evidence out toward no shift at all. They re-bet, and the room has to say whether the earlier confidence lived in the numbers or in a choice of pool. Second, two students hold cards at the very start of the sequence, with a note saying that they know they were first and could not have found themselves anywhere later; they bet under the same rules. Payoffs must involve no real money, a proper scoring rule or equivalent points, and should include a group-level component that rewards keeping some probability on whichever hypothesis turns out true, so that someone always has a stake in not collapsing to certainty.

Give me materials, rules, a timeline, and a debrief that maps moments of the game onto: the move from a single random position to a conclusion about total size; the choice between treating your own position as a random sample and giving extra prior weight to hypotheses containing more observers; the reference-class problem; and the worry that you could not have found yourself not existing. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time, and be plain about which part of the real argument the game cannot stage and I will have to supply in words.
