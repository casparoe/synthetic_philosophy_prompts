I'm a third-year computer science student taking an undergraduate seminar on promissory obligation this term. My systems vocabulary is the only precise vocabulary I have for normative notions, and I'd like to use it carefully rather than loosely — so I want a translation, and then an honest report on what the translation fails to carry.

We've been working through three families of answer to why a promise binds: the practice-or-convention answer, on which promising is an invented artifact whose rules constitute the obligation, so that the question of whether to keep one can only be raised from inside the practice; the expectation-and-assurance answer, on which the function of the practice is to let one person make her future conduct reliably predictable to another, with obligation explained by what reliance and assurance require; and the self-binding answer, on which a promisor exercises a normative power, giving the promisee a claim and a standing to demand performance, so that the reason created is both the agent's own creation and owed to that person in particular.

Please restate each in distributed-systems terms: nodes and messages, coordinators and participants, commit and abort, atomicity and rollback, compensating actions for what cannot be undone, safety versus liveness, failure detectors and indefinite blocking, quorums, staking or reputation as enforcement, and the distinction between a specification and the implementations that satisfy it. Mark explicitly where you are paraphrasing or inventing an analogue rather than translating.

The residue report is what I actually want. First, what has no expression in my vocabulary at all: I suspect the directed character of the obligation and the idea of a reason an agent creates merely by intending, but tell me if the untranslatable part is somewhere else. Second, what survives only by being distorted — in particular, whether the convention answer translates so smoothly only because protocols already presuppose an external author of the spec and an institution that runs it, in which case my growing conviction that obligation must be artificial would be an artifact of my choice of vocabulary rather than a finding. Third, whether anything in my vocabulary is more fine-grained than the seminar readings, and whether that matters: philosophy so far has treated kept and broken as a binary, whereas I have to distinguish committed, blocked, aborted, compensated, and in-doubt, and I want to know if the finer distinctions are doing real work or are merely an engineer's obsession.

Two cases, please, because they are where I lose my grip: a promise to someone who will never be in a position to learn whether it was kept, so that no message reporting the breach is ever delivered; and a promise made now, in full knowledge that I will later want to break it, by someone who has already arranged that no penalty can reach him. Say what each family of answer does with these, in my terms, and where the terms stop working.
