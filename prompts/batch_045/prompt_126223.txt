I've been a lurker on this stuff since the Sequences and I help run a little monthly meetup where we argue about exactly this kind of thing, and someone in the group chat — same circles, read Meditations on Moloch back in 2014 and has been posting about multipolar traps ever since — has an argument I can't find the break in, and it seems to prove far too much. He isn't denying that the AI capabilities race is a trap; he's arguing that if you take the Moloch frame seriously it either collapses into vacuity or it stops covering the AI case, both of which I'd rather not pay. Here it is, cleaned up into steps from what was originally about forty messages ofvoice notes:

(1) A situation is a Moloch trap iff: (a) holding fixed that everyone else keeps going, it's best for each party to keep going; (b) where they end up is worse for them than some alternative they'd all prefer; and (c) the treadmill condition — a party who unilaterally stops gets displaced or selected out, not merely made somewhat poorer, so the trap can't be dissolved by agreement, reputation, or repeated play, because the parties who'd honor the agreement don't survive to keep playing.
(2) Drop (c) and you've just got a garden-variety collective action problem, and those routinely get solved by norms, contracts, and iterated interaction. So (c) is doing all the distinguishing work; Moloch is the name for the subset where the standard fixes fail.
(3) But (a) through (c) are satisfied by any ordinary competitive industry with free entry: a firm that declines to match its rivals on price or quality loses customers and exits, the survivors all end up worse off than they'd be under a cartel they could actually enforce, and the cartel can't be enforced precisely because of (a).
(4) Those industries are also the mechanism behind most of what got better in the last two centuries. So the frame flags the thing we celebrate as a trap — and trap is meant to be a criticism.
(5) The obvious reply is that in the market cases the gains land on third parties outside the game, while in real Moloch cases the costs land on people who aren't racing. But then the whole content of the view becomes a question about who's on the ledger, and the people who'd gain from capable AI — including the medical and scientific goods — are structurally just like the consumers in (4).
(6) Push on (c) instead and it gets worse: if what makes a trap a trap is that whoever steps off the treadmill gets displaced, then any fix that is itself another agreement is temporary, since the enforcers of the pause, treaty, or regulator are in a game with the same treadmill, and so are the enforcers of that, and so on up. The honest upshot is that no level of coordination ever helps with anything.
(7) But (6) is obviously false — whaling, CFCs, parts of the nuclear mess — and (4) and (5) together make the label useless. So Moloch-talk is either empty or it doesn't apply to the AI race.

I'd much rather give up (4) or (5) than (1) through (3): the AI race really does feel like a trap in the way he describes, and I use that claim in basically every talk I give. So which step breaks? If it's (3), tell me which of (a), (b), or (c) a competitive market actually fails, because I've stared at it for a week and can't see it. If it's (5), I want the principled rule for whose welfare goes in the ledger, and why future people or accident victims end up inside it while consumers of cheaper goods end up outside — I don't want a rule that just returns the answer I already wanted. If it's (6), I want to know what kind of intervention isn't just another agreement, and why I ever thought it had to be. And if the real answer is that Moloch-hood was never by itself a reason to intervene, then what was the extra premise doing all the work this whole time, and why did the frame feel like it was carrying weight? Price the alternatives for me — I'll pay in a premise before I pay in the conclusion.
