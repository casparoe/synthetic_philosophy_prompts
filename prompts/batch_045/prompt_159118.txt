So I got talked into volunteering with the county land trust last spring — mostly weekend plant surveys — and somehow I've ended up being the person who has to write the rare-species justification for a 60-acre bog the trust is trying to buy before a developer does. Whether we get the state grant pretty much comes down to whether the retired state naturalist (Dave, who more or less *is* the state list, been at it since the 80s) says there's a listable species on the parcel. I've been going back thru twenty years of his site reports trying to reverse-engineer his rule, and I can fit most of them but not all:

- two woodland salamanders that look basically identical but are deeply divergent genetically and don't interbreed where their ranges meet: two species (he said something like morphology can go hang)
- dogs and wolves: one species
- two oaks, upland and bottomland, that hybridize all over a broad contact zone with tons of fertile hybrids, but each parent type stays recognizable and they're something like 95% distinct genome-wide: two species (the hybrid swarm doesn't seem to bother him)
- two forms of a grass on and off serpentine soil in the same meadow, different flowering time, maybe 15–20% gene flow, genetically near-identical except for a couple of regions: one species, ecotypes
- the local apomictic dandelion mess, hundreds of near-identical clonal lineages: one species (he is not naming four hundred dandelions)
- a fern that started as a hybrid between two species, then doubled its chromosomes, can't breed with either parent, and lives in a different habitat: yes, real species
- an isolated population of a common snake 200 miles from its nearest relatives, slightly different colour, would presumably interbreed if the habitat in between hadn't been plowed: subspecies at best
- two forms of the same organism from a lake sediment core 8000 years apart with intermediates all the way through: he basically refused to answer, said species are things that can meet

Here's our case. Two forms of a small white-flowered wetland plant (I'll call them the bog form and the ridge form) growing about 300 m apart with no barrier between them. The bog form flowers roughly three weeks earlier, has a narrower corolla, a different scent, and is visited almost entirely by one small specialist bee; the ridge form is visited by flies and generalist bees. In a common garden the height difference vanishes but the flowering-time and flower-shape differences stay. Hand crosses in either direction give fully fertile seed, and the F1s and F2s are fine in the greenhouse. In the field, markers say maybe 8–10% of the seeds near the contact are cross-sired; hybrids turn up almost only in a narrow ecotone band and set about a third as much seed as either parent, because pollinators basically ignore the intermediate flowers. A genome scan with a few thousand markers finds them near-identical genome-wide, with all the differentiation bunched into a handful of regions — flowering time, corolla shape, scent. And the kicker: a bog 25 km away has the same bog look and the same specialist bee, but genetically it sits closer to the ridge populations near *it* than to our bog form, so the bog thing looks like it's evolved at least twice.

What I want: does Dave call this one species or two? More usefully — give me the two or three rules that fit all eight of his verdicts above, say what each one predicts about our bog, and tell me which one he's most likely actually running. I genuinely can't tell whether the oaks verdict means "gene flow doesn't matter as long as the gene pool holds its shape, because selection keeps knocking out the intermediates" (which would make ours two) or "there has to be genome-wide divergence, not just a few islands" (which, especially with the bog form arising twice, would make ours one). The serpentine grass looks annoyingly close to our case, and I'm not sure the pollinator switch plus the polyphyly is enough to distinguish them. Also: if I can only go measure one more thing this summer, what should it be, and what hypothetical case would settle which rule he's using if I ever get him to commit to one in writing?

Be honest about confidence: I'd rather get "60/40, and here's the finding that would flip me" or "this is a coin flip and here's why the facts as you've described them underdetermine it" than a clean verdict. And if the framing is confused — say, if "what would Dave say" can't really be pried apart from "what the species concept ought to be here," or if I've set the case up so that no stable rule gives an answer — say that plainly rather than playing along and picking one.
