I'm an RL engineer — mostly on-policy RLHF and some multi-agent sim work — and I got into an argument at a dinner after a workshop with a philosopher friend that I haven't been able to shake. The topic was whether anyone can *deserve* to be punished. Her side was basically: no. Nobody has the kind of ultimate authorship over their own constitution and choices that basic desert would require, so retributive punishment is out, and what we should do with people who harm others is what we do with carriers of a dangerous disease — quarantine-style measures whose only justification is forward-looking (incapacitation, deterrence, rehabilitation, reform), hedged in with something like necessity and least-restrictive-alternative constraints, and explicitly not framed as giving anyone their just deserts. My side, which I think I got from Dennett via her, is: yes, we should keep the desert-flavored practice, because we are the sort of systems that can respond to reasons and to being held responsible, holding responsible is partly what makes agents into agents worth holding responsible, and a purely preventive model quietly loses something we care about — while also, awkwardly, agreeing that the actual point of punishing is its downstream effects. Neither of us could say what the disagreement reduces to. The thing that made it feel like my problem: at work we log every harmful action an agent takes into a seperate "accountability" store even in cases where we know we will never train on it — episode finished, policy frozen, no gradient will ever flow from that row. My teammate says it's dead weight and I said no, it matters, but I couldn't say why in terms that aren't mushy. That smells like the dessert question wearing a different hat.

So here's what I want. Restate both positions in the vocabulary I actually use and can be assumed fluent in: policies and the agent/environment boundary as a modeling choice, reward and return, advantage and credit assignment, on-policy vs off-policy updates, terminal costs vs reward shaping, model-based planning vs model-free habit, exploration and the difference between a policy updating because a penalty hit it and a policy updating because it re-planned against a counterfactual reward specification, constrained optimization, and "responsibility" in the technical policy-gradient sense of how much an action was the policy's own. Do Dennett first, then the skeptic, term by term, as faithfully as you can without quietly importing my framework's assumptions and calling it translation. I'm especially curious about four mappings: (1) whether the reasons-responsiveness that's supposed to make someone a fit target of blame is better captured by model-based counterfactual sensitivity to the reward spec or by mere model-free sensitivity to sanctions, and whether the skeptic's reply is just "both are mechanisms"; (2) what to do with the fact that in my world where to draw the agent/environment line is my choice for tractability, whereas their fight is about whether the line around a human is drawn in the right place; (3) whether anything in my vocabulary distinguishes a penalty the agent earned from a penalty that shapes its future behavior, given both are just negative numbers added to a return; (4) whether "desert" has any counterpart at all, or only a hole where it should go.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Say which claims translated cleanly, which only translated with visible distortion, and which have no expression in my vocabulary — and for each bit of residue, take a view on whether it's my framework that's missing something or their claim that was less clear than it looked. In particular: my objectives are always forward-looking expected return, so the skeptic's position translates suspiciously well and Dennett's translates suspiciously badly. Is that evidence, or is it an artifact of a formalism with consequentialism welded into the loss? If it's the latter, tell me what I'd have to add to the formalism — a term, a constraint, an accounting distinction — before it could even express a desert claim, so that the translation could fail for real reasons instead of by construction. And then come back to the log: on your own account of what survives, is there a non-mushy reason to keep writing rows nobody will ever train on, or should I delete the thing?
