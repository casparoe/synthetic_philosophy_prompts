I'm on my university's Accommodations Committee (staff and faculty cases, we meet monthly) and last night I got into a three-hour argument with a friend about whether anything really counts as a disease, so now I'm the annoying one on the committee. We have two petitions to decide next week and I want a short decision memo I can circulate before the meeting.

Our policy just says accommodations require "a diagnosed disease, disorder or medical condition" and defines nothing. The two cases look to me like the same kind of thing with the verdict pulling in opposite directions:

- A 47-year-old lab manager. A screening panel flagged a hormone/metabolic pattern that's outside the range for healthy 25-year-olds but shows up in about 40% of people her age. She reports crushing afternoon fatigue and wants a shifted schedule and a rest space. A daily drug moves her numbers back into the 25-year-old range, but in the trial it didn't change how tired anyone said they felt.
- A 30-year-old programmer with a congenital variation (roughly 2% of people have it). No pain, no measurable loss of any capacity, feels fine — but on our actual floor, with the lighting and the open plan and the standing desks, he can't get through a full day. He says he isn't sick, the building is. He wants the same accommodation.

Half the committee thinks the second petitioner isn't diseased at all and we should approve him anyway on some other ground; the other half thinks that if we do that we've quietly given up on having a concept of disease. What I want from the memo is a rule we can actually apply to both, and it has to take a position on the reference-class question: do we compare people against healthy young adults or against their own age group? That one choice decides a lot of future cases, including whether the first petitioner has a condition at all. Recommend approve, deny, or approve with conditions for each petitioner, with a time frame, and give the losing side its best argument properly — if you go with departures from normal functioning, deal with the guy who's fine until he walks into our building; if you go with harm and values, explain why we aren't just a complaints desk with a stethoscope. About two pages.
