I teach a graduate seminar on concepts and analogy, and I am convening a two-day adversarial collaboration between two colleagues who have been arguing past each other for three years about how to read the Copycat project of Hofstadter and Mitchell — the letter-string microdomain of the form *abc is to abd as ijk is to what?*, the slipnet of concepts with its spreading activation, the probabilistic codelets, and the claim that analogy-making is high-level perception rather than rule application. One of them holds what I will call the stable-concepts, mapping-first view: the concepts deployed in a microdomain (LETTER, SUCCESSOR, GROUP, RUN, FIRST, LAST) have stable content, and what varies from problem to problem is the correspondence constructed between two structured representations, after which further structure is projected along that correspondence; graded, noisy answer distributions reflect competition among candidate mappings, not instability in the concepts. The other holds the fluid-concept view: the concepts themselves are context-dependent and graded, the same concept stretches and slides under structural pressure, and the distribution of answers is the phenomenon to be explained rather than noise around a rule. I will moderate. I need a draft of the joint statement to circulate in advance, and I want it to be a text both of them could put their names to. Please do not cite or recommend any literature: the value of the document lies entirely in the formulations, and I will supply background myself.

They have agreed to test the disagreement in a variant microdomain I constructed for the occasion, in which the familiar letter-string problems are rewritten on a ring, so that a string has no distinguished first or last character. My hope is that removing the endpoints forces into the open a slippage that the ordinary notation conceals.

Appendix A. The ring-string microdomain. The alphabet is the lowercase letters with the usual successor relation (b succeeds a, c succeeds b, and so on); it is left open whether the successor relation wraps from z to a, and whether it does is itself one of the matters at issue. A ring-string is a cyclic sequence of letters, written in square brackets; two inscriptions denote the same ring-string just in case they are rotations of one another, so that [abc] and [bca] and [cab] are one object, while [cba] is a different object, the one with the opposite cyclic orientation. A run is a maximal cyclic sequence of letters each of which succeeds the previous one; thus [ijk] contains the run ijk, and [ace] contains no run longer than one letter. A probe consists of a base transformation and a target ring-string, and the task is to produce the analogue of the target: [abc] ⇒ [abd]; [ijk] ⇒ ?

Appendix B. The probe battery. The base transformation is [abc] ⇒ [abd] throughout. The candidate answers listed are the ones the two camps have already agreed are live; each camp may argue that a listed candidate is not live or that a further one is, but the pre-registration has to be recorded over a fixed answer set if it is to bite at all.

P1. [ijk] ⇒ ? The end of the run, the last letter as written, and the alphabetically highest letter all coincide. This is the calibration item.
P2. [kij] ⇒ ? The same ring-string as in P1, written from a different letter, so that the run ends at k while the last letter as written is j. Candidates: [ijl] (the letter k advances) and [kik] (the letter j advances).
P3. [xyz] ⇒ ? The run ends at the last letter of the alphabet. Candidates: [xya] (successor wraps), [xyy] (the relation slips from successor to predecessor), and balking or no answer (z has no successor).
P4. [abcc] ⇒ ? The maximal run ends at the first of two identical letters. Candidates: [abdc] (the letter that ends the run advances), [abcd] (the last letter as written advances, and the run is thereby extended), and [abdd] (the group cc advances as a unit).
P5. [ace] ⇒ ? No run at all. Candidates: [acf] (the alphabetically last, and last-written, letter advances), [ade] (the middle letter advances, creating a run where there was none), and balking.
P6. [abcfg] ⇒ ? Two runs of unequal length. Candidates: [abdfg] (the longer run's end advances) and [abcfh] (the later run's end advances).

I. A numbered section of agreed claims: the substantive things both sides can sign about the phenomena, about what a correct account of the microdomain would have to explain, about what the removal of endpoints does and does not show, and about what would count as a competent account of a probe. I want claims with content; a list of truisms is useless to me, and so is agreement bought by vagueness.

II. A table of the residual disagreements, with a row for each and the following columns: the issue; the stable-concepts side's own formulation of its position, phrased so that side would accept the wording; the fluid-concept side's own formulation of its position, likewise; a neutral restatement both would accept as fair; and a verdict, with a sentence of justification, on whether the disagreement is genuine or merely verbal. Where you think a dispute is verbal, say precisely which part is verbal and why — for instance whether the claim that a concept changed and the claim that a mapping over stable concepts changed differ in content once both sides accept the same description of the mechanism at the micro-level.

III. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, give the test, argument, or case both would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. In particular, for each probe in the battery, give each side's predicted modal answer and a rough predicted distribution over the candidates, and say what pattern of results across the six probes would move which side, and how far. Include what each camp would count as evidence against its own view — for the stable-concepts side, something like a systematic dependence of answers on rotation of the notation that is explained by conceptual content rather than by mapping search; for the fluid-concept side, something like a single fixed inventory of concepts plus a ranking over mappings predicting the whole distribution, balking cases included, without any context-sensitivity in concept identity.

IV. A short closing section, signed jointly in tone, on what the collaboration has established and what it has not, and on which of the remaining questions the two sides agree are answerable at all.

V. A brief private note to me as moderator, kept separate from the statement: where this collaboration is most likely to stall, which of your formulations each side is most likely to reject and why, and whether any crux should be reformulated before the meeting.

Aim for roughly 1,200 words for sections I through IV. Two standing constraints: no position may be stated in a form its own holder would disown, and no disagreement may be either manufactured by stipulation or dissolved by drafting it into platitude.
