I've been going to a fortnightly metaphysics reading group with some graduate students, and last week one of them played us a few minutes of a popular philosophy podcast on personal identity that he thought was unusually careful. I've since listened to it twice and I can't tell which parts to trust. Here is roughly what the host says, transcribed as best I can:

Start with something nobody disputes: identity is necessary. If A and B are one and the same thing, then they are the same in every possible world, so there is no such thing as merely contingent identity. Now take a person — the host uses the Queen. It certainly seems possible that her parents might have conceived a different child: a different sperm fertilises a different egg, and, intuitively, that child could still have been *her*, just with a different genome and a different life. But that intuition is incoherent. For suppose it were true. Then there is a possible world in which the actual sperm S and the actual egg E do unite, but the person who results from them is not the Queen — call her Sophie. That must be possible, given what we've conceded. But it is also possible, since it is what actually happened, that S and E produce the Queen herself. So it is possible that Sophie is the Queen and possible that Sophie is not the Queen; and since identity and distinctness are both necessary, that is a flat contradiction. The only way out is to deny the intuition: nothing that originated from a different sperm and a different egg could have been the Queen. Generalising: every one of us has its origins of necessity, and that is what philosophers mean by origin essentialism — a thesis Kripke established from the bare logic of identity, not from any dubious metaphysics. The host then stresses that this is not a biological or genetic thesis at all: it says nothing about how like your actual self you have to be, so you could have been raised by wolves, gestated in an artificial womb, or had your genome edited as an embryo and still have been numerically the same human being, provided you came from that sperm and that egg. He adds, a bit triumphantly, that because a sperm and an egg are themselves things with origins, the thesis iterates: no link anywhere in your causal ancestry could have been different either, so if any one of a vast number of tiny events in the distant past had gone otherwise, you would simply not have existed. He admits this sounds wild but says it is just what the argument delivers and that discomfort is not a refutation. Finally, he draws an ethical moral: since a child conceived a month later would have come from a different sperm and egg, and so would have been a numerically different person, anyone who delays conceiving thereby deprives a particular possible child of the life it would otherwise have had — and this, he says, is really just the non-identity problem from population ethics (the one associated with Parfit) wearing a metaphysical costume.

What I want is an accuracy audit I can act on. Please go through the host's claims and separate what a philosopher working on this would accept, what is a common simplification, and what is a genuine misreading, and then tell me whether the ethical conclusion at the end survives once the rest is corrected, or whether it has the direction of the non-identity argument backwards. Four things in particular I'd like pinned down: whether it is fair to present the thesis as something Kripke *proved* from the necessity of identity, rather than as something he floated and others later reconstructed and disputed; whether the move from "identity is necessary" to "nothing with different origins could have been me" really goes through, or whether the argument quietly rests on a suppressed premise and ends in a dilemma rather than a proof; whether the slogan "your origins are your essence" is the same claim as the thesis about sperm and egg, or a stronger and more misleading one; and whether the iterated-ancestry consequence is something defenders of the view actually accept, or something they regard as a reductio that the host should have noticed.

I should say where I already have hunches, since I'd like them checked rather than flattered. I have a vague memory that Kripke himself is noticeably more tentative than the podcast makes him sound, and that the argument from the necessity of identity is a later reconstruction which counterpart theorists think they can wriggle out of; a member of the group insisted that the whole discussion quietly conflates persons with human organisms and that the thesis is far more plausible for one than for the other; and another said that identical twins, where one zygote becomes two, and fusion cases, where two become one, are problems the summary sails straight past. I have no idea whether any of these are real objections or just clever-sounding interruptions. Please flag the places where the literature is genuinely unsettled instead of smoothing them over, and don't bother with a bibliography or reading list — I have no library access, and I'd rather you didn't rest anything on exact quotations from the literature, since I have no way to check them.
