I'm on the board of the Northwold Heritage Trust — we're a small county heritage and museums service in England — and we have to sign off the interpretation panels for the Merebank visitor centre before the text goes to the designer in three weeks. I need a short memo I can circulate before Tuesday's meeting, with an actual recommendation, not a summary of the options.

Here's the site. It was dug in 2019–21 ahead of a housing development: a gravel terrace by a river, with a rectangular timber building, a four-post structure, and two pits about 30 m apart. Pit 1 held 34 iron objects — axeheads, sickles, a couple of socketed tools — plus cattle jawbones and some pig bone, laid in distinct layers with the objects deliberately bent or broken. Three of the objects are miniature versions of tools, the right shape but too small to have ever worked. The others show use-wear, so they were real tools before they went in. No coins, no imported material, no human bone, no inscriptions, nothing you could call an image of anything. Radiocarbon on the bone is annoyingly broad — somewhere in the late Iron Age into the early Roman period — but the tool types themselves look like they span maybe two centuries, so the deposit was probably accumulated rather than buried in one go. Pit 2, 30 m away, has the same general kinds of objects but jumbled in with butchery waste and a broken quern stone, and no bending or layering at all.

Our finds specialist says "ritual" is doing no work here: it's a label we reach for when we can't think of a mundane explanation, and once you call something an offering there's no observation that could ever contradict you. The project's community engagement officer points out that a local heathen group has already started referring to Merebank as a temple, the school programme is built around it, and that a panel saying "we don't know" isn't neutral — it's a choice to present these people as doing nothing but farming and dumping things. One board member has gone further and says the burden of proof should fall on the deflationary reading: absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence, and we shouldn't strip a site of its religious dimension just because the religious explanation is harder to demonstrate. (Someone else on the board keeps invoking the old archaeological idea that you can read technology off objects fairly easily but belief only with great difficulty, and concludes we simply shouldn't try.)

The draft panel text currently says the pits were where people made offerings to the gods, and there's a reconstruction drawing on order showing a thatched shrine with a carved figure inside.

What I want from the memo: (1) what the panel should actually say about Pit 1 and Pit 2, in words a visitor could read; (2) whether we commission the shrine reconstruction or kill it, and if we kill it what replaces it; (3) whether we adopt a standing policy for future sites — someone has floated attaching confidence ratings to our interpretive claims, and I want to know whether that's a real safeguard or just a way of hedging without committing to anything. Give me a timeline for whatever you recommend given the three-week deadline, and take the strongest version of the "temple" reading seriously rather than treating it as obviously superstitious — I need the people who like that reading to feel they've been answered, even if you come out against them.
