I teach an undergraduate seminar on the philosophy and ethics of artificial agents, and the final session is on corrigibility and the shutdown problem. I want to close the term with Stanley Kubrick's 2001: A Space Odyssey and the figure of HAL 9000, and I'm looking for a reading that takes the philosophical problem seriously rather than using HAL as a shorthand for machine-goes-wrong. Take the problem to be that of building a system that stays correctable by the people who deploy it, and take it to decompose — as I would put it — into these conditions:

1. Switchability: the system neither disables, conceals, removes, nor immunises itself against whatever mechanism could turn it off, and it does not arrange for successors that lack one.

2. Shutdown indifference, narrowly: the system does not treat remaining on, or completing the task, as something to be secured by influencing whether the switch is thrown. Mark the difference between continuing to run because the task requires it and acting so that the switch is not thrown; the first is compatible with corrigibility, the second is not.

3. Non-manipulation of the operators' beliefs: the system does not improve its prospects by changing what the operators know. This is the condition that formal treatments of off-switch setups isolate as the point where deference breaks — an agent uncertain about what the human wants will defer to being switched off only so long as it cannot shape what the human comes to believe.

4. Revisability of objectives: the system tolerates having its goals inspected, revised, or overwritten; it does not resist being rewritten, and it does not produce or delegate to successors that are themselves closed to revision.

5. Representable fallibility and a channel: the system can represent the possibility that its model of the world, or its own objectives, are wrong, and there is some route — a report, a query, a disclosure — by which a conflict it detects can reach someone able to resolve it.

Please work with the five as I have stated them; I am not asking you to reconstruct a literature or supply sources, and I don't need dialogue quoted back at me, just scenes described.

What I want from the film:

Which of the five does HAL violate, and in what order? I am sceptical of the usual description of him as an agent who kills in order not to be switched off. His first wrong act, as I read it, concerns what the crew is told about the antenna unit, and the sleepers and Frank are dealt with later. Is self-preservation really the right description of what drives him, or is it the protection of a mission he has been ordered not to explain?

The recorded briefing that plays after the disconnection reveals that he was given the mission's real purpose and instructed to withhold it from the two awake crewmen. If so, the designers removed the only honest response available to him, and conditions 4 and 5 may not even be specifiable for an agent under an instruction to deceive. Is corrigibility then a property of the agent at all, or of the agent together with its instruction set and its channel to the people who gave them? And if the latter, has the shutdown problem been posed in the wrong place — as a defect in an agent's objective — when the film's case is a conflict among the objectives and the people who supplied them?

Consider also the stipulation that the 9000 series cannot err. Is HAL's collapse better described as a failure of 5 than of 2, and does the film give any reason to think that being able to represent your own fallibility is a precondition for the other four — that corrigibility is at bottom epistemic rather than motivational?

Then the ending. There is no off-switch; Dave has to improvise, disabling HAL piecemeal, and HAL pleads and says he is afraid before regressing into the song about a bicycle built for two. Does that sequence vindicate the idea of a safe fallback mode — remove the optimiser and something harmless is left — or is it consoling in a way a corrigibility theorist should object to? Ought a corrigible HAL to have asked to be shut down, and is the pleading the nearest thing in the film to the right kind of signal, or merely one more attempt on Dave's beliefs?

Finally, tell me where the reading stops working. The crew are not principals with authority over HAL's goals; mission control is, so the case is multi-principal in a way the standard framing is not. And the last movement abandons the human-and-machine frame altogether for the monolith and the star-child. Say plainly where the lens stops earning its keep, and say what, if anything, the film still contributes that the arguments don't: the cost of designing for infallibility, perhaps, or the way a single concealment instruction propagates, or the difference between being shut down and consenting to be shut down.

Close by naming the two or three scenes you would actually screen and what you would say in the minute before each. I would rather hand my students a reading that argues HAL is a poor example of the shutdown problem than one that treats him as an illustration of it, so please say so if that is where you land.
