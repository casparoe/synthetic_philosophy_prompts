I teach a final-year seminar in moral and political philosophy, and I want to use R v Dudley and Stephens, the 1884 Mignonette case, as a test of the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism rather than as another staging of the trolley problem. I know the case and the standard statement of the objection, so please spend no words reciting either: go straight to the analysis.

The details I want you to work with are these. Four crewmen — the captain Dudley, the mate Stephens, the seaman Brooks, and the seventeen-year-old cabin boy Richard Parker — were adrift in an open boat some seven hundred miles from land after their yacht sank. Parker, the youngest and least experienced of them, fell ill after drinking seawater and was probably in a coma by the time he was killed. Drawing lots to select a victim was discussed on several occasions over the preceding week; Brooks refused to agree, and no lots were drawn. Dudley and Stephens, the two who had wives and children, then killed Parker themselves with a penknife, and all three fed on his body until a passing vessel picked them up days later. They were convicted of murder; their death sentences were commuted and they served only a few months.

What I am after is a disciplined mapping in both directions.

First, the theory applied to the case. Set out what the separateness objection says these men faced: whose claim is what, what the available options were on the objection's own terms, and which of those options it rules out as against which it leaves open. I am particularly interested in whether the objection is doing work in its anti-aggregation form — the thought that the numbers simply do not count, that there is no entity whose good is the sum of the four men's goods — or in its rights form, as a prohibition on using, for the benefit of others, a person who is not threatening anyone. Do those two forms come apart on these facts? My suspicion is that they do, and that this case is a sharper test than the standard set-up precisely because the identity of the victim was not fixed in advance: there were four candidates, and someone had to select one.

Second, the parts the theory does not reach. The objection seems to me to say nothing about how a choice among candidates should be made, yet a choice had to be made. The natural candidates are a lottery and consent, and neither is obviously available to it: a lottery looks like a device for maximising expected survivors read back through equal respect, and consent was never sought from Parker, who was in no condition to give it. Say plainly where the objection is silent rather than forcing it to speak.

Third, the case applied to the theory. The objection was formulated against utilitarianism as a doctrine about the distribution of benefits and burdens across a society; a one-off emergency in an open boat may be the wrong kind of case for it, or it may be the case that shows the objection was never really about aggregation in the first place. Which is it? And does the law's refusal to accept necessity — the conviction, and the refusal to let the men's own weighing of lives stand as a defence — vindicate the separateness view, or is it better explained by worries about moral hazard and about who gets to do the weighing?

Please answer in under 800 words, in continuous prose divided into at most four short sections. No citations, no reading lists, no suggestions for further reading: I want the argument, not the literature.
