Hey — total disclaimer, I'm not an academic, I got into this stuff through late-night YouTube rabbit holes and haven't taken a philosophy class since high school, so go easy on the jargon. I've been chewing on something and arguing about it with my movie-night group for weeks now. The thing that set me off: somewhere (a blog comment I think, no idea where anymore) I ran into the suggestion that next to the famous hard problem — why there's experience at all and not just the functional, behavioural, information-processing machinery — there's a second and genuinely different problem, which isn't about explaining consciousness but about explaining us: why we insist we have it, why we keep producing reports along the lines of it feeling like something to be me, why the conviction that there's a further fact beyond all the machinery is so stubborn and so hard to talk anyone out of. Someone called this the meta-problem. My first reaction was that it's a dodge, a way of changing the subject, but the more I sit with it the more I think it's a real separate target — you could in principle finish the whole job of explaining our reports and still not have touched the thing the reports are about, and I can't tell whether that's a virtue of the framing or a confession that it's empty.

We rewatched Ex Machina last weekend and I can't shake the feeling that the film is secretly about the meta-problem and not the hard problem at all. The setup looks like a test of her, but it reads to me like a machine for manufacturing a particular report out of Caleb: the conversations through the glass, the fact that she's built to be someone he'll want to rescue, the power cuts where he can't even tell what he witnessed, the way he's being observed the whole time. I've been playing with a three-way split and I want it stress-tested: (a) whether Ava has experiences, (b) why Caleb ends up saying she does, (c) why we in the audience can't stop caring about her even once the ending has more or less told us we were had. My hunch is that the film's whole point is that these can come apart, and that (a) is the one it never answers and maybe never could, and that Nathan's actual achievement is a working engineering-level solution to the meta-problem with the hard question left wide open — but I might be drawing the lines in the wrong place, or it might really only be two things.

So: read the film through that lens for me, as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up sentence at the end. I'd like: 1) the single scene or bit of staging you'd point to as the film doing the meta-problem rather than the hard problem, and why that one; 2) whether my three-way split holds up or collapses into two things (or needs a fourth); 3) whether the ending — her walking out into the world and leaving him sealed in the glass room — is the film taking a position on whether a full solution to the meta-problem would settle the hard problem, or whether it's just a twist; 4) where the reading breaks, the bit of the film that resists it and that I'd have to ignore to keep my hunch, because I don't want a reading that just flatters me; 5) what, if anything, the film shows about this that the arguments don't — something you couldn't get from a paper. If you think the whole lens is wrong for this movie, say that instead, but still in five points.
