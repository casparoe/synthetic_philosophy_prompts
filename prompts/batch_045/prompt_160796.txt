My roommate and I have this argument like twice a month and neither of us are Hume scholars, so I want someone to actually audit the comparison he keeps leaning on.

His move is the lottery. Somebody wins the Powerball or whatever every few weeks and we all believe it on the strength of a press release, a news site, maybe a cousin who knows the guy. The prior on any particular ticket winning is something like one in three hundred million. So: if a miracle is just a very improbable event, and Hume's argument is just that the prior is low so its always more likely the testimony is mistaken than that the event happened, then Hume's argument convicts us of being irrational for believing completely ordinary things. His conclusion: the argument proves too much, there's nothing specifically about miracles doing the work, its just crude probabilism in a wig — and once you see that, there's no in-principle bar to testimony establishing a miracle, up to and including the resurrection.

He also has a ratchet version. Keep turning it up: one guy wins once, then the same guy wins twice, then six times running. At some point you'd stop believing the press release, even though it's the same kind of testimony from the same kind of institution. So testimony does have a breaking point, and he says Hume can only locate that breaking point by how improbable the event is, and nobody has a principled place to put the threshold, so the whole thing is arbitrary. And separately he says the sheer stock of miracle reports is no evidence against them, because with eight billion people a one-in-a-billion thing happens to somebody every week, so of course reports pile up.
