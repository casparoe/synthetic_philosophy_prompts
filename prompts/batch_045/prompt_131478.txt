**Question 4 (30 points).** The knowledge norm of assertion says, roughly, that one may assert that p only if one knows that p. One rival says that one may assert that p only if one's belief that p is reasonable / justified. A weaker rival says only that one may assert that p only if p is true.

(a) Why is it hard to test the knowledge norm against the reasonable-belief norm using ordinary, run-of-the-mill cases? Say what a defender of the knowledge norm has to do to make the case, and what the most natural reply from the reasonable-belief side is.

(b) Now consider a proposed replacement norm: (SENS) One may assert that p only if one would not believe that p if p were false. This is a counterfactual condition — in the nearest possible worlds in which p is false, the speaker does not believe p. Take the lottery case: Dana reads in a normally reliable newspaper that ticket 4471 lost, and asserts to her friend "your ticket lost." The ticket did lose; the paper was right. Many people's intuition is that Dana's assertion is fine, but Dana doesn't know the ticket lost, since her evidence is purely statistical. Now take the bridge case: Bernie asserts "the bridge is safe" on the basis of a single visual inspection from 200 yards away, in heavy fog. The bridge is in fact safe, but it would have looked exactly the same to him from that distance if the main support cable had snapped. Assess whether SENS handles these two cases better than the knowledge norm does.

(c) Someone in my section proposed a hearer-relative norm: (EPH) One may assert that p to a hearer H only if H is in a position to come to know that p on the basis of the assertion. Does EPH collapse into the knowledge norm? Does it have any advantage over it? Try to construct a case where EPH and the knowledge norm give different verdicts, and say which verdict you think is right.

**Student answer (ID #47):**

(a) The knowledge norm says you should only assert what you know. The reasonable belief norm says you should only assert what you have justification for. Knowledge is justified true belief plus an anti-Gettier condition, so the knowledge norm is strictly stronger than the reasonable belief norm: everything you know you also have justification for, but not the other way round. The reason it's hard to test the two norms against each other is that knowledge is factive — if you know p then p is true — so the knowledge norm and the truth norm give the same verdict on every case where the assertion is true, and they only differ over cases where the speaker says something false, and both norms say that's impermissible anyway, so you can't get a grip. To make the case, a defender of the knowledge norm needs a case where the speaker has a justified true belief that isn't knowledge and the assertion still seems bad — a Gettier case, or a lottery case. The natural reply from the other side is that the assertion isn't actually bad in those cases, we just feel that it is because we're focussed on how lucky the speaker was; or that the speaker does have knowledge after all, because justification comes in degrees and the standards are low in that context.

(b) SENS is really a safety condition in disguise: it says that in nearby worlds where you believe p, p is true. The lottery case shows that the knowledge norm is too permissive, because Dana's assertion is intuitively fine but she doesn't know. SENS does better here: Dana's belief is safe, since in the nearby worlds where she reads the paper and it says the ticket lost, the ticket did lose, so SENS permits her assertion — the right result where the knowledge norm gets the wrong one. In the bridge case Bernie's belief is also safe, since he checked and the bridge is fine, so SENS permits his assertion too, which is again the right result, because inspectors and engineers assert things on the basis of one look all the time. So SENS beats the knowledge norm in both cases.

(c) EPH says the hearer has to be in a position to come to know. If the hearer is in a position to know p, then p is true, so EPH is just the truth norm with extra steps, and the truth norm is too weak — it lets you assert the winning lottery number if you happened to guess it. So EPH collapses into the truth norm rather than the knowledge norm. EPH also can't handle a case where you assert something to a room full of people, since some of them might be in a position to know and some not, so the very same assertion would be both permissible and impermissible, which is absurd. I think the knowledge norm is the right one, and the constitutive rule is what makes assertion the speech act it is: if you assert without knowing, you haven't really asserted at all, you've just made noise.
