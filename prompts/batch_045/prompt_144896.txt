I work in security engineering, and I keep running into the same oddity in how my field talks and how it acts. I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of computational hardness that our practice presupposes, say which parts of it the practice strictly requires and which it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me what would have to change if we replaced that theory with a different one.

Here is the practice, concretely. Nearly every secure interaction on the internet — session key establishment, code signing, disk encryption — depends on the assumption that some problem (factoring, discrete logarithms, and, in the lattice-based key-encapsulation schemes that national standards bodies have recently selected, problems of the learning-with-errors kind) cannot be solved by any adversary we are likely to face. Nobody has a proof of this: P versus NP is open, and even a proof of P ≠ NP would not by itself give us what we need.

Our *theory* is asymptotic. Adversaries are modeled as polynomial-time machines; keys and secrets are indexed by a security parameter; a scheme counts as secure when every such adversary's advantage is a negligible function of that parameter. Everything here is a claim about a limit, with no bound on the exponent, the constant, or the cleverness of the strategy.

Our *parameters* are concrete. Committees adopt security levels measured in bits, and convert them into key sizes by pricing the best attack anyone has published — sieve-style index calculus attacks for factoring and finite-field discrete log, the best known lattice reduction and sieving attacks for the newer schemes — against plausible hardware and a plausible number of years. Nothing in this step follows from the previous one, and the two can come apart in both directions: an algorithm running in n^20 counts as efficient under the asymptotic theory and is worthless in the field, while an algorithm that is exponential but only mildly so may be declared infeasible asymptotically and be perfectly practical at the sizes we actually use.

Sometimes we hold a reduction showing that breaking the scheme is at least as hard as the hardest instances of a named problem, which is roughly the situation for the lattice-based families. Sometimes we hold nothing of the kind and have only the failure of decades of concerted effort, which is roughly the situation for the factoring- and discrete-log-based schemes. We do not appear to treat this difference as making much practical difference: both get parameter tables.

So, four things I want from you.

First: what picture of hardness does this presuppose? Is the working commitment a claim about the physical world — that these computations cannot be carried out by any machine we can build within realistic resource and energy budgets, something like a resource-bounded version of the physical Church–Turing thesis — or is it a claim about our epistemic situation, that no one has found a way and the search has been long and well funded? Say which of these the practice strictly needs, which it is merely consistent with, and where it is underdetermined: my suspicion is that a realist about hardness and a thoroughgoing inductivist about our own algorithmic abilities would end up recommending exactly the same key sizes, and I want to know whether that suspicion is right and what it shows.

Second: the formal model quantifies over all polynomial-time adversaries, while the parameter tables price the attacks we currently know about. Is this an outright contradiction, or are two different questions being answered to two different standards? If the latter, say what each is for and whether the practice ever needs them to agree.

Third, take three grades of hardness as I'll define them here: asymptotic hardness, meaning no polynomial-time algorithm exists at all; distributional hardness, meaning no efficient algorithm succeeds on the instances our key-generation procedure actually samples, so that the problem is hard on the distribution we generate rather than only on adversarial worst cases; and concrete hardness, meaning the best available attack costs more than any adversary we care about can spend, at the parameter sizes we use. Which of these does the practice strictly require? Is any one sufficient? And does even distributional hardness give us what we need, or is a further premise required — that hardness of this kind can be converted into a usable cryptographic object, which, as far as anyone can show, is an extra step and not a consequence?

Fourth, the stress-test. Suppose we got a proof of P = NP by way of an algorithm running in n^500 with astronomical constants. Or suppose instead we got a proof of P ≠ NP together with a proof that every problem in NP is easy on every distribution we can efficiently sample — hard in the worst case, easy on every instance we could ever actually generate. Or suppose, finally, we learned only that someone had found a factoring method with no interesting asymptotic behavior at all that breaks 2048-bit keys in a week on existing hardware. Rank these by how much of our practice they would actually overturn, be explicit about how the ranking depends on whether the discovery arrives with a usable algorithm or only as an existence proof, and say what the ranking reveals about which of the three grades is doing the real work.

And then the last question: what would have to change if we dropped the asymptotic apparatus entirely and said, plainly and without pretense, that we have no evidence anyone can break this scheme at this parameter size within this many years? Would we lose anything of genuine justificatory value — the reductions, the security-parameter formalism, the ability to compare schemes against a common standard — or is the asymptotic vocabulary mostly doing a rhetorical job? I am not asking for citations or a literature survey; argue from the structure of the practice as I have described it.
