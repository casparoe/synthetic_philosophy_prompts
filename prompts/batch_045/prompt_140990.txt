I host a recorded interview series on moral philosophy, and my guest next week is Dr. Miriam Calloway, a moral philosopher who defends what she calls lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. Roughly: pleasure and pain are genuinely commensurable, and there is a single hedonic scale on which they trade off against one another at some finite rate — but only up to a point. Once suffering passes a certain intensity, it becomes lexically dominant: no finite quantity of pleasure, however distributed, can compensate for it, and the resulting comparison is decided entirely by the suffering. She is careful to distinguish this from the softer negative-utilitarian slogan that public policy should concern itself with the relief of suffering rather than the promotion of happiness, which she regards as far too concessive, since on her view it is not merely that suffering matters more but that above the threshold it is the only thing that matters.

Last night I spent several hours arguing about her position with a friend, and neither of us gave ground, so I would like to turn that argument into the backbone of the interview. My friend's objection, as we finally sharpened it, was a continuity argument. Suppose the sub-threshold exchange rate is fixed — call the unit a hedon — so that some quantity of mild discomfort is exactly offset by some larger quantity of mild enjoyment. Now construct a series of cases in which the discomfort is raised by one barely discriminable step at a time, and in each case ask how much enjoyment it would take to make the outcome exactly as good as the original. Either there is a step at which the required quantity of enjoyment jumps from finite to infinite, which is a discontinuity in value with no evident discontinuity in the underlying phenomenology or neurophysiology to explain it; or there is no such step, in which case the threshold does no work that a merely steep weighting curve would not do. Calloway's reply, as I have heard her give it, is that the threshold is not a kink in the trade-off curve at all but a structural feature of the experience itself: agony above the threshold does not merely outweigh enjoyment, it silences or eclipses the claims of enjoyment entirely, so that the question of how much enjoyment would compensate does not arise.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions — in American English — built around that exchange, together with a follow-up for each one keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want the sequence to escalate: start where she is strongest and end somewhere she is not. Do not include anything she could answer by restating her definition, and do not include conversational warm-up questions. Among the questions there must be at least one on each of the following: (1) the continuity argument above, including the version in which she accepts that we may be permanently ignorant of where the threshold falls; (2) the units problem — in what units is above-threshold suffering worse than pleasure if the answer is infinite, and what, if anything, infinity does here that a very large finite weight does not; (3) operationalization, given that reported pain intensity is famously sensitive to attention, expectation, and context, and given the reported condition in which a subject registers the full sensory intensity of a noxious stimulus while insisting that it does not bother them — ask whether that counts as suffering on her view; (4) the level of aggregation, and specifically whether the threshold is absolute or relative to a creature's affective range, since on an absolute reading a being whose mildest discomfort is as intense as our worst agony would be lexically dominant in every conflict with us; (5) whether the threshold applies to momentary experiences or to the shape of a whole life, and how that interacts with the finding that retrospective evaluations of painful episodes track peak and end intensity far more closely than total duration; (6) the breakdown of expected-value reasoning when a value function is discontinuous, and, relatedly, whether a nonzero chance of above-threshold suffering makes creating a person impermissible — and here I want a question that forces her to say whether that implication is a feature of her view or a bullet she bites; (7) whether her eclipse claim is really a claim about the incommensurability of intense suffering with any quantity of pleasure, and if so why she prefers the lexical formulation given that incommensurability would appear to sidestep the continuity argument altogether. Close with one question about what kind of evidence could count against the view — what finding, or what argument, would get her to drop the threshold rather than relocate it.
