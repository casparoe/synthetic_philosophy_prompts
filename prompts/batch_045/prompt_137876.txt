so I'm on our department's graduate studies committee and we keep trying to delete this rule that every PhD student has to hand in a bound paper copy of their dissertation to the department reading room. Nobody can say why it's there, and the archivist says the shelf has never once been signed out. Every time I raise it one of the senior members says some version of the Chesterton's fence line — don't tear down a fence until you know why it was put up — and that ends the discussion.

The thing is, I think that line is doing more work than it can carry, and I want to know what it could precisely be claiming, because the candidates pull in different directions:

- maybe it's about the original reason, which we partly do know (back when it was added the only other copy was microfilm), and which is now dead
- maybe it's about what the thing is holding up right now, which is a different question entirely — I found out last week that the bound copies are what trigger the library's cataloguing workflow, so if we drop the requirement the library apparently just stops receiving dissertations at all. The fence is propping up something it was never built for.
- maybe it's the Burkean-ish one: the practice embodies accumulated local knowledge nobody can fully state, so my inability to see the use is evidence about the practice and not only about me
- maybe it's just an epistemic-humility platitude about not breaking what you don't understand
- or maybe it's really a remark about the person proposing — you haven't earned the right to touch this

Also relevant: I offered a compromise, sunset the requirement for two years with an automatic restore unless we affirmatively vote to keep it gone, and he said that misses the point entirely, the fence principle isnt about reversibility. That genuienly surprised me and made me think I dont know what he thinks he's saying.

Can you lay out the distinct readings, from the near-trivial to the genuinely substantive, with a verdict on each: is it plausible, and would it actually deliver what he's using it for — blocking my proposal, and blocking the reversible version of it too? And tell me where the fence picture itself misleads: what does it quietly import about institutions that a decades-old departmental bylaw doesn't have (a single builder with a single purpose, an artefact whose function is visible from the outside, nobody with a stake in keeping it, whatever else you see).

Exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no summing up at the end. And if my framing is confused — if the original-purpose / present-function distinction isn't load bearing, or the maxim just isn't the sort of thing that has determinate readings — say so plainly instead of politely filling five bullets.
