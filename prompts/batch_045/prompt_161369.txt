I am a PhD student in science and technology studies, and my dissertation chapter is on the epistemology of peer review. What interests me is a rule that every working scientist seems to know and no journal ever writes down: a distinction between objections that must be answered and objections that do not count as objections at all. If a referee says that the data do not support the conclusion, that there is a missing control, that the confidence intervals were computed wrong, or that a better-powered study found the opposite, the authors must answer, and the paper dies if they cannot. But if a referee says that the measurement procedure presupposes that the instruments track an external world, or that the entire published literature in the field might be the product of a coordinated fraud, or that the laws of nature might have shifted between yesterday's run and today's, then the referee is treated as not having engaged with the paper at all: editors do not forward the report, and the objection is set aside without argument. Three further features of the same practice seem to me to matter. First, a challenge with exactly that global shape — the whole framework is resting on a mistake — is sometimes, decades later, credited as a turning point in the field, so the practice clearly does not simply forbid framework-level criticism forever. Second, authors must cite or evidence every substantive claim, but nobody demands a citation for the claim that the samples were collected on Earth or that the notebook records measurements that were actually taken, and the regress of justification appears to stop somewhere different in molecular biology than it does in paleoclimate reconstruction. Third, my supervisor, who is not a Wittgensteinian, says the whole phenomenon is an artefact of incentives: referees have limited time, editors want publishable decisions, and nobody has ever been rewarded for raising a doubt that no possible experiment could settle.

My own tentative hypothesis is that this practice is the institutional expression of hinge epistemology, and I want it stress-tested. I sort objections into three kinds: evidence-objections, which cite contrary data or flawed inference; coherence-objections, which say the claims conflict with each other or with well-confirmed theory; and framework-objections, which challenge whether the quantities or concepts the paper deploys are well defined or applicable at all. My proposal is that framework-objections are objections aimed at hinge commitments, and that the practice's dismissal of them encodes Wittgenstein's idea that there are things which stand fast not because they have been justified but because they are what justification and doubt run up against — that at the end of justifications we find not a proposition known with certainty but something more like a hinge on which the inquiry turns. So here is what I need from you. Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of the practice as I have described it, in your own terms. Then tell me what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely fits well with: does it require that hinge commitments be unjustifiable, or only that they not currently be in question? Does it require them to be true, or merely accepted? Does it require that they be shared across the community, or could a practice with the same shape survive widespread disagreement about them? Does it require them to be non-propositional, in the sense of being more like rules of the game than like beliefs, or would a reading on which they are genuine beliefs with an unusual kind of warrant license the practice equally well?
