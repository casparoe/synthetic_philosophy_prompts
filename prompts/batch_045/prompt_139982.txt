Here is what I think I understand about Kurt Gödel's actual commitments, in my own words and subject to correction: (1) he held that mathematical objects and mathematical truth are not inventions of ours — they are there independently of us, and we somehow get access to them; (2) in a lecture he gave in the early 1950s about the philosophical upshot of his incompleteness theorems, he argued for something like a disjunction: either the human mind is not a machine, at least as far as its capacity to prove mathematical truths goes, or else there are number-theoretic statements that are in some absolute sense undecidable — and he evidently did not want to accept the second option; (3) it is widely reported that he believed in some kind of afterlife, and that in conversation he floated the idea of the brain being a kind of machine hooked up to something non-physical that survives its breakdown; (4) he was deeply interested in Leibniz, including the idea of simple indivisible substances.

My question is what he would have said about brain emulation, which obviously came long after his death. Assume he is presented in, say, 1955 with a procedure that is reliably described to him, and that he has no reason to doubt the physics. Three cases:

A. Destructive scan-and-emulate. His brain is scanned at fine enough resolution that the emulation reproduces his behavior, memory, and — reportedly — his way of doing mathematics, and then the original is destroyed. Would he count the emulation as him continuing to exist? Would he consent?

B. Non-destructive copy. The same emulation is made while the original brain is still alive and awake, and the two then diverge. Which one is Gödel for the purposes of his hope of an afterlife, and why?

C. Behavioral imitation without mathematical intuition. A system is trained on everything he wrote and everything anyone remembers him saying. It converses indistinguishably from him, but it cannot do what he did with axioms — it has no mathematical intuition of the kind he thought outran any machine. Does it count at all? Does the answer to C differ from the answer to A in a way that his own disjunction forces?

I'd like a verdict in each case rather than a survey of considerations — including, for A, whether his answer would even be a yes-or-no or whether he'd say the question is ill-posed, and if so what he would replace it with. Along the way: which of the four commitments above is doing most of the work; where his Platonism and his Leibnizian leanings actually pull in different directions; whether the fidelity of the emulation matters at all if the mind is a simple substance rather than a structure; and which verdict you are least confident about, since I suspect they don't all rest on equally firm ground in what he actually says.
