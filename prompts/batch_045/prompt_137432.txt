I am putting together the opening session of an MPhil seminar on metaphysics and the special sciences, and before the students encounter any of the live accounts of natural kinds I want them to have in front of them a specification of what an account of this sort is meant to deliver. Please write that specification, in British English, in roughly a thousand words.

Fix the purpose first, since adequacy is relative to it: I want a theory of natural kinds that a working scientist could in principle use to decide whether a proposed new taxon is a genuine kind or merely a convenient filing category, and that a metaphysician could use to say what is at stake in that decision. Three stylised cases, invented for teaching, should anchor the discussion:

1. A condensed-matter collaboration proposes to count as a new kind of stuff a phase of matter defined by a topological invariant rather than by any symmetry-breaking pattern. It has no distinctive chemical composition; it is realised in a dozen materials with almost nothing in common at the microstructural level beyond the invariant itself; and it survives only below a third of a kelvin in a large applied field.
2. A microbial systematics group proposes a taxon defined by possession of a metabolic module that appears to have been transferred horizontally, and independently, on at least six occasions. Its members are scattered across the tree, and some lineages descended from ancestral bearers have lost the module.
3. A clinical consortium proposes a psychiatric category defined by a biomarker profile. The profile predicts treatment response better than the existing symptom-defined categories do, but it cuts straight across them, its members need share no single symptom, and it is common in people who would never present for care.

Give me the requirements list: what any theory adequate to that purpose has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. I want the phenomena it must accommodate (multiple realisation, revision and abandonment of would-be kinds, the way the same domain admits several cross-cutting but apparently respectable taxonomies at different grains, the mismatch between folk categories and scientific ones), the distinctions it must not collapse (a kind versus a property, versus an individual, versus a mere collection; being a kind versus being useful to someone; having a name versus having a referent), and the results it cannot contradict (that some special-science classifications project inductively and support counterfactuals even where no fundamental laws are stated over them, and that kind boundaries are frequently vague or interest-relative at the edges). I also want a verdict on whether it is a genuine requirement that kind membership be intrinsic, and on whether the sciences must be thought to carve at unique joints or may carve at many cross-cutting ones.

For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable or the prejudice of one camp. Flag any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and tell me whether that impossibility is a discovery about kinds or a sign that one of the desiderata was badly framed. Identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and say why. Close with at most a sentence or two each on four families of view — a microessentialist picture, a homeostatic-property-cluster picture, a causal-structuralist picture, and an interest-relative or conventionalist one — naming only the requirement each one fails, if any.

Please argue for the criteria rather than survey the literature: no reading lists, no citations, and nothing that would require me or you to look anything up. If you need a further example beyond the three cases above, invent one.
