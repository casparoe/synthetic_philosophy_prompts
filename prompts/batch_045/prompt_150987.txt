Our food co-op has an ethical sourcing working group (yes, really) and we're arguing about whether to put this term's effort into a vegan pledge drive or into campaigning for the uni to switch its catering supplier. Every time someone points out that one person giving up chicken moves the wholesale price by basically nothing, and so maybe saves no chicken at all, Rachel shuts it down with 'you vote with your fork'. She says it like it settles things: every purchase is a vote for the world you want, so of course it matters, and therefore the pledge drive is obviously what we should be doing.

I think the slogan is doing about four jobs at once and nobody is spelling out which one, and I'd rather bring an actual menu to the next meeting than just repeat it louder. So: what are the precise claims 'you vote with your fork' could be making? My guess is there's a nearly trivial one about demand curves sloping downwards, then more interesting ones: that food buying works like an election with thresholds, because suppliers commit to flock sizes and planting decisions in advance so individual purchases can tip a lumpy decision; that it's really about expression or complicity rather than outcomes, i.e. declaring which side you're on or refusing to take part; that it's about signalling to other people and shifting norms; and maybe something much stronger about markets being a kind of democracy whose outputs are legitimate because everyone has voted. But I can't tell which of those are platitudes, which are substantive and might be true, and which are just false.

The bit I keep getting stuck on — I've started calling it the pivot problem — is that votes and purchases have completely different mechanics. A vote only matters if it's pivotal, whereas a purchase always nudges the price a bit. So if the election reading is the real one, it inherits the standard worry that a single vote almost never changes an outcome, which is exactly the objection the slogan was being used to defeat. Is there a version of the election reading that escapes that, or does the slogan only work on the readings that aren't really about elections?

It's also got harder since a couple of us started reading about wild animal suffering. Under the boring causal reading, what you're voting for when you buy a chicken breast isn't just a chicken — it's the feed crop acreage and the field animals killed harvesting it and so on, and it's not obvious the vote is 'for' what you meant to vote for. As far as I can tell beef uses far more land per calorie but kills far fewer animals per calorie than chicken, and I might have that backwards, but either way I've no idea how to weigh it into a vote. Does any reading tell you how to choose between foods, rather than just telling you to eat less of the animal ones, or is the slogan useless for that?

What would actually help: a list of the distinct readings from trivial to radical, each with a verdict — platitude, contested but defensible, or wrong — and then for each one whether it would really rescue Rachel's conclusion that the pledge drive beats the supplier campaign. And if the honest answer is that it only works on a reading that doesn't support her conclusion, say so plainly, I'd definately rather have that than a diplomatic maybe.
