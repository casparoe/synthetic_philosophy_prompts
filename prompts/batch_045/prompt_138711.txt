ok so I just had a two-hour fight with my roommate about whether you can ever really compare how much one person gains against how much another person loses. Her side: utility functions are only defined up to a positive affine transformation, so you can move the zero and stretch the units however you like and nothing about the person changes, which means there is no fact of the matter about whether my bad headache outweighs your bad Tuesday, and anything that acts like there is has just quietly picked a convention and forgotten it. I basically grant all of that. But then she says cost-benefit analysis, quality-adjusted life years, and my family deciding whose turn it is to pick the restaurant are all therefore garbage, and there I think shes being useless — somebody still has to allocate the last ventilator, and they will do it with a spreadsheet.

So I want to make fun of the people who have to do the comparing anyway. Write me a deadpan internal training bulletin for new caseworkers at some agency that performs interpersonal welfare comparisons every working day while officially maintaining that they are impossible. Exactly five numbered sections, each with a short bureaucratic heading, 150-180 words each, no intro and no conclusion, no footnotes, no citations, and don't name any real philosopher or real paper. About 850 words total.

The jokes have to come from the theory being right, not from slapstick. Things I want exaggerated rather than invented:

- the calibration convention where each citizen's scale is normalized so their own worst feasible outcome is 0 and their best is 1, and how this means a person whose worst outcome is a slightly underboiled egg counts as much as a person whose worst outcome is eviction, plus the coaching that follows about how to describe your worst case, and what happens to your share when a bad option nobody would ever pick gets added to your option set
- the small class of citizens who convert resources into welfare at rates orders of magnitude above the median, and the bureau's audit procedure for them
- turning utils into money, and why the dollar figure depends on whether you ask what someone would pay or what they'd accept, and on how rich they already are
- self-reported wellbeing surveys, and the discount applied to people whose expectations have shrunk to fit a bad situation
- a final section that states flatly that comparisons are impossible, switches to asking only whether each citizen would rather have someone else's bundle, and then reintroduces comparison through a checkbox offering either the total or the floor

Never let the document say the word arbitrary, or otherwise admit what it's doing: a welfare economist should be able to read it and laugh without wincing, and a non-economist should get that something has gone badly wrong.
