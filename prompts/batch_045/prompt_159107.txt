I cover AI and science for a magazine and do a weekly-ish newsletter on the side, and I've let myself be talked into giving the after-dinner talk at a two-day closed-door workshop next month — maybe 25 people, off the record, so I can be blunter than I'd be in print. I've been on this beat for about six years, so I know the vocabulary, but I'm not going to win a formalism fight and I definately can't read an equation off a slide without embarrassing myself. Here's the room: roughly half are ML researchers from the big labs, two of whom think "takeoff speed" isn't even a coherent object of study — their line is that capabilities are jagged, there's no scalar you can put a doubling time on, and the whole fast-vs-slow debate is a fiction invented by people who read too much science fiction. There's also one growth economist who I'm pretty sure will ask me what my production function is, a historian of technology who has spent her career arguing that general-purpose technologies always look discontinuous from the inside, one person who actually works on model evals at a lab, a political scientist who will say the whole thing is unfalsifiable, and two foundation program officers who only care what any of this implies for grantmaking.

The argument I want to make is that the fast-takeoff vs. slow-takeoff framing is the wrong axis, and I'd rather not spend the talk re-litigating it. What I want to say is that there are two partly independent clocks: the capability clock (how fast the systems actually get better) and what I've been calling the legibility clock (how fast the people and institutions who would have to notice and respond can update their picture of what these systems can do). The thing that matters isn't the speed of the first clock on its own but the gap between the two — call it the recognition lag — and the uncomfortable punchline is that slow takeoff may be the worse case for governance, not the reassuring one, because gradual improvement is exactly the version nobody can see, whereas a real discontinuity would at least be visible and force a response. I also want to float a third shape besides fast and slow: a stuttering or stepwise takeoff, long plateaus and then jumps, which is roughly what the eval person's job looks like from the inside. My one historical example is the electrification story — factories keeping steam-era layouts for decades after the motors showed up, so the capability arrived long before anyone could see what it was for — but I have a feeling the historian is going to eat me alive on it.
