I teach a grad seminar on the set-theoretic paradoxes, and I keep bumping into an argumentative shape I don't have a word for. I want a name for it if there is one, and if there isn't I want a definition I can put on a handout — so please carve the thing rather than label it. And definately don't give me a reading list; I can find the literature myself, it's the concept I'm after.

Here's the shape. You know the setup: a theory that proves there are uncountably many reals has a model with only countably many things in it, because the one-to-one pairings that would show the reals to be countable are all missing from the model. The routine move is to shrug and say: fine, cardinality was never nailed down independently of what a model happens to contain, nothing to see here. And then — this is the bit I'm after — people *stop* shrugging. Point out that the same style of construction gives you a model whose natural numbers have extra ones parked after all the familiar ones, and that no first-order sentence rules those out, and nobody replies "oh, so finitude shifts too, fine." They reply "sure, but those aren't the real naturals." Ask what makes them the real ones and the answer always comes from outside the formalism: we can pin them down uniquely if we're allowed to quantify over all their subsets; or our grip on "and so on" just isn't first-order; or, most often, the theorem itself was proved by ordinary finitary reasoning, so I get to keep that much.

So: the conclusion is worded universally — nothing is nailed down, the quantifiers never reach the domain we mean — but it gets applied selectively, and where it stops is set by whatever the arguer needed in order to state it in the first place. Give up more background and the conclusion gets wider; help yourself to more and it gets narrower. What looks like one thesis is really a family indexed by how much the speaker is standing on. And what the two sides in these fights are actually haggling over is the exchange rate: how much skepticism you buy per unit of background surrendered. They agree on every formal result and disagree about a rate. That's the object I want a handle on.

I think I see the same shape elsewhere: the undefinability of truth — you can only define truth for a language from a richer one, so the notion always lives a level up, and the standard response is the hierarchy, which is fine except that the bottom level is always just declared healthy, and how much you declare healthy determines how much bite is left.

Things I want sorted out. First: is there an established concept for the scope-setting structure — a conclusion whose reach is fixed by the resources of its own statement, plus a disagreement that reduces to an exchange rate? Second, it isn't just a self-undermining claim: "nothing is nailed down" doesn't contradict itself the way "nothing is justified" does, it just fails to reach itself. Is there a term for that seperate thing, or does the literature on self-refutation swallow it? Third, it isn't the hierarchy either, I don't think: in a hierarchy the levels are ordered and there's a canonical next one, whereas here the stopping point looks like a choice with no privileged setting. Fourth, it isn't just the formal fact that these notions shift when you change models — lots of people note that; I'm after the *use* the fact gets put to.

If nothing established fits, coin something: define it, and give me boundary cases, one that clearly is an instance and one that looks like one and isn't. And tell me where your answer is weakest — especially whether my two cases really are the same shape at all, and what would convince you they aren't, or that there's a standard term you're failing to recall, or that my "exchange rate" framing misdescribes what the two sides are disagreeing about.
