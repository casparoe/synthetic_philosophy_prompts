I'm a master's student in philosophy of science, currently in a seminar on causal and mechanistic explanation, and I'm writing my final paper on what licenses a verdict of mechanistic novelty. The engineering setup I keep coming back to is mechanistic anomaly detection as it is applied to controllers that are trusted because they are understood: you log the internal state trajectory of a system — the intermediate variables it actually traverses, not merely its inputs and outputs — across a period judged to be healthy; you fit some representation of the region of internal-state space that healthy operation occupies; and thereafter you flag as anomalous any episode whose internal trajectory departs from that region, including episodes whose overt behavior is entirely correct. The point of the exercise is to catch the case in which the system is still doing the right thing but no longer doing it in the way it used to, before that change starts showing up in its outputs.

What I cannot get straight is the question the practice quietly depends on: when is a new route to the same behavior the *same* mechanism as the old one? And the practice has to answer this, because every flag is a claim that something has changed mechanistically while nothing has changed functionally, and every non-flag is a claim that the change was superficial.

Please write a dialogue between two people who disagree about this, of roughly 1,400 to 1,800 words. One holds that mechanisms are individuated by their causal-constitutive structure: same mechanism only if the same organized set of parts, activities, and constitutive relations is doing the work, with sameness tested by interventions on the intervening variables. The other holds that mechanisms are individuated by the role they play in producing a capacity of the containing system: same mechanism if the component occupies the same node in the right causal abstraction, supports the same counterfactuals at the level that matters, and would be substituted for the old one without explanatory loss. Neither should be a foil. Assume both have read everything, neither is confused about the other's view, and neither is allowed to win by stipulating a definition. They should work through all four of the following, because these are the cases my paper turns on:

1. *Rerouting.* A rain gauge feeding a flood-control sluice controller fails; the controller recovers the same inflow estimate from a river-level sensor plus a hydraulic model, and continues to open the gates correctly. Is this a new mechanism for the same decision, or the same mechanism differently realized?

2. *Drift.* Over two years the controller's parameters are refit forty-one times, each refit tiny enough that behavior is unchanged on that day's tests, and the final controller shares no internal component with the original though every adjacent pair behaved identically. Is there a fact of the matter about when it stopped being the same mechanism, and if the sameness predicate is vague or thresholdless, does the anomaly detector have any principled point at which to fire?

3. *Mimicry under an untested intervention.* The new mechanism is indistinguishable from the old under every intervention anyone thought to test, but diverges under a rare conjunction — say, sensor icing together with a surge — where it fails catastrophically. Is sameness of mechanism then relative to a set of interventions or a contrast class, and if so, relative to whose interests; or is it a mind-independent structural fact that the monitor simply failed to detect?

4. *The damper.* The controller contains a component whose entire job is to keep gate actuation from oscillating, so that when it works, nothing observable happens. Two implementations — a deadband and a predictive pre-emptive adjustment — both yield no oscillation. Are these the same mechanism of oscillation suppression? If the functionalist's criterion appeals to behavioral counterfactuals, what counterfactuals are left when success is an absence; and if the structuralist says they are obviously different, what was the detector even monitoring during the healthy period?

Finally, I want both of them to face the operationalist challenge I have not been able to shake. The detector learns a similarity metric over internal states, and whatever metric we train implicitly defines which internal differences count as the same mechanism going on. Perhaps *same mechanism* should just mean: same equivalence class under whatever similarity metric best predicts the failures we care about. Both characters should say why that is or is not an answer rather than a change of subject — in particular, whether it makes mechanism talk collapse into prediction, and whether the residual disagreement is about metaphysics, about the grain at which mechanisms should be specified, or only about what an engineer is permitted to care about.

Two constraints. Please do not give me a literature survey: no citations, no summary of who has argued what, no reading suggestions — I need the arguments stated and defended in the characters' own terms, since I have to present this in seminar and defend the framing myself. And please have the dialogue end with both characters having conceded something real and having located the precise point at which they still diverge, rather than with one of them refuted or with the disagreement dissolved as merely verbal. If you think the disagreement *is* merely verbal, say so inside the dialogue and let the other character answer that.
