I chair the ethics consultation subcommittee at a 300-bed community hospital, and eight months ago I was named healthcare proxy for my father, who has since been diagnosed with Lewy body dementia. I want to replace the way our team currently handles conflicts between an advance directive and what the patient is saying now.

Our consultations run about twenty-five a year, plus bedside calls that a hospitalist or charge nurse makes alone at 2 a.m. with no philosopher in the building. The decision is always the same shape: a document signed years earlier says one thing, the person in front of us says another, and we have to decide whose voice governs — or, when the person can no longer speak for themselves at all, what we are bound to do with a document whose author is still alive and sitting in the bed. The stakes are asymmetric and both directions are bad: the wrong override can mean treatment someone spent a decade arranging to avoid, or an irreversible withholding from someone who wanted to live. Sign-off is the attending plus the proxy, with escalation to the full committee or to our legal department only when the conflict survives the first pass. Whatever we decide goes in the chart and may eventually be read by a family or a judge, so documentation is part of the deliverable, not an afterthought.

I have read enough of the philosophical literature to know the shape of the argument — the view that an earlier self's settled, identity-constituting values should be honored over the desires of a later self who has lost the capacity to weigh them, and the rival view that the person now in the bed is the one who actually experiences what happens and that precedent autonomy is a fiction imposed on a stranger. What I have not found is anything that tells a nurse at 2 a.m. what to do next. So please build me a decision procedure, not an essay.

What I want it to do:

Take as input a written directive and a present clinical situation in which honoring the directive and honoring the patient's present expression come apart, and output one of three things — honor the document, override it, or escalate — by way of ordered questions, each with a stated threshold for what counts as a sufficient answer. Mark every point at which the procedure terminates early with a no, and every point at which it must hand the decision back to a person because the remaining question is a values question rather than a factual one. I expect the handoff points to be the most useful part of the document.

Four distinctions I want the procedure to carry, because I think they are where our current practice goes wrong:

Counterfactual unawareness. The earlier self was not choosing under the description that now applies. She was choosing between abstractions she filled in with the worst case she could picture. I want the procedure to test, as a factual matter, whether the situation that has actually arrived is one the directive can be read as addressing at all, and to distinguish that question from the question of whether the earlier self would have extended it. My sense is that we routinely collapse the two and call the result fidelity.

Asymmetric scrutiny. We audit a present refusal for delirium, sedation, depression, and the influence of whoever last visited, and we do not audit the document for provenance — whether it was a form ticked at an admission desk, whether it was written during a bad month, whether the person who signed it had ever been inside an ICU. I want both voices held to the same evidentiary standard, and I want that symmetry to be an explicit step rather than a reminder.

Graded authenticity in place of the capacity gate. Capacity is a binary legal finding, but in practice it is where our entire judgment gets smuggled in: once the patient is found to lack capacity the directive controls automatically, and once she is found to have it the document becomes irrelevant. I want a graded profile of how much weight the present utterance carries — consistency across time and across questioners, whether it tracks features of the present situation or repeats regardless of context, whether stated reasons match observed behavior — used to decide how much evidence it takes to defeat the document, not merely whether it counts at all.

Rationale falsification. Some directives state their reasons. When a stated reason turns out to be false — the burden feared was never real, the prognosis assumed has been overtaken by a treatment that did not exist when the document was signed, the person named as the one who would suffer is dead — I want a test for whether the directive fails with its reason or survives independently of it, and I want the procedure to say what kind of evidence would settle that.

Two structural constraints. First, reversibility: where one option can be undone later and the other cannot, the procedure should have something to say about which error we should prefer, and should not pretend the two errors are symmetric. Second, the law: in our jurisdiction a prior directive is presumptively binding on an incapacitated patient, so any step that recommends overriding one needs to say plainly what the legal exposure is and who has to be told, without letting that replace the ethical question.

Then run it on three cases:

A 41-year-old woman with bipolar I and three prior admissions, each ending with her abandoning lithium mid-episode. During a documented period of stability she completed a detailed directive refusing lithium and ECT during any future manic episode and named a friend as proxy. She is now manic, refusing lithium, and offers a reason the earlier self did not weigh: a tremor on the medication ended her paid violin work, and she says she would rather be ill than never play again. Her proxy agrees with her current refusal and describes her as unusually lucid.

A 74-year-old man with advanced dementia who can no longer state a preference. In 2003, after watching his mother die on a ventilator following a massive stroke, he signed a directive refusing machines to keep him alive and heroic measures, with a clause limiting this to cases where there is no reasonable hope of recovery. He has now fractured a hip; surgery and a few days of postoperative ventilation would very likely return him to his baseline of walking with a frame and singing in a choir. He shows clear pleasure in music and food and no signs of suffering. His son says the document settles it; his daughter says her father never meant this.

A 68-year-old woman with moderate early-onset Alzheimer's, living at home with a paid caregiver. Six years ago, after a friend's long ICU stay, she wrote a directive by hand following two conversations with her physician, refusing hospitalisation and all life-prolonging treatment, and giving as her sole reason that she would not let her daughter Anna give up her life to care for her. Anna died in a car crash two years ago; there is no other family; the caregiver is funded by an insurance settlement. She now has urosepsis and has said several times, calmly and consistently, that she does not want to die and that we should do whatever it takes. I should tell you that I have argued on our committee that a considered, specific, handwritten directive should be very close to conclusive, and I expect my colleagues will expect it too — so if your procedure says otherwise in this case, I want it to say so and to show the work, because that is exactly the case I will be asked about.

Finally, I want the failure section to be honest rather than reassuring. Tell me which steps are theater we will document because they look like reasoning rather than because they change anything; tell me what the procedure cannot settle — the patient whose capacity and whose wishes both fluctuate day to day, the non-verbal patient whose objection is pure behavior, the proxy who is also the residuary beneficiary, and the case where the earlier self pre-committed in full awareness that she would later resist and the resistance is therefore evidence the mechanism is working rather than evidence against it. And give me a one-page version, in chart-note form, that the 2 a.m. hospitalist can actually use.
