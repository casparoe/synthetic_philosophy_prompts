hey — im in a seminar on collective intentionality and joint action this term (it's cross-listed philosophy of action / social ontology) and for the last session our TA said we could make something instead of writing a paper. I want to build a "which theory of joint action do you actually hold?" quiz for the rest of the class: ten concrete cases, you answer them, and at the end you get told what you're committed to. Can you write it for me?

Please think carefully about this as an actual design problem, and take on the role of a professional philosopher who has TA'd a Bratman-vs-Gilbert seminar a dozen times. I definately don't want the encyclopedia version where each question is a paraphrase of a famous paper; I want the version where the cases do real work and someone who knows the labels still has to think.

What I care about:

- The questions have to be cases, not labels. Nobody gets asked "do you think shared intention is irreducible?" or "is the group an agent?". Each question is a small scenario with two or three answers you could pick without knowing any theory name, and the answers should be the sort of thing reasonable people in the room would split on.
- Ten questions, three options each (A/B/C), for other students who've had maybe six weeks of this. Readable cases, but not soft — no question should have an option nobody would pick.
- The leaves have to include at minimum: Searle-style collective or we-intentions as something primitive in individual heads; Bratman's shared cooperative activity built out of interlocking individual plans and mutual responsiveness; Gilbert's plural subject / joint commitment; Kutz-style participatory intentions and the overdetermining-end account; Tuomela's we-mode versus I-mode; List-and-Pettit style robust group agents; David Lewis-style convention as the fallback for cases that aren't joint action at all; and one deflationary option where there is nothing over and above individual intentions plus expectations about others. If you think a leaf belongs that I haven't listed, add it and say why.
- Start from the logical space rather than from my name list: work out the discriminating questions or axes first, see which combinations of answers they allow, then drop the actual views into the cells.
- I made up three cases I really want used, because the textbook ones (painting the house together, walking together, the rowboat) feel like everyone already knows what they're for:
  - relay editing: two people revise the same document in sequence, never meet and never message, each only ever sees the version the other last left, and each is trying to make the document better. Did they jointly write it? Then two variants: one of them secretly doesn't care about the document and is doing it purely for the money; and the other "person" is a language model with no intentions of its own.
  - the saboteur in the crew: five sailors hoist a sail together; one of them is quietly planning to let the rope slip at the crucial moment. Is there a joint action here at all, and is the saboteur a participant in it?
  - the announced plan: someone walks into a room, announces "we're going to clean up the park this afternoon", and four people silently pick up trash bags. Nobody agreed out loud, nobody refused, nobody was asked. Joint action? And if so, whose?
- Don't stop at the questions: tell me which pairs of views your quiz can't seperate and what a question that would separate them would have to look like, and tell me which combinations of answers are empty and whether that's because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Format — use these section names, in this order:

1. Brainstorm (~200 words) — the discriminating questions and axes before any theory names; show your work on what actually pulls the views apart.
2. The quiz (~700 words) — the ten cases with their A/B/C options, numbered, each with a one-line note in brackets saying which distinction that question is probing.
3. Scoring key (~400 words) — how patterns of answers map onto positions; name each position with one line of gloss; say what to do with mixed or unclear patterns.
4. Logical space (~300 words) — the grid or tree of answer-combinations, which cells the known views occupy, and a diagnosis for each empty cell.
5. Where it breaks (~200 words) — the pairs of views the ten questions can't distinguish, what a discriminating question for them would need to look like, and anything the quiz will systematically misdiagnose (people answering by vibe, people who've read one author, etc.).
