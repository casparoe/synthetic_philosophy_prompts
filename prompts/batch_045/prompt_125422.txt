"Question 3. (a) Define 'rigid designator'. (b) Kripke holds that 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is both necessary and knowable only a posteriori. Explain how that combination is supposed to be coherent. (c) Does this result follow from his causal, anti-descriptivist account of how names get their reference? Why or why not?"

And here's the student's answer, more or less verbatim:

"'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' were both names for what turned out to be the planet Venus; people simply didn't realise for a long time that they were looking at one object rather than two.

(a) A rigid designator is a term that designates the same object in every possible world. This is why names are rigid and most definite descriptions are not: in another possible world 'the morning star' might have picked out Mars, whereas 'Phosphorus' picks out Venus no matter what. The rigidity of a name comes from the fact that it has no descriptive content — it's a tag fastened onto the object at an original baptism and then passed along a causal chain of speakers, so there is no description for the object to fail to satisfy in some other possible world. Descriptions, by contrast, pick out whatever satisfies them, and that varies from world to world.

(b) Since both 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' are rigid designators, there is no possible world in which they designate different things, and hence no possible world in which the identity is false. But we had to do astronomy to discover that they designate the same thing, so our knowledge of it is a posteriori. This looks paradoxical only because we tend to run necessity and apriority together. Kripke's point is that they belong to different categories: necessity is a metaphysical feature of the proposition, apriority an epistemic feature of our relation to it, so nothing prevents one and the same proposition from being both necessary and a posteriori. Note that it isn't the sentence that's necessary in some merely verbal sense — the objects themselves are identical of necessity, and the sentence inherits that. What the case really shows is that having two different ways of picking out the same object can leave the identity epistemically open for us even though there is no world in which it fails. Kripke's other famous example works the same way: the claim that the standard metre bar in Paris is one metre long is necessary but knowable only a posteriori.

(c) Yes, essentially — this is where the causal picture does the real work. If names got their reference via descriptions, as Frege and Russell thought, then whether 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is true would vary from world to world, and the identity would come out contingent. Once reference is fixed causally rather than descriptively, the name hooks directly onto the object itself, and the statement inherits the necessity of Venus's being identical with Venus. So the necessary a posteriori is basically a corollary of the causal theory of reference: fix reference descriptively and you get contingency, fix it causally and you get necessity."
