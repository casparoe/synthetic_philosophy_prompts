I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, working on internal tooling for an ML research team. I keep running into the "fast takeoff versus slow takeoff" argument in ML-adjacent writing, and I'd like it restated in the vocabulary I actually use — and, more importantly, I'd like to know what that vocabulary can't carry.

The vocabulary I reach for: critical path and slack; throughput versus latency; Amdahl's law and parallel speedup; queueing, utilization, and Little's law; build times and CI cycle time; self-hosting and bootstrapping (a compiler written in the language it compiles); staged rollout with canaries; blast radius, rollback, and SLOs; and above all observability — what you can and cannot infer about a running process from outside it.

My own half-finished translation, which I suspect is too glib: internally we use what we call the "queue-migration heuristic." Every time we automate a stage of the research loop, the end-to-end iteration time does not fall by the duration of that stage; it falls only to the cost of the next-slowest stage, and that stage is almost never a thinking stage but a waiting stage — cluster queue, data licensing, wet-lab turnaround, security review, procurement, regulators. Extrapolated, the heuristic says takeoff is slow, and not because capability stops improving but because the critical path runs through things that aren't reasoning.

What I want:

1. Restate the dispute as faithfully as you can in those terms: what is each side claiming about the critical path, about what can be parallelized by copying versus what has to be improved serially, and about whether "takeoff speed" is one number at all.
2. A residue report. Which claims translate cleanly; which survive only after being distorted (name the distortion); and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary — in particular the claim that capability could jump discontinuously while deployment stays gradual, and the claim that most economically important work is serial cognitive labor that becomes parallel once you can copy a model. If a step of the translation needs an assumption my vocabulary doesn't contain, flag it rather than quietly supplying it.
3. An audit of the "queue-migration heuristic." Where does it break? I can see two candidate problems — it may be assuming a fixed topology of stages, and it measures iteration latency when the argument is about capability — but I want the failure modes I can't see, including whether any version of the fast-takeoff claim survives it untouched.
4. Finally: is the residue a blind spot in my framework, or evidence that the forecasters are arguing about something less well-defined than it sounds? If it's a blind spot, name the concept I'm missing and define it in engineer's terms.

Please define any forecasting term of art you need, but don't explain my own engineering concepts back to me. No citations, bibliographies, or suggested reading — I can't look anything up. Please answer in American English, with American spelling and idiom.
