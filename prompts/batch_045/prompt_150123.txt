I'm retired — thirty-odd years in experimental fluid mechanics, mostly instrumentation and measurement — and since last autumn I've been sitting in on a philosphy of science reading group at the university near where I live. I'm the only person there who isn't a graduate student, which has advantages and disadvantages. I've ended up defending a position about thought experiments that the rest of the group keeps poking at, and I'd like to know how exposed it really is.

The position, roughly: a thought experiment in physics never delivers any content that wasn't already in the theory and the side assumptions we brought to it. What it does is force us to notice a commitment we already had. It's bookkeeping, not discovery. When Galileo points out that if heavy bodies fall faster, then tying a light stone to a heavy one should both slow the pair down, since the slower stone holds it back, and speed it up, since the pair is heavier than the heavy stone alone, nothing new enters the picture. We're just being made to see that the rule contradicts itself once you allow the tying move. The bucket is the same sort of thing: we already had views about absolute and relative motion, and the bucket only makes us say out loud which one we're dropping.

The bit I added myself, so be suspicious of it: I claim the apparent cases of discovery — Einstein wondering what he'd see if he ran alongside a light wave, the elevator, the demon sorting fast molecules from slow ones — are cases where the scenario is secretly specifying a measurement protocol. It tells you what would have to be measurable, or what a measurement would have to show, for a theory to be in trouble. The thought experiment proposes an instrument. So still no new content: the new content comes from the physics we do once we take the protocol seriously. I've started calling these protocol cases and the Galileo ones bookkeeping cases, and I claim every thought experiment that has ever persuaded me is one or the other.

What would count against this? Not just that it's too sweeping. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to say which ones a defender of my view should concede rather than shrug off. In particular:

- Is there a thought experiment that changed the physics where no instrument was being specified and no inconsistency was being exposed? If there's one clear case, I'd probably give up — that's the thing that would move me most.
- Does the demon case count against me on its own? The eventual answer was that finding out which molecules are fast costs something, and turning a sorting device into real thermodynamics looks an awful lot like discovery to me.
- I've given myself a test: strip the story away and ask whether the conclusion survives as a claim about what some instrument would read. If it does, protocol case; if it doesn't and the thing still persuades, I'm wrong. Is that test any good, or does it bake in my conclusion?
- Does the view collapse into the standard one, that thought experiments are arguments with suppressed premises? I think mine is different, because on my reading what the scenario adds is an operationalisation that wasn't already in the theory — but I may be kidding myself.
- My taxonomy can absorb nearly any example by declaring it a protocol case. Is that a real problem or just untidy vocabulary? Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so does that matter for a view of this kind?
- Does any of this travel outside physics? There's an evolutionary biologist in the group who says thought experiments do something else entirely in her field, and I dont know what to make of that.

I gather there's a long-running fight in the literature between people who think thought experiments are arguments in disguise and people who think some of them are closer to a direct grasp of how nature has to be. I can never remember who holds which and I don't need the names, but tell me if one of those camps has already made my argument better than I have.

Please say how confident you are in what you claim, and flag the single point you're least sure of. Keep it under 700 words — terse bullets are fine, I'd rather have short and partial than definately padded out.
