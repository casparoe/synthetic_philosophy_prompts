I'm working on a LW-ish post arguing that "safe Pareto improvement" is a *local* notion that doesn't compose, and I want a worked case to hang it on rather than another hand-wave about Moloch. So: build me a ratchet chain.

By a safe Pareto improvement I mean roughly: (a) everyone affected is better off than immediately before, by their own lights at the time; (b) you don't have to be *right* about anything hard for that to hold — the benefit survives across a broad class of models of how the world works, not just your favourite one. Call a chain **gradient-safe** if every step is a safe PI relative to the state it starts from, and **terminally bad** if the endpoint is worse for every affected party than the origin, judged by the preferences those parties had back at the origin.

Specs:

1. Three or four steps. Ordinary institution — a hospital ward, a university department, a condo board, a church, a small company. No AI, no x-risk, no trolleys, no sci-fi.
2. Every step passes (a) and (b). For each step name the affected parties, what they get, and the class of models across which the benefit holds.
3. The endpoint is worse than the start for *every* affected party — a genuine Pareto regression, not a tradeoff where the losers are compensated for on net.
4. Nobody in the story is stupid, malicious, self-deceived, or ignorant in a way they could have fixed by being more careful. The steps are not mistakes. If your case only works because someone should have foreseen a hidden interaction, it's useless to me — I need the local verdicts to be genuinely correct at the time there made.
5. The steps are taken by different people, or by the same people at different times, each facing only their own decision.

Then the walkthrough, with verdicts:

- **Step-local safe-Pareto filter** (*is this a safe PI from here?*): GO at every step.
- **Step-local expected value**, taking the situation as given and counting the full consequences of the step: also GO at every step. I want the case where the safety filter and multiplying-out *agree* locally — not the easy case where the filter is just dumber than the EV calculation.
- **The ex ante view**, from before step one, knowing the whole chain: nobody wants it, and the rule *when a gradient-safe improvement is available, take it* is net-negative for everyone affected.

The point being that the two local evaluators agree all the way down and still walk off a cliff. Tell me what's doing the work: my guess is that safety is being checked against a *state* while the steps are quietly changing the *dynamics* (or the preferences), so local robustness buys you nothing globally — say which it is in your case, and ideally make it both.

Also tell me what would make such a chain impossible in principle. If preferences were fixed and the world were a well-behaved state you could move around in reversibly, does the ratchet collapse into something harmless? Which of those conditions does your case break, and is that break doing real work or just decorating the story?

Then remove the feature and report: rewrite it so the same steps are decided simultaneously by one committee that can see the whole package. Does the problem survive, or does it dissolve into an ordinary coordination failure that a meeting would fix?

And give me the strongest version of the obvious pushback — that a real SPI check belongs on the *policy* of SPI-taking rather than on individual steps, so nothing here tells against the rule at all — and say honestly whether that dissolves my case or just relocates the problem one level up. I suspect the relocated check quietly stops being safe in sense (b), which would be the actual payload of the post, but I'd rather you argued me out of it if that's wrong.

Scenario in one paragraph; the analysis can be as long as it needs. Please flag how confident you are in each of the verdicts and single out the claim you're least sure of — especially whether specs 2 and 3 can really both hold, since that's the joint I think is most likely to be unsatisfiable.

Lead with one sentence saying whether the case you built actually meets specs 1 through 4 and what the trick is, then the scenario, then the walkthrough. No preamble, no restating the question.
