You are a political philosopher and institutional economist who has spent a decade advising regulators, standards bodies and collective-action funders on why coordination fails, and who is unusually good at turning game-theoretic ideas into checklists that intelligent non-specialists can actually run. Please think carefully before you write: brainstorm two or three candidate procedures, note in a sentence or two what each one loses, and then give me the one you recommend. Write the whole thing in British English.

I direct the secretariat of a small philanthropic fund that makes around twenty-five grants a year, mostly between £200k and £2m, to interventions whose stated purpose is to stop a race to the bottom in some domain: a voluntary cap, a shared audit standard, a no-first-use pledge, a joint liability arrangement, an information-sharing or monitoring scheme. Our analysts get roughly one working day per proposal, the proposal pack is often thin on the numbers, and the final decision is made by a committee of seven trustees, four of whom have no economics or philosophy background and will not read anything longer than four pages. Last year we funded a voluntary moratorium in one sector and watched the race reappear a few months later as a fight over who got to write the standard, and I do not want to repeat that. So I need a decision procedure my analysts can run on any such proposal: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the gates where we simply stop with a no, the gates where we hand the decision to the trustees because the question is one of values rather than facts, and an explicit flag on every step that is really a judgement call dressed up as a procedure. Tell me what an analyst should do when a number cannot be obtained. Keep the procedure itself to something readable in fifteen minutes.

I also want you to build in, as explicit steps, some distinctions that our discussions keep collapsing. First, whether the bad outcome is genuinely structural, in the sense that each party would prefer the outcome in which nobody races yet each still gains by racing, or whether it is really produced by a handful of identifiable actors whose behaviour we could sanction directly, because the two call for very different interventions. Second, the entrant margin: even if the post-intervention payoff matrix makes restraint rational for existing signatories, would a new actor who never signed still find defection dominant, and is that actor likely to matter? Third, axis relocation: suppressing a race along one dimension usually moves it to another, such as jurisdictional arbitrage, acquisition of the restrained, redefinition of the metric, or competition among the monitors, so require the proposal to name the axis the race is expected to move to and why competition there is less destructive. Fourth, detection latency: if the gains from defection land before defection can be verified, no monitoring regime saves the arrangement. Fifth, whether the good being competed away is one the racers themselves value, one that only outsiders value, or one nobody values, and what follows for each. Sixth, the asymmetry question, whether the arrangement degrades gracefully when it partly fails or fails catastrophically, and who bears the residual risk, including people who never signed. Finally, apply the procedure once recursively to whoever enforces the arrangement, since enforcers are themselves subject to races.

Then run the finished procedure on three proposals and show your working, including where it stalls. One: a pledge among nine frontier AI developers not to train above a stated compute threshold until a shared evaluation regime exists, with signatories covering most but not all of the credible actors, and with the evaluators funded by the developers themselves. Two: a common third-party safety and quality audit standard written and adopted by fifteen small and mid-sized firms in a fragmented subcontracting industry, while the four largest buyers, who hold around sixty per cent of the market, have declined to sign and say they will revisit it next year; compliance costs fall mostly on the signatories. Three, and this is the one where I expect an answer I will not like: a two-year convening and norm-building programme we have already half-promised to an ally, consisting of a secretariat, an annual summit, a shared vocabulary and a pledge text, with no monitoring and no change to anyone's payoffs, which several trustees are enthusiastic about. Be blunt about what the procedure says on that one, and do not soften it to keep me happy. Close with a short section on what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of race it is structurally blind to, the proposals it will systematically underrate, and the failure mode in which having a procedure becomes a reason to fund nothing at all.
