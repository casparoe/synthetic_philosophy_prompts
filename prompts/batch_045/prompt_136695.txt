I teach political philosophy, and I am preparing a graduate seminar on the socialist calculation debate. At a departmental colloquium last week a colleague from another department brought a discussion to a close with a line I keep meeting in one form or another: the market is only a computing device, so the planners can simulate it. He was using it to settle a particular question: whether Mises's and Hayek's arguments against economic calculation under socialism were, at bottom, complaints about the scarcity of computing power in the 1920s and 1930s, and are therefore obsolete now that computation is cheap and data arrive in real time. I want to know what that sentence could really be asserting.

Two stipulations before you begin. Grant the planner everything: honest and accurate quantity reports, no political interference, no corruption, and as much computing power as the argument needs. And do not rest anything on the historical record of twentieth-century planned economies — my colleague will say, with some justice, that none of them was the simulation anyone actually described. The question is whether the slogan can be true on those generous terms.

Give me the readings as a menu, ordered roughly from the trivial to the demanding, rather than picking one and defending it. For each reading I want the precise thesis it would be expressing, a verdict on it — platitude, substantive and defensible, genuinely contested, or simply false — and, the part I care most about, whether that reading actually supports the use my colleague made of it, that is, whether it really does turn the older objection into a merely technological complaint.

Three distinctions from my own notes may be useful, though do not feel bound by them. First, outcome-fidelity versus process-fidelity: must the simulation reproduce the allocation a market would have produced, or reproduce the procedure by which the information needed to compute that allocation comes into being? Second, a closed versus an open set of production possibilities: is the list of goods, techniques and attainable plans given in advance, or is it partly discovered by the very activity being simulated? Third, the strategic layer: a simulation that must also be immune to strategic misreporting runs against the familiar impossibility results of mechanism design, on which efficiency, voluntary participation, budget balance and truthfulness cannot all be secured at once, so a fully specified simulation may be forced to sacrifice one of them.

If one of your readings is the Hayekian point about knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place, state it as a thesis with a truth-condition rather than as a slogan in its turn. Finally, for whichever readings survive, tell me whether the dispute has become an empirical one about magnitudes or remains a priori, and give me the most charitable version of the line I could have conceded to my colleague in the room without conceding him his conclusion. I do not need citations or suggestions for reading; this is purely a matter of getting the claims straight.
