I'm writing from a hospital bed, so let me be brief about the personal part: I'm a final-year PhD candidate in philosophy, and since last spring I have had a treatment-refractory neuropathic pain condition that has not responded to the three medication classes we have tried. My topic is no longer abstract for me. In two weeks I present the third chapter of my dissertation to the Bioethics Center seminar, and I need Q&A preparation, not encouragement.

The chapter defends a deliberately narrowed version of the abolitionist project sometimes called the hedonistic imperative. Four moves, in order.

First, a scope restriction. I am not talking about pain, grief, effort, or the ordinary discomfort of a hard project. I restrict the claim to what I call the refractory floor: sustained states that are unresponsive to any available intervention and that globally degrade the subject's capacities.

Second, the priority claim: when we are choosing among welfare-affecting interventions, raising the floor dominates raising the ceiling. I do not argue this from diminishing marginal welfare; that argument is in the literature and I set it aside. I argue it from what I call the erosion reply: the states at the refractory floor degrade the very capacities on which our standard objections to intervention depend — the capacity to form and revise preferences, to integrate an experience into a narrative, to refuse on reflection, to take up a reason. So the objections available higher up the scale (that the subject must consent, that the suffering is meaning-constitutive for them) are self-undermining exactly where my claim bites.

Third, a criterion for distinguishing abolition from obliteration, which I call the aim-integrity test: an intervention is admissible only if the agent's standing aims survive it in the counterfactual sense that, under the intervention, the agent would still revise their aims in response to reasons roughly as they would have done had the pain simply been absent. This is meant to be what separates my view from wireheading.

Fourth, because the erosion reply cuts against ordinary consent-gating, I propose replacing informed consent as the gate for this population with an advance-authorization protocol: the patient authorizes in a lucid period, authorization lapses unless re-affirmed at scheduled windows, and revocation takes effect immediately with no requirement that the patient give a good reason.

The audience. Two senior scholars from disability studies and medical humanities who hold the social model and are alert to medicalization and to the expressive force of a research program like mine. Three clinicians: a palliative care physician, an interventional pain physician, and an addiction psychiatrist, who think in functional outcomes, iatrogenesis, tolerance, adaptation, and adherence. Two philosophers: a welfare pluralist with narrative-identity sympathies, and someone who works on autonomy and informed consent. A health lawyer chairs.
