Hi — I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've got myself into a bit of a muddle that I'd really like an actual expert in Madhyamaka / Buddhist philosophy to sort out for me. Please take your time with it: maybe start by brainstorming two or three very different ways of reading what I've done before you settle on one, and show me a bit of that working rather than just the polished verdict. Some background, since I think it matters: my new book is about a translator who finds a manuscript that may or may not be her own grandmother's, and one of the characters — a retired logic tutor who keeps turning up in the margins — has this habit of answering perfectly ordinary questions by declining all four corners. I started off just nicking the tetralemma as a bit of texture, and then, annoyingly, it turned out to be useful, and now I can't stop running it over every question the book raises. I mentioned it to my writing group in Norwich last week and got about six incompatible accounts of what the thing even is, so: consider yourself hired as my very patient consultant on Nāgārjuna and the four corners.

Here's where I've landed, going purely on gut, case by case. (1) Did the narrator's mother die in the fire? — there is a right answer inside the fiction; the tetralemma has no business here at all. (2) Is the woman narrating the last chapter the same person as the child in the first? — my gut says neither the same nor different, and I want to keep that. (3) Does the protagonist exist? — I want to say both: she does conventionally, she doesn't ultimately, and I feel the pull of that third corner much more than I feel it for anything else. (4) The stock cosmic questions — does a liberated one exist after death, is the world finite in extent — I want to refuse all four and not quietly settle on the fourth as the real answer. (5) Is the meaning of the book what I intended it to mean? — neither identical to my intention nor unrelated to it. (6) Is emptiness itself empty? — yes, and it had better be, otherwise I've just swapped one absolute for another; but that's me flatly asserting something, which seems to collide with the bit where I'm not supposed to have a thesis of my own. (7) Is the narrator lying about the fire? — yes, straightforwardly, and I don't want any of this to let her off. (8) Was the manuscript written by one person? — neither one author nor many, which sounds great in a sentence and may be nonsense. (9) Should I run the tetralemma on the question of whether to run the tetralemma? — no idea; I go in circles. My own stab at a principle, so you can test it rather than guess it: a question is tetralemma-eligible only if either a yes or a no would have to be true of the thing *from its own side*, independent of anything's being picked out or designated as such — call it the own-side test — and for eligible questions all four corners get shown to fail with nothing left over; for everything else, convention settles it and there's no puzzle.

So, three things back please. First, does the own-side test actually deliver my verdicts, and if it needs repair, say exactly which clause each of my cases is doing the motivating for — I'd rather have a principle I can hold in my head while I'm drafting than a tour of the literature. Second, give me its verdicts on three cases I haven't listed: whether the protagonist is still alive in a sequel I haven't written yet, whether there's a fact of the matter about how many chapters the finished book will have, and whether the reader's version of a character and mine are the same character. Third — and I care about this more than the rest — be straight with me about what's settled and what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this: I take it nobody disputes that emptiness is the absence of own-being rather than nothing-at-all, but I get the sense that real fights remain over whether "neither" is itself a position or just a refusal, over whether you may argue by reductio alone or owe the world some independent inference of your own (my friend Dev mentioned a falling-out between two early commentators over exactly that, so tell me if I've got it right), and over whether any of this is a metaphysical claim or a therapeutic one. Don't hand me a contested reading dressed up as the standard one; where you have to pick a side, say that you're picking, and tell me how much of your answer you'd bet on. And if no single principle covers the lot, tell me which of my verdicts has to go and what that would cost the book.
