I'm running a Tolkien-and-philosophy night for a bunch of undergrads in a couple weeks, and I've got stuck on the session I volunteered to do on weakness of will. I want to build it around Gollum/Sméagol and maybe Frodo at the very end, but I want an actual reading, not "Gollum is addicted, lol" with footnotes.

Here's the frame I've been chewing on, which may be wrong and you should tell me if it is. The standard setup says akrasia is acting against your own better judgment, in the moment, eyes open. Then economics gives you hyperbolic discounting: nothing goes wrong with judgment at all, the discount function just isn't exponential, and the flip is fully predictable from outside. I want to add a third thing that I think the books need, and I'm calling it proximity reversal: the flip isn't a smooth function of how soon the reward arrives, it's a function of whether the thing is right there, perceptually present, in the room. On that model somebody can be steady for months while the object is only ever described to them and then come apart in thirty seconds once it's in front of them, which is more or less what happens with Boromir. So:

1. Which of these is Tolkien actually giving us with Gollum — competing judgments, a discount function, or my proximity thing? And does the two-voices business help the philosophy or dodge it? My worry is that Gollum is so literally divided that he stops being a case of weakness of will and becomes two agents in one body, which is exactly the objection people raise against partitioning the soul. If that's right, the genuinely interesting case in the book isn't Gollum at all, it's Frodo, who isn't divided: he says all along what he intends to do, and then at the Cracks of Doom doesn't do it.

2. Which brings up the thing I can't settle. Plenty of readers hold that nobody could have destroyed the Ring at the one place where it has to be destroyed, that the thing was made to be unresistable exactly there. If that's right, then Frodo's failure isn't weakness of will, it's much closer to being pushed, and the tradition going back to Socrates draws that line pretty sharply. So is the ending a counterexample to the reading rather than an illustration of it? Or is there a way to keep both without cheating?

3. I also want the precommitment angle. Gandalf and Galadriel both refuse the thing outright, and Faramir refuses it and talks as though the reason is self-knowledge about what he'd become once he had it. That looks like textbook Ulysses-contract reasoning: don't trust your future will, remove the option. But Frodo never sets up anything like that — he doesn't ask anyone to stop him, or to carry it, or to push him in. Is that a second-order failure, not doing what you'd judge best about your own future weakness? I'd like to call it prospective akrasia and I want to know whether that's a real distinction worth teaching or whether there's already a better name for it.

4. And what does any of this actually add? If the takeaway is just "Tolkien illustrates present bias," I'd rather drop the session. Where does the story complicate the model? I'm thinking of the Boromir/Faramir contrast — same temptation, same blood, opposite outcome, and the book seems to think the difference is what each of them already loves rather than how strong their wills are — and of Bilbo, who hands the Ring over voluntarily and is still never quite free of it.

Tell me which scenes you'd actually put in front of the group (I can use the book or the films, whichever makes the point cleaner), and be straight with me about the places where the philosophy and the story are just pulling in different directions. Around 800-1000 words, no citations needed.
