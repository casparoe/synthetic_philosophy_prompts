I've drifted into holding something I'd call bookkeeping relationism, and I want it stress-tested before I write it up. The view: there is no fact of the matter about absolute rotation over and above the dynamical and symmetry structure of the total matter distribution, so the inertial structure appearing in our laws is a codification — a bookkeeping device that records which symmetries the total dynamics happens to have — rather than a description of some further thing that exists. Please don't spend words on the standard setup: I know the bucket and the two globes from the Scholium, the Machian complaint, the hole argument and the Leibniz-equivalence reply, and the Barbour-style programmes that try to derive inertial structure from relative configurations. Assume all that and go past it.

What made me uneasy is a case a condensed-matter friend raised, which I haven't seen discussed in the philosophical literature. Take superfluid helium-4 below the lambda point in a slowly rotating cylindrical vessel. Its normal, viscous fraction is dragged along with the walls. The superfluid fraction, whose circulation is quantized and which is irrotational in the absence of vortices, does not co-rotate at low angular velocity: as I understand the phenomenology it stays effectively at rest in the lab frame, and only once the rotation rate passes the threshold at which quantized vortex lines become energetically favourable does it begin to mimic rigid-body rotation, entering in discrete steps as vortices appear. So one bucket, one time, two interpenetrating matter distributions occupying the same region, with plenty of relative motion between all the material parts you like — and yet the inertial verdict differs across the components and flips at a threshold fixed by the dynamics. My attempted reply is that the relationist's relata were never "the fluid versus the vessel" but the entire matter distribution plus the law, and the law is precisely where the threshold lives. But at that point I worry I've packed the inertial structure into the dynamics I was supposed to be explaining, and that the view has become a substantive claim about which laws are admissible rather than a claim about what exists — and if I then also count the quantum vacuum as "matter" in order to handle the empty-universe limit, I can't tell whether I have a research programme or an unfalsifiable one.

So: what would count against bookkeeping relationism, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? Include the defeaters a defender of my stripe would concede rather than dismiss, distinguish those from the ones only critics find telling, and say what the view predicts that its rivals — substantivalism of any sophistication, or a primitivism that just takes facts about rotation as basic and unexplained — do not. Tell me honestly whether I have qualified the position into something no observation could touch, and whether that is a failing for a view of this kind or merely a change of subject. Finally, keep separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is contested, and flag explicitly where your answer depends on a contested reading — of what Newtonian absolute space was supposed to be, of how much weight the hole argument bears, of whether general relativity is or isn't Machian in the relevant sense; don't present a contested reading to me as the majority one. I don't want citations or a reading list, just the argumentative map.
