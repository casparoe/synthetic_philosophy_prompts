I'm doing a reading course with my supervisor on moral revolutions (Appiah-ish territory: dueling, footbinding, Atlantic slavery) and she has this running bit where she claims she can name the real engine behind any reform. Her verdicts so far:

- dueling: honor. Nobody was argued out of it; the code of the gentleman shifted and dueling started to look absurd
- the end of footbinding in China: honor. The arguments had been around for ages and moved nobody
- British abolition of the slave trade and then slavery: honor, and explicitly not interests (she insists it was still profitable) and not moral argument either
- emancipation of the serfs in Russia, 1861: interests. State finances, the Crimean mess, no honor story available
- the disappearance of judicial torture in Europe: arguments, genuinely — the one case where she lets reasoning be the main engine

So on the surface it's an everything-is-honor rule, but I think at least two others fit: something like "arguments only do the work when the practice isn't load-bearing for anyone's money or identity," or a broader interests story where reputation is just one interest among others and she quietly handles the profitability problem by talking about long-run industrial interest instead of short-run sugar profits.

Three cases I want to spring on her: the decline of the death penalty in the US (where people give three competing stories — DNA exonerations, the sheer cost of the litigation, and not wanting to belong to a club with Iran), the end of apartheid (sanctions and capital flight vs pariah status vs the moral argument finally landing), and the collapse of fur in the 90s. What would she say about each? Which one would actually tell the rules apart, and what verdict on it would make me give up the honor reading? I care less about who's historically right than about the rule shes running.
