In a graduate seminar on early Chinese philosophy we spent a session on the White Horse Dialogue, the short chapter of the received Gongsun Longzi in which an advocate defends the proposition usually rendered in English as "a white horse is not a horse." The discussion ended inconclusively, and I now think that is because two people were using the line for opposite purposes without anyone pinning down what, exactly, it asserts.

Our instructor used it to argue that the pre-Qin disputers already had something like a notion of a universal or abstract object — whiteness itself, over and above white things — and hence that the familiar claim that classical Chinese thought makes no room for abstracta is simply false. A visiting philosopher replied that the line is no deeper than "a cheese sandwich is not a sandwich": a pun on two readings of the copula, from which nothing about universals follows. I would like to know what work the line can actually do, because I suspect each of them was relying on a reading neither had isolated.

Here, in my own words rather than quoted, is what I take the material to contain: an argument to the effect that horse designates a shape while white designates a color, and that what designates a color is not what designates a shape; an argument to the effect that if one asks for a horse, a yellow or a black horse may be brought, whereas if one asks for a white horse they may not, so what is sought in the two cases cannot be the same; an objector's reminder that if there is a white horse one cannot say there is no horse, so that having a white horse is having a horse; and, in the companion chapter on hard and white, the point that sight presents the stone's whiteness but not its hardness while touch presents its hardness but not its whiteness. I am reasonably confident of these in outline; if I have garbled one, say so before you use it.

What I want is a menu of the distinct precise theses the one-liner could express, ordered from the trivial to the radical. For each: whether it is trivial-but-true, substantive and defensible, or straightforwardly false; whether the dialogue's own arguments actually support it; and which reading each of my two interlocutors needed in order to get their conclusion.

Three precisifications of my own, to be assessed as candidates rather than taken for granted:

1. A class reading: the white horses form a proper subclass of the horses, and the claim is only that the two are not identical.
2. A term reading: horse and white horse are different names with different conditions of application, so the one is not substitutable for the other in every context — the seeking argument being the case in point, since what is sought, wanted, or requested seems sensitive to the description under which it is sought. Is it anachronistic to hear this as a discovery that substitution fails in intensional contexts, or is that simply what the argument is?
3. A kind-term reading: horse supplies a principle for counting and reidentifying its instances, and white horse supplies none, so white horse fails to be a kind-term at all. On this reading the hard-and-white chapter would be reinforcing the same point, by prying a single stone's attributes apart across two sense modalities.

Two further questions. Does the hard-and-white argument really support the same reading as the white-horse argument, or does it push toward a different one — about the separability of attributes, or about what a single sense faculty can present to us? And does the objector's move succeed against any reading other than the class reading, or does the advocate have a reply available to it?

Please commit: name the single reading you think the text as it has come down to us best supports, say whether on that reading the instructor's inference to universals survives, whether the visitor's charge of punning survives, both, or neither, and give your reasons. Where you are taking a position on a genuinely contested interpretive question rather than reporting something the commentarial tradition is agreed about, say so explicitly rather than hedging the whole answer into a survey.
