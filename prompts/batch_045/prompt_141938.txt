I work in an applied ML group, mostly on off-policy evaluation and causal inference for sequential decisions — doubly robust estimators, importance ratios, positivity/coverage assumptions, do-calculus at the level where I can actually use it — and I have no formal philosophy training beyond one graduate seminar. We recently spent a reading-group session on two Newcomb-style puzzles and I came away unable to tell whether my colleagues were disagreeing about something my toolkit already has a precise name for, or about something it cannot express at all. The first is the counterfactual mugging: a highly reliable predictor flips a fair coin in advance; if it comes up heads the predictor hands you $10,000 conditional on its having predicted that you would have paid $100 if it had come up tails; the coin in fact came up tails and you are now being asked for the $100. The second is XOR blackmail: some rare event (prior probability around 1%) would destroy your house, costing you roughly $1,000,000; a blackmailer who knows whether the event will occur sends you a demand for $1,000 if and only if exactly one of the following is true — the event will occur, or you will pay. You have received the letter. I know the standard verdicts (roughly: causal decision theory refuses to pay in both, evidential decision theory pays only in the second, functional decision theory pays in both), but I want to understand what is actually being contested rather than which labels attach where.

Could you restate both setups and all three prescriptions as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I work in — policies, environments, logging versus target policy, trajectories, interventions versus conditioning, the assumption that transition dynamics do not depend on the agent's policy, model-based rollouts, off-policy value estimates, regret? Two wrinkles matter to me, because I suspect they are where the translation either earns its keep or quietly cheats. First, suppose the predictor is not a perfect simulator of me but a learned model fitted to logged behaviour of an earlier checkpoint of my policy, call it v1, with maybe 90% accuracy on the deployment distribution and no guarantee at all off it; I can edit myself into v2 at negligible cost before the prediction is made. Then the counterfactual query about what I would have done in the unreached branch is an off-policy query with thin overlap, and it is also unclear which function is supposed to be the one whose output I am evaluating. Second, the two puzzles differ structurally: in the mugging, the branch that carries the payoff is one in which I never act at all, whereas in the blackmail case my policy helps determine whether the letter exists. I want to know whether that asymmetry shows up as a difference in how hard each case is to express in my terms.

Please keep the translation honest: where my framework silently forces a choice that the philosophical debate leaves open, flag it as an imported assumption rather than presenting it as the content of the theories. Then give me the residue report — which claims map cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all — and tell me what you think the residue means. My own two hypotheses, which I would like you to argue against if they are wrong, are that both puzzles just describe an environment that is not policy-independent (so a misspecified MDP, and the whole dispute is a fight about which assumption to relax), and that the notion of counterfactual dependence on the output of one's own decision function is doing work that no amount of statistical counterfactual machinery can replicate. I do not want pointers to the literature; I want the argument, in about 800 to 1,000 words.
