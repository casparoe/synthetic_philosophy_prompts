Bit of background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm retired, thirty-odd years as an ocean modeller, and the last couple of years I've drifted into philosophy of computation and started getting annoyed at how loosely people say a system is "computationally irreducible" or "can't be simulated." So I tried to write down a criterion myself, and I want your credence on whether it can be made to work.

My proposal, call it the Re-encoding Test: a claim that some physical system is hard to predict or hard to simulate is a fact about the system rather than about our description of it only if the verdict doesnt flip when you re-encode the system's states in a way any working physicist would call merely notational — changing units, coordinates, or basis, that sort of thing.

Here's what's bugging me. In textbook CS the knapsack problem is NP-hard when the numbers are written in binary but polynomial-time when they're written in unary, and the difference between those two encodings feels exactly like the sort of notational business my test was supposed to rule out. So I'm sitting at about 0.45 that the Re-encoding Test, or something within spitting distance of it, can definately be repaired, and I want your number plus the decomposition.

Sub-questions I'd want kept apart: whether "merely notational" can be cashed out as a translation between encodings that's cheap to compute and cheap to invert, and whether unary-versus-binary is a counterexample to that or just a case where the translation is expensive in the one direction that matters; whether complexity being a statement about infinite families of instances rather than about any single finite system already dooms it to being about our models; whether the whole answer depends on which machine model nature is supposed to be running — plain Turing machines, machines that do exact arithmetic on real numbers, quantum ones — and whether there's any physical fact of the matter there; and whether the cost of translating between two descriptions is itself a physical quantity or only bookkeeping. Use my split or swap in your own.

Give me a number with one line of backing for each, say how they combine, and tell me which one your bottom line is most sensitive to. Also tell me what you'd count as settling this at all: a theorem, an experiment, or the discovery that the question was badly posed. And what single argument would move you most, either direction.
