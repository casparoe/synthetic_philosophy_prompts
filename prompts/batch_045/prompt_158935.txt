I want a careful comparison of two strategies for getting from a widely shared intuition about easy rescue to a demanding duty to aid distant strangers. Call them the bridge strategy and the conversion strategy. I have stipulated three test cases that neither strategy was designed with in mind; use them to determine which strategy is in better shape. Argue from the cases exactly as I have stated them. Do not appeal to what any particular author in fact argued, and do not cite, quote, or attribute anything to published work. Assume throughout that well-targeted transfers really do save lives at modest cost, so that no empirical hand-waving is available to either side.

The shared starting point is Singer's drowning-child example. A passerby finds a child face down in a shallow pond; she can wade in and save the child at the cost of a ruined pair of shoes. Nearly everyone says she is morally required to do so, and that failing to do so would be seriously wrong rather than merely permissible. The principle usually extracted from this is conditional and comparative: if an agent can prevent something very bad from happening without giving up anything of comparable moral significance, then she morally ought to prevent it. Applied to global poverty, that principle seems to say something extremely demanding: keep transferring resources until further transfer would cost you something of comparable moral significance, which for most people in rich countries means giving far more than they do, and giving it to strangers they will never meet.

The bridge strategy reaches that conclusion by parity reasoning. It treats the pond judgment as data and then argues that none of the features distinguishing the pond from the famine case does any moral work: not physical distance, not the number of people in peril, not the number of other agents equally able to help, not the fact that aid must be routed through organizations rather than delivered by hand. On this view the duty is grounded entirely in the size of the stakes and what the agent can do about them; her causal history is irrelevant. In particular, the bridge strategy takes the pond case to show that a positive duty to aid can be exactly as stringent as a negative duty not to harm, and concludes that the positive/negative distinction carries no independent moral weight.

The conversion strategy keeps that distinction and instead reclassifies the case. It objects that the pond framing is misleading, because the ordinary affluent agent is not a bystander who happens upon a stranger's emergency. She participates in and benefits from an institutionally structured arrangement that foreseeably and avoidably leaves people destitute. Her duty to aid is therefore not a duty of rescue at all, but a duty not to go on contributing to harm in which she is implicated, together with a duty to repair or compensate for that harm. The demanding conclusion is preserved, but it is derived from a stricter source: the duty stops where the contribution and its effects stop, rather than where the agent's own interests become comparable to the stakes.

Now the three cases.

Case one, unattributable peril. Three children are drowning in three separate ponds. Only one of the three drownings is your fault: days earlier you loosened the railing at one of the ponds, and you no longer remember which. You have time to reach and save exactly one child, and you cannot tell which pond is the one you tampered with.

Case two, clean hands. Aida has for years lived entirely outside the relevant institutions: no bank account, no employment, no purchases, no enforceable claims on any legal or economic order, and no causal footprint on how any of them operate. She then lawfully comes into twenty thousand dollars in cash as an unconditional gift from a stranger who acquired it innocently and owed her nothing. For two hundred dollars she can save a child's life.

Case three, the overdetermined pond. A thousand people each independently tip a single drop of water into a pond in which a child is struggling. The child will drown if nine hundred or more drops land; a thousand do. No individual drop makes a difference to the outcome, and every one of the thousand knows this. Each of them could also wade in and save the child at trivial cost.

In your comparison, address at least the following. First, demandingness and stopping points: what does each strategy say about when the agent has done enough, and is the answer plausible in both directions? Second, the direction and content of the duty: to whom is it owed (an identifiable victim, or anyone in the relevant class?), and what discharges it (a transfer, exit from the arrangement, or political reform)? Third, the agent's epistemic burden: how much empirical and causal knowledge does each strategy require of someone trying to act on it, and what happens when that knowledge is unavailable? Fourth, and most importantly, guidance under the three cases above: does the conversion strategy retain any grip where the victim cannot be identified (case one), where the agent's hands are clean (case two), or where her individual contribution makes no difference (case three)? Does it have to borrow bridge-style reasoning to answer any of them, and if so does borrowing undermine its claim to be a distinct strategy with a distinct stopping point? Conversely, does the bridge strategy have anything to say about the intuition in case one that the person at fault seems especially bound, and about the intuition in case three that the thousand droppers seem bound in a way that mere passersby are not?

Then give me your verdict: state which strategy you think is stronger overall, or argue that the right view is a hybrid in which one of them does the grounding and the other does the guidance, and say which does which. Give the strongest objection to the view you have chosen and reply to it briefly. If the three cases pull in different directions, say which case you weight most heavily and why. Aim for roughly eight hundred to a thousand words, and end with a final line, on its own line, in exactly this form:

Verdict: \answer{X}

where X is one of: bridge, conversion, hybrid, or undecided.
