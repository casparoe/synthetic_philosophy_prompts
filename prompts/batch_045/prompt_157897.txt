I co-facilitate a student-run reading group on cause selection — about 16 undergraduates, mixed majors, one 90-minute session, and I can offer only a trivial prize. They have all met the standard framework in which a cause is scored as importance × tractability × neglectedness, and they have started using it as a checklist: rate each factor out of five, multiply, rank, move on. I want them to run into three things rather than be told them: that neglectedness is a claim about a counterfactual baseline — what happens without you, and whether your contribution partly displaces someone else's contribution — rather than a count of how few people are already working on the cause; that multiplying the three factors presupposes that causes are separable, so it silently misfires where a cause only pays off once total funding passes a threshold, or where two causes are complements; and that the fact that others have stayed away from a cause is ambiguous evidence, pointing either to an overlooked opportunity or to their having looked and found it bad.

Design a runnable activity for this group that produces all three collisions without any lecture beforehand and without the words crowding out, fungibility, complementarity, or threshold being uttered before the debrief. I am imagining a simulation with real but tiny stakes: token budgets, cause cards, and a payoff rule the facilitators apply from a table that participants cannot see. Please give me the materials and setup; the rules and payoff mechanics, including how the hidden structure gets implemented without being announced — displacement on one crowded cause, a coalition threshold on another, complementarity between two others, and one neglected cause that is neglected for a bad reason; a resource asymmetry, with some groups holding money and others holding specified kinds of skilled labour, so that a cause can be neglected for dollars while crowded for person-years; a minute-by-minute timeline; and a debrief that maps specific moments onto each of the three points, saying for the ambiguous-signal case what would have to be true for neglect to count as a reason to move in. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which of the three points the activity can only stage artificially and which I will have to supply in discussion myself.
