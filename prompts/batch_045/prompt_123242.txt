The gender-equality paradox — the claim that societies ranked as more gender-equal display *more* gender-differentiated educational and occupational choices, not less — has been circulating for well over a decade, and as far as I can tell the argument has stopped moving. In every discussion I encounter, the same three readings recur. First, a causal reading: that underlying differences in interests, or in the relative weight people place on intrinsic versus extrinsic rewards, express themselves more fully once material constraint and overt discrimination relax, so that equality reveals what coercion concealed. Second, a measurement reading: that the indices used to rank countries by "gender equality" do not measure what the argument requires — they bundle formal legal equality with labor-force outcomes and political representation, and they track GDP and the sectoral composition of the economy closely enough that the correlation may be a development gradient wearing an equality label. Third, an inertness reading: that even granting the correlation, nothing follows about justice, since justice concerns the spread of options, the returns to each field, and the treatment of people within them, not the sex ratio across them.

What I have never found is a single exchange in which a proponent of one of these readings conceded anything to another. That is what I want explained. My own hypothesis is that the standoff is not primarily a factual dispute about sex differences at all, but a disagreement about which counterfactual is doing the work in the word "paradox." An unequal country can be read as a picture of what the equal country would look like under greater constraint; as a picture of a different stage of the same developmental path; or as a picture of a different culture altogether. These are three different baselines, and each is compatible with a different one of the three readings above, which would make the original observation far less discriminating than either side treats it as.

Two further suspicions, which I would like you to test rather than humor. The first is a selection artifact: in the societies at the bottom of these rankings women are largely outside the credentialed labor market, so the women who appear in engineering or medicine there are a heavily selected group, and the denominator changes systematically as development proceeds. If so, the pattern may be about the intensive versus extensive margin of participation rather than about choice under freedom. The second is what I would call the expression-shift: as income security rises, occupational choice shifts from extrinsic reward toward intrinsic interest, so if the average sex difference in intrinsic interests is small but nonzero and roughly stable across societies, measured segregation will rise monotonically with development while the underlying causes stay exactly where they were. On that picture the headline statistic is consistent with innate preference, with a constant level of differently-targeted socialization, and with unchanged norms of who supports a household — which would make it evidence for none of them.

I want a diagnosis, with reasons, of what kind of disagreement this is: verbal, substantive-empirical, a clash of baselines, a clash about which cases count as data, or a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for. Tell me which of my three suspicions is doing real work, if any, and do not assume I have the empirical details right — if the diagnosis depends on how the data actually go, say so explicitly rather than stipulating. Finally, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: an identification argument, a decomposition, a natural experiment, a reformulation of the question into one about which mechanisms would give anyone a reason to act, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and that no resolution should be expected, with an account of why.
