I'm on the patient advisory panel at the fertility clinic where my wife and I did IVF last year, and they've asked me to speak for twenty minutes to their ethics committee before they decide whether to start offering embryo ranking by polygenic score for non-medical traits — height, predicted years of education, that kind of thing. The committee is two reproductive endocrinologists who basically think of this as ordinary medical beneficence, a genetic counselor who is fiercely non-directive, the hospital's legal counsel, a disability-rights advocate, and a Catholic bioethicist. Twenty minutes of Q&A after, and I'm told they go hard.

My thesis, very short: the nonidentity problem cuts both ways here. Whichever embryo gets transferred exists only because we picked it, so none of them can be harmed by being picked or passed over — which means the usual objection to ranking (you're hurting the child you create) and the usual defense of it (you're promoting the welfare of the child you transfer) both collapse. What I think is actually doing the moral work is an expressivist-plus-role-based norm: a clinic shouldn't be in the business of rank-ordering possible people by how much they're wanted, and that norm binds institutions even where it doesn't bind an individual parent — which is how I get to defending a parent's right to choose while opposing the clinic offering it.

What I need is a prep sheet for the Q&A. Give me the seven or eight objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging, each in the mouth of whoever on that committee would raise it: the endocrinologist's version should sound like a doctor, the lawyer's like a lawyer, the disability advocate's like someone who has heard every reassurance before and wasn't reassured. For each, a reply I can give in under a minute, plus one honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes before anyone asks anything; tell me the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks; and flag the objection that definately has enough force that I should soften the thesis rather than fight it.
