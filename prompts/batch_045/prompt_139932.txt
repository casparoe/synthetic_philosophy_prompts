i've got myself into a bit of a argument with my mate dave and it's been going for weeks now, mostly over pints. It started over whether my favourite mug is still fragile if it's never been dropped and never will be. Dave's line is the simple one: fragile just means it would smash if you dropped it, soluble just means it would disolve if you chucked it in water — a disposition is nothing but a claim about what would happen, nothing hidden underneath. I've been reading about this on my own in the evenings (no uni background, I do maintenance at a leisure centre, so go easy on the jargon) and I think finks and masks break his view. A fink, the way I understand it, is when the test itself kills the disposition: the thing would of manifested it, but going to test for it destroys it first. A mask is when the disposition is still there but something gets in the way so it doesn't show. Dave says those are philosophers' contraptions — lab benches and sensors — and that in a normal house the simple "would do M if S" formula survives fine. He's offered to get the next round in if I can produce one case from ordinary life.

So build me one. Constraints:

- One paragraph, ordinary household. Nothing you couldn't find in a kitchen, a garage or a shed. No sci fi, no demons, no perfect predictors, no ideal conditions I'd have to stipulate — I want to be able to describe it out loud and have it land.
- Same object, same disposition, two different ways of testing it. Under one way of testing it's got to be a fink (the disposition goes because you tested it); under the other it's got to be a mask (the disposition stays, it just doesn't show). Only the test changes. That's the bit Dave needs, because otherwise he'll just say I'm comparing two different objects.
- The fink has to be the testing doing the work — not time passing, not someone turning up afterwards and fixing the thing.
- For each route, spell out: the disposition in "would do M if S" form, what actually happens when you test it, whether the object still has the disposition afterwards, and why that follows from how the thing genuinely works rather than from you just deciding it does.
- Then tell me which bit of the case is doing the real work, and what happens if you take that bit out — does the whole thing collapse back into a boring case where the thing just manifests normally? And what's the strongest thing Dave can say back? Not a strawman, his actual best move, even if it's a good one.

My own attempt, which I don't trust: the trip switch in the consumer unit. The socket's live, so it's disposed to give you a belt if you touch it, but touching it is what lets current leak to earth, and the switch cuts the power, so by the time anything could happen it isn't live any more. Dave says that's not a fink at all, it's a mask with extra steps. Is he right? If my example is duff, say exactly why and build me a better one.

Last thing, and this is my own idea, so I want to know whether it's real or whether I've made it up wrong: call it a reverse fink. The thing doesn't have the disposition at rest, but testing it gives it the disposition, so it does manifest — and then whether it "really" had it goes all slippery. Can you get all three into the one case, or does the third need a different object? And be straight with me: is a reverse fink actually a third kind of thing, or just an ordinary disposition where I've described the trigger wrong? And does it cause trouble for both views — Dave's "would do M if S" and the "it's really the structure underneath" one — or only for one of them, and which?
