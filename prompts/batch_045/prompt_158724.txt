I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background beyond a couple of podcasts, and I got into an argument at a barbecue with a friend who did grad work in economic history about whether history gets locked in. He kept saying path dependence, I kept saying sure, but wouldn't we have arrived at the same place eventually, and we were both basically just asserting. I'd like to redo it properly on a case where I actually know the technical facts, which is the IPv6 transition rather than his keyboard example. Facts as I understand them, and correct me if I have the shape wrong, I'm not fussy about exact numbers: IPv4 addresses are 32-bit and the free pool of them ran out in the early 2010s; IPv6, with 128-bit addresses, was standardized back in the late 90s and isn't backwards compatible, so everything has to run dual-stack or go through translation; NAT and then carrier-grade NAT let everyone keep running IPv4 long after the addresses ran out; for about two decades IPv6 deployment went nowhere and the year of IPv6 became a standing joke; now, depending on who's measuring, something like 40-something percent of traffic to Google arrives over IPv6, some big mobile carriers onboarded new subscribers IPv6-only, and most corporate networks and plenty of consumer gear are still on IPv4.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not a vibe. Take path dependence, and be explicit about which of the several things people mean by it is doing the work: sensitivity to early or small events, increasing returns or positive feedback that make staying cheaper than switching, lock-in in the sense that reversing now costs more than switching would gain, and the stronger non-ergodic claim that there's no stable distribution over where this kind of process ends up. For each, say what the state variable would be here, what the feedback actually consists of (network effects between access networks, content providers, OS defaults, enterprise IT?), who the relevant actors are and what their options and payoffs look like — is this a coordination game, something with a first-mover problem, something else — and what the theory predicts each actor should have done, and how it ends. Then be honest about what it doesn't explain. To me the striking thing is that the constraint which was supposed to force the migration, running out of addresses, got relieved by a workaround, so the forcing function just vanished, and the migration is now happening less by converting the installed base than by new networks and devices replacing it, with some regions skipping IPv4 altogether. That doesn't look to me like the standard increasing-returns story, and if the theory has no name for a lock-in sustained by a substitute that absorbs the pressure, say so instead of stretching the usual story to fit.

Then run it the other direction. My claim at the barbecue was that we'd have ended up with a big flat address space eventually anyway and IPv6 just took the scenic route, which I realise needs a distinction: is that the same outcome by a different route, convergence on a destination robust to how you get there, or did the route change the destination, so that what we actually got is a permanently translated, mostly-NATted hybrid rather than the clean thing the original design imagined? Tell me what would count as evidence for each, and whether the question is even well posed — my friend muttered something about re-running the tape versus transplanting today's state back into the past, and about there being no ensemble to average over if the process really is non-ergodic, and I didn't follow it. Also say where the theory goes quiet: does it tell us anything about whether the outcome was bad, or about whether government procurement mandates and that sort of thing would have helped, or is it silent on all of that? Finish by telling me whether this case is a confirmation of the path-dependence story, an anomaly for it, or a case it was never built for, and dont pad it out with reading suggestions, I can't follow up on them anyway.
