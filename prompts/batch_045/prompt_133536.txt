I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all, and I recently ended up in a rationalist-adjacent reading group where someone explained the "unilateralist's curse" — the gist as I took it being that when any one person can bring about some outcome that affects everyone, the outcome that actually happens is the one that looked best to whoever was most optimistic about it, so you should act as if your own estimate is an overestimate. I mentioned it at work the next day, two of my teammates went at it in our team channel, and now they both keep asking me who won. For context: 12 engineers, one shared production Postgres, anyone with oncall access can push anything with no review required outside business hours. This all started after a retro about a 2am hotfix that fixed an outage but also left about 40 minutes of inconsistent data that other teams had to clean up.

Yuki opened by saying our hotfix policy is a textbook case. Twelve people each have some noisy estimate of whether a given change is safe, and the changes that actually get pushed are the ones where the top estimate happened to clear the bar — so the realized set of unilateral pushes is worse than what any individual's numbers predicted, and a two-person rule screens exactly that out. Marcus said the curse is about costs imposed on people who didn't consent, and that isn't his situation: he's the one on call, he eats most of the downside, so his solo push is just a risky decision under time pressure, and adding an approver at 2am mostly adds latency, which has its own price during an outage.

Yuki came back with two things. First, not all of the downside landed on Marcus — the downstream teams and customers ate the inconsistency. Second, and she said this was the real point, the "I bear the cost myself" exemption is self-selecting: the people most confident they can go it alone are exactly the ones who will claim the exemption, so the population of solo pushes skews toward the overconfident even if every individual pusher is being sincere. Marcus said that argument generalizes into absurdity — if every self-assessment is contaminated by the fact that the person chose to make it, then no decentralized decision is ever fine and you'd need a committee for every commit. And he added that there is a mirror-image selection effect on inaction: nobody gets credit for not pushing, and the cost of not pushing (outage continues, orders fail) is diffuse and invisible, so if you only correct the selection effect on action you've built a machine whose output is "do nothing."

Yuki said the asymmetry isn't an illusion, because the current production state is tested and running while the change is new and untested; the status quo has evidence behind it that the change doesn't, so defaulting to it isn't the same as a general bias toward inaction. Marcus replied that this only holds when nothing is on fire: during an active incident the status quo is precisely the thing that's broken, so "the tested state" is the state with a known defect, and an asymmetry that flips depending on whether there's an incident is a bad foundation for a blanket rule. He also pointed out that we do have a unilateral veto — anyone can hit rollback — and if unilateral action is cursed because it gets triggered by the most optimistic estimate, then unilateral rollback is triggered by the most alarmed one, same structure pointing the other way, yet nobody is proposing that two people approve a rollback.

Yuki then conceded something and turned it around: two approvals only buy anything if the second person forms a genuinely independent judgement, because if she just skims the diff and rubber-stamps it, correlated errors don't screen out anything at all — but that's an argument for making review real, not for dropping the requirement, and anyway the latency objection is itself distorted, since the cost of waiting is immediate and vivid while the cost of a bad push is probabilistic, which makes waiting feel more expensive than it is. Marcus's last move was that if reviews are made genuinely independent you roughly double review time, and during a sev-1 that cost is also borne collectively, so the proposed cure has its own collective-action structure; and on top of that, with only twelve people the maximum of a dozen noisy estimates isn't that extreme, so even granting the mechanism it's weak at our scale — which he said is a seperate question from whether it's real in the giant-group cases the reading group cared about.

I stayed out of it, and honestly I couldnt tell who was winning.
