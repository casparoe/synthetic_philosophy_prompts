Read Chinua Achebe's Things Fall Apart through the philosophical debate about guilt and shame—specifically, the question of which is the better moral emotion and whether a culture can deliberately choose to replace one with the other. I want you to use a distinction I'll call 'sanction substitution': the project of replacing a shame economy, in which wrongdoing threatens one's standing before a real or internalized community, with a guilt economy, in which wrongdoing is measured by an internalized rule and the agent's own conscience, as a way of preserving moral order when the old hierarchy is discredited. In the novel, the Christian mission wins its earliest mass converts among the outcasts (osu) and marginalized, while Okonkwo's son Nwoye abandons his family for the new faith; Okonkwo himself is destroyed by a suicide that, in the clan's terms, is an unspeakable abomination, and the District Commissioner ultimately reduces his death to a paragraph in an ethnographic book. Using the 'sanction substitution' lens, assess what the novel shows about whether guilt can be deliberately substituted for shame: do the outcasts' and Nwoye's conversions show that a guilt-based morality can be chosen, perhaps because it offers full moral standing to those whom the shame culture has stigmatized? Does Okonkwo's tragedy vindicate the critique that shame is destructively tied to honor and the gaze of others, or does it support the rehabilitation of shame (as in Bernard Williams's account of an internalized spectator) by showing that his identity collapses when the collective audience he cares about fails to fight? Where does the novel complicate the binary—does Okonkwo's killing of Ikemefuna or his treatment of Nwoye suggest that guilt and shame are already entangled in the clan's own moral psychology, and does the church's own complicity with colonial power undermine the claim that guilt is the more egalitarian or better moral emotion? Finally, say what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that a straight argument about guilt versus shame misses, and where, if anywhere, the work resists the 'sanction substitution' reading.
