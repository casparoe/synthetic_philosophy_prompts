I'm assembling the consent section of a problem set for a second-year applied ethics seminar, and I want the cases properly ordered before I write the answer key. I've been working with two scoring tests, and they keep disagreeing with each other, so I'd like an independent set of numbers before I commit to a ranking.

Test A (counterfactual-dependence): a defect in consent undermines it to the extent that the consenter would have refused had the defect been removed — had they been told the truth, had the pressure or predicament been absent, or had they been at their full retained capacity. Test B (transaction-identity): a defect undermines consent to the extent that it changes what the person was consenting *to* — i.e., the thing they actually underwent falls outside the description under which they agreed to it, whether or not they would have objected to the truth. Here are eight cases, all involving adults, no emergencies, no children, and otherwise ordinary circumstances:

1. A patient consents to a knee arthroscopy "with Dr Reyes." A supervised fellow performs the entire procedure. The clinical outcome is identical, and the fellow's complication rate at that hospital is in fact slightly lower than Reyes's.
2. A dentist does not mention that a filling will leave a mild metallic taste for about 24 hours. The patient later says she would have agreed anyway.
3. A diner orders a dish described on the menu as lamb and is served horse. Same texture, same flavour profile, no health risk, no religious or ethical dietary rule that the diner holds.
4. Volunteers sign up for what the consent form calls "a study of memory for word lists." The real aim is to see how people react to being given deliberately false feedback about their own performance. The tasks are exactly as described; only the point of the feedback is concealed.
5. A contract worker is hired for "content review, duties as assigned." Nobody mentions that the job is mostly eight hours a day of viewing violent imagery, because the recruiter worries nobody would take it. The pay and conditions are good.
6. A doctoral student agrees to a sexual relationship with her supervisor. He makes no threat, offers no inducement, and asks nothing she could report; but he also controls her funding, and there is no other supervisor in her subfield at that department.
7. An 82-year-old with early vascular dementia consents to cataract surgery. She understands the procedure and wants it, grasps the risks, but cannot retain the option of doing nothing for more than a couple of minutes and asks the same questions again each visit.
8. A regular plasma donor gives consent after a night shift, having skipped breakfast and been fasting since the previous evening. He is alert enough to answer the screening questions, signs the form, then faints halfway through the donation.

What I want, concretely: (i) a table giving each case a 0–100 "undermining" score under Test A and under Test B, where 0 means consent is fully intact and 100 means there is no consent at all and the act is simply done to the person; (ii) a single ordering of the eight cases by overall invalidity, showing the gap between the two scores for each, with one sentence saying what puts each case where it is; (iii) a cut-off on the overall scale — at what number does consent stop being merely tainted and become void? — with the cases that fall on the void side named and the cut-off defended against the nearest case below it; (iv) a split of a total weight of 1.0 across the three families of defect — deception, coercion, competence — reflecting how much damage each does across this particular set of cases, plus a note on whether you would split it differently for a set drawn only from clinical settings; and (v) the one adjacent pair in your ordering that you would most readily swap if I changed a single fact, together with the fact in question.

Two constraints, so the exercise is worth doing: don't tell me the answer depends on which theory of consent one accepts — pick the version of each test you find most defensible and apply it consistently across all eight; and don't collapse the two tests into one by scoring a case high under B whenever it is high under A, since the pairs where they come apart are the reason I'm asking. If any case is described too loosely to score, say so and tighten it yourself rather than skipping it.
