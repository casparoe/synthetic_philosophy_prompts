I work on a small fifth-millennium settlement, and for three years my team has been deadlocked over it in a way I no longer think is really about that site. The relevant material is this: a single-room structure set apart from the domestic cluster, with a raised platform, a cache of cattle crania, and painted vessels unlike the household wares; a scatter of anthropomorphic figurines, many deliberately broken, mostly from midden deposits; and, in the houses, ordinary hearths and storage, plus two unusually large rooms with plastered bins. Half of us read the separate structure as a place of cult with attached specialists. The other half read it as competitive feasting by an emergent household, and they say the cult reading imports a category — religion as a distinct institutional sphere with its own personnel — that the material cannot carry and that may not have existed at all. We agree on the objects, the contexts, the phasing, and the dates. Nobody has moved anybody in three years.

And it is not only us. The same standoff reappears under different labels: ritual versus routine, temple versus house, chiefdom versus heterarchy, storage as risk management versus storage as political finance, image as symbol versus image as decoration. The exchange always ends the same way, with one side charging projection and the other charging evasiveness. The methodological argument has run in a loop for about forty years: the familiar ladder picture on which technology and subsistence are recoverable, social organisation less so, and belief barely at all; the demand for middle-range theory and ethnographic analogy; the reply that analogy is unconstrained because meaning is not in the objects; the conciliatory claim that material culture is meaningfully constituted while still tractable. Nothing settles.

My hypothesis is that at bottom this is not a disagreement about the fifth millennium. I can see at least four candidates for what it is instead. First, a verbal dispute: 'ritual' is used with different contrast classes — ritual versus profane, versus practical, versus mere routine — and 'institution' sometimes means a durable social role and sometimes only a recurrent practice. Second, different questions under one label: whether behaviour of a certain kind occurred at the site; whether the participants held certain beliefs; whether a durable role existed across generations — three claims with very different evidentiary standards that we slide between. Third, a disagreement about what entitles you to an inference: inference to the best explanation of the total pattern, versus a requirement that the data actually discriminate between the rival hypotheses, a requirement my colleagues invoke constantly but never state. Fourth, a hidden empirical or formal question: how often, cross-culturally, these activities are housed in special buildings rather than houses; and whether, at our sample size and with our data types, the two generative models are even statistically distinguishable — whether the hypothesis pair is identifiable at all, which is a question one could in principle settle by simulation, or by calibrating against historically documented settlements where the institution is known from texts and we can ask what its material signature in fact looks like.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey of positions. Which of these is doing the real work, is it more than one of them, and is there something I have missed — a disagreement about which cases count as data, or bedrock intuitions about uniformity that neither side can argue for? Argue it from what each side actually says and what each side would refuse to grant. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a designed test, a change of question. Or, if your answer is that no progress should be expected here, say why — and say what follows about how we ought to write up the site.
