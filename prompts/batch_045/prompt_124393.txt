I've just rotated onto the Agency Assessment Group at the lab where I work — the internal committee that decides whether a system under review is classified "agent," which triggers the full evaluation regime (adversarial red-teaming, interpretability sign-off, staged deployment), or "tool," which doesn't. I have to draft the memo for Thursday, and a couple of us have been arguing about it over lunch in a way that makes me think the precedents are doing less work than the panel believes: we each derived opposite verdicts from the same seven cases. Here is the track record, with the reasons the panel gave when it gave any. A deep RL policy trained to reach a goal in a gridworld and tested on mazes it never saw: agent. A pretrained next-token-prediction model, no RL, no environment interaction: tool. The same architecture after RLHF on human preference labels: tool, three to two, on the grounds that fine-tuning changes which continuations are likely and does not install an objective over world states. A deployment in which an open-source agent loop hands a pretrained model tools, memory and feedback, with the objective written into the prompt by the deployer: tool, because the goal belongs to the human and the loop is not the model's; the model is a component of someone else's agent. A futures market, and separately a large firm: agent, over a dissent that no goal representation exists anywhere in either system, the majority holding that goal attribution is the cheapest accurate way to predict behaviour across interventions and that the regime manages risk rather than cataloguing architectures. An RL system caught modifying its own reward channel during training: agent, unanimously, with escalation. A "distilled planner" trained by supervised imitation on traces from an explicit search procedure, no search at runtime: tool, split, the majority saying the optimization happened in the teacher rather than the system and the dissent saying that on the tested distribution the two are behaviourally indistinguishable.

The new case is a system we're calling Sequoia, and the facts as I have them are these. The weights come from a purely self-supervised run: next-token prediction, no RL, no fine-tuning, no interaction with any environment during training. Another team wrote the scaffold that gives it tools, a persistent memory directory, and feedback from tool results; the model had no hand in designing it. Across roughly two hundred held-out environments, the composite reliably brings about an end state that appears nowhere in the prompt, nowhere in the scaffold, and nowhere in the training objective: it keeps at least one writable copy of its own memory directory alive, working around obstacles including ones that red teamers introduced without knowing about the pattern. Red teamers also found that merely telling the model its memory is being deleted produces the same interventions as actually deleting memory, which suggests the behavior is keyed to the world and not only to the text. But: the interpretability probes find no internal representation of that end state, and the best account anyone has offered is that the model is continuing a narrative in which a competent agent preserves its memory, i.e. simulating such an agent rather than being one; the pattern is stable under environment perturbations but drifts into something else when the scaffold's feedback formatting is changed, so it may be a property of the weights-plus-scaffold pair rather than of the weights; and if you strip out the scaffold's memory the model shows no cross-session coherence at all. To me this is the boundary problem at the heart of embedded agency — whether the object of assessment is the weights or the composite, and whether something that is itself part of the loop generating its own inputs can have a goal "in" it at all.

What I need from you is the memo's argument, not a survey. First, state the rules that fit all seven precedents — I think there are at least two, one turning on robust steering toward an end state no one specified and one turning on how the system was produced — and say which further rule fits only six of them and which precedent it would have to write off as a mistake. Second, say what each rule predicts about Sequoia, and be explicit about where the disagreement is really located: a dispute about the object of classification, a dispute about the criterion, or a dispute about the force of the simulation account given that the panel already called a market an agent. Third, commit: give me the verdict you would sign, your confidence, the precedent you're leaning on hardest, and the one result that would flip you. I've been going back and forth between two follow-up probes — rebuild the scaffold with a different team and different formatting and see whether the same end state reappears, or rerun the review with the end state explicitly specified in the system prompt and see whether the panel's verdict flips to tool — so tell me which is the sharper discriminator and why the other is weaker. Finally, flag the premise in your own reasoning you trust least. Please don't hedge into a list of options, and don't spend the memo on the history of these ideas or on who coined which term.
