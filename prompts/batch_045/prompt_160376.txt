Hey — one I keep going in circles on. I'm co-teaching a seminar on precommitment with someone from the law school, and we've hit the same wall three weeks running.

He leans on a single analogy for everything: a constitution is a Ulysses contract. The people are Ulysses, later majorities are Ulysses once the singing starts, the sirens are the passing panic of the moment (an attack, a crash, a demagogue with a good voice), the mast is entrenchment, the ropes are the amendment rule, and the crew — mainly courts — are the sailors with wax in their ears who were told in advance to ignore whatever he shouts later. He uses it to license two conclusions: (a) constitutional constraints on ordinary politics are legitimate only where you can point, in advance, to a diagnosable impairment of the later self, an actual siren song rather than just we might change our minds; and (b) for exactly that reason, eternity clauses and unamendable provisions are illegitimate, because a founding generation can't bind people who aren't born yet the way Ulysses could bind his own future self.

I'd rather audit the analogy than just grumble at it. So: lay out the correspondence table, then the places the mapping fails. The ones I keep circling: Ulysses is one agent across time while a polity is a succession of different people, so the binding is interpersonal rather than intrapersonal; Ulysses knew what the song would be and that he'd hear it, whereas framers are guessing; the sirens sit on one island you sail past once, not a standing condition of politics; the crew were bound by the order too and had no stake in the outcome, while courts interpret the rope as well as hold it; and the epistemic asymmetry can run backwards — Ulysses before the song is calm and better informed, whereas a founding generation may be *worse* informed than the successors it binds. Which of those actually do work for his two conclusions, and which are just curiosities? My hunch is the crew one is carrying more than he admits.

Then two things I want run through the frame, which I don't think get put to it very often.

First, a second-mast clause. Suppose the constitution lets itself be overridden, but only by a majority that reaffirms the identical text after an intervening general election and a two-year wait, with no appeal in between — a cooling-off override. Under the analogy this is Ulysses telling the crew to untie him only if, an hour after the singing stops, he asks again in writing, twice. It looks *more* defensible than ordinary entrenchment on the analogy's own logic, and yet almost no real constitution has one. I can't tell whether that's a point against the analogy or against real constitutions, and I want you to say which.

Second, sirens who can imitate Ulysses's voice. In the story the threat is that he'll beg to be released, and the test is simply: he begged. But suppose whatever wants him released can also corrupt the signal you were using to tell singing from silence — manufacture the appearance of calm deliberation, control what counts as evidence that the hour is cool. Does the precommitment story survive that? It seems to me it has to become a story about precommitting to a *verification procedure* rather than to an outcome, and I'm not sure whether that's still the same analogy or whether it has quietly turned into something else.

And if the Ulysses frame can't carry (b), is there a better picture for binding across generations — an advance directive, a club charter with rotating membership, something else — or is the honest answer that the interpersonal case needs a different object entirely? Don't bother with reading suggestions or citations, I've got that side covered; I want the audit.
