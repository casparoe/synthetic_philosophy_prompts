I'm in a seminar this term on representation, meaning, and computation in cognitive science, and I help edit the back-page satire supplement our department puts out at the end of each term. We have one slot left, and I would like to commission it from you on the symbol grounding problem. I've written the brief fairly tightly: the humour we want is the kind that depends on getting the philosophy exactly right, and a piece that only gestures at the Chinese Room would be useless to us.

What I'm after is a press release of roughly 700 words, in completely straight-faced tech-industry corporate voice, announcing a product that claims to have solved symbol grounding. Please invent the company, the product and the spokespeople; for the sake of the brief I'll call them Percepta Labs and GroundLoop 1.0, but use whatever names you like. Format: a headline, a dateline, three or four paragraphs of launch copy with two or three quotes from invented executives and one from an unnamed outside skeptic, then a short FAQ of four or five questions and answers.

The target is not any named philosopher and not any real company. It is a pattern of claim I keep running into in the readings and in talks: the idea that grounding is a quantity you accumulate by coupling a symbol system to further symbol systems — a larger corpus, a cascade of dictionaries or ontologies, an embedding space, another model's verbal description of what a camera sees, a simulated robot in a simulated room — so that what is really a widening circle of symbol-to-symbol relations gets sold as contact with the world. Every boast the release makes should be a recognizable exaggeration of something people in this area genuinely say, not an invented confusion.

To that end, the jokes should come out of the details. A few things I'd like to see:

- The flagship metric should be a "grounding depth" figure: the mean number of symbol systems a term passes through before it resolves (an ontology, a multilingual dictionary cascade, a vector space, a "Veridical Sense Lexicon"), with the copy presenting greater depth as greater grounding and the number rising with each release. The word "circular" should never appear.
- One FAQ entry should ask whether this is just the Chinese Room with extra steps. The answer should be that the room now has a window: a live camera feed, delivered to the occupant as another system's natural-language description of the scene. An adjacent entry can add that pixels are not symbols, so the matter is settled.
- The sensorimotor suite, and the claim to have passed the robotic, total version of the Turing test, should be entirely simulated — a simulated body in a simulated world, certified by a simulator — with the copy proud of the fidelity of the physics.
- The "categorical perception" module should recover the invariants that let a system single out an object and pick it from among unfamiliar shapes, and should recover them from corpus co-occurrence statistics.
- The company should be proudest of having solved the abstract half of the vocabulary: terms like "justice" or "because", grounded by definitional cascade from a small set of directly grounded primitives.

Two constraints on tone. First, no one may break character or explain the joke: the piece should stay legible to a first-year who has had only the one lecture, but nothing should be flagged as a mistake, and the executives should never concede anything. Second, the skeptic's quote is the only place where the actual problem may be stated plainly, and the company's reply to it should be transparently not an answer.

What matters most to me is that someone who has read Harnad's classic paper on the symbol grounding problem and knows the Chinese Room argument should laugh rather than wince. The failure the piece is built on should be the real one: that connecting symbols to more symbols, or to a description of the world, or to a simulation of the world, does not do the work that transduction and the capacity to identify and pick out things are supposed to do. Assume the readership is our department mailing list — mostly cognitive science and philosophy students, plus a few faculty who will read it on a phone.
