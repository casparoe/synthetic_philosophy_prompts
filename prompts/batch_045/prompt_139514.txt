Me and a friend spent about two hours after dinner last night arguing about The Truman Show and I want to settle it before I run a one-off philosophy-and-film night in a couple of weeks. Everyone always reaches for Plato's cave or Baudrillard with that movie and I'm sick of that reading, so here's where we ended up.

My friend says its actually a near-perfect dramatisation of Berkeley's idealism, and specifically of the master argument: the move where your invited to imagine a tree existing unconceived, and the point is supposed to be that the moment you try, you've conceived it, so the supposition eats itself and there's no genuine thought there to be had. His version goes like this. Every frame of the film is either a camera feed or the control room watching a camera feed, so the film literally cannot show you an unperceived world — the form of the thing does what Berkeley's argument says. The closest it gets is the long stretch where Truman slips out through the basement and the show goes off air for the first time in decades, and even then what we're shown is the crew scrambling and the audience staring at a blank screen. Call this the off-air test: try to say what is going on inside the dome during those minutes without thereby making it something somebody is watching. My friend thinks the film is doing that test on us for two hours.

I think he's cherry-picking. Berkeley's perceiver-of-last-resort is God, who never blinks, but the show's stand-in is a ratings audience plus a crew who sleep, get bored, and can lose the signal — so the movie gives us a finite, fallible guarantor, and I read that as a picture of the nightmare Berkeley was trying to avoid, not of his view. And the ending: Truman walks out through the door into the so-called real world, and the whole emotional weight is that this is a genuine escape. On Berkeley's terms he's just swapped one set of ideas for another, with a different producer. I also think the film asserts all sorts of things no character ever perceives — that Lauren was telling the truth, that his father was staged off the show, that there really are thousands of hidden cameras — and it needs those to be true for the deception to be a wrong at all.

So: who is more right, me or him, and where are we both being sloppy? Give me a reading that takes both the movie and the argument seriously, and that doesn't just use the film as a puppet show for the philosophy. Three things I'd especially like. One: is the film on the side of Berkeley or on the side of the people who say the master argument is basically a pun on the word conceive — and can the film's form support the strong reading even if the argument itself can't? Two: name three scenes I can put on the projector and say what to draw out of each, including at least one scene that resists the Berkeleyan reading and that an honest critic has to concede. Three: what does the film add here that the argument alone doesn't — my suspicion is that it relocates the whole thing, so that what's chilling isn't that the furniture might blink out when you leave the room but that you are never not being watched, and that if that's right then the master argument is secretly about trust in other perceivers rather than about trees. Tell me if that last idea is a real insight or just me being drunk on a metaphor.
