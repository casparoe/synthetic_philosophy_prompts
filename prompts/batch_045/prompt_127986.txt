hey — hope this isnt too woolly a request, but here's what I'm after.

I'm running a reading group session on thought experiments and I want the specification *first*, before anyone starts scoring favourite cases. So: what would a test actually have to be able to do to count as telling you whether a given intuition pump is doing real evidential work rather than just leaning on the reader? I don't mean "is this a good thought experiment" — I mean the full desiderata list for a criterion of evidential competence for a case: the phenomena it has to catch, the failure modes it has to flag, the distinctions it must not collapse (vivid vs. load-bearing, widely shared vs. actually about the thing under discussion, "intuition about the concept" vs. "intuition about the scenario"), each with the case or consideration that earns it a place on the list.

Places I want you to take a stand rather than just enumerate:

- Which of the usual demands are genuine desiderata and which are just the preferences of one camp. Specifically: is stability of the verdict across populations, orderings and phrasings (the sort of thing people find when they actually run these cases on undergraduates) a real requirement, or a prejudice of the survey-running camp? Is "the case should be realistic" a requirement or a taste? Is "the intuition should be shared by trained specialists" a requirement or special pleading?
- Which pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied. My candidate: spareness (strip the setup until exactly one variable does the work) versus ecological richness (keep enough texture that our ordinary competence is genuinely engaged rather than guessing at an alien situation). Tell me if that's the true tension or if I've picked a fake one.
- Which single requirement does the most exclusionary work — the one that, if you enforce only that, kills off most of the cases in circulation.

Some failure modes I've got names for; put them wherever they belong, and bin them if they're bogus: (a) the idle wheel — a detail nobody can show changes the verdict but that obviously changes the feeling; (b) the shadow case — the pull comes from a structurally similar case the reader has much stronger feelings about and that nobody mentioned out loud; (c) the modal gap — the case asks you to treat an impossibility as a possibility and then quietly draws on the incoherence as a source of intuition; (d) borrowed intensity — swapping affect-laden vocabulary for thin vocabulary ("kill" for "cause to die", "the child" for "the occupant of bed 4") does the argument's work. I'd also want something like an export clause: a case should say in advance what would move the verdict, and I want to know whether that's definately a requirement or an optional extra.

Two of mine to run whatever list you produce against:

Case A. A night ward. One nurse is on duty; a monitor is supposed to alert her if a patient's oxygen saturation falls. Unknown to her the alert has been switched off by a contractor doing maintenance. The patient deteriorates and dies. Did she let him die, fail to save him, or neither?
Case B. Identical, except the alert had never been installed, and the hospital had decided not to buy one.

If your criterion says the verdict in A and B should differ, say which requirement that's a violation of and why; if it says they shouldn't, say what that tells you about the nurse's blame being pumped rather than argued.

Keep the scorecard tiny — a line each, at most, on three broad styles (spare analytic cases in the Gettier-ish mould, escalating science-fiction setups of the zombie/teleporter sort, and realistic vignettes of the kind moral psychologists use). The criteria are the deliverable, not the scoring.

British English throughout please, keep it in prose with the list in some kind of table, and at the very end give me \answer{...} containing the short name of the one requirement you think does the most exclusionary work.
