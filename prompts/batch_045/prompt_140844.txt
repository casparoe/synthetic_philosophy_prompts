I am a junior in an honors world history course, and our final project has to be organized around a period. My teacher vets each student's period before anyone starts writing, and over the year she has said yes to some and no to others, always with a reason. I want to write about the Anthropocene. Before I ask her, I would like to know what she will say — and, more usefully, what rule she is actually running, since I suspect she has one and would deny having one.

Her rulings so far, with the reasons she gave:

- The Middle Ages, Europe, roughly 500 to 1500: rejected. A thousand years is not a period, and in any case it is defined by what it is not.
- The medieval Mediterranean, 1000 to 1300: accepted.
- 476 as a boundary: rejected. Nothing happened in 476 that anyone alive would have noticed; what we call the fall of Rome took a century and was not experienced as a fall.
- 1348, the Black Death: accepted. In some regions half the population died, and wages, landholding, and inheritance all changed afterward.
- 1492: accepted. Whatever else it was, it changed what ordinary people ate, what killed them, and who ruled them, on four continents.
- 1453, the fall of Constantinople: rejected. It mattered enormously to the people in Constantinople and to people who wanted to crusade; it did not change how anyone farmed, traded, or was governed elsewhere, and the trade routes had already moved.
- 1543 as the start of the Scientific Revolution: rejected. Two books were published. Nobody's life changed.
- The Renaissance: rejected. It is a word some Italians used to congratulate themselves, and it describes the reading habits of a few thousand men and nothing else.
- The Enlightenment: rejected, on much the same grounds.
- The Industrial Revolution: accepted. Say what you like about the term; it changed where people lived, how long they worked, and what counted as a day.
- The Cold War: accepted. Two armed blocs, a stable structure, and everyone alive knew which side they were on.
- 1450 and 1500 as boundaries: rejected. Those are round numbers someone picked so a textbook chapter could start somewhere.
- The Information Age: rejected. Ask again in fifty years; from inside it you cannot tell a turning point from a fad.

The case I want to bring her is the Anthropocene. Some geologists have argued for making it a formal unit of Earth's time scale, and the candidates for its start that I have seen are the mid-twentieth century, marked by the radioactive fallout from hydrogen bomb tests that shows up in sediment layers around 1950; the spread of coal and the steam engine around 1780; the dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide around 1610 that has been linked to the collapse of Indigenous populations in the Americas after 1492; and the beginnings of agriculture thousands of years earlier. A committee of stratigraphers considered the mid-century proposal and declined to adopt it, though the word is still used informally everywhere. It is not a period anyone has lived through as a break, its start date is a measurement rather than an event, it is not over, and it is planetary rather than European — which is the one complaint of hers I think it escapes.

My own guesses at her rule are something like: periods must be carved by changes in how ordinary people lived and how power and production were organized, rather than by books, ideas, or artistic styles; or periods must be things people at the time would have recognized, and must be over before you name them; or some combination. Please do the following: lay out the candidate rules that fit all thirteen of her verdicts, not just the convenient ones, including any that fit better than mine; say what each predicts about the Anthropocene and about which start date, if any, she would tolerate; give me your best guess with your reasons for preferring it; and tell me which further case would most cleanly separate the surviving rules. I was going to probe her with the Neolithic Revolution, since it plainly changed how people lived but was not experienced as a change by anyone, and I am not sure whether the Bronze Age or something like the Little Ice Age would be a better probe. Finally, if her own precedents really do not decide this, say what principle she would be committing herself to either way, and whether there is a way to frame my proposal — as a boundary rather than a period, or with a narrower scope, or under a blander name — that a teacher running her rules ought to accept.
