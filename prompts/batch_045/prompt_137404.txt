I build and calibrate simulation models for a living, mostly population-dynamic and agent-based ones, and I read philosophy of science in the evenings. I keep running into one configuration of the unrealistic-assumptions problem that I cannot find a word for, and I would like to know whether anyone has carved it out, under any name, or whether it genuinely needs a new one.

I know roughly where I am. There is the methodological claim associated with Friedman's essay on positive economics, that the descriptive accuracy of a theory's assumptions is not what licenses the theory, and there is the stronger twist sometimes extracted from it, that unrealistic assumptions are a sign of a theory's power rather than a cost it bears. My configuration does not feel like a faction in that argument. In my cases nobody is claiming the assumption is harmless, and nobody is claiming it is a virtue. The peculiar thing is that it is hard to see how to replace it with a true statement at all, because every candidate replacement says more than the original did and therefore covers less.

Three cases, so that the pattern is visible rather than merely asserted.

First. Take an old-style population model: infinitely large population, completely well-mixed, no spatial structure, no age structure. Out comes a clean threshold result about which traits will spread. Real populations are finite and structured, and when you write down a concrete structured model the result shifts, and shifts differently depending on whether you put in a lattice, a network, local dispersal, or overlapping generations. Yet the qualitative result of the unstructured infinite model keeps turning up in data drawn from all of these very different systems. Notice that my complaint is not the familiar one that finite models fail to converge on the infinite one as the distortion is dialled down, though sometimes they do; my complaint is that there is no single true replacement, that the available replacements disagree with one another about the mechanism, and that the ideal model somehow sits above all of them at once.

Second. The same shape appears in any model with a single representative agent, or in mechanics with rigid bodies and frictionless constraints. Replace the representative agent with any specific heterogeneous-agent model and you must pick a heterogeneity; the clean comparative static then either survives for reasons internal to that choice or vanishes, and what the representative-agent model stated is not identical to any of the heterogeneous results but is rather what that whole family has in common.

Third, a case that feels different in kind and that I am least sure about. A colleague tells me that in statistical mechanics the sharp discontinuities we call phase transitions only appear in the limit of infinite system size, because for any finite system the relevant functions are perfectly smooth. If that is right, then the infinite-size assumption is not something one corrects by adding finite-size corrections; it is what makes the phenomenon stateable in the first place. Part of what I want to know is what kind of claim that is.

My provisional three-way cut is this. Type A: distortions you can dial down, with the predictions converging as you do. Type B: distortions that are load-bearing, where dialling them down does not converge back and the derivation collapses instead. Type C: the ones I am asking about, which seem not false so much as uncommitted, functioning like a place-holder that ranges over many incompatible but individually more truthful stories, none of which has the coverage of the original.

So, concretely, what I want to know:

One, does type C have an established name, or is it distributed across several named concepts that nobody has noticed overlap here?

Two, I suspect type C is not one thing. Sometimes the various concretions agree on the target result, in which case the ideal model looks like a robustness argument compressed into a single model. Sometimes they disagree, and yet each is empirically adequate in its own domain, in which case the model looks like it is tracking a constraint or an invariance rather than a mechanism. Should that split be drawn, and if so where exactly, and does the difference change what the model's success entitles us to believe?

Three, what is the right thing to say about the truth-status of a type-C assumption: that it is not the sort of thing that can be true or false; that it is true of a capacity or tendency that no real system ever instantiates on its own; that it defines a class of systems rather than describing any of them? These seem to me to give different advice about what to do next, and I want to know which is defensible and why the others are not.

Four, is my three-way cut the right one at all, or am I collapsing distinctions that do real work?

What I am not asking for: a reading list, a bibliography, or citations. I have no library access where I am writing and could not follow any of them up, so a list of names and papers would be useless to me even if accurate. I want the carving. Specifically: state what you take the nearest established concepts to be, in your own words, and say of each exactly where it fits my cases and where it fails them; then either identify the existing concept that fits best or, if none fits, coin and define one tightly enough that another person could apply the definition to a new case; then give me one hard case that sits on the boundary between type B and type C and say what turns on which side of the line it falls, or, if you think that line is not worth drawing, say what should replace it; finally, say what follows for the methodological instruction to de-idealize, since in type-C cases I cannot see that de-idealization is even a coherent demand.

Aim for roughly 800 to 1100 words of prose, and please do not close by observing that scientific models are diverse and that different tools suit different purposes. I know that. Commit to a classification and defend it.
