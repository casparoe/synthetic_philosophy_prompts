I am a retired physical chemist. For the past four years I have been auditing the epistemology seminar at the university I spent my career at, and I have been invited to give the department's work-in-progress talk next month. I would like to use the occasion to put forward a view I have been circling for some time, and I would be grateful for a preparation sheet for the question period.

The view, which I have been calling the stabilization account of justification, runs roughly as follows.

1. The three standard answers to the regress problem — foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism — seem to me to share an unexamined assumption: that a justificatory chain is in good epistemic standing only in virtue of a structural property of the chain as a whole. Foundationalism wants the chain to stop; coherentism wants it to close into a loop; infinitism wants it to go on forever but still to be, in some sense, complete. All three are asking a question about the shape of the series.

2. In my own field we learned to stop asking that question and to ask a different one. A self-consistent-field calculation iterates an electron density: you guess a density, compute the field it generates, solve for a new density, and repeat. The iteration does not terminate, and in principle it never terminates. Nobody regards the absence of a final step as a defect, and nobody regards the absence of a privileged first guess as a defect either, provided the iteration converges — provided successive iterates differ by less than any fixed tolerance beyond some finite depth, and provided the convergence rate is good enough that the result is not hostage to where you started. Renormalization-group flows have the same character. The question is not termination but convergence, and after convergence, rate.

3. My proposal is that justification has this structure. A subject's belief that p is justified when the iterated process of answering why believe p? stabilizes: there is a depth beyond which further reasons continue to be available, and continue to be given when demanded, but successive layers differ by less than any fixed evidential margin — the answers keep coming, but they stop adding anything that could change the standing of p. Call this the stabilization condition. The regress is then not vicious, because nothing in the account requires an endpoint; and the thing foundationalism wanted — reasons that have effectively bottomed out in something not itself in need of support — is recovered as a limiting property of the series rather than as a first member of it.

4. On this way of setting things up, the three traditional positions are not rival theories but three special cases of one theory, distinguished by the mode and rate of stabilization: foundationalism is stabilization after one step, coherentism is stabilization into a cycle, infinitism is stabilization without arrival. The substantive question becomes what the convergence rate actually is for creatures like us, and in which domains of inquiry the iteration fails to converge at all.

5. I want to suggest one consequence: where reason-chains fail to stabilize — long testimonial chains, chains of merely statistical support, cases where each new layer imports as much new content as it discharges — we should expect a distinctive and recognizable epistemic unease, of the sort that ordinary people report about hearsay and about purely statistical evidence, and which the standard theories have to treat as a separate phenomenon.

The room will be about twenty people, and I can describe it fairly precisely. There is a senior externalist of reliabilist sympathies who holds that the entire regress literature is a pseudo-problem produced by an over-intellectualized picture of knowledge, and who will think the whole exercise is misplaced. There is another senior member who has published a well-known defense of infinitism; he will be the most interested person in the room and by some distance the most dangerous. There are two younger formal epistemologists who work on Bayesian models of evidence and on infinite and loopy dependency structures; they will want to know exactly what it means for two reasons to differ by less than a margin, what the space is in which that distance is measured, and whether the whole apparatus is well defined. There is a coherentist with idealist sympathies who will suspect the view collapses into something already familiar. There is a historian of early modern philosophy who will want to know whether this is simply an older position in a laboratory coat. And there are several graduate students, at least one of whom works on machine-learning interpretability and will ask whether I am claiming that human cognition literally performs an iteration. I will be the only person present with scientific training, and I would like neither to condescend nor to be patted on the head.

What I want from you is a Q&A preparation sheet for a twenty-minute question period:

- The eight to ten objections I am most likely to get from this specific room, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by their philosophical depth, and phrased the way these particular people would phrase them — in the vocabulary of reliabilism, of infinitism, of formal epistemology, and so on, not in the vocabulary of a general survey of the regress problem.
- For each objection, a spoken reply I could give in about two minutes without notes, and an explicit note on where that reply is soft, question-begging, or merely verbal.
- What I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks anything. I take it I must concede up front that the self-consistent-field and renormalization-group material is a source of a formal analogy and not an argument, and that the burden is on me to state the stabilization condition without leaning on it. Tell me what else belongs in that list, and tell me if that concession is itself a mistake.
- The single reply that has to land if the view is to survive the hour.
- The question I am hoping nobody asks. My own guess is the formal one: what fixes the metric on the space of reasons, and is the honest answer not simply coherence, in which case I have produced an elaborate restatement of coherentism? Tell me whether you think that is really the worst one, or whether there is something worse that I am not seeing.
- Where the physical analogy is not merely unhelpful but actively misleading, and which parts of it I should drop or hedge rather than defend.
- A fallback claim I could retreat to if the strong version of the view is lost in the first ten minutes, and which would still be worth having said.
