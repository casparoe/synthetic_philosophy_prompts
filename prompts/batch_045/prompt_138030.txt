Two friends of mine got into it at a dinner party last weekend about the butterfly passage in the Zhuangzi and I've been turning it over since. You know the bit: Zhuang Zhou falls asleep, dreams he's a butterfly flapping about and enjoying himself, wakes up, and can't tell whether he's Zhuang Zhou who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming it's Zhuang Zhou.

Dan reads it as straight dream scepticism. He says it's the Cartesian worry in a nicer outfit: from the inside nothing distinguishes the two states, so you don't know which one you're in, and you're never going to.

Maya says that's a misreading. On her view the story isn't about whether experience is veridical at all — the butterfly in the story is really flying around and really enjoying itself in its own world, nothing in it is an illusion — it's about the boundaries of the self being far less fixed than we assume. She says the passage is usually read as ending on some line about the transformation of things, and that its point is therapeutic, not epistemological: it's meant to loosen your grip on "I am definitely this one sort of thing", not to raise a hypothesis you can't rule out.

Then it went back and forth, roughly like this:

(1) Dan: fine, maybe Zhuangzi himself wasn't trying to do epistemology, but the argument stands on its own. If no feature available from the inside marks the difference between the states, the conclusion follows whether or not the author meant it.

(2) Maya: and note what the last line actually grants — that there are two distinct things, not that reality is in doubt. Compare Descartes: his worry is that all of your experience might be non-veridical. Here both candidate states are ones in which a real creature is really doing something. So the most you can get is "I don't know what I am", not "I don't know whether anything is real".

(3) Dan: "I don't know what I am" is bad enough. Huge numbers of my ordinary beliefs are indexed to being the man — that I've got a job, a mortgage, a species, a bank account. If I can't tell whether I'm the man or the butterfly, they're all in the same boat.

(4) Maya: then take away the asymmetries and see what's left. In the story the man wakes up with a memory of the butterfly and does the wondering; the butterfly doesn't seem to remember being a man, so there's an asymmetry in the contents and it's doing the work. Now run the symmetric version — the butterfly, mid-flight, also suddenly remembers being a man at a desk and wonders whether it's a man dreaming of flying. Nothing separates the two states from within any more. And my answer there is: there's no fact of the matter which one you "really" are, or better, the question is malformed, because the "I" is just whatever the current run of experience takes itself to be. That's the therapeutic point again, so the symmetric version supports me, not you.

(5) Dan: "No fact of the matter" is a metaphysical claim you'd now have to defend, and it's not obviously more attractive than "there's a fact and I can't get at it". Also, if we're allowed outside criteria, here's one: in the man-state the butterfly episode is a short, contained, physiologically datable event inside one night's sleep, whereas the man-episode, seen from the butterfly side, is a whole lifetime. Doesn't that break the tie?

(6) Maya: that criterion comes out of the man-world's physics, so using it just assumes the answer. And in general every criterion you can reach for lives inside one of the two states, which is what shows this isn't an answerable knowledge question at all — it's a pseudo-question.

(7) Dan: or it's a real question that's permanently unanswerable, and the honest thing is to leave it open rather than dissolve it.

Then they got onto whether any of it matters. Maya said it's idle: whichever you are, the sensible thing to do next is the same, so nothing practical turns on it. Dan said that's only true if you're indifferent about what you are — if you'd rather be the butterfly, or you'd be upset to learn the man is the dream, it's not practically inert, and you end up somewhere close to a Sleeping Beauty setup where what you should do depends on which state you're in.

I sat there and genuinely couldn't tell who was winning, and they both asked me to referee. So: can you go through it move by move — for each of (1) to (7), and then the bit about practical stakes, say who's right, who's only partly right, and who's just wrong, and why? Then say who's more right overall, and whether anything changes if you take the question as "which one am I" rather than "is any of this real". Most useful to me would be what they're both missing — I've got a feeling the whole argument is running on some assumption neither of them stated, but I can't put my finger on it.
