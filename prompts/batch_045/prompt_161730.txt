Hey, I need Q&A prep for a paper I'm giving next week and I want it to hurt.

The setup: 40-minute talk at a small one-day workshop on measurement and institutional design hosted by a central bank's research division. Audience, roughly: (1) two monetary-policy economists who work on inflation targeting and forecasting respectively — the first one literally maintains the internal indicator dashboard, the second thinks most talk about metrics is philosophy done badly; (2) a public administration scholar who studies target regimes in health and education and who thinks calling it "gaming" moralizes what is really just professionals re-prioritizing under resource pressure; (3) a psychometrician who cares about construct validity and measurement invariance and will suspect my central distinction is one she already has a name for; (4) one ML alignment person passing through who will say this is reward hacking and ask what I'm adding; (5) a political theorist who will ask whose target it is and whether any of this is just a technical gloss on a power question. Maybe fifteen people total, one of whom is on my committee.

The thesis: Goodhart failures and specification gaming are only pathological when the metric is *diagnostic*. Some metrics are *constitutive* — hitting the number is part of what makes the good real, not just evidence that it's real — and for those, what looks like gaming is achievement. The mistake worth naming is treating constitutive metrics as diagnostic (or the reverse), and I propose a test plus a policy upshot.

Sketch of the argument, so you can aim at it:
- Diagnostic vs. constitutive proxies. Waiting-time targets in emergency care: how long patients wait is what we care about, the clock is evidence, so gaming the clock (parking patients in ambulances, reclassifying when the clock starts) leaves the target untouched and destroys the instrument. Contrast inflation expectations: part of what price stability *is* is that people expect it, so the announcement of the target and the credible hitting of it are partly what constitutes the outcome, not merely a read-out of it. (The details of either case don't matter — I use them as stylized, and I'd rather you attack the structure than the cases.)
- The switch-off test: if you stopped measuring and stopped steering on the number, would the good state persist on its own (diagnostic — gaming corrupts) or would it fail to come about / collapse (constitutive — steering builds)? Cases where the answer is indeterminate are the interesting ones.
- The irreversibility asymmetry, which is where my policy upshot comes from: over-optimizing a diagnostic proxy destroys it permanently — once the causal link is broken you've lost your instrument and you can't get the information back — whereas under-building a constitutive target is usually recoverable later, you can make it up next period. So when the switch-off test is indeterminate, the expected costs are asymmetric and you should deliberately optimize less than maximally. I call this optimization slack and it's the thing I actually want people to walk away with.
- A regress point: adding a second metric to catch gaming of the first doesn't terminate the problem unless the second metric is itself constitutive; otherwise you've just moved the gaming up one level, and you can show this by induction on however many layers of audit you like.
