I teach an upper-level seminar on the metaphysics of mind — the kind where students have already read their way through functionalism, the intentional stance, and the hard problem, and can produce fluent prose about all three. I'm setting the final, and I want one essay question on Aunt Hillary: Hofstadter's ant colony from the "Ant Fugue" in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* (1979), the one you can apparently hold a conversation with by tapping signals on the surface of her mound, whose replies arrive as the colony's coordinated activity over minutes or hours. Students sit the exam closed-book, so the question has to be answerable from the machinery of the seminar alone — no citations, no secondary sources, and, since I don't want marks turning on how well anyone remembers a 1979 book, the question should present the case itself and say explicitly that no textual exegesis is wanted. The problem is that my students can already turn out two stock answers in their sleep: the generous one (the intentional stance earns its keep on the colony's behaviour, the colony is a real pattern, and a real pattern is all anyone has ever meant by having beliefs) and the sceptical one (no individual ant understands anything, colony-level description is a convenient fiction, and in any case there is nothing it is like to be Aunt Hillary). Both of those paragraphs currently earn a solid B, and I want the question built so that they top out there.

What I think the A answer has to add is a decision about what fixes the boundaries of a subject of mental states, and here is the twist I want built into the question. Suppose field myrmecologists install a "Mound Readout": a sensor array that tracks pheromone concentration and traffic rates across the galleries of Aunt Hillary's mound in real time. It yields two findings. Finding One: the best predictive model of what the colony does next must posit a single slow-moving variable that tracks the colony's own nutritional deficit and defensive posture — a quantity no individual ant represents, stored nowhere in particular, realized in the large-scale chemical gradient of the mound and in the rates of traffic through particular galleries, and one that the colony's foraging and relocation decisions are regulated by. So there is, apparently, a colony-level self-model: a representation of the colony's own overall condition, used to steer its behaviour, physically realized partly outside the ants. Finding Two: the control is thoroughly decentralized and the self-model is dispensable. When the myrmecologists destroy the galleries that carry the gradient, no ants die and the colony's behaviour degrades only briefly, recovering within a fortnight; and a neighbouring colony, Aunt Beatrice, whose architecture is a patchwork of purely local rules with no such integrated variable at all, is indistinguishable from Aunt Hillary over months, though not over minutes. I want students forced to say which of these findings, if either, should change anyone's mind about whether Aunt Hillary is a mind — and, more importantly, why that finding rather than the other one is the one that carries the argumentative weight, rather than it being an accident of which side they liked before they read it.

So, concretely, please give me three things. First, the exam-ready question stem, as it would appear on the paper: self-contained (stating both findings), neutral with respect to the right answer, answerable in ninety minutes and roughly 1,200 words, and phrased so that "the colony has emergent properties," "no single ant understands," and "there's nothing it is like to be her" are each visibly not yet answers. Second, a model answer of about 700–800 words that actually takes a position and shows the work. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong. The five I have in mind, which you should use or improve on, are: a criterion for what makes a collection of goings-on one subject rather than many or none, applied to Aunt Hillary; a treatment of the fact that the colony-level state is realized partly in the mound and its traffic, including what does or doesn't stop the subject from swallowing the mound and then the surrounding ecosystem; a verdict on Aunt Beatrice — whether functional equivalence over months suffices, and, if not, what the mentality-constituting difference is and why naming it isn't ad hoc; a non-stipulative answer to the proliferation worry, since sub-colonies inside Aunt Hillary and the wider ecology around her also admit real-pattern descriptions; and a meta-level word about whether this dispute is substantive at all, or whether it quietly becomes verbal once both sides grant the real pattern, including what work the appeal to consciousness or phenomenality is genuinely doing in the argument. Finally, add a short note on what should cap an otherwise polished answer at a B — my suspicion is that it is answering both findings in whatever direction preserves the view the student walked in with, but I'd like your diagnosis.
