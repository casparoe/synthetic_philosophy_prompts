Hi — I'm a novelist (two books out, neither of which has ever won anything, both of which sold approximately nothing) and this year I let myself get talked onto the fiction jury for the Voss Prize, which is a mid-sized award with a check attached and, as it turns out, no written rule at all for how we actually pick a winner. We just talk for an hour and then whoever is chair calls the question. This year that stopped working. Our three finalists are The Salt Year (a multigenerational thing set on a drying inland lake), Nine Rooms in Winter (linked stories, very formal, my favorite), and The Understudy (a campus novel that everyone agrees is extremely good and nobody agrees about anything else). There are six of us. I collected ranked ballots privately so I'm fairly sure they're honest, and they came out like this: two of us have Salt Year > Understudy > Winter; two have Understudy > Winter > Salt Year; two have Winter > Salt Year > Understudy. So Salt Year beats Understudy 4–2, Understudy beats Winter 4–2, and Winter beats Salt Year 4–2. It's worse than that, which I discovered at 1 a.m. with the calculator app: first-place votes are 2/2/2, and if you do a Borda count (2 points for a first-place ranking, 1 for second, 0 for third) all three books come out at exactly 6. Every procedure I could think of either ties or returns a different winner depending on how I ask the question. My partner, who did a PhD in econ about ten years ago and will not let it go, sat up in bed and said "that's just Arrow's impossibility theorem" and then went back to sleep, which was not as helpful as he seemed to think.

So here's what I need: a memo addressed to the jury, with a bottom line up top, recommending a concrete decision rule we could actually adopt before our call on Sunday, written in American English, maybe 700–900 words. Please don't just tell me that Arrow shows no system is perfect so any of them will do — the whole question is which imperfection we can live with, and I need to be able to argue with the two other jurors who will definately hate whatever we pick. Specifically: for each rule you consider (plurality, instant runoff with some tie-break, Borda, pairwise with a fixed order of eliminations, a grades-not-rankings scheme where each juror assigns each book a letter grade and the highest median wins, or anything smarter), tell me which of Arrow's conditions it violates — the unrestricted-domain one, the Pareto one, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-dictatorship — and where the strategic-voting result bites for a six-person room where everyone can see everyone else's ballot. Two things on the table from actual board members that I want you to take seriously and rule on: (1) our chair picks the order in which books are eliminated head-to-head, and (2) a proposal that we pre-commit to a fixed elimination order in the bylaws now, before anyone knows next year's shortlist, on the theory that this removes the manipulation problem. Does it?

Two extra facts you should use rather than assume away. First, intensity: the two Salt Year–first jurors are close to indifferent between the other two books, while the two Winter-first jurors feel strongly that The Understudy is the worst of the three — so anything that can only see order and not distance is throwing away information we actually have. Second, a complication from left field: there's a plagiarism allegation against one of the finalists that probably won't resolve until after we vote, so whatever rule we adopt needs to say what happens if a book is withdrawn from a three-book field after ballots are in. I do not want our winner to change just because a non-winner disappeared, but I also don't want a rule that's so rigid it can't handle a recieve-and-remove situation. And if the honest recommendation is that we should randomize, or that we should go back to unstructured discussion and take seriously that deliberation changes preferences rather than just recording them, say that too, but give me the reasoning and say plainly what we'd be giving up.
