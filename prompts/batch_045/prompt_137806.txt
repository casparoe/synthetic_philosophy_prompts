I want a map of the exposure of a position I have been defending in a manuscript on scientific thought experiments, and I would rather have the map than the reassurance.

The position, stated in the form I have been forced to state it. Call it the surplus thesis, in two components.

(S1) At least some scientific thought experiments deliver conclusions about the actual world that the experimenter could not have obtained, at the time, by deriving them from what they then accepted — where the obstacle is not merely that nobody had yet performed the derivation, but that the derivation would have to route through premises the experimenter did not have access to and did not smuggle in as tacit empirical knowledge. I concede at once, and gladly, the point that gets made against this: if the background theory entails the conclusion, then the conclusion was always in the theory, and entailment is cheap. The surplus I claim is epistemic, not logical — which is exactly the move that may have insulated the thesis into emptiness, and I want that on the table.

(S2) The mechanism is commitment-pull: by imagining a case in enough detail we gain access to what a theory commits itself to about that case, and in the good cases we find that its commitments to one and the same imagined setup conflict. The surplus-generating cases are inconsistency exposures, not applications. The contrast class is the recycling case, where the imagined scenario merely makes vivid a consequence the theory was already known to have (imagine a perfectly elastic collision between equal bodies and conclude that they exchange velocities), and the hypothesis-generating case, where the scenario merely suggests a conjecture that later experiment decides.

My worked example of surplus is the tied-stones argument against the view that fall-speed increases with weight: bind the light stone to the heavy one and the composite is, by the theory's own lights, both heavier than the heavy stone alone (so it should fall faster) and retarded by the slower light stone (so it should fall slower). My worked example of recycling is the frictionless bowl, where the ball's returning to its original height is just the background principle made imaginable. I claim my own diagnostic separates them: the first exposes a conflict among the theory's commitments to a single case, the second only applies one.

What I want from you:

1. Defeaters, ranked. What would have to be true for the surplus thesis to be false? Give me each in the form of a thing that could actually happen or a fact that could actually obtain, not as a bare logical possibility, and sort them from fatal through serious to merely awkward. Include, where relevant, things that arguably already have happened.

2. Which ones my side should concede. For each defeater, say whether a committed surplus theorist ought to treat it as counting against the thesis as I have stated it, or as counting only against a version nobody defends. Be willing to say that most of the standard objections are strawmen; be equally willing to say that one of them is not, and to say which.

3. Four I am most worried about. Press each as hard as you can rather than reassuring me.
   (a) The reconstruction industry. For case after celebrated case, someone eventually produces a derivation from premises available to the experimenter, and the surplus evaporates. How many successful reconstructions does it take before the thesis is empty? Is there a principled stopping point, or does the surplus theorist simply retreat to the next unreconstructed case, and is that retreat itself evidence?
   (b) The idealisation artifact. The setups are unphysical: frictionless planes, perfectly rigid rods, point masses, infinitely slow pistons, demons who see individual molecules. If the conflict I find is a conflict among mutually inconsistent idealisations rather than among the theory's commitments to anything physically possible, then what the thought experiment teaches me is a fact about my own stipulations. Say how much of the surplus literature this sinks and whether any version of the thesis survives it.
   (c) Contested verdicts. Competent people describe the same imagined case as having opposite outcomes — the demon was read by some as breaking the second law and by others as confirming it, and the rotating disk, the bucket, the hole argument, and the cat have each been read in incompatible ways. Does disagreement of that kind bear on the reliability of commitment-pull as a capacity, or only on its authority? What observation would discriminate between those two readings?
   (d) Reverse calibration. Perhaps we trust thought experiments only because the ones that survived were later confirmed and the ones that failed are forgotten. If the deliverance is trustworthy only ex post, does the surplus thesis collapse into something much less interesting — thought experiments as a good heuristic for guessing — and is that collapse a defeater or a friendly amendment?

4. A falsifiability audit. State what the surplus thesis predicts that its derivability rival does not, both about the practice of science and about the history of it. Then say plainly whether the move from logical to epistemic surplus has insulated the thesis, and whether that is a problem for a thesis of this kind: I take it that a claim about a source of knowledge is a claim about a practice and should be revisable in light of that practice, but I may be protecting it with a meta-principle rather than with evidence.

5. My own diagnostic on trial. Is the inconsistency-exposure criterion genuinely separating the tied-stones case from the frictionless bowl, or is it gerrymandered to fit two cases I chose because I already knew which verdict I wanted? If it is gerrymandered, say what a non-question-begging criterion would have to look like.

6. What I would personally find most persuasive, so you can aim at it: an argument that ties the reliability of commitment-pull to something independently checkable about the theory under test — so that I could tell in advance, and not only in hindsight, which thought experiments to trust. Failing that, a case where a thought experiment produced a confident, convergent, expert-stable verdict that was later overturned, with the overturning not attributable to a tacit empirical premise that was false.

Constraints: work from cases you can describe in enough detail for me to reconstruct the reasoning; do not cite, do not name-drop, and give me no bibliography — if a case matters, describe it, and flag where you are unsure of the history. If an objection you want to press depends on a fact you are not confident of, formulate it conditionally and tell me what the fact would have to be. Be concise; no section padded.
