Hey — I'm running a one-off session for my perception reading group next month on Molyneux's problem (person blind from birth, sight restored, cube and a sphere on the table, does he know which is which before he touches them), and I'd rather open with a diagnostic instrument than with a lecture. So: build me a decision tree / taxonomy that starts from a reader's answer to one concrete question and walks them down to a position on the puzzle. I want the tree built out of the logical space first and only then labelled with the answers people actually give; I don't care about pinning leaves to historical figures or to the literature, so please don't cite anything or hand me a reading list, and don't bother reproducing the original wording of the question either, I've got it in front of me. Roughly eight to ten branch questions, ten to twelve leaves, every branch question phrased as a case or a claim that someone with no philosophy training could answer cold, and no branch that just asks them which theory they like or what they'd call themselves.

The distinctions I want doing the work are these. What counts as succeeding: naming the shape out loud, pointing at it on request, matching it reliably to the one they touched, merely telling the two apart, or showing something like surprise or longer looking when the wrong object turns up. When: the very first instant, an hour in, or after weeks of actually using their eyes. Whether you are treating the whole thing as an armchair question about concepts or as a prediction about real patients who've had sight restored. Whether the cube-and-sphere pairing is doing any work, or whether you'd say the same about two shapes that are much harder to distinguish by touch, or about a familiar object versus an unfamiliar one. The direction of transfer — I want a branch for the reverse case (someone sighted from birth who loses their sight and has to do the matching by touch) and one for a genuinely new modality (a sighted person given something like echolocation or a magnetic sense). Two variants I made up and want squeezed in: a subject who has never used hands but only ever explored things with a stick, and a subject with no shared language who can only be tested by looking time. Plus one branch on whether success has to be an immediate perceptual seeming or is allowed to be a quick conscious inference, and one for people who think the question is just ill-posed or has no determinate answer.

For each leaf give the name of the position if it plausibly has one, say unnamed if you think nobody holds it, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then tell me where the tree leaves gaps — combinations of answers nobody occupies — and whether that's because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely untried, and flag any two leaves the questions genuinely can't seperate instead of inventing a question to force a split. Write the whole thing in continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, nothing in bold, no tables, the way you'd explain it in an email to a friend — I'm typesetting the handout myself, so I'd rather have sentences I can lay out how I want.
