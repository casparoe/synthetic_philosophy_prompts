I'm a PhD student in political theory, and the chapter I'm currently drafting is about whether long-run social forecasting can be a legitimate object of policy reliance. Two people in my reading group got into an argument last week about Popper's case against laws of history, and I found myself switching sides each time one of them spoke. I'd like a careful adjudication of the exchange as it was actually made, not a survey of the secondary literature.

Here is the exchange, as close as I can reconstruct it; I think both of them would accept this version.

Ines opens by reconstructing Popper's three-step argument: the course of human history is strongly influenced by the growth of human knowledge; we cannot predict, by rational or scientific methods, the future growth of our own knowledge; therefore we cannot predict the future course of human history. She adds the Oedipus effect as a second and independent obstacle: a published prediction of a social event can itself cause or prevent that event, so the object of social prediction is not independent of the prediction itself in the way the objects of natural science are. On that basis she says the historicist programme is dead, and that what remains is piecemeal social engineering plus situational logic, which is just what Popper said remained.

Marek replies that the three-step argument, even if sound, shows at most that there can be no *unconditional* law of the whole course of history, and that this is a conclusion almost nobody needs to dispute. What working social scientists use are conditional, domain-limited generalizations — that fertility falls some decades after infant mortality falls, that pension outlays track the age structure with a long lag — and Popper himself distinguished laws proper from mere trend statements and endorsed situational explanation rather than holism. Second, he says the Oedipus effect is not peculiar to history: measurement perturbs physical systems, and self-fulfilling and self-defeating expectations are modelled routinely in economics, where one simply includes agents' beliefs about the forecast among the model's variables. So responsiveness to prediction is a modelling complication, not an impossibility proof.

Ines answers that the measurement analogy cuts against Marek. In physics the perturbation is itself law-governed, which is why you can correct for it; in the social case the response to a forecast depends on what people come to believe and expect, and that in turn depends on knowledge that does not yet exist, so there is no law-governed correction term to add. And she grants the conditional laws happily but calls the concession empty for the historicist's purposes: once the antecedent of your conditional includes the future state of knowledge, it is an antecedent no one can evaluate in advance, so the conditional law can never yield the unconditional large-scale forecast that historicism promised.

Marek then makes three points. First, many social forecasts do not depend on future discoveries at all: the number of people who will turn sixty-five in twenty years is already fixed, and mortality and fertility trends have been stable enough for long enough to support projections that have in fact been borne out. Second, when forecasts are public and self-affecting, predictability and responsiveness can be compatible: one can look for the fixed point at which agents' expectations of the event, formed in light of the forecast, still produce the event — which is just what rational-expectations modelling does, and there is nothing incoherent about it. Third, he accuses Popper of relying on a trend claim of his own, roughly that technological change accelerates, and says this is the very kind of large-scale historical generalization Popper was supposed to have ruled out.

Ines replies that the first point changes the subject: what Marek has described is a trend extrapolation, and Popper's distinction between trends and laws is precisely designed to show why trends cannot underwrite prophecy, since a trend holds only as long as the conditions that sustain it hold. On the second, she says fixed points are no help in general: where they exist they may be multiple or unstable, and identifying the relevant one requires knowing the agents' theory-laden expectations, which drags future knowledge back in by the back door. On the third, she says the existence of a trend is not the existence of a law, so there is no tension.

Marek closes by saying that Ines's own framing concedes too much: if the only obstacle is that we cannot anticipate future knowledge, then the obstacle is epistemic rather than metaphysical, and history may still be perfectly law-governed for all Popper has shown. On that reading Popper has not established that history has no laws; he has established that creatures embedded in time cannot do large-scale prophecy, which is a conclusion no demographer would think worth arguing about.
