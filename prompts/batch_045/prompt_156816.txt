Hi — I'm a PhD student and I keep getting cornered on this one. I think the exclusion argument more or less works (every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, no systematic overdetermination, so if phenomenal properties aren't physical they do no causal work), which leaves me an epiphenominalist — the old steam-whistle picture, consciousness along for the ride. But every time I say that out loud someone wheels out the paradox of phenomenal judgment and tells me my view eats itself. So I tried to build the strongest version of that argument I could, to see what's actually doing the work. Now I think it proves way too much, and I can't tell whether that means it's broken or whether I'm supposed to swallow something even weirder.

(1) Epiphenomenalism: phenomenal states cause nothing.
(2) I have an occurrent judgment right now that I am in phenomenal state Q, and I go on to assert that I am in Q.
(3) My assertion has a cause, and by (1) that cause isn't Q.
(4) A belief that p is justified — and is about the thing that makes p true — only if that thing is causally relevant to my having the belief. Call this the causal relevance constraint.
(5) So by (1) my judgment that I'm in Q is unjustified, isn't knowledge, and isn't really about Q at all: nothing put it in contact with Q.
(6) So if epiphenomenalism is true I can neither know nor even express it. Self-stultifying, therefore (1) is false.

Here's my problem. (4) is clearly carrying everything, and as stated it's wildly too strong: numbers and other abstracta cause nothing, the past causes nothing now, and I take myself to know plenty about both. So (4) is false and (6) doesn't follow. Great. Except I don't feel out of the woods, because the bit that actually nags me was never about justification — it's about aboutness. If Q plays no causal role in producing the state that purports to be about Q, what makes that state about Q rather than about whichever physical state did the causing? And "reference is sometimes non-causal" feels like an answer that works fine for numbers but somehow not for my own headache, for a reason I can't articulate. Also: if my saying "I am in Q" is fully explained without Q, then something with no qualia at all would say exactly the same thing, which makes me feel like I've lost my evidence even if I haven't lost my justification.

Two escapes I've been playing with, and I can't price either:

A. Restrict epiphenomenalism to the physical — qualia cause other mental states, so Q causes the judgment even though it causes no behavior. But then the judgment never causally reaches my mouth, so maybe the paradox just reappears one level up? Or maybe not?
B. Swap causation for constitution — Q doesn't cause the judgment, it's partly constitutive of it: the experience is a proper part of, or grounds, the very state that's about it. Content without oomph. But I can't tell if that's coherent, if it secretly smuggles causation back in, or if it's just relabeling the mystery.

So: which step do I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? Is the residual aboutness worry real once (4) is gone, and does either A or B actually buy me anything or just move the bump under the carpet? If it matters for pricing: I'd much rather keep (1) than keep (4), and I'd rather keep mathematical knowledge than keep the conclusion that epiphenomenalism is false.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the step I should give up and what it costs me — then give me the reasoning. No preamble, and please don't restate my argument back at me. Six or seven hundred words is plenty, and if you can, price A and B separately at the end. Don't bother with reading lists or citations, I just want the diagnosis.
