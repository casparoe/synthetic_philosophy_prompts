I'm a senior at a large public high school, and I'm one of five students on the committee that allocates the Hollis Fund: an alumni gift of $20,000 that this year has to be divided among roughly forty seniors, each of whom has to complete an independent project in order to graduate. We've been meeting for two months and going in circles, and I've started to suspect the disagreement isn't really about the money, so I'd be grateful for your help — and please answer in American English, since this will end up in front of our faculty advisor.

The simplest proposal is an even split, $500 each, no questions asked. Several of us think that's a fake kind of fairness, because $500 doesn't buy the same thing for everyone: one girl in my class is deaf and needs an interpreter for the interviews she wants to do, another kid has a garage full of tools and a father who fabricates metal for a living, and a third wants to record an album and would spend the whole sum on two days of studio time. The natural fix — give more to whoever needs more in order to pull off the same kind of project — was attacked by another committee member on the grounds that it rewards exactly the wrong people, since the students with the most ambitious plans simply soak up the money. His counter-proposal was to aim at the other end instead: what should be equal is how people feel when it's over, so the money should go to wherever it produces the least satisfaction on its own. When I objected that this seems to hand the most to whoever is hardest to please and the least to the cheerful kid who'd be thrilled with a used camera, he said that's just what taking equality seriously costs, and I didn't have an answer. Then my friend Ravi proposed something none of us had considered: that the thing to equalize isn't the money, isn't what people can actually manage to do, and isn't how happy they end up, but how many times they get to change their minds. His argument was that some students can scrap a project in week three and start over because a parent quietly absorbs the loss or a teacher rescues them, while others have exactly one shot and if it collapses they fail the requirement; on his view the fund should buy the second attempt for whoever doesn't already have one, even if that means some students receive nothing at all this year. Half the committee thinks this is absurd and half thinks it's obviously right, and I can't tell which half is correct.

There's also one case that seems to break every rule we've come up with. A boy in my advisory works about twenty hours a week at a grocery store and helps look after his younger brother, so his problem isn't money at all — it's hours. You could give him $5,000 and he still couldn't do what the girl with the garage can do in a weekend, and someone has suggested we use part of the fund to replace the wages he'd lose by cutting back his shifts. That strikes me as either the most generous thing we could possibly do or a total misunderstanding of what a project fund is for, and I genuinely cannot say which. What I need is something I can defend: what should I argue the committee actually do, and why? More importantly, I'd like to understand what we're even arguing about — what is the thing that a fair distribution is supposed to make equal, how would I go about deciding between these options rather than just picking the one that feels nicest, and what does the strongest version of the objection to your recommendation look like? Our faculty advisor has asked for one page by Friday, so I'd rather have a single position defended carefully than a survey of everything we could say.
