ESSAY NOTES: Discarded for what? Reference classes, error-accounting, and the pessimistic meta-induction

1. The target. The pessimistic meta-induction argues from the historical record that most theories which were once predictively successful turned out to be false in their central claims about unobservables, that our current theories are not relevantly different from those, and hence that our current theories are probably false in those claims too. The familiar inventories of abandoned-but-successful theories do the real work: the ether, the calor fluid, the humors, and so on.

2. First move: the argument needs a base rate, and base rates need a reference class. Three candidate classes:
(A) all theoretical posits ever proposed;
(B) posits belonging to theories that achieved some notable predictive success;
(C) posits belonging to theories that achieved such success and whose load-bearing role in generating those successes was preserved by the successor theory.
Class A is useless to anyone: it is dominated by speculative posits, and its discard rate is near one for reasons that have nothing to do with unobservability. Class B is what the pessimistic meta-induction actually uses. I want to argue that C is the class we should be reasoning about, and that the discard rate in C is much lower than in B.

3. Second move: a mechanism for retention. When a theory is superseded, the successor normally owes us an account of why the old theory worked where it worked and failed where it failed. Call this the error-accounting requirement (my own phrase, not one I have taken from anywhere). My claim: a posit of the old theory survives supersession just in case something playing its role is needed to discharge that debt. Where the successor can rederive the old successes without anything in that role, the posit dies: the optical ether is the clean case, since the successes get rederived from symmetry principles and the posit does no work. Where the successor cannot reproduce the successes without something in that role, the posit survives in revised form: atoms and genes are the cases I have in mind. On this picture what kills a posit is not falsity but redundancy, and what survives is whatever the successor still needs in order to explain its predecessor's track record.

4. Why this is not just structural realism. The structural-realist move says that what is retained across theory change is structure rather than the nature of the entities. My claim is different in two ways. It is a claim about the mechanism of retention rather than about the metaphysical character of what is retained, and it is permissive about entities: on my criterion the retained item can be an object whose nature is later revised beyond recognition, provided it is still needed to explain the earlier successes. It is also meant as a forward-looking selection rule about which posits to believe now, not only as a description of what happened to past ones.

5. The selection rule, and the regularity I am guessing at. If retention tracks error-accounting necessity, then the realist should not believe whole theories. She should believe those posits that are load-bearing in the error-accounting of a broadly successful theory. And I think there is a further empirical regularity, which is the substantive guess I most want tested: the retention rate is strongly conditioned on the breadth of the predecessor's success. Posits from theories whose success was narrow, single-domain, and qualitative are discarded freely. Posits from theories with multi-domain, quantitatively precise, and genuinely novel predictive success are retained far more often.

6. Applied honestly to myself, this hurts. The framework that motivated my detector never had that kind of track record. It was supported by theoretical elegance and by a fine-tuning worry, not by a history of novel quantitative predictions. On my own criterion, my posit looks like a class-B posit rather than a class-C posit, which is to say it looks like the sort of thing that gets discarded. This is, I take it, some evidence that the criterion is not merely gerrymandered to save whatever I happen to like.

7. A possible rescue, which I do not trust. Call it constraint inheritance. Even if the posit is dead, the gravitational evidence for dark matter — rotation curves, the acoustic structure of the microwave background, lensing — is something any successor must account for, and my experiment can be described as testing not whether the particle exists but whether the successor's account of those phenomena will place anything in my detector's mass range. On this description the experiment presupposes nothing about the ontology of the abandoned theory, and it remains worth running. My worry is that this is just instrumentalism with better manners, and that a rationale which survives every possible revision of the theory carries no more realist content than no rationale at all.

8. Two objections I can already see. First, hindsight: both error-accounting status and breadth of success are typically visible only after the successor arrives, so the selection rule cannot guide present belief about current posits, which is the only belief the realist cares about. Second, a selection artifact in the other direction: our catalogue of discarded successful theories is arguably sampled on the outcome, since we remember the dramatic discards, which would inflate the discard rate in class B — but I cannot tell how much weight this can bear, and I suspect it cuts against me as much as for me.
