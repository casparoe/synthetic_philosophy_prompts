You are a political philosopher who specializes in property, land use, and urban ethics. Please reason carefully before answering. Before you write anything, brainstorm the different principles that could be at stake here and construct the strongest version of each, including the ones you ultimately reject. I don't want a "both sides have a point" shrug; I want you to tell me which distinctions actually do moral work, and then to tell me what you would do in my position.

Here is the situation. I'm on the board of the neighborhood association in the older, close-in part of a mid-sized American city. A developer wants to replace a single house on my block with a nine-unit apartment building, and the planning department says the project is allowed as of right. Most of my neighbors want to fight it. Their reasons: they bought here expecting the block to stay low-rise, the developer is from out of state and will probably sell to an institutional landlord, and there would be a year of construction traffic on a narrow street.

I'm stuck, and not only about the building. I rent half of a duplex, so more housing looks good to me personally, and I know people who grew up here and moved away because they couldn't afford to stay. But I also grew up here: my grandmother's house was one of dozens demolished for a highway in the 1960s, and the families who were pushed out mostly ended up across town, where the schools are worse and the bus service is thin. So when my neighbors say they did this to us once and now you want to let them do it again, I feel the force of it. When the developer's lawyer says the neighbors are just protecting their property values, I feel the force of that too.

Now the wrinkle, which is what I actually need help with. Someone on the board has floated a rule the city council might take up: in any neighborhood vote on a rezoning or a variance, a resident would get one vote plus one additional vote for each decade they have lived on that block. The pitch is that it protects the people with the deepest stake in a place from being outvoted by newcomers and absentee owners, and that a rule like it would have protected the neighborhood my grandmother lived in. I'm uneasy. It seems to treat mere length of residence as a moral claim, and to hand the most blocking power to exactly the people who gain from housing being scarce. But I can't say precisely what is wrong with it, or whether my unease is just my own interest talking.

So: what is the strongest case for weighting a neighborhood's say by how long people have lived there, and the strongest case against it? More generally, what kind of claim, if any, does a current resident have over what gets built next door, and how does that compare to the claim of someone who wants to move in and can't afford to, or of someone who was displaced decades ago and never got to come back? Does the fact that my neighbors' objection is partly about money defeat it, and if not, why not? Does the history of my grandmother's neighborhood change the argument, or am I using that history to dress up a conclusion I reached for other reasons? And should I vote to endorse the weighted-vote rule?

Finally, as you answer: say how confident you are in each of your main claims and single out the one you are least sure of. Flag any place where your argument turns on an empirical question about how housing markets actually respond to new construction, since I would rather know where you are guessing than be handed a confident answer that rests on it.
