Right, slightly odd one. I've been on sertraline for about a year and somewhere around month three my emotions went sort of flat — not the thoughts, not the crying at adverts, definitely not the irritability, just no felt anything. If you asked me whether I'm sad about my dad I'd say yes, and I mean it, and I'll tear up at the funeral, but there's no *there* there. My partner says that proves I obviously still feel things; I say it proves feeling is a separate ingredient from the rest. We've been going round on this for months and it's turned into an arguement about what an emotion even is.

So I'm making a "which theory of emotion are you actually committed to?" quiz for my reading group — five of us, none of us philosophers, and one of them flatly refuses to answer anything phrased abstractly. Nine questions, eight leaves. British English spelling throughout please.

How I want it built:

First, three dissociation questions that give a three-letter code. (1) The judgement is gone but everything else stays — you know the dog is harmless, you're still terrified. (2) The felt feeling is gone but the judgement, the crying and the physiology all stay — that's my case, use it, but put it in the third person and call him Sam. (3) There's nothing to act on and nothing that can be acted on — fury at a printer, grief over a fictional character, fear of a cliff edge you're watching from behind glass. Ask each one as "is there still an emotion here or not", with a "yes / no / it depends and here's what on" option set, and try not to make all three obviously push toward the same answer.

Then six more, and these are the ones I actually care about: whether you can be wrong about what you're feeling; whether a labrador or a ten-month-old can have the *same* emotion as me or only something that looks like it from outside; whether being irritable for no reason at all is the same kind of thing as being angry at someone; whether an emotion can survive its object (still furious at someone who died, still afraid of the dark you've just proved is empty); whether the thing you're afraid of is what your fear is *about* when you know perfectly well there's no threat; and one case where two emotions that go by the same word seem to share nothing at all underneath.

The leaves I want named: the view that emotions just are evaluative judgements; the view that they're perceptions of value; the view that they're feelings or bodily states; appraisal views; the view that they're states of being ready to act; the "it's a package, several components, no single one is the emotion" view; and the view that "emotion" isn't one kind of thing we're theorising about at all. Call them by those family names — I really don't care who said what first, and I can't look anything up anyway, so no reading list, no papers, no names attached to positions.

The bit I most want is the mapping: give me the scoring key as answer patterns from the first three questions (ABA → such and such a leaf, and so on), tell me which of the eight combos nobody actually occupies and whether that's because it's incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any two leaves the quiz genuinely can't tell apart — including what extra question *would* separate them even if it's an ugly or leading question. Also tell me where a question is underdetermined, i.e. where three different theories all give the same answer and so it isn't doing any diagnostic work.

One last thing: every question has to be answerable by someone who has never heard any of these labels, and no question may just be "do you think emotions are judgements?" in disguise.
