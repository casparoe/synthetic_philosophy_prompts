hey — I need help settling an argument with my flatmate that's been running for three weeks and I think at this point we're mostly just re-asserting our intuitions louder.

The setup is the boring one: six will die unless they get the drug, one will die unless he gets the whole dose, no one else is affected, everyone is a stranger to me. I gave something like the Taurek answer — each of the seven has an equal claim on the drug, the six deaths don't pile up into one big harm that anybody suffers, so give everyone an equal shot at it (draw lots, whatever) instead of counting heads. My flatmate says that's obviously monstrous, and his whole case is one analogy: adding up across people is just like adding up across times in a single life. The version he keeps coming back to — you'd take a sore arm today to avoid being seriously ill next month, and nobody thinks the sore arm wins because the later you is a different person, so of course you take the small cost to one to spare five the big one. If the analogy holds, he says, my lottery is like refusing a vaccine on behalf of my future self, and the entire anti-utilitarian tradition is just this analogy run in reverse, the separateness-of-persons thing and all that.

So: how far does it hold? I want the mapping written out explicitly — what plays the role of the earlier self, the later self, the boundary of a life, prudence, compensation, consent, and the bit where a person can bind their future self (insurance, precommitment, pouring the whisky down the sink). Then where the mapping breaks. But the part I actually care about: which of those disanalogies are doing work for this particular conclusion and which are just decoration. My guess is that some are noise (I don't think it matters that my future self can't literally sign anything today) and some are load-bearing (nobody can compensate the one, nobody consented, there's no one for whom the summed loss is a loss), but I definately can't tell which is which and I suspect I'm just picking whichever ones help me.

Here's the bit I can't shake, and it's a case I made up, so I've no idea if it's a reductio or a red herring. Suppose the six aren't strangers. Suppose two of them are a couple who've run the same farm together for forty years — no separate projects, no separate money, neither can make sense of going on without the other — or crank it up and imagine conjoined twins wired so that a lot of what one feels, the other feels too. Call it a "thick unit". If the analogy is doing real work — aggregation licensed by costs and benefits landing in the same unit of concern — then aggregation should get steadily less objectionable as the unit gets thicker, and the stranger case is only the thin end of a slope. That seems like trouble for both of us. For me, because "each person is a separate unit" then can't be a binary switch I get to just assert at the stranger end; I'd owe a cutoff and a reason for it. For him, because the analogy now owes a story about what makes my present and future self one unit — identity, psychological continuity, the fact that I care about my future — and every story I can think of has consequences he won't want. If it's continuity, continuity comes in degrees, so the slope is real and the lottery answer leaks at the margins even if it's fine in the middle. If it's brute identity, I don't see why identity is the thing that licenses the tradeoff, since someone who'll be a total stranger to their own present self in twenty years still seems to have reason to buy insurance.

So audit it. Give me the correspondence table, sort the disanalogies into the ones that carry weight for this conclusion and the ones that don't, tell me what the analogy quietly imports about what turns a bunch of separate welfare into one countable sum, say whether the thick-unit case helps me or him or neither, and whether the conclusion he wanted — counting is obviously right, my lottery is obviously monstrous — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. And if prudence is the wrong analogy here, what's the better one? My worry is that the honest picture is closer to a bankruptcy court dividing one indivisible asset among equal claimants, which would hand me the lottery back for reasons that have nothing to do with his analogy at all.
