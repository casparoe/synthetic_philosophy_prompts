I hold a specific view about how to compare outcomes in an infinite world, and I want to know what would have to be true for it to be false.

Call it Eventual Dominance (ED). Represent an outcome as a utility stream: a real-valued amount of value attached to each person-moment, or each spacetime region, with no finite upper bound on how many there are. Compare two outcomes by their cumulative totals over finite horizons. If outcome A's running total is ahead of B's at every horizon past some point, A is better than B. If the lead changes hands infinitely often, A and B are equally good, however large the swings are. ED is designed to escape the familiar paralysis of infinite ethics: it never has to sum an infinite total, it never declares two outcomes incomparable, and it agrees with finite-horizon Pareto whenever finite-horizon Pareto has a verdict. What it buys this with is insensitivity to magnitude: a permanent lead of one millionth of a util per region counts as just as good as a permanent lead of a billion.

Tell me what would count against ED, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and say which defeaters a committed ED theorist should concede as counting against the view and which they are entitled to dismiss. I am interested in at least these: whether its indifference relation is genuinely an equivalence (can A cross B and B cross C while A eventually dominates C, and does that matter?); whether trading enormous long-run swings for a permanent epsilon lead is a cost or a feature; whether ED is even well defined when the infinity is spatial rather than temporal, since then "finite horizons" require choosing an exhaustion of regions by finite sets and different exhaustions may disagree; whether the no-incomparability claim survives outcomes whose streams have no well-defined running total at all; and whether ED collapses into a rival view (a lexicographic one, an ultrafilter-style one, or a catching-up-style one) once the details are filled in. I would personally find a diachronic-inconsistency or money-pump style argument most persuasive, so give that one its due. Be honest about whether ED has been qualified into insulating itself, and whether that insulation is legitimate for a view of this kind.

End with a single last line of the form: Verdict: <fatal | serious but survivable | insulated by its own qualifications>.
