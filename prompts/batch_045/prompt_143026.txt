I chair the allocations committee of a foundation that pays out about four million dollars a year in four quarterly rounds. Four of us meet for one afternoon; our recommendation then goes to a lay board the next morning, so the whole thing has to be runnable in about three hours by people who are not specialists (I have some graduate training in philosophy, the others have none). Our disagreements are not empirical: we agree on what the money would do. They trace to which moral outlook each of us half accepts — two of us lean toward impartial concern for everyone affected, one toward duties and side-constraints that refuse to aggregate, one toward the claim that what matters is who is worst off and whether anyone falls below a decent minimum. Our credences across these outlooks and their variants are diffuse; no single outlook has more than about a third, and one member refuses to attach numbers at all.

Two procedures have already been voted down, and I need one that avoids both failures. The first — follow whichever outlook currently has the highest credence — was rejected because our credences drift between rounds and the board does not want last year's payout to have been decided by a ranking that has since changed. The second — score each candidate payout on each outlook and take an expectation across credences — was rejected because the scoring step is where the decision is really made: converting each outlook's verdicts onto a common scale looks like a technical fix that silently settles the substantive question of how much of one outlook's concern counts compared to another's.

Please write me a procedure that my committee could actually run, and that is explicit about the following. How to construct the shortlist of candidate payouts and the list of outlooks, including what to do with near-variants that share a single underlying intuition and would otherwise be counted as independent support. How to get from each outlook's verdicts to something comparable without pretending there is a common currency: say what normalization rule you recommend, what it assumes, and how we should check whether the ranking it produces is an artifact of that rule. What to do with an option that is nearly worthless on every outlook we take seriously but very valuable if a low-credence variant is correct — whether we hedge toward it, how much, and how we keep that hedge from swallowing the budget. Whether any part of the budget should go to reducing our moral uncertainty itself, given that the value of such work is itself outlook-dependent. What to do when a member will not give numbers. Where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the question to the board on the grounds that it is a values question and not a technical one.

One further constraint, which the board imposed and which I think is the hardest part. Any recommendation must be presentable: it has to be statable as a reason that a donor who accepts only one of our outlooks could recognize as a genuine reason, though not a conclusive one. A bare expected-value calculation that no such donor can see the force of will be sent back to us.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals now on our table: a large grant to a well-evidenced program preventing a parasitic disease, which is cheap per life improved and looks best to the aggregating outlooks; a grant funding impact litigation to enforce a legal right on behalf of a small and badly off group, which looks best to the side-constraint and threshold outlooks; and a small speculative grant aimed at reducing a poorly understood long-run risk, worth little on most outlooks and enormous on a long-horizon variant we credit at about eight percent. At least one of the three should come out somewhere the committee will not like. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — in particular, whether it can be trusted to pick itself, since our choice of procedure looks like one more decision we are making under the same uncertainty.
