Hey — I write historical novels about real people who are long dead. My current one is about a labor organizer, Ada Kowalczyk, dead since 1961; before that it was a 1919 newsroom book. I'm not writing straight biography and I'm not writing about anyone living: I invent scenes, dialogue, and inner life, a dozen or so times a book. I've been deciding each one by gut and I'd like an actual procedure instead.

What I need is something I can run in an afternoon per scene, by myself, no philosophy degree, and where the final call is still mine — I have an editor and sometimes an estate lawyer, but neither of them answers the question I actually have, which is whether this is okay *to her*. Legal clearance is a different question, and I've stopped believing the "this is a work of fiction" line on the copyright page does any work at all. I've heard people say the dead can't be harmed so nothing is owed, and other people say they have interests that outlive them; I don't need to settle that in the abstract, I need it turned into steps.

So: give me the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the answer is stop-don't-write-it, where it's pass, and where it hands the decision back to me because it's a values call no procedure can close. Things I keep getting stuck on and want the procedure to actually handle rather than hand-wave:

- scenes where the historical record is silent versus scenes that go against what the letters and papers actually say
- making her *better* than the documents support, not worse — the flattering invention, which nobody ever warns you about because all the rules are about defamation
- the fact that a vivid scene is very hard for a reader to un-remember, so an authors note in the back basically never gets it back
- whether it matters that she had a project she gave her life to (forty years arguing that nonviolence works) and I'd be showing her privately not believing it, which seems to defeat something she actually aimed at even though nothing happens to her now
- where living descendants come in, if at all, and what kind of claim is theirs as opposed to borrowed

Then run it on three scenes from my draft: (1) she sits up all night with a scab's sick kid the night before a strike vote — never happened, but it's exactly the kind of thing her letters show her doing; (2) I definately want her to have an affair with the married editor who printed her pieces, which would make the book work structurally; (3) a private conversation with her sister where she admits the whole movement was a mistake, and every surviving letter says the opposite.

I want at least one of those to come out as an answer I dont like, ideally on the scene I most want to keep. And tell me what this kind of procedure is structurally blind to — what it can't see no matter how well it's built.
