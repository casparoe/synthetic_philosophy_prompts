As student editor of my school's ethics journal, I am assembling its spring feature, which will run as a single document co-signed by two juniors on the editorial board who have argued about "cause swaps" all year. A cause swap is this: two people who care about different causes each agree to put in hours for the other person's cause, on the theory that both come out ahead by their own lights, the way two people good at different tasks can both gain by trading. One of them defends this as straightforwardly good cooperation between people who disagree about what matters. The other holds that it is corrupt: that logging hours for a cause you take to be pointless, or worse, in exchange for someone else's hours on yours is buying your way out of having to act on your own values, and makes you an accomplice to whatever the other person is doing.

Please draft the joint statement they could both put their names to. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both can sign, including about what a swap is worth when each side thinks its own cause's hours count for more per hour, so that they cannot agree on the exchange rate; (2) a table of their remaining disagreements, each in wording its own side would accept, covering at least the accomplice objection, whether the gain depends on comparative advantage rather than on there simply being more total hours worked, and what follows from the fact that neither side can check whether the other actually did the work; (3) the cruxes, each with one concrete case both would accept as bearing on it and with each side's prediction recorded in advance — include the case in which a swap adds hours overall but each organization gets a volunteer with no real commitment to its cause, and the case in which the accomplice objection, if it works, would seem to condemn ordinary promises and ordinary political compromise as well.

If you conclude that some part of their dispute is verbal rather than real, say which part and why. Do not cite anything or suggest further reading; the piece has to stand on its own argument. Begin with the agreed-claims section itself, with no preface and no commentary on how you approached the task. American English, please.
