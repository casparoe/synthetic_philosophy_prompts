I have just come away from a long argument with a friend — a working jazz improviser — about whether creativity is exhaustively a matter of producing variations on a theme. He holds something close to Hofstadter's knob-twiddling picture: a creative domain is a stylistic space with graded dimensions, and to be creative is to slide along those knobs, sometimes to a region nobody has visited. I kept producing cases that seemed to me to change the knobs rather than move along them: the abandonment of a tonal centre, the admission of notated silence as music, the reclassification of zero as a number, the relaxation of the parallel postulate. He replied that each of those, viewed from far enough away, is another position of a higher-order knob — a knob about how much a rule may be bent. I replied that a knob fitted after the fact to connect two items explains nothing. By the end we agreed on exactly one thing: we had been using the words space, knob and novelty differently, and neither of us could say what would have changed our minds.

Please draft the joint statement we should have written, as an adversarial collaboration between the variationist (creativity is movement along the knobs of a stylistic space; apparent rule-changes are twiddles of higher-order knobs) and the transformationist (some creative acts alter which dimensions exist, and are not representable as movement along any knobs antecedently available to the creator).

Deliverable, in about 1000–1400 words:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — eight to twelve of them — that both parties could actually sign. They must be substantive and capable of being false: no claims of the form that creativity is complicated, or that both kinds matter. I want things like a reusability constraint on knobs (a candidate knob counts as genuine only if it is independently nameable and also applies to a third item it was not introduced to connect, so that a continuous space cannot be fitted ad hoc between any two works); the distinction between novelty of the product and novelty of the procedure that produced it; and the distinction between the space as indexed to what the creator could antecedently traverse and the space as reconstructed by a later theorist with the finished corpus in hand. Add whatever further agreements you think are real.

2. A disagreements table. One row per crux, with columns for: the crux; the variationist's formulation of their own position on it, in wording a variationist would accept; the transformationist's, likewise; a case, corpus, or test both would accept as bearing on that crux; and each side's prediction about that case, written down in advance, together with what each would count as evidence against their own view. The cruxes I expect to matter include: whether the relevant space is agent-relative or hindsight-relative; whether importing a knob from a foreign domain (applying to music a knob first defined on visual caricature, say) is a species of twiddling or of transformation; how to tell a change of dimensionality from a very large step along an existing dimension — I propose, as one operationalisation, that a transformation is a case where every continuous path in the antecedent space from the old region to the new one passes through items that are merely ill-formed or incompetent rather than intermediate works, and I want each side to say what pattern of results on a small coded corpus would confirm or disconfirm it; and whether the regress of knobs-for-creating-knobs bottoms out benignly or shows that variationism needs a non-variational primitive somewhere.

3. A short closing section saying which parts of our argument you judge to have been merely verbal, why, and which crux is genuinely empirical and could in principle be settled by the coding exercise or a machine case — a generative system whose machinery is fixed but which searches a vast continuous parameter space, and which expert judges some of whose outputs as rule-changing.

Constraints: neutral, polished prose; no citations, bibliographies or reading lists; each side stated at its strongest and in its own preferred terms; and where no neutral formulation exists, say so explicitly rather than splitting the difference.
