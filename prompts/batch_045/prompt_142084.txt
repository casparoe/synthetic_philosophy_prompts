Hey — I'm a third-year currently in a philosophy of mind seminar, and I need help with my final paper but also just for my own sanity, because our seminar has completely stalled out and I've been going in cirlces.

We've spent three weeks on the causal exclusion problem and every session is the same loop. Someone reconstructs the argument roughly like this: (1) mental properties aren't identical to physical properties; (2) every physical effect that has a cause at all has a sufficient physical cause; (3) effects aren't systematically overdetermined by two independent sufficient causes; so (4) the mental property either is identical to the physical one or it isn't doing any causal work. Then everyone picks which premise to deny and repeats themselves. The nonreductive physicalists say the overdetermination here isn't the objectionable kind, because the mental and physical causes aren't independent of each other. The reductionists say, right, so stop being nonreductive. One guy says causation just is counterfactual dependence, and if the mental counterfactually matters there's literally nothing left to argue about. Someone else mutters that the argument proves too much, and then we run out of time. Nobody has moved an inch.

The hypothesis I keep pushing, and nobody liked, is that the argument form generalizes way further than anyone wants to admit, and that once you see that, the interesting question isn't about the mind at all. Take: the recession caused the firm to go under. The firm's going under has a complete microphysical history of people making calls, signing forms, money moving between accounts. The same shape of reasoning would give you: the recession either is that microphysical clutter or it caused nothing. Same with the program crashed because of a memory leak, or a typo in the contract cost us the deal. Everyone in the room seems to think recessions and memory leaks are perfectly good causes, and the special sciences traffic in this kind of thing constantly. So something in the argument must be doing work that has nothing to do with mentality, and I want to know what it is.

Concretely, here's what I want diagnosed:

1. Is the stall because there are actually two or three arguments under one name? I think I see a no-work version (fix all the microphysical facts and the mental facts and their effects are already fixed, so the mental changes nothing) and a seperate no-room version (one effect, one sufficient cause; add the mental and you've got overdetermination), and I suspect people answer one while being asked about the other. Is that a real distinction or am I making it up?

2. Is part of it verbal? The counterfactual-dependence guy seems to mean something different by cause than the person who's worried about production or generation, and they may just be talking past each other. But then is there a fact of the matter about what causation is, or is that itself one of the stalled questions?

3. Is there a hidden empirical question? Premise (2) sounds like it should be settled in a lab — physics might be indeterministic, and brains might be exactly the sort of place where that matters — but nobody in the seminar treats it as something evidence could touch. Is it empirical, metaphysical, or a methodological commitment we'd never give up?

4. And is the real crux whether the physical base screens off the mental, i.e. whether once the microphysics is fixed there's nothing left for the mental to be a difference-maker with respect to?

Also, my TA keeps saying that even if the argument works, it only shows mental properties aren't causes at the fundamental level, and that we never should have wanted them to be. Is that a genuine position or is it changing the subject?

I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the options. Tell me which of my hypotheses is right, which one is confused, and — given the diagnosis — what would actually count as progress here: a distinction, a formal result, giving up a premise, changing the question? Or should we expect no progress at all, and why.
