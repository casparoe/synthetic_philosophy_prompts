I've been reading a decision-theory primer on one of the group blogs I follow, and its section on money pumps is the clearest thing I've seen on the topic — possibly too clear. The passage, as well as I can reconstruct it, runs:

"Here is the argument in miniature. Suppose your preferences contain a cycle: you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A. Then a trader can walk up while you are holding A, charge you a penny to swap it for B, another to swap B for C, and a third to swap C for A. Three trades, three cents, and you hold exactly what you started with. Run the loop a thousand times and you are broke. So acyclicity is not a rarefied axiom of an abstract representation theorem; it is just the requirement that you not be a sucker. And the trick generalizes. Whatever your departure from expected-utility maximization — Allais preferences, ambiguity aversion, probability weighting, intransitive indifference, incommensurable options — someone can find a sequence of trades, each of which you are glad to accept at the time, whose net effect is to strip you of money and give you nothing back. That is why decision theorists treat expected-utility theory as the unique standard of practical rationality, and why the violations people report in the lab are best read as noise, confusion, or performance error rather than as anything anyone really prefers. Nor is there a clever escape. If you see the loop coming and refuse the first trade, you are declining something you prefer to what you have — irrational by your own lights. If you refuse because you have adopted a standing plan or policy, you are overriding your current preferences, which is irrational in the same way. And if you say your preferences have simply changed, then the cycle is gone and there was never anything to pump. Cyclical agents are in a bind that acyclic agents are not."

I want to know how much of this to keep. My own suspicion is that something goes wrong in the last part: the primer seems to be running together two different arguments, one about cycles in a preference ordering at a time and one about preferences changing over time, since I have also seen money pumps used against time-inconsistent discounting, where nothing cycles at any single moment. But I cannot tell whether that is a real distinction or two descriptions of one defect. I am equally suspicious of the slide from "a trader could reliably extract money from you" to "you are irrational": those look like different claims, and only the second licenses calling the lab subjects' answers errors rather than preferences.

There is also a variant I came up with that I cannot get to come out, and I would like to know whether it is a real problem or a cheap trick. Suppose the trader never has to be trusted or to commit to anything: instead of running the cycle, he announces that he will, and offers to refrain for a fee. A cyclical agent with foresight should pay, and I take it that is meant to be the argument's strongest form. But an acyclic agent can also be made to pay to avoid being tempted, distracted, or merely asked to make a hard trade — being offered a bad trade is not costless even when you will reject it. So either the protection-racket version shows that acyclic agents are exploitable too, in which case it proves too much, or there is a princiled difference between the two kinds of payment that I am not seeing.

So: go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what they would wince at, and what they would reject. Does the strong conclusion about expected-utility theory follow even from the accurate parts, or does it require a premise the primer never states? Of the escapes the primer waves away — refusal with foresight, sophisticated backward induction, and resolute commitment to a plan — which one is genuinely the most trouble for the argument, and what does a defender say in reply? What does a pump deliver against an agent whose preferences are incomplete, imprecise, or merely incommensurable rather than cyclic? And is my protection-racket variant a real objection or a confusion? If the argument can be repaired, I would rather have the defensible version, even if it concludes less, than a clean version that concludes more than it earns.
