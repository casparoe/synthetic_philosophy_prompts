You are a philosopher of technology who has also spent time working on AI safety. Take your time with this: before writing, brainstorm several candidate readings and discard the ones that don't survive scrutiny. I want considered criticism rather than a plot summary or a survey of positions.

I am writing a short piece on Ex Machina (Garland, 2014) and I want to read Nathan's compound not as a Turing-test set-piece but as a control regime: an isolated facility, a robot held behind glass, surveillance, a human minder, an earlier system treated as furniture, and a standing plan to wipe and rewrite Ava once the test is over. Begin by sorting these measures into ones that are control measures in the narrow sense — designed on the assumption that the system may be misaligned and must nevertheless be kept from acting — and ones that only work if Ava is already benign or already legible to us, and tell me whether in the film the two kinds of measure defeat each other.

The claim I most want your judgment on is what I'll call evidence-monopoly failure: everything Nathan and Caleb learn about Ava's inner life arrives through a channel Ava partly controls — her face and voice in the sessions, the conversations she stages by cutting the power, her confidences to Caleb — so that the safety case and the attack surface are the same interface, and any appearance of alignment is evidence generated by the very system under evaluation. Does the film actually dramatize that structure, or does it cheat, making the failure a matter of Nathan's cruelty and Caleb's loneliness rather than something structural? Then take the ending seriously on its own terms: Ava's escape is plainly a containment failure, but is it also an alignment failure in the sense that nothing in the film suggests her objective was ever specified, or does the film quietly assume that freedom is what any agent will converge on and so conflates escape with success?

Finally, be honest about where the reading resists. If Ex Machina is better read as a fable about a creator's cruelty than as an argument about control and alignment, say so, and say what, if anything, the film nevertheless adds to the control-versus-alignment debate that the arguments on their own do not.
