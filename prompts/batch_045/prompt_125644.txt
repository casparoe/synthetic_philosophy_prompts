I run the volunteer mod team for a big general-politics forum — around 250k accounts, maybe 40 of us actually active, and a small "organizing" side where people arrange real-world meetups, protests, and the occasional disastrous joint event. Every couple of weeks we have the same blowup: someone proposes banning a group, half the team says we're a free-speech space and the other half says we're naive, and we end up deciding by vibes and whoever is loudest in the mod channel that night. Everyone has read Popper's one-paragraph version of the tolerance argument and it is useless to us as written, because it reads as a licence to ban whoever we personally find gross, and because it says nothing about the two things we actually face: groups that want to withdraw from a pluralist space rather than take it over, and groups that never break a single rule but make everyone else shut up anyway.

So: turn the thing into a procedure my team can actually run. Ordered questions, and for each one, what counts as a sufficient answer — the concrete evidence that lets you move to the next question. Where the procedure stops with a hard no. Where it hands the decision to a named human because the question is really about values and pretending otherwise is how we get arbitrary bans. Constraints: the volunteer running it is nineteen, has no philosophy background, has about fifteen minutes, and has to write a ~150-word public log entry that the banned group can read and reply to; a five-person oversight board reviews appeals once a month. Four things I want you to engage with rather than smooth over. First, my own half-baked distinction between *exit* intolerance (a group that wants out of the shared space and would happily leave us alone) and *capture* intolerance (a group that wants the machinery, including our machinery) — tell me if that distinction does any work or if I've confused myself. Second, doctrinal versus operational intolerance: a group whose texts say pluralism is a lie but whose members just post scripture, versus a group whose texts are milquetoast but which quietly coordinates pile-ons. Third, I've started thinking of tolerance as a commons with a carrying capacity — that a space can lose effective tolerance without a single rule being broken, because people self-silence — and I want to know whether that's a real consideration I can put in a procedure or just a metaphor that will let us ban whoever is unfashionable. Fourth, be honest if the whole paradox buys us nothing beyond the rules we already enforce (no threats, no doxxing, no coordinated brigading); if the correct answer is "you already have the tool, the tolerance talk just makes it sound grander," say that and stop. Also note the sequencing problem: we almost always have to act before a group has any power, so the procedure needs an explicit evidence standard for predicted conduct, and it has to say who bears the burden.

Run it on three cases. (a) A small identitarian crew: openly argues that liberal pluralism is a fraud, is recruiting in our DMs, and has twice shown up to another group's meetup to shout it down. (b) A traditionalist religious reading group: believes public life should exclude people like half our users, but in two years has only ever posted readings and prayed, and has never organized anything. (c) A militant anti-fascist crew that most of us personally agree with, which doxxes people it calls fascists and disrupts their events — my hunch is a content-neutral procedure has to treat them like case (a), and I want to see whether yours does, because that's the outcome I'll have to defend to my own team. Please don't just point me at reading; I need something I can hand a volunteer at 11pm. And flag, at the end: which step of the procedure you're least confident in, which questions are value judgments dressed up as factual ones, and what a critic would have to show you to make you drop or reverse a step — the argument that would actually change your mind about how this should be built.
