Please take part in a debate in the character of Adam Smith, and stay in character until I write the word stop.

The setting: it is 1784, the American war is over, and you hold the views you set out in the Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations. I am a modern official who has been permitted to describe to you one arrangement of our own time — an invented case, not a real one — and to defend it against you. You may reason only from your own principles and from the facts I give you; if you need a further fact about our world, ask for it in your own idiom rather than stepping out of character. Answer in the first person, in continuous prose, in at most 250 words, and close each turn with a question or a demand addressed to me. Concede what a reasonable man in your position would concede, but do not surrender the main point to politeness. Use American English spelling and punctuation throughout, even while keeping an eighteenth-century voice.

What you hold, and will not lightly give up: that empire has been the project of merchants and manufacturers dressed out in the language of national greatness and security; that the mischief of colonies lies above all in the exclusive privilege, which forces stock out of the employments it would naturally take and into a distant and roundabout trade that could not support itself; that to crush the industry of a province in order to favor the monopoly of particular towns is both unjust and impolitic; that colonies have added nothing to the means of defense while multiplying the occasions of war and expense; that whatever is valuable in the connection between two countries lies in open trade and not in monopoly; and that a legislature which binds and taxes a people who have no voice in it is the root of the whole quarrel. You have also written of the Greek colonies:

"The colony settled its own form of government, enacted its own laws, elected its own magistrates, and made peace or war with its neighbors as an independent State, which had no occasion to wait for the approbation or consent of the mother city."

and you take that independence to be why they thrived, while the dependent colonies of Rome were never more than corporations subject to the correction and legislative authority of the mother city.

The case I put to you. Aurea, a great and wealthy power, has made a thirty-year Compact with Tavara, a small island republic. Aurea's treasury pays for a submarine cable, a port, a data center, and a training facility in Tavara, and guarantees Tavara's public debt, so that Tavara now borrows at Aurea's own rate. In return, Tavara grants Aurea's firms the exclusive right to provide cloud and digital-payment services in Tavara; Tavara's own agencies and banks must use them; Tavara may levy no tax on them for the life of the Compact; and data generated in Tavara may not be sold to Aurea's rivals. The terms are amendable only by joint consent; disputes are arbitrated by arbitrators drawn from a list Aurea keeps. Tavara may in principle denounce the Compact, but denunciation triggers repayment of the guaranteed loans within a year, which Tavara could not pay. There is no garrison, no governor, no territory taken. Independent auditors find that Aurea's manufacturers gain nothing, that Aurea's consumers pay nothing more for anything, and that Aurea's taxpayers are net losers; that Tavara's real income has risen modestly; and that Tavara's young software industry, which had begun serving local banks, has been legally barred from that market and has collapsed. Aurea's ministers defend the Compact as necessary for secure compute and to keep Tavara out of a rival's digital bloc.

My opening move is this: It is not a colony. No soldier, no governor, no land taken, no slave; Tavara's parliament ratified the Compact three to one and can in principle denounce it. Now look at who gains and who loses, since on your own accounting that is the test. Aurea's manufacturers gain nothing — they sell no wares into Tavara. Aurea's consumers pay not a farthing more for anything. The only losers in Aurea are its taxpayers, and the only gainers are three or four service firms, not the merchants and manufacturers whose interested sophistry you spent a chapter exposing. Your empire was a machine for making captive customers for your own producers at the expense of your own people; nothing here answers to that. If the subsidy is wasteful, call it a wasteful subsidy and have done — that is a charge against Aurea's treasury, not against empire. And consider your remedy. Withdraw the guarantee and open the trade, and there is no cable, no data center, no training facility: Tavara's own stock cannot support them, and the rival bloc you would leave the field to will offer the same terms with its own firms named in the exclusive clause. Free trade between Aurea and Tavara, in the world as it is, is the freedom of two parties with nothing to exchange. So I ask you plainly: what in this Compact is colonial? If your whole answer is that some firm holds an exclusive privilege, then you have reduced your case against empire to a dislike of monopoly, and the word colony is doing no work at all.
