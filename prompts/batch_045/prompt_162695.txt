I am writing to ask for your judgment on a matter currently before our faculty, because my colleagues and I appear to have reached an impasse that I suspect is conceptual rather than empirical.

Three years ago our university replaced the committee that assigned teaching duties with an automated allocation system. Requests are submitted through a form; the system grants roughly ninety-seven percent of them, and where it refuses it emits a brief written rationale that no one in the administration is obliged to read, explain, or defend. There is no appeal. The old committee was slower, more intrusive, and considerably less generous: it denied close to a third of requests, asked colleagues to justify their preferences, and occasionally told someone what he ought to be teaching. But its decisions were given in person, by people who could be argued with, and were sometimes reversed on the spot.

The reaction puzzles me. Most of my colleagues now describe themselves as freer than they have ever been under our previous arrangements, and they cite the approval rate as though it settled the matter. A smaller group says the opposite and cannot be moved by the statistics; when pressed, they say the change is that they now have nothing to argue with. One of them put it to me as follows: under the committee he was frequently refused, but he was someone to whom a reason was owed; now he is almost never refused, and he is nobody.

There is also a junior colleague in my department who has begun to want precisely what the system gives her. She has reshaped her research agenda around the kinds of courses it reliably approves, and reports that an old low-grade anxiety has disappeared entirely. When I asked whether she would prefer the committee restored, she said she would not: she now prefers what she receives, and regards the question of what she would have wanted before she arrived as beside the point. The system's designers have offered two modifications. The first would publish the complete decision rule in advance, so that everyone could predict with certainty which requests will be granted. The second would establish a right to contest a refusal before a panel empowered to overturn it, though the panel would convene only after the schedule is fixed and could offer no remedy until a later semester.

Two complications bear on the first option. The rule is re-estimated each semester from the preceding semesters' data, so whatever we publish would, by the time it governed, already describe a rule that has drifted. And my junior colleague's adaptation is itself part of the data: the more colleagues reshape their requests to fit the system, the more the system's future approvals will reflect that reshaping.

My committee must recommend one of these modifications, both, or neither, and I am no longer confident which of the attitudes I have described is tracking something worth caring about. Would you set out for me: what the disagreement between the first two groups is really about; what each of the three attitudes I have described takes a person's being free to consist in; whether my junior colleague is free, unfree, or simply a case to which the question does not apply; and which modification, if any, we ought to adopt. I am after the structure of the problem, not generalities about technology, efficiency, or shared governance.
