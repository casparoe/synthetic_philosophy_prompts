Ok so next tuesday I'm pitched to the grants committee of a foundation that funds farmed-animal welfare work, asking for roughly $1.5M to start an insect sentience research program -- research only, no campaigns, no interventions. The committee is six people and I know roughly where each of them is coming from: three are finance/ops types who think in cost per animal helped and want a single number they can put in a donor update; two are animal welfare scientists (one fish, one poultry) who are used to asking what the actual mechanism is; and one is an entomologist who thinks the whole premise is soft. I've got twenty minutes plus questions. I'm a philosopher by training, hired as a program officer last year, and they already half suspect I'm trying to smuggle a philosophy project past them on a biology budget.

Here's the shape of what I'm arguing, so you know what to aim at.

The scale setup: insect farming is scaling fast (black soldier fly, mealworms, that whole sector), my slide says order of magnitude 10^18 insects alive at any one time, and so even a tiny credence in valence -- I use 1 in 1000 as the headline number -- times those numbers beats anything on the vertebrate side per dollar.

But I'm not going to ask them to act on that. My first move is that the expected-value framing is unusable here, because the output is basically linear in a credence nobody can defend, so the number on the slide is just the guess wearing a costume. Worse, the reasoning doesn't stop: applied consistently it pushes past insects to mites and nematodes and soil microfauna and eventually to speculative digital minds, so it never tells you where to spend, it only tells you to keep moving the target. I've been calling this the regress problem for scale arguments.

So: buy information, not action. Cheap experiments -- does the response outlast the stimulus, are there motivational tradeoffs, does the animal trade a cost for analgesia, that kind of marker battery -- can swing the credence by orders of magnitude for a rounding error next to what a single campaign costs.

And the bit I think is actually new, and that nobody on the committee will have heard: even if the marker work comes back positive, the scale argument still might not run, because welfare aggregation is normally over the lives of persisting subjects and it's a live possibility that insects have momentary affective states with no diachronic unity -- no one whose life it is. If that's right the unit isn't the insect-year but the experience-moment, and whether that makes the numbers bigger or smaller depends on how fine-grained the moments are, which is a question nobody has priced. I call it the unit-of-aggregation problem. My ask therefore has two gates: fund measurement first, and treat the unit question as upstream of the scale question rather than a detail to be settled later.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get, in the order they're likely to land, and phrase each one the way that person would actually say it -- the finance version is different from the poultry scientist's version is different from the entomologist's, and I need to hear the difference. For each: a reply I can give in about two minutes, one thing I should concede outright so it doesn't look like I'm dodging, one thing I should refuse to concede, and a note on where my reply is weakest. Please skip the philosophy-seminar stuff -- no other minds problem, no what is it like to be a bat -- that's not this room, and I can handle it if someone goes there. Do include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks. Also: no citations, no papers, no reading suggestions; I can't look anything up before tuesday and I'd only get it wrong.
