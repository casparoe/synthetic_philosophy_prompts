I'm about a year into a PhD in philosophy and I'm trying to decide what to actually spend the next three years on. My background is logic plus some probability and decision theory, almost no empirical ML, and I'd like the dissertation to be the kind of thing someone building real systems could use — systems that have to take soft or uncertain inputs from other systems: one model's confidence scores feeding another model, a human reading a classifier's output, a forecast aggregator taking probabilities off a panel of forecasters, sensor fusion, that sort of thing. So I keep circling back to Jeffrey conditionalization and the whole cluster of problems around updating on uncertain evidence, and I really don't trust myself to tell which threads there are worth years and which are dead ends that just feel deep.

Heres roughly how I see the landscape, and please tell me where I'm wrong. (1) The rigidity or invariance condition: the rule needs the conditional probability of your hypothesis given each cell of the partition to stay put, and people worry both that this is too strong and that it's secretly just conditioning on something else. Underneath that there's the question of whether soft updating is eliminable at all — whether you can always redescribe what happened as plain conditioning on some richer proposition, or as a likelihood ratio, in which case the special rule does no real work. (2) Order-dependence: sequences of soft updates commute on the same partition and generally don't across different ones, and I can't tell if that's a bug to be fixed, a sign that the real input to an update should have been a whole package at once, or just an artifact of asking the wrong question. (3) The minimum-information / minimum-relative-entropy alternatives to Jeffrey's rule, and the Judy Benjamin style case where the most conservative update gives an answer that looks obviously wrong because the cells of the partition are themselves evidentially loaded. (4) Then everything about where the numbers on the partition come from in the first place — whether any of these rules has anything to say about an input that is itself unjustified, biased, or produced by a channel you have independent reason to distrust.

What I actually want is an ordering and a defense of it. Say you're me: one person, three years, logic-and-probability tools, and the goal is that the output changes how somebody who isn't a philosopher thinks about soft evidence — either by giving them a usable normative standard for systems that ingest uncertain evidence, or by showing that the standard they're implicitly using (some minimum-information rule, some just-treat-confidences-as-probabilities-and-multiply heuristic) is broken in a way they'd care about. Which two or three of those threads, or ones I've missed, go first, and why: what's upstream of what, which are genuinely tractable for someone with my toolkit instead of needing empirical work, which are crowded or basically settled, and which have an importance that's mostly philosophical self-image? I'd also like to know which alleged implications I should stop believing. I keep hearing that getting the right rule for uncertain evidence is what makes or brakes Bayesian treatments of perceptual experience, or of scientific confirmation, or of whether you should defer to an expert's probability — and I can't tell which of those really turn on the fine details of the update rule and which come out the same under basically any sane rule.

Two things I've been chewing on that nobody I ask has a canned answer for, in case they're worth more than my garbage intuition. Call the first partition misalignment: in every case I actually care about, the partition the evidence comes packaged in is chosen by whoever built the evidence source, not by me — the classifier gives probabilities over its label set, the sensor gives a soft verdict over its bins, the forecaster answers their question — and then I have to update on a partition that cuts across the propositions my decision problem is really about. Nothing in the textbook setup says what to do when the imposed partition doesn't line up with yours, or what it costs to transport a constraint from one partition to another. Call the second rigidity drift: even when rigidity is fine for a single update, a running system does long chains of soft updates, and the conditional probabilities I'm supposed to be holding fixed are slowly drifting because the world is changing and because each update is on a slightly different partition, and the errors don't look like they cancel. Is there a principled bound on how bad that gets, a way to design the partition or an audit so the drift stays bounded, or a reason to think this is an engineering nuisance rather than a conceptual problem? I genuinely can't tell whether these are the two most important open things in the area or two things I invented because I don't know the literature well enough, so say so if it's the latter.
