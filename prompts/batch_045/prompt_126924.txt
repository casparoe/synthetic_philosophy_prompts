I sit on my city's transit accessibility advisory committee, and I keep noticing that our discussions quietly assume a philosophy of technology that nobody on the committee has ever actually argued for. Help me dig it out.

Here's the practice. Over the last twenty-odd years transit agencies moved from high-floor buses with a wheelchair lift that the driver had to leave the cab and operate, to low-floor buses with a ramp that deploys at the push of a button and that the rider can in principle manage without asking anyone for anything. Everyone involved talks about the second arrangement as a moral improvement and not just a mechanical one: boarding is supposed to be a thing you're entitled to, not a favour a stranger can be grumpy about, and the ramp is supposed to have made it that way by building the entitlement into the vehicle. Three wrinkles I can't stop thinking about.

1. The ramp fails. It gets salted up in winter, the sensors go out of alignment, and riders get stranded at the stop. Under the old setup a driver could (and often did) just help someone up the steps. Now there's a liability rule and a union work-rule that basically keep the driver in the seat, so when the artifact breaks the accommodation vanishes entirely instead of reverting to a human.
2. Deploying the ramp takes about forty seconds, and forty seconds counts against the agency's on-time performance metric, which is what dispatch actually monitors and what drivers get talked to about. So there's a second artifact in the story — the scheduling and monitoring system — whose politics points the other way, and which one wins is settled by what's enforced, not by what's bolted to the floor.
3. Alongside all this we still run paratransit: door-to-door, flexible, but you have to book a day ahead and accept a pickup window so wide it makes spontaneous trips impossible. The flexible service is segregated; the integrated service is rigid. Same agency, same riders, two opposite commitments.

So: what does this way of doing things presuppose? I think it presupposes that political content can be moved out of people's attitudes and into material arrangements, and that this move is an improvement — but I want that spelled out properly. Specifically:

- Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does the practice actually require the Winner-style claim that artifacts can bear political properties themselves, or does it only require the weaker claim that artifacts are good levers for managing human behaviour, with all the politics still living in the law, the contract and the driver? I've read the 1980 Daedalus essay, so don't just summarise it at me, and please don't reach for the parkway overpasses or the mechanical tomato harvester — we've done those to death and they don't touch the failure case I care about.
- Tell me what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely sits well with. Is it underdetermined between the artifact-has-politics view and a social-constructivist or neutral-tool view that says the ramp's meaning is fixed entirely by the regime around it? What would a defender of the deflationary view say is really doing the work here?
- Is there an inconsistency between the non-discretionary design on the fixed-route bus and the discretionary, means-tested, advance-booking paratransit standing right next to it, or can one theory license both without straining?
- Finally, the test case: the agency has money for exactly one fix — either a maintenance and driver-assist regime that guarantees a human fallback whenever the ramp fails, or a rebuilt scheduling metric that stops punishing the forty seconds. Which one does each rival view recommend, and what would it mean for the artifact-has-politics claim if the second fix turns out to matter more than the first?

I'm hazy on the exact regulations and I'm probably wrong about some of the maintenance details, so please argue from the setup as I've described it rather than from the real-world rulebook.
