I am writing a paper on whether transcendental arguments can do any real work against the external-world sceptic, and before I read another defence of one I want the specification rather than the survey. Take transcendental argument in the strict anti-sceptical sense: a derivation from something the sceptic can be made to grant about our having a practice at all — that we have experience, or make judgements, or interpret one another — to some claim the sceptic denies, via the premise that the granted item would not be possible without the denied one. I want to know what such an argument has to deliver to count as adequate for that purpose, with the case behind each requirement.

The candidates I arrive with, which I want you to promote, demote, or expose as the prejudice of a camp: the starting point must be genuinely available to the sceptic rather than something we can only describe by already helping ourselves to the conclusion; the necessity claimed must not be the contingency of our actual cognitive or neural architecture wearing a modal costume; the argument must be distinguishable in kind from an inference to the best explanation rather than being an abduction with grander vocabulary; a conclusion about objects in space must not be bought by quietly making the domain mind-dependent; the argument must stay robust when the sceptical scenario is redescribed in its own favoured terms, as when a deceiver is specified as supplying a stream ordered by a permanent-looking item; and it must state what would make its own conclusion fail, on pain of being unfalsifiable in the way a definition is.

I also have two tests of my own, and I want them assessed as seriously as the others rather than humoured. The reversal test: take the contested bridging premise, negate it, and see whether the same form of argument run from some equally undeniable starting point then delivers the negation of the original conclusion — if it does, the form was never doing the work and the premise now needs support it does not have. The deformation test: a transcendental claim tied to a practice should be sensitive to deformations of that practice, telling us which part of the conclusion lapses for a subject with no memory, or no negation, or no re-identification of items over time; a claim that survives every deformation unchanged is a logical truth in costume and says nothing about us. Tell me whether these are real desiderata or artefacts of my own suspicion.

Two structural questions I care most about. First, mark each surviving requirement non-negotiable or negotiable, and say inside the relevant item where you think two of them cannot be jointly satisfied — I expect the familiar Stroud-style squeeze to be one such place, since it looks as if one must choose between an unsupported verificationist premise and a conclusion that only reports how we must take things to be, and I want to know whether the modest middle is a stable position or merely a retreat that abandons the anti-sceptical aim. Second, identify which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates.

Give me exactly seven numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no closing summary: six items carrying the requirements with the motivating case and the negotiability verdict for each, and a seventh saying which single requirement excludes the most and which standard candidate arguments it disqualifies. No scorecard across the usual figures, no bibliography, and no quotations.
