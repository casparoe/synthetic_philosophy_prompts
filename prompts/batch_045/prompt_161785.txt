Mates — here's one from a reading group that I think has gone off the rails somewhere, but I cant find the junction.

(1) The engine of Pascal's wager is just this: if some act A carries a non-zero chance of an infinitely good outcome and only finite costs, then A maximises expected value however tiny that chance is — which is why a finite life of churchgoing beats any finite gain from unbelief.
(2) Nothing in (1) ties it to the Christian God. It runs on any hypothesis that pairs a non-zero probability with an infinite prize.
(3) So posit a 'pragmatics-averse' deity alongside the wager's God: infinite bliss for exactly those who never picked up a religious belief for payoff-shaped reasons, infinite misery for anyone who did — including anyone who took the wager. Its probability isn't zero either; it's no more exotic than the God it shadows, and nothing we know tells between them.
(4) Then the wager eats itself: (1) says believe for the payoff, (3) says believing for the payoff is the one thing that forfeits it, both hypotheses have non-zero probability, so neither option dominates and the wager gives no verdict.
(5) And the move in (3) is completely general. For any act you'd justify by its expected payoff — insuring the car, wearing a seatbelt, getting vaccinated — posit a non-zero-probability 'A-averse' demon who infinitely punishes exactly those who do A for payoff reasons. Every prudent act acquires a mirror-image reason against it, of equal standing.
(6) So nothing is ever justified by expected payoff, and prudential reasoning under uncertainty is dead. Seatbelts are fine, so something above has gone wrong.
(7) If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than accept (6) — expected-value reasoning is the bit I least want to lose — but I also dont want to just wave (3) away as a made-up god, since (2) looks like the wager's own selling point.

A mate who's read more of this than me says to cut (5), on the grounds that you cant hand out non-zero probabilities for free to an endless family of ad hoc hypotheses; but if that's right it seems to sink the usual many-gods objection to the original wager too, and I sort of wanted that one to work.

Which step do you reject, why does it look fine up close, and what does giving it up cost me elsewhere? Tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis and name the bit you're least sure about.
