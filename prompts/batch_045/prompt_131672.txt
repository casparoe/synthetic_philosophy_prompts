I hold the following position about climate policy design, and I want its exposure mapped. Call a policy substitution-shaped when it changes which good or process satisfies a demand whose size it leaves roughly intact — a carbon price that moves generation from coal to gas, or that moves cement production toward a jurisdiction with weaker rules — and destruction-shaped when it removes the demand's object outright, so that the expenditure and the material throughput it would have caused do not occur at all: a moratorium on new private-aviation infrastructure, an absolute annual cap on floor area permitted, a ban on a product class with no drop-in substitute. My position is that, for a wealthy democracy whose binding problem is cumulative emissions rather than static cost-effectiveness, the destruction-shaped instrument is underrated and the carbon tax overrated, and not for the usual leakage reasons alone. The three claims doing the work are: that most of what a carbon price actually buys is substitution; that a destruction-shaped rule generates what I will stipulatively call negative leakage, since demand removed rather than rerouted destroys some of the material flow and expenditure that would otherwise have gone somewhere, so the enacting jurisdiction's measured reduction understates the global effect; and that such rules have a durability advantage, because they operate by changing what gets built, and built capital resists repeal in a way that a statutory price does not.

Tell me what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and say for each whether a defender of the view should concede it as a genuine cost or is entitled to dismiss it as special pleading. I want you to address: (a) what empirical pattern, in the jurisdictions that have actually priced carbon or actually imposed absolute quantity restrictions or moratoria, would show either that substitution-shaped policies do produce destruction after all or that my negative-leakage term is too small to matter, naming the real cases your argument is leaning on; (b) whether the durability claim survives the fact that quantity restrictions are typically written with exemptions, credit trading, and sunset provisions that reintroduce substitution through the back door; (c) whether the substitution/destruction distinction is doing independent work or merely redescribing inelastic demand and general-equilibrium price effects in new vocabulary; and (d) whether the qualifier "for a wealthy democracy whose binding problem is cumulative emissions" has insulated the view into emptiness, and whether that would be a fault in a claim of this kind. I would personally be most moved by an argument that the two instrument types converge in general equilibrium, and least moved by the charge that destruction-shaped policies are simply politically unavailable.
