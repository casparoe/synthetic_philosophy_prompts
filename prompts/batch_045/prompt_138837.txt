I was an experimental chemist before I retired and have recently started reading philosophy of physics, so forgive me if I am missing something standard. What I keep finding is this: either the substantivalist and the relationist agree on every observable quantity and differ only in how they describe the same observation, or they are made to disagree by spinning the entire star sphere around a stationary bucket — a manoeuvre no one can perform, and one that tells me nothing about the disagreement I actually care about.

So: design me a case where they disagree observationally without that manoeuvre. Concretely, a scenario in which a substantivalist predicts a flat water surface in a stationary bucket and a Machian relationist predicts a curved one (or the reverse direction, if that is the one you can genuinely construct — what I insist on is that the predictions differ, not that they differ my way). Constraints:

(i) The manipulation must be local. You may not translate, rotate or boost the whole material contents of the universe, or any large part of it; whatever you do happens in a laboratory-sized region over minutes, and the distant matter is left as it is.
(ii) No appeal to absolute position or absolute velocity. Both sides already agree those do no work, and a case resting on them is no advance.
(iii) Any matter whose relations the relationist invokes must be detectable in principle — no unobservable masses doing the work by stipulation.
(iv) If your device is a rotating room or a massive shell, note that a featureless sphere spinning about its axis is not rotating by anyone's lights, and say how you keep the shell's ordinary gravitational and mechanical effects on the water from explaining the result.
(v) Stay within classical mechanics if you can. If you need general relativity — a very dense rotating shell, frame-dragging, that sort of thing — keep it qualitative: no coefficients, nothing I could not find in a good textbook.
(vi) The verdicts must be generated by the theories. If your relationist's prediction diverges only because you have built a distance-weighted rule into the definition of relationism, say so plainly, and say whether the case survives a less loaded version.

After the scenario: give each side's prediction and the reasoning that produces it; isolate the single feature doing the work; show me what the case becomes if that feature is removed; and then give the losing side's best reply — in particular, whether she can plausibly say that this is only Newton's bucket with the star-sphere trick scaled down to laboratory size, and therefore no progress at all.

The case is for settling whether this dispute is empirical or merely verbal, which is why a scenario that restates one observation in two vocabularies would miss the point. And if within these constraints no such case exists — if the dispute really is observationally empty — I would rather be told that, with the reason, than be handed a case whose verdict has been quietly arranged.
