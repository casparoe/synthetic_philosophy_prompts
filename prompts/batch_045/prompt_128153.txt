I'm a journalist who writes about war, international law, and the vocabulary we use for both. I'm at work on a long-form piece about the quarrel that now reliably follows every mass atrocity: survivors and their advocates fight to have it called a genocide, while foreign ministries and their legal advisers reach for cooler words — ethnic cleansing, crimes against humanity, a campaign of terror, a tragedy. I'd like you to write the analytical spine of that piece: an essay that works through the strongest arguments on both sides of the naming question and then commits to a verdict about what the word should mean and who should get to use it.

A few ground rules, because of how I'll use the result. Argue from principle and from examples you construct yourself. Do not cite cases, statutes, treaties by number, or legal and academic writers by name, and do not rest anything on details a reader would have to look up: I want an argument I could hand to a smart general reader, not a brief. Assume no training in law, define every term of art at first use, and write in American English in prose meant for a magazine — concrete nouns, no throat-clearing, no bullet lists inside the body of a section, and no abstract of the whole thing before you begin.

Here is the background as I understand it, together with some apparatus I've been developing in my notebooks; take both as given, and tell me in passing if either seems confused. Lemkin coined the word by joining a Greek root for a people or tribe to a Latin root for killing, because he wanted a name for a crime aimed at the existence of a group rather than at the individuals who compose it. The convention he campaigned for, and that states adopted in the aftermath of the Second World War, came out narrower than his idea: it listed a handful of specified destructive acts, and it required what lawyers call a special or specific intent — not merely killing on a vast scale, but aiming at the destruction of the group as such, in whole or in substantial part. Proposals to cover the deliberate destruction of a group's culture, language, and institutions were pressed and were largely set aside. So the legal category is narrow and intent-centered, while in the public imagination it has become supreme: it is the word that means the worst thing.

Three notions I want you to work with. First, the destructive footprint: not what the perpetrator hoped for, but the difference the conduct actually makes to the prospects of the group — its numbers, its cohesion, its capacity to hand itself on. Second, the attrition test, which I want considered as a rival to the intent requirement: an episode counts as genocidal when its destructive footprint foreseeably cuts short the group's persistence as a distinguishable group — say, when it makes it likely that a group which would otherwise have persisted for roughly two more generations will not — whether or not anyone aimed at that outcome. Third, the problem of archival selection: the suspicion that a standard turning on provable intent systematically favors tidy, bureaucratic, paperwork-generating atrocities over ones that destroy through dispersal, neglect, market pressure, environmental ruin, or the accumulated acts of many unconnected hands.

The question the essay should answer: should the concept keep its narrow, intent-centered shape, with other and less freighted words carrying the remaining moral load — or should it be re-centered on consequences, in something like the attrition test, with its institutional uses (prosecution, diplomatic pressure, the triggering of outside duties, the claims of survivors on memory and repair) uncoupled from the label?

Please organize the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. "The Stakes" (about 250 words) — why the naming fight is not merely semantic: what the label does, outside the courtroom, for victims, for bystanders, and for perpetrators deciding how to document their own conduct.

2. "The Case for Keeping the Category Narrow" (about 400 words) — the best available defense of the intent requirement and of a restricted vocabulary: what a mental-element standard protects, why the distinctive wrong might genuinely be the aim rather than the outcome, and what is lost if the word thins out.

3. "The Case Against Intent" (about 400 words) — press the other side, including the archival-selection worry and the position of a group dissolved by policies that nobody designed to destroy it.

4. "Attrition and Its Discontents" (about 350 words) — take the attrition test seriously as a proposal and then attack it: what it would sweep in, what it cannot measure, how any court could administer a counterfactual about a group that would have existed, and whether it quietly converts every misfortune that thins a people into a crime.

5. "Two Cases" (about 300 words) — invent and work through two stylized episodes: one that the intent-centered definition misses and the attrition test catches, and one that the attrition test catches but that you think should not count. Use them as tests of the theories, not as decoration.

6. "My Verdict" (about 350 words) — take a position on the question above and defend it without retreating into "it depends." If your answer is a split one — a narrow legal category alongside a separate public or political vocabulary, for instance — say precisely how the split is meant to work in practice, and what a prosecutor and a newsroom editor should each do differently on Monday morning because of it.
