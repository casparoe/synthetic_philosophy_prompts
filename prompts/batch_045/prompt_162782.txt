I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I have been invited to give the closing keynote at a conference on societal collapse next spring. The working title is something like "The Grain of the Evidence," and I want to be ready for the questions.

My thesis has two parts.

First, a measurement claim. What gets called a collapse is very often an artifact of the resolution at which the surviving record is sampled. A society that stops building in stone, stops keeping central accounts, or redistributes itself from a few large settlements into many small ones does not thereby become worse off or less complex; it becomes less legible to whoever is looking at it five hundred or three thousand years later. Separate the fate of a population from the fate of its most durable and most archive-friendly institutions — palaces, temples, monument-building, literacy — and a good deal of what is described as collapse in the standard cases (the eastern Mediterranean at the end of the Bronze Age, the Maya lowlands, Rapa Nui) turns out to be a change in the grain of what survives rather than an established fall in welfare or in organizational complexity. Call this the legibility fallacy: reading a drop in the resolution of the evidence as a drop in the thing evidenced.

Second, an identity claim. We have no agreed criteria of identity for a society over time — nothing like an answer to the question of how much institutional, demographic, and spatial continuity has to hold before we are dealing with the same society transformed rather than one society replaced by its successors. Until that question is answered, the choice between it fell and it changed into something else is underdetermined by the evidence, and is settled instead by whatever contrast the writer wants to draw. Which means collapse talk is doing rhetorical work it does not advertise.

The room will be roughly two hundred archaeologists and anthropologists, perhaps a third of them specialists in one of the three cases I discuss, plus a few historians of environmental thought and two people who work professionally on climate communication. Their likely commitments: they have spent two decades pushing back on popular accounts that turn their sites into moral fables about ecological suicide, and they will initially assume I am there to write another one; several of them think collapse is a perfectly serviceable and genuinely important category and that transformation language is a euphemism that flatters the present; the specialists will each be protecting a specific evidentiary record against a generalizer; and the climate-communication people will want to know whether my argument gives ammunition to people who say nothing much is at stake.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet for this exact room. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get — not the objections a philosopher or an online audience would raise — ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, and tell me which subgroup each one comes from. Phrase each objection as that person would phrase it, in their vocabulary. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes without notes, plus a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes of the talk itself, before anyone asks anything, so that the room stops bracing for the wrong argument. And include the one question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it concerns how I would ever establish that people were better off after the record goes quiet, but tell me if you think the real danger lies elsewhere.
