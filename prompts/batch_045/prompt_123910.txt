I sit on the ethics committee of a mid-sized community hospital, and after about a year of cases I have noticed that my votes do not line up with any rule I am able to state out loud. I would like help finding the rule, or finding out that I do not have one. I am not asking what the law here requires; I know that, and it settles less than people expect. I want the moral principle. Here are the cases as I remember them, with how I voted.

(1) Marta, 34, bipolar I, three hospitalizations, each following her decision to stop lithium during a manic episode. During a stable period she signed a document asking that if she became manic and refused medication, she be treated anyway, including against her will. Six months later she was manic, sleeping two hours a night, had spent her savings, and said she had never felt better and that lithium was poison. I voted to treat her. This is the case I am most confident about: the person who signed it seems to me simply to have been thinking more clearly than the person objecting.

(2) Mrs. H., 74, moderate Alzheimer's. She recognizes no one, speaks almost nothing, but smiles, eats well, sings along with songs from her twenties, and appears content. At 62, after sitting through her sister's death in intensive care, she signed a directive refusing artificial feeding, ventilators, and antibiotics for infection if she ever had advanced dementia. She now has pneumonia. Her daughter asks for a course of antibiotics, which would almost certainly return her to exactly the state I have described. I voted to treat.

(3) A man in his late thirties, unconscious after a road accident, intubated in intensive care. A directive surfaces that he signed at 26, during what his family describes as a long untreated depression — he was never declared incompetent and was working full time at the time — refusing life-sustaining treatment of any kind. His wife says he has been well for a decade, that the document does not reflect him, and that he would want to be treated. I voted to treat.

(4) Mr. R., 58, in a persistent vegetative state after an anoxic brain injury. Two years earlier he had written, and told his family repeatedly, that he wanted everything done for as long as there was any chance, and he gave a religious reason: life is a gift that is not his to end. His wife and two adult children ask that treatment stop; they say this is not what he would have wanted, that he never pictured this. I voted to stop. This is the vote I am least comfortable with, since I am overriding an explicit, competently made wish in favor of a family's judgment about a patient who cannot speak.

(5) Mr. D., 71, advanced dementia, cheerful, apparently free of distress, with a urinary infection. His directive, written at 55, says in effect: I have read that people with dementia often seem content and report good quality of life. I believe that, and I still do not want that life. What made my life mine was following my own thoughts and being able to share them with the people I love; a life without that is not one I want prolonged by treating infections or by tube feeding. I voted to withhold antibiotics — with far more hesitation than in (1), and I cannot say why my votes in (2) and (5) differ, since the two patients' situations look to me the same and the outcomes I am choosing are opposite.

(6) Ms. K., 46, terminal ovarian cancer, entirely competent, a devout Catholic for six years. At 28 she signed a directive refusing heroic measures; she never revoked it. She now wants a second surgery and intensive care if it comes to that, and says that suffering accepted willingly has meaning. Her brother says the document should stand and that her conversion is a product of fear. I voted for her current wishes.

My tentative principle is this: an advance directive binds just when the person who wrote it is the person who will have to live through — or die through — what it asks for. Marta will come back and be the one who bears the outcome of being medicated, so her document binds. Mr. R. will not be there to bear anything, so his does not. But (5) does not fit my own principle: Mr. D. is not the one living through the infection either, and I still voted to honor him.

What I would like from you: state a principle precisely enough that I could take it into a meeting, clause by clause, with the case that forces each clause. Test mine against the six cases and repair it if it can be repaired. Say plainly which of my votes a defensible principle would force me to give up, and which vote you think is the weakest. Then give me its verdicts on three cases I have not put to it: a demented patient whose apparent contentment is the only evidence of her wishes and who never signed anything; a directive whose enforcement would commit a family to years of caregiving they cannot afford; and a directive written before a treatment existed that now substantially improves the outlook it was written against. Finally, tell me whether the principle you land on has to take a position on whether the earlier self and the later self are the same person — and if it does, whether it can be restated so that it does not have to.
