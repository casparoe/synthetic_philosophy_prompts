I run the weekly eighty-minute discussion section attached to my seminar on nineteenth-century European philosophy, and this week we're on Kierkegaard's attack on the press and on what he calls the public. I have fourteen to eighteen students, a seminar room with a whiteboard and a projector but no wifi, and one session. The reading was his long 1846 review-essay on the present age: the pages on levelling, on an age that is reflective and without passion, on the press as the thing that makes a public possible in the first place, together with his claim that truth is had only by the single individual and never by an aggregate of people. I do not want a lecture and I do not want a seminar discussion of the reading. Last week I simply asked them whether he was right and got twenty minutes of "social media is bad," which misses almost everything: they heard his complaint as being that the press circulates bad opinions, when the interesting complaint is that the press brings into existence a new kind of entity, a public, which is no one in particular, is therefore answerable for nothing, and yet has more power over each of them than any one of them has over it — and that joining it costs you your singularity. That is what I want them to feel in their gut rather than nod at.

So: design a runnable activity in which the students themselves build the thing and then discover what they have built. Constraints. No mini-lecture up front, and please don't let the facilitator use the words levelling, the public, or the crowd until the debrief. My group will bolt straight for Twitter if you let them, so I'd rather the medium in the room be something physical, slow, and operated by them — a one-page newspaper they produce in the session, a tally on the board, index cards — so that the mechanism is something they can watch operating rather than something they assume. By the end they should have produced an artifact (a headline, a percentage, a one-sentence statement of "what the class thinks") that no individual wrote, that no individual fully endorses, and that they then have to do something with — ratify it, or refuse it, or be stuck between the two. Rough ideas I've been toying with, which you should improve on or replace: everyone first writes a considered private judgment on a genuinely hard case; the judgments are collected, aggregated into a number and a sentence, and read back to them as their own view; a second pass redoes the same question with signed judgments read aloud in turn, so they can compare the two outputs and notice that the signed version is slower, milder, and less decisive, which cuts against the reading of Kierkegaard as merely wanting sincerity; and some small incentive — points, or weight in a later vote — for getting your phrasing into the published statement even though the statement will be attributed to nobody. Please also tell me whether there should be an editorial team and whether it should be anonymous, since I suspect that matters.

What I need: the rules; the materials; a minute-by-minute timeline for the eighty minutes; any roles and the scoring scheme if there is one; what the facilitator does and pointedly does not say while it runs; and a debrief with three or four specific moments from the activity mapped onto specific claims in the reading. Then be honest with me about two things. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I can already see three risks: someone refuses to state a private view at all, the aggregate comes out so lopsided that nothing strange is visible, and someone guesses where this is heading in the first ten minutes and says so out loud — and how I should handle each. Second, which part of his view the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in my own voice. My worry is that it will only ever show that anonymity makes people irresponsible, which is banal and is not his point; if your design blocks that reading, say explicitly how it does, and say plainly if it doesn't.
