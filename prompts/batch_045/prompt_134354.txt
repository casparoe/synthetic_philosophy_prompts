I'm working on a feature about credibility — the who-gets-believed beat — and I've got a pile of cases where I trust my gut and no idea what my gut is actually doing. Cases first, verdicts as I have them:

1. A nurse flags a dosage error to a senior consultant, he waves her off, the patient has a bad night. Injustice, obviously.
2. A source who has lied to me twice before calls with a tip. I discount him. Not injustice, I don't feel bad at all.
3. A junior researcher, non-native English speaker, gets accused of having AI-written her grant proposal because the prose is suspiciously clean. Nobody disputes her facts — they just stop trusting that she wrote it. This feels like case 1, which surprised me, because nobody doubted a single thing she asserted.
4. An insurance adjuster runs a fraud model that's well calibrated on average, but the inputs track neighborhood and income, and the claimant is a Somali immigrant with a busted boiler. Nobody in the chain is prejudiced, the number just comes out low. I want to say injustice, but I can't point at a person who did it.
5. A woman in 1996 describes symptoms we'd now file under a recognized chronic condition; her GP concludes she's exaggerating. I want to call this a harm, but not the GP's fault — and swapping the woman for a rich white man wouldn't have helped.
6. We ran a first-person piece mostly because of who the writer was, and it turned out she'd embellished. Bad journalism, something is off, but I resist calling it an injustice to her in the way case 1 is an injustice.
7. A witness is a bit drunk. I discount him. Fine, obviously fine.

My rough rule so far has two clauses: a swap test — if the credibility would have been higher had a different kind of person said the same thing in the same way, and the difference traces back to something identity-ish about the speaker, it's a case of epistemic injustice — plus a fault clause, somebody has to have done something wrong. But 4 and 5 break the fault clause and I don't want to drop them, 6 isn't a deficit at all so the swap test has nothing to say to it, and 3 makes me think the swap test is tracking the wrong quantity: its about authorship, not credibility in the normal sense. Also 2 and 4 are the same shape — an accurate, identity-correlated downgrade — and I have opposite feelings about them.

The one I keep going back and forth on: when I interview someone whose English is halting, I paraphrase instead of quoting them, to protect them from looking bad in print, and I never do this for the fluent people I talk to. Protective or patronizing? Genuinely can't tell.

Labels I've been using in my notes, use them or junk them: testimonial smog for case 4, nobody's fault and ambient; synthetic doubt for case 3; credibility laundering for when a claim only lands after someone credentialed repeats it back.

What I need is the sharpest principle you can state that saves as many of my verdicts as possible, an honest list of the ones it can't save, and what it says about the paraphrase case. Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no sign-off — it's for a sidebar and I've got about ninety words of space.
