I volunteer on this county sentencing-advisory panel (we write non-binding recommendations on local sentencing, diversion programs, that kind of thing), and one of the other members, a retired cop named Dale, wraps up pretty much every discussion with "the punishment should fit the crime," like that settles it. The thing is he uses it in ways that feel like they pull in opposite directions, and I can't tell if he's being inconsistent or if I'm just missing what the sentence means.

Last month we talked about a proposed mandatory minimum for a nonviolent property offense — repeat shoplifting, maybe $400 worth of stuff total — and Dale was against it. Five years just doesn't fit that crime, he said, you're blowing it way out of proportion. I agreed with him, obviously. But then a week later we discussed whether a guy convicted of a DUI that put somebody in the hospital should be allowed to serve his six months as house arrest in his own fairly nice house instead of in the county jail, and Dale said no — "doesn't fit the crime" again — even though on paper it was the exact same six months everyone else with that charge does. And someone brought up two guys who did basically the same drunk driving, except one of them hit a pedestrian and killed her and the other got lucky and hit nothing, and Dale said the punishment definatly shouldn't be the same for those two. Which I sort of get, but it also seems weird to punish someone harder because of how the world happened to turn out. And once, on a fraud case, someone suggested freezing the guy's assets in a way that mirrors what he did to his victims, and Dale loved that too — "fits the crime" in what felt like a totally different sense, like the punishment is supposed to look like the offense instead of just be as bad as it.
