I'm setting one of the questions for our PhD comprehensives this year — philosophy of mind / ethics of AI, 90 minutes in the room, no notes. Topic is the attribution problem: which computations matter morally, and where exactly sentience stops.

Give them a principle and make them earn it. Call it the Minimal Implementation Rule: when a physical system implements several computations at once, the only ones that matter morally are those that are minimal among the sentience-sufficient computations the system implements — minimal in the sense that no proper sub-computation of them would itself be sufficient for the same valence-bearing states. So the "same network plus an idle register bolted on" variants drop out, and we don't end up with a separate mind per notational variant.

I want them to (a) explain why any such rule is needed in the first place — why overgeneration is a genuine problem for views that pin moral status to implemented computations rather than to substrate; (b) say what the rule fails to fix: no guarantee of a unique minimum, incommensurable minimal candidates, gerrymandered tiny computations, and the fact that under permissive views of implementation even a rock implements the minimal ones, so minimality has to be paired with some constraint on what counts as implementing at all; (c) deal with the labeling gap — formal causal structure doesn't by itself settle which states are pleasant and which unpleasant, and minimality inherits that; (d) apply it to a boundary case: a model-free RL critic carrying a scalar reward-prediction-error signal and nothing else, no world model, no recurrence — what the rule delivers, whether that's the right verdict, and what has to be added if it isn't.
