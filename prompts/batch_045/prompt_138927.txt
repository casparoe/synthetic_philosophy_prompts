I advise the disputes committee of a national trade-union federation. The committee decides whether an affiliate's proposed tactic counts as sanctioned industrial action — that is, action eligible for federation strike pay, for defence costs, and for solidarity action by other affiliates. Its remit is political and ethical rather than legal: please reason from its own track record and from principles about what a labour movement ought to be doing, and do not appeal to the labour law of any particular jurisdiction. Its options in any case are to sanction, to sanction subject to stated conditions, or to decline.

Here is the track record, with the ground given in each minute.

1. Sanitation workers: indefinite full withdrawal of labour, after a ballot and the notice the federation's rules require, over pay. Sanctioned — collective mandate, open declaration, and the cost borne by the strikers themselves in lost wages.
2. Rail maintenance staff: refusal of all non-contractual overtime together with scrupulous adherence to every written procedure in the safety manual, announced in advance and sustained for six weeks; several thousand cancellations followed. Sanctioned — workers are entitled to withhold what they were never obliged to give, and the resulting disruption is the employer's own understaffing coming home to roost.
3. Warehouse workers: a three-day unannounced walkout after management suspended a safety representative; no ballot, and the action fell inside a period covered by a no-strike undertaking. Declined — no collective mandate, and the employer was given no opportunity to prepare or respond. The minute adds that the same stoppage, had it been balloted and notified, would in all likelihood have been sanctioned.
4. Dockers: refusal to handle cargo from a plant where another union's members were locked out; no grievance of any kind against their own employer; balloted and notified. Sanctioned, narrowly, over a dissent — the dissent holds that the action conscripts an uninvolved employer and its customers into a dispute none of them can settle.
5. Insurance clerks: files processed at roughly sixty per cent of the normal rate while full hours were recorded and full pay drawn. Declined — taking pay for labour one is deliberately withholding misrepresents the exchange; the cost is shifted to the employer covertly rather than borne openly.
6. Hospital technicians: full withdrawal of labour, except that a skeleton service for sterilisation and dialysis was maintained under a carve-out agreed with management in advance and published. Sanctioned — the right to withdraw labour is compatible with a duty to avert grave harm to third parties, provided the carve-out is public and does not defeat the point of the action.
7. A one-day stoppage over a government immigration policy: no grievance against any employer, but balloted and notified. Declined — strike action is tied to the employment relation, and a general political dispute is to be pursued by other means.

The case now before us. Bus drivers and depot maintenance crews at a municipal operator, in a dispute over pay and rostering. They have balloted, and have given the required notice. What they propose is to strike intermittently: a series of stoppages of one to three hours, several per week, at times they will not disclose in advance, falling somewhere inside a three-day window that they will publish, continuing until the dispute is settled. They lose the pay for the hours they stop. The employer's submission is that it cannot plan around such a pattern: it must hold buses, depots and crews on standby across the whole window, it cannot tell passengers in advance which services will run, and it estimates the disruption per worker-hour lost at several times that of a continuous strike of the same total length. The union offers to keep a small number of routes serving the city's two hospitals running at all times, and to publicise that commitment.

What I want from you:

First, set out the general rules — at least three, each stated so that it could actually be applied to a fresh case — that fit the whole track record rather than only part of it. I strongly suspect the minutes overstate how much these verdicts settle: in case 3 the want of a ballot and the want of notice are confounded, and in case 5 dislike of covert cost-shifting and dislike of the tactic's deceptiveness are confounded. I need to know which feature is really carrying each verdict.

Second, say what each of those rules predicts about the bus dispute, and state precisely where the rules come apart.

Third, give me two verdicts and keep them separate: your best guess at what this committee will in fact do, and the verdict you would recommend as the one that best coheres with its own precedents. Identify the closest analog among cases 1 to 7 and say why — or tell me plainly that the precedents underdetermine the matter and why they do.

Fourth, if they do underdetermine it: state the principle the committee would be adopting under each of the live options, including any conditions you would attach to a conditional sanction, and say which of those principles it would least like to see printed as its own.

Fifth, name one further case — specified as concretely as the bus dispute is — whose verdict would most sharply separate the rules you have identified.

Sixth, and most important: tell me which single consideration would move you to the opposite verdict, and where the reasoning in your answer is most likely to be going wrong.

I would rather have seven hundred words that discriminate between rules than two thousand that survey the landscape.
