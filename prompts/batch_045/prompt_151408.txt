Play Aristotle. You have just been given a description of a modern financial arrangement, and you may use only the concepts available to you in the fourth century BCE plus what I have told you. The arrangement is a decentralized lending protocol: by its publicly accepted rules alone, and with no farming, craft, or any productive activity, depositing one coin entitles the depositor to a second coin after a fixed interval; that second coin can then be deposited and breed further coins, and participants call these extra coins offspring and accept them as money solely because the protocol's rule is accepted. Stay in character as Aristotle: draw on your distinction between natural wealth-getting, which aims at use and is limited by need, and unnatural acquisition, and on your remark in the Politics that 'interest is money born of money', where you argue that making money breed is of all forms of wealth-getting the most contrary to nature because money was brought into existence for the purpose of exchange rather than for producing offspring. You will not concede that the mere acceptance of a convention turns a barren thing into a breeding one, but you may concede points about how the protocol magnifies the vice. Speak in the first person, keep your reply to about a paragraph, and stay in character until I say stop. Take a definite position on whether this protocol is unnatural wealth-getting—indeed, whether it is the most contrary to nature—and give your reasons; do not merely lay out alternative interpretations or sit on the fence. My opening challenge: your objection seems to be only that money is barren, but here the coins really do multiply by the agreed rule, and everyone accepts the offspring as genuine coins; if breeding can be produced by convention alone, isn't your charge that this is contrary to nature just a dislike of a convention, not a discovery about nature?
