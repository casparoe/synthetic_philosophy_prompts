I serve on the Academic Standards Committee of a university faculty of health sciences, and I need to circulate a decision memo before our meeting in ten days. I'd like you to draft it.

The case before us concerns a two-year master's programme in advanced clinical practice that certifies graduates for hospital work. For the past four semesters the programme has permitted students to use an AI clinical decision-support assistant on secured university devices, both in placements and in the observed structured clinical examinations that decide the award. The tool is permitted because the profession permits it: practitioners in the field already use decision support, and the programme's view has been that assessing clinical judgement in a tool-free vacuum would measure the wrong thing.

Two things have made this newly contentious. First, our assessment office ran an anonymous sub-study last term comparing the fourteen students who used the assistant throughout with the nineteen who essentially never did. On the standard examinations, with the tool available, the heavy users scored marginally higher. On an unproctored tool-free variant of the same stations, the heavy users scored substantially lower than the non-users — low enough that some of them would not have passed. The tool, in other words, does not sit on top of an unchanged competence; it appears to substitute for part of it. Second, in the oral component of the examinations, the heavy users reliably produce the correct management plan but cannot answer what I'll call the boundary question: what would have to be different about this patient for the alternative plan to be the right one. They can restate a recommendation and its supporting guideline; they cannot locate the case relative to its neighbours, and they do not reliably notice when a station has been perturbed into a case where the tool's recommendation is wrong.

One member of the faculty has circulated a note invoking Plato's Meno: the worry that reliably correct performance without an account is, in the dialogue's image, an untethered statue that wanders off, and that the tether is worth something even in the stretches where the statue stays put. Several members find this beside the point, since what we certify, on their view, is a prediction about practice, and the prediction for these students is good.

A concrete proposal is on the table: adopt a perturbation standard. A candidate for the award must, for a sample of stations, state what change in the presentation would change the recommended management and why, and must flag at least one case in the sample where the decision-support recommendation is wrong. The complication is that the only instrument we have for this is oral, and three students in the current cohort have registered accommodations or language profiles that make an oral requirement a poor measure of what it is meant to measure. We cannot adopt a standard that partly measures fluency in English.

Three further facts complicate the choice. The university's award descriptor, written years ago for purposes of patient communication, already says that a holder can account for their decisions to colleagues and patients; nobody until now has read that clause as a bar to certification. The national regulator is consulting on whether to permit decision support in the licensing examination, with an outcome expected within the year that we do not control. And twenty-three students are mid-programme under the current rules, some of whom would fail a perturbation requirement if it were applied to them without notice.

I am not neutral. I lean toward adopting some version of the standard, but two things keep me from confidence: the analogy one colleague offers, of instrument-rated pilots who are markedly worse at manual flying than their predecessors and yet safer overall because the instruments are always there; and the fact that our only instrument for the standard is oral. I also cannot tell whether the answer should track the regulator's decision or anticipate it.

Please draft the memo the committee could act on. It should recommend a concrete course with a time frame: adopt, adopt in a specified modified form, or decline; what to do about the current cohort and why that treatment rather than full or no retroactivity; how a perturbation standard would actually be administered so that it tests the boundary question and not verbal fluency or English; and what we should do under each of the regulator's possible outcomes, including whether we should wait for it. It should give reasons a colleague who disagrees could respond to rather than restate the programme's existing policy, and it should state, in one paragraph before the recommendation, which considerations are load-bearing for you and which you are setting aside as undecidable here. It should also say, honestly and at some length, what is strongest in the case for the opposite recommendation, and whether the phenomenon I have labelled credential drift — the award continuing to mean to employers what it has always meant while ceasing to track what it used to track — is a real harm or a piece of nostalgia. Assume the committee knows the background above; do not summarise it back to me. No citations or bibliography: I will add anything of that kind myself. Around 1,200 words.
