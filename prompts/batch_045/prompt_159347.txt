So I had a three-hour argument with my friend last night about the thing where you wake up not knowing whether you're in the world with ten people or the world with ten billion — the self-sampling vs self-indication disagreement, and the presumptuous philosopher version of it. I'm the one who thinks your own existence is evidence that there are more observers; he thinks that's a cheat. His actual objection, the one I couldn't answer, was that self-indication only looks good because the scoring is per-observer: in the big world there are a hundred copies of you all placing the same bet and all winning, so of course the "existence is evidence" rule racks up points. He says that's rigged, and that if payoffs were per-world instead of per-copy, the advantage would evaporate and we'd see that self-sampling was right all along. I don't think it does evaporate, but I definately couldn't say why in the moment.

I run a fortnightly reading group — 12 to 14 people, 90 minutes, half philosophy majors half maths people, one whiteboard, no budget and no real money for stakes. He's coming to the next session and I want an activity where he has to feel the disagreement rather than argue past it. So: design me a runnable game.

What I want from it:
- Participants get assigned into worlds by a die roll and given an observer slot, they don't know which world they're in, and they have to commit to odds or a bet. No lecture up front, and nobody hears the two names until afterwards.
- One round where they get no information about which observer they are, and one where they're told their serial number is very early (like, they're observer #2) — that second one should flip the self-sampling people hard toward the small world while leaving the self-indication people back at the prior, and I want that crossover to happen in the room rather than on the whiteboard.
- Then run the same cases again with per-world scoring (a world's total winnings split among its observers, or otherwise normalised) instead of per-observer, so the group can see whether his objection actually kills the edge or just changes what's being measured. If the honest answer is that it depends on choices we have to make before we can even score the game, I want the activity to make that unavoidable.
- Concrete materials (dice, index cards, envelopes, chocolate, whatever), and enough repetitions that 13 people produce something other than noise. Tell me how many rounds that actually takes.
- A debrief that maps specific moments onto specific moves: where the per-observer vs per-world choice bites, where the early-serial-number flip comes from, and what each of us should concede.
- What will go wrong. One I can predict: everyone will say the die was fair so it's fifty-fifty and refuse to budge. Tell me how to handle that without just giving them the answer. Someone will also notice that the room itself is a sample of thirteen — say whether to lean into that or rule it out, and why.
- Be frank about what the game can't reproduce: it has finite, known population sizes, whereas the real dispute is about unknown and possibly enormous ones; the reference class is my choice, not theirs; and it's not obvious that betting behaviour is the right test for what to believe.

Keep the whole thing under 1000 words. I'd rather have a loose timeline and a really tight debrief than the other way round.
