Could you write a dialogue (two characters, somewhere around 1000–1400 words, neither of them a pushover) between someone who holds that higher-order evidence has to move your first-order confidence, and someone who holds that it can leave you in a genuine no-win: rationally believing P while at the same time rationally judging that your belief in P was formed by an unreliable process. Set it somewhere with forced proximity — a 5am airport gate after the conference, or a broken-down train — and let them be colleagues who have been through this before, so they can talk in shorthand. Call them whatever you like. Nobody should name-drop papers or authors; the arguments need to be self-contained, and if a technical term comes up it should get a one-line gloss in the dialogue itself.

Here are the moves I want them to actually make, since these are the bits my flatmate and I kept circling. They should start from a shared case: you reason carefully from excellent evidence to P, then learn you'd been given a drug that degrades reasoning, with no felt signature — no fog, no sluggishness, nothing to check your work against. Then: (1) one of them introduces a distinction between impairments that leave a *trace* you can go looking for (a checkable error signature, the ability to re-derive the result by a different route) and silent ones where the error hides from the person making it, and claims the real puzzle only arises for the silent kind — the other pushes back that failing to find a trace is itself weak evidence when the errors are the hiding kind. (2) The asymmetry worry: if bad news about your reliability licenses discounting, symmetry says good news should license boosting, yet boosting feels like double-counting your first-order evidence; let them fight about whether that asymmetry is defensible or just squeamishness. (3) The self-undermining case, which is the one I actually care about: a condition that makes you prone to falsely *believing* that you got misleading higher-order evidence, so that if the impairment is global your very belief that you took the drug is also suspect and the higher-order evidence can't get any traction; they should argue about whether a *local but silent* impairment is even a coherent state, and if it is, whether re-running your reasoning by a formally different method is a real substitute for a trace or just the same broken system admiring itself. (4) The comparison to Moore's paradox-style absurdity — the akratic combination is odd to avow even if both halves can be true — and whether that shows a coherence requirement across levels or only that assertion has awkward pragmatics. Have each of them land at least one point the other can't answer cleanly and make at least one explicit concession, and end not with a winner but with them agreeing on what a case would have to look like to settle it.

And because I'll be showing this to my flatmate to make a point (possibly a bad one), please add a short coda — 150 words or so, separate from the dialogue — flagging which of the premises they rely on are close to universal working assumptions in this area and which are genuinely contested, which of the moves above are standard ones that people in the field would recognise, and which are inventions that a real opponent could simply refuse to grant.
