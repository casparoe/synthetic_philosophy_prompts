I'm TAing a first-year seminar module on moral objectivity this term and I've drifted into a position I can't quite talk myself out of, so I want you to map how exposed it actually is rather than argue me out of it. Roughly the view: the variation we keep finding in folk moral judgment across cultures — the usual exhibits people wheel out, WEIRD samples turning out to be outliers on a lot of measures, the moral/conventional distinction not landing the same way in some small-scale societies, divergent verdicts on sacrificial-dilemma cases and on violations that are mostly about loyalty or deference, big differences in how much weight gets put on intentions versus outcomes — isn't measurement error around a single shared target. It's evidence that the apparent target isn't there, or at least that our judgments aren't latching onto whatever is there. I've started calling it the variation argument, mostly so I can stop re-explaining it to myself.

Here's what I want: what would have to be true for that to be false. Please give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and separate the ones a defender would concede count against the view from the ones they'd correctly dismiss as resting on a rival framing. A couple of things I want handled directly. First, my flatmate does fieldwork and has this idea she calls reason-convergence under verdict-divergence: stop asking thin vignettes for a yes/no verdict and instead ask people to justify themselves, then check whether the reasons converge even where the verdicts don't — pilgrimages to the same place by different routes, effectively. If that turned out to be common, is that a defeater for the variation argument, or just the disagreement relocating one level down? Second, suppose the data shook out so that almost all the variation sits in one region of the space while a small set of cases — gratuitous cruelty to an identifiable child, say — show near-zero variance in every population anyone has tested, including the small-scale ones. Call those the fixed points. Would that near-universality support objectivism or undermine it, given that everyone will immediately have an explanation for why we'd converge there anyway? I can't tell whether my argument treats convergence and divergence both as wins, and if it does, whether that's a bug. Third, I want the honest version of the methods worry: if cross-cultural vignette work systematically under-describes the cases, respondents in different places fill in different background stories, so some of my variation is an artifact of thin stimuli — how much of my evidence would that deflate, and is there a way to tell in advance, or do I only get to say "well, probably artifact" after the fact?

Two more constraints on the answer. Please say what I'd personally find most persuasive, since I think I know: what would actually move me is (a) the variation shrinking a lot under better-designed studies — incentive-compatible, comprehension-checked, within-subject, translation-checked, ideally with people giving reasons rather than just ratings — rather than persisting, and (b) someone specifying in advance, not after seeing the data, which items are supposed to be fixed points and why. And I want you to press on the size question, because I don't know what my argument even predicts: is there a threshold proportion of moral judgment types that have to vary before the inference goes through, or does it depend on which ones vary, or is the honest answer that the argument never had a threshold and is doing something else entirely — showing that a realist owes us a story about why the pattern looks like this, rather than refuting them. If it's the latter, say so, and tell me whether that makes it a stronger argument or a much weaker one than I've been treating it as.
