Hey, I'm marking a stack of short-answer responses for an intro metaethics module and would like a second opinion on this one. Here's the question I set:

Question. The Companions in Guilt move says: whatever makes moral facts look metaphysically or epistemologically queer (they'd be irreducibly normative, mind-independent, and yet capable of giving any agent a reason) is shared by epistemic facts about what we have reason to believe. So the anti-realist who rejects moral facts for that reason must reject epistemic facts too, and that's a cost she can't pay. Now consider the Division of Labour objection: Companions in Guilt at most shows that moral and epistemic facts stand or fall together; it doesn't say which way they fall, and a theorist can coherently bite the bullet and be a global error theorist about reasons. In about 300 words: (a) does the Division of Labour objection defeat the Companions in Guilt move? (b) if not, what would the realist have to add in order to break the tie? (c) assess one proposed tie-breaker: we have a pragmatic reason to accept epistemic norms, since true belief is useful, whereas no comparable pragmatic story supports moral norms.

And here's one student's answer:

The Companions in Guilt argument shows that the queerness objection proves too much. Mackie's worry is that objective moral values would be entities unlike anything else: facts that lug their own reason-giving force around with them. But facts about what you have reason to believe look the same. If that's enough to rule out moral facts it's enough to rule out reasons for belief, and then you can't even argue for the error theory, since arguing presupposes that some considerations are better reasons than others. So the anti-realist has to drop the queerness objection.

The Division of Labour objection doesn't defeat the move. Parity alone doesn't tell you which way to go, and a global error theorist can hold that no normative claim is ever truth-apt or true. But that isn't really available, because it removes the norms of reasoning themselves, and any argument offered for it has to use those very norms. So the objection fails and the tie stays a tie until the realist adds something.

The pragmatic tie-breaker supplies what's missing. We have a pragmatic reason to accept epistemic norms, because having true beliefs helps us get what we want, and pragmatic reasons are themselves a species of normative reason, so this gives us a genuinely normative reason to believe truly. Moral facts have nothing parallel: there is no useful outcome that moral norms are the means to. Epistemic normativity therefore earns its keep and moral normativity doesn't, which is exactly the asymmetry the realist needs.
