I'm 71, a retired plant physiologist — thirty-odd years at a horticultural research station, mostly glasshouses, irrigation rigs and an awful lot of fiddly instrument calibration — and about a year ago I drifted into a philosophy reading group at the local library. Last month we did the extended mind material: the original Clark and Chalmers paper, Otto and his notebook, the parity principle, and the objection everybody raises, that being causally coupled to something isn't the same as being partly made up of it. I went home and wrote the draft below, which I'm supposed to read out in a fortnight.

What I don't want is a summary of the debate — I can read that stuff myself, and we've already been through it once. What I want is somebody poking at the argument. Specific questions at the end.

---

DRIFT

It strikes me that this debate has been run backwards. Everyone starts with an internal process we're already happy to call cognitive, notices an external process doing a similar job, and then argues about whether the external one gets to count too. That's the parity move, and the standard complaint about it is that it begs the question: similarity of job isn't sameness of constitution. I think the complaint is right but that it points somewhere else. In the lab we never decided whether an instrument was part of the apparatus by comparing it to an organ. We decided by calibrating it.

So here's my candidate. Call it drift-tracking: an external thing X is a proper part of a subject S's cognitive system for some task T only if S's ongoing performance of T is organised around X's error profile. Not merely uses X. Not merely trusts X. But has built X's failure modes into the way she acts: she checks it where it's flaky, discounts it where it lies, cross-references it where it's silent, and notices when the pattern changes.

The point of the calibration analogy is that it's supposed to be a third thing, sitting between mere causal influence and mere reliability. A thermometer I never calibrate is just an object in the room. A thermometer whose known lag I compensate for, whose drift I log in a book and correct against a standard once a month, has been taken into the setup — it is part of how the measurement gets made. Apply that to Otto. If Otto merely trusts the notebook, he is a man with a lucky book. If Otto knows the ink fades in sunlight and keeps it in an inside pocket, knows that his handwriting from a bad winter is unreliable and recopies those entries, knows the page for the museum address is the one he's most likely to have misremembered — then the notebook has been calibrated in, and the claim that it's part of him starts to look like the claim that the thermistor is part of the rig.

Two things then go wrong, and I can't tell whether they're fatal.

First, calibration seems to require memory. To carry an error profile around you have to represent the device's past behaviour, and that's precisely the faculty whose boundaries were in question. So my criterion looks like it can only ever be an add-on to internal memory, never a substitute for it, which means it would rule against Otto by construction — I'd be begging the question in exactly the way I've just accused the parity argument of doing. I have a repair: the error profile doesn't have to be in the head, it can be in a second external device (a maintenance log, a margin note), so what I've got is a regress rather than a circle, and the regress has to stop somewhere, plausibly in the body — posture, habit, skilled coping that isn't propositional. But I suspect that last move is hand-waving, and I'd like it tested.

Second, three cases where I don't trust my own intuitions.

(a) My glasshouse. A tensiometer in bed 3 reads about eight per cent high in cold weather; I've known this for years and I water accordingly, without thinking and without being able to state the correction factor if you asked me. By my own criterion the probe is part of how I keep track of soil moisture. That feels right to me. But then: the manufacturer ships a firmware update that compensates for temperature automatically. I stop knowing anything about the drift. Intuitively it's absurd to say my mind shrank because the firmware improved — yet my criterion seems to say exactly that. Is that a reductio of the criterion, or is it a sign that I've quietly switched from talking about the person to talking about a wider system (grower plus probe plus firmware) without noticing? And if it's the latter, which of the two was ever supposed to be the subject of the thesis?

(b) The case where nobody has the model. A lab technician works for years off a spreadsheet that, unbeknownst to anyone, rounds half-values down instead of up. Her judgements come out subtly skewed the whole time. Something is definitely steering her cognition here; no one is tracking it. So drift-tracking can't be necessary for being a cognitive vehicle. Maybe it's only sufficient. Or maybe it was never a mark of constitution at all but a mark of something neighbouring — of the arrangement being in good working order, or of the subject being answerable for the errors.

(c) Bloat. My car's fuel gauge reads optimistic on hills, and I've absorbed that; I plan fuel stops around it. Is the gauge part of my practical reasoning about where to refuel? My criterion says yes, and I think that's the wrong answer, but I can't see how to block it without an ad hoc patch.

The deflationary thought I end on, and I'm not sure I believe it: maybe the constitution question was never the interesting one. What we actually care about in all of these cases is who is answerable for the error — who is in a position to notice that the thing has drifted. That's a question you can ask without deciding whether the notebook is in Otto, and it's the question that made the difference in the lab.
