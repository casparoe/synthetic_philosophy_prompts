I run a small reading group with two friends on decision theory (one of them is a hardline Bayesian who thinks the whole imprecise-probability programme is incoherent and keeps baiting me about it), and I'm writing up my position as a long post before the next session. Before I post it I'd like someone to check whether it actually hangs together, because I can't locate the problem myself. Roughly where I've landed:

1. In the Ellsberg set-up — one urn known to be 50 red / 50 black, another with 100 balls in an unknown proportion — I think the evidence *requires* an imprecise credal state, a set of priors, rather than a single number. Choosing 0.5 for the ambiguous urn would be inventing precision I don't have.
2. More generally, rationality is permissive here: given the same evidence a range of credal states is rationally fine, and there need be no fact of the matter about which member of the set is the correct prior. But every member of the set still has to obey the probability axioms.
3. For action I use maxmin: rank options by their worst case over the set. And I think the standard Ellsberg preferences — paying a premium to bet on the known urn, both for red and for black — are rationally permissible, not a bias to be trained out of.
4. I update by conditioning every prior in the set on whatever I learn, with no extra rigidity or structural assumptions, and I'm not bothered by the consequence that my interval can come out *wider* after I learn something I already knew I was going to learn.
5. The whole appeal of the framework for me, though, is that imprecision tracks ignorance: imprecision is supposed to recede as evidence accumulates, so that a well-evidenced opinion is sharper than a guess.
6. I've also been using it for peer disagreement: when someone whose judgement I respect disagrees with me, I widen my interval instead of splitting the difference, and I like that this lets me concede something without pretending I know how to average.
7. And I have one intuition I really don't want to lose: I shouldn't be exploitable. If a bookie can hand me a sequence of bets, each of which is acceptable by my own lights and each of which I accept, and the package is guaranteed to lose me money, then something is wrong with my view — the same reason I don't want to be money pumped in sequential choice, or to pay to tie my own hands in a way I'd regret later.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Go through the list and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, ordered by how bad they are rather than all flagged equally; for each real tension say exactly what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the list; and if there's a single revision that cleans up the most, name it. Commit to a definite position on which commitment has to go — don't just lay out the options and leave me to pick — and put a rough confidence on each verdict, plus a note on where you're relying on a contested result or a contested interpretation of one of my claims, since my Bayesian friend is going to read this and will happily exploit anything shaky.
