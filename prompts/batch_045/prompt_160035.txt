Im in a grad seminar on epistemology and we did Nozick's tracking account last week, and a guy in my cohort rebuilt it over drinks into something I can't find the exit from. The version we were taught: S knows p only if p is true, S believes p, if p were false S wouldn't believe p by the method she actually used, and if p were true across the nearby variant situations S would believe it. Payoff: I track the claim that there's a coffee cup on my desk (call it C), because if there weren't one I'd be looking at an empty desk; I don't track the claim that I'm not a brain in a vat, because if I were envatted I'd believe exactly what I believe now; and I know that the first entails the second. So knowledge isn't closed under known entailment, the skeptic's argument needs closure, and it breaks. I liked that. His version swaps the vat for something homemade, because he says the vat is too exotic to get intuitions moving. Call it the Override: a rig that can, for up to forty seconds at a time and undetectably from the inside, replace everything you're seeing with a rendered scene that needn't match the room. Let O be the claim that the Override isn't running on me right now.

(1) Same three verdicts: I know C; I don't know ¬O; I know that C entails ¬O (call that E). (2) Principle (T): if S knows p, S knows that p entails q, and S comes to believe q by competently deducing q from just those two things, then S knows q. It's weaker than straight closure — it requires the new belief to actually rest on a competent deduction rather than on a hunch or a feeling — and it's what people appeal to when they say you can extend what you know by reasoning from it. (3) So I do the deduction: C; C entails ¬O; therefore ¬O. It's competent, and I believe ¬O because of it, so by (T) I know ¬O. (4) Tracking says I don't. The belief the deduction produces fails the third condition all over again: if the Override were running I'd have the same cup-experience, run the same deduction, and land on the same conclusion. And before you tell me to swap sensitivity for safety: the rig is in the next room with a remote on the desk, so worlds where it is running and I believe ¬O anyway aren't far off at all — the belief isn't safe either. (5) Dropping (T) looks suicidal. If competent deduction from known premises never transmits knowledge, then nothing anybody infers is ever known: every proof, every bit of reasoning from a measurement, every time you put two things you know together. Each of those would have to be separately trackable by some non-inferential method, and most of them aren't. Reasoning would be epistemically decorative. (6) So (T) survives, and now the verdicts in (1) eat each other: given K(C) and K(E), and given that I did the deduction, (T) yields K(¬O); the tracking account yields ¬K(¬O); I'm not going to say I don't know E, that's just logic; so what goes is K(C). The theory whose whole selling point was that it disarms the skeptic ends up handing him my coffee cup and every other ordinary thing I thought I knew.

The extra thing that bugs me: someone else in the group said (T) proves too much in the other direction too — if I can deduce my way to ¬O from a cup-belief, then from C plus introspection that I believe C I can deduce that I've got a true belief, do that a handful of times, and conclude my vision is reliable, which is knowledge that's supposed to be expensive. So (T) simultaneously rescues the skeptic and hands out reliability for free, while the only alternative I can see makes inference useless. I want to keep that I know there's a cup on my desk, and I'd like to keep something of the thought that the skeptic's argument has a hole in it. Which numbered step do you give up, why does it look fine right up until it doesn't, and what am I stuck paying once I've given it up — including what I now have to say about the reliability case?
