Hi — hoping you can help with something I'm putting together for my third-year philosophy of mind module. I've got a lecture on the multiple realizability argument coming up and I want to end it with a problem case that runs in the opposite direction from the usual one, and I can't get mine to behave.

The dialectic as my students will have met it: type identity theory is the fussy view (same mental state ⇒ same physical state), role functionalism is the liberal one (same causal role, whatever the hardware — human, octopus, Martian, all in pain, etc). Multiple realizability is the battering ram that gets you from the first to the second, and the liberality is the whole selling point.

So here's what I want: a case where that liberality runs out. A case where a plain role functionalist — machine-state / psychofunctionalist, nothing fancy — should say that two systems are *not* in the same mental state, even though both of them satisfy the same functional specification. I want the reverse of the usual direction: functionalism as the restrictive view and a realizer-focused view (type identity, or something in that neighborhood) as the one with the relaxed verdict. And I want it generated, not stipulated.

Constraints:

1. Realistic. A clinic, a ward, a workplace, a school, a lab — your pick. No aliens, no uploads, no galaxy-sized lookup tables, no brain the size of a country.
2. Keep it to a plain cognitive state. Believing the meeting is at noon, remembering where you parked, expecting the bus. No qualia, no inverted spectra, no zombies — I want the case to be hard without anyone getting to play the consciousness card.
3. The two systems can't differ in inputs or outputs over the stretch of the case, including the nearby counterfactual ones. The difference has to be somewhere else.
4. No cheating in the setup: if it only works because you described one system as "not really a believer" or "just simulating," bin it. The verdict has to fall out of the view plus a feature of the case.
5. The feature that does the work has to be stateable in functionalist vocabulary — roles, causal structure, counterfactuals over the system's own dynamics. Not "made of neurons," not "conscious," not "the right stuff." Otherwise you've just snuck identity theory in through the back door, and I'll spot it immediately.
6. Ideally give me three systems, not two: A and B, where the functionalist should be restrictive; and then a C, realized wildly differently from both, where the same functionalist should happily say "same state after all." I want the contrast pair, so the case isolates one variable instead of just making functionalism look stingy.

What I need back:

- The scenario. One paragraph, 150–200 words, and readable on a projector screen.
- The verdicts. One short paragraph each for (i) plain role functionalism, (ii) a realizer-focused / type-identity-ish view, (iii) whatever refined condition you end up using. Derive them, don't just announce them.
- The toggle. Name the single feature doing the work, then flip it and tell me what happens: does the case collapse back into the bog-standard multiple realizability setup, or does it flip the verdicts? Either is fine, but say which.
- The best functionalist reply. Honestly now: what does someone who likes the plain view say? Bite the bullet ("yes, same state, so much the worse for your intuition")? Attack the setup? Give me the strongest version, not the straw one.
- One line on whether this is really different from the old lookup-table worry (Block-style: a system that matches the role by brute tabulation) or just a glossy rewrite of it. If it is a rewrite, tell me and I'll rethink the lecture.

In case its useful, an idea I've been kicking around — feel free to bin it or replace it: call it the continuation condition. A realizer of a role counts only if the system's *own* dynamics would keep it in the role as circumstances drift, without an outside maintainer re-tuning it (a clinician, a carer, a software update, a supervisor). The thought is that this is still functional vocabulary — it's just counterfactuals over the system's own causal structure — so it isn't neurons smuggled in wearing a disguise, and it gives you a principled way to be liberal about C and stingy about B. Its also obviously in danger of collapsing into something circular, so if you think that, say so and give me something better. Or ignore the whole thing and build the case some other way; the case is the deliverable, not my half-baked condition.

What it's for, so you know what counts as cheating: I want students to see that "multiple realizability" isn't just the slogan "anything can realize anything," it's a claim about the *shape* of a tolerance, and the interesting question is what the tolerance is a tolerance of. Then in seminar they get the case and have to find the toggle themselves.
