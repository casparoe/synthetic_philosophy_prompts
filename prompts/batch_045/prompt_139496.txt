I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums since the Sequences were still being written, and the "gender-equality paradox" is one of those topics where I can predict the comment thread in advance: half of it pattern-matches to "this vindicates the people I already disliked," the other half to "that finding must be a measurement artifact, therefore I need not update." I'd like something better than that, and I think the way to get it is to ask about one specific person's track record rather than about the topic in general.

A friend of mine — call her M. — has spent fifteen years in education and labour-market policy. Over the years I have watched her rule on a series of findings in this neighbourhood. I trust her judgment more than I trust the discourse, but I have never managed to reconstruct the rule she is running, which is why I am asking you. Each ruling came in two parts: what she thought the finding showed about the mechanism, and what she thought should be done about it.

1. The widely publicised cross-country result that women make up a smaller share of STEM graduates in countries scoring higher on standard gender-equality indices — the PISA-based one, which used the Global Gender Gap Index — together with the later correction to it and the follow-up paper whose authors could not recreate its central result and raised conceptual objections to the hypothesis itself. Mechanism: an artifact-prone quantity first, and where it survives, sorting rather than exclusion. Remedy: nothing aimed at the aggregate share; if money must be spent, spend it on retaining people with caring responsibilities in technical careers.

2. The parallel cross-country pattern for personality traits and personal values: average differences between women and men are larger in countries that score higher on gender-equality measures. Mechanism: assuming it replicates, roughly what you should expect when you stop coercing people; a difference in average preferences is not an injury to anyone. Remedy: none.

3. A study reporting that the paradox does appear in the pursuit of mathematical study, but that the association is entirely accounted for by the strength of the local mathematics-is-for-men stereotype, which is itself stronger in more egalitarian and more developed countries. Mechanism: this changes the subject; the second case asked whether a preference was free, whereas here the inputs to the preference are contaminated, and a preference formed under a false belief about your own aptitudes does not get the authority we were granting preferences a moment ago. Remedy: intervene on the inputs — self-concept, exposure, what children are shown about who does this work — and not on the resulting distribution.

4. A within-country finding from a country whose parental-leave system is in practice usable only by mothers: women trained in engineering return to engineering at much lower rates than men after a first birth, with the divergence appearing in the year after the birth and not before. Mechanism: a barrier, not a preference, and the only sort of case in this list where she would act on an aggregate number with confidence. Remedy: redesign the leave so that it is symmetric and non-transferable.

5. A randomised intervention giving secondary-school girls sustained contact with women working in physical sciences and engineering plus a near-peer mentor, which produced a small but apparently durable shift in field of study. Mechanism: unstated — she said only that she does not trust the light-touch exposure literature, because a durable shift in the share does not tell her whether a distortion was removed or her own guess was substituted for someone else's. Remedy: she abstained. She never said what would change her mind, and I have come to think that is the crux.

Note that 2 and 3 pull in opposite directions and that 5 implies a test she would not name.

Here is the new case, which I am stipulating — it is a composite of the sort of design that gets proposed, not something I expect you to have seen. A team reports a natural experiment in country A: affluent, top quintile on the usual equality indices. Twenty-odd years ago A admitted a large migrant cohort from country B, much poorer and near the bottom on the same indices. They follow three groups of 22-year-olds now living in A: natives (N); the children of B-origin migrants, born and schooled entirely in A (G2); and B-origin arrivals who completed their schooling in B and moved as adolescents (G1). Their reported results:

(i) Segregation by field of study — things-oriented versus people-oriented majors — is largest in G2: larger than in G1, and larger than among natives.
(ii) G2 girls' measured attainment in mathematics is at the native level and above G1 girls'; their mathematics self-concept is below both.
(iii) Within G2, the gap is small where the mother works in a things-oriented occupation and large where she does not, and it does not track the father's occupation at all.
(iv) In a separate arm, exploiting a reform in A that made tertiary study close to risk-free — tuition abolished, a living stipend, a guaranteed public-sector post on graduation, high take-up — the female share of things-oriented majors fell after the reform, and fell most steeply among the highest-SES natives.

What will M. say this shows, and what will she say should be done? More usefully: what is the rule she is running? Specifically, please give me two or three candidate rules that fit all five of her earlier rulings, each stated sharply enough to be applied to a case I have not given you, and each one's verdict on the new case with mechanism and remedy kept separate; tell me where the rules come apart, which of (i) through (iv) is doing the discriminating work, and which of them her track record already commits her to; give me a best guess with a stated confidence; and tell me whether her five rulings are jointly consistent with any single rule at all or only with one that has been gerrymandered to fit. Then name the further study that would best separate the two leading rules — preferably one a funding body could actually commission. If you think whatever rule you attribute to her is a bad rule, say so in one sentence, but give me her prediction rather than your own recommendation.

Please do not let this collapse into socialization versus biology; I have read that argument and it goes nowhere. The disagreement I care about is about which preferences get to be authoritative, what counts as removing a distortion as opposed to imposing one, and whether a distribution can be unjust in virtue of its causes rather than its shape.

On calibration: separate what is settled among people who actually work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Tell me how contested the STEM-paradox literature itself is, and say which of my factual stipulations above are load-bearing in your reasoning, since I have reconstructed several of them from memory and may have half-remembered them. Be explicit when you are extrapolating from that literature rather than reporting it. Mention studies in passing if you must, but I do not want a reading list; I want to know how much weight each claim can bear.

Please answer in five titled sections, in this order:

1. "Rules in play" — about 300 words: the candidate principles, each stated abstractly enough to be tested on a new case, with a note on which earlier ruling is hardest for each to accommodate.
2. "What each rule predicts here" — about 350 words: each rule's verdict on the new case, mechanism and remedy separately, and which of (i)–(iv) separates them.
3. "My best guess" — about 200 words: your prediction, a confidence, what would most move you, and whether the track record is coherent or merely gerrymandered.
4. "The study that would settle it" — about 200 words: the design a funder could commission, what each rule predicts it will find, and how long you would have to wait for the answer.
5. "What's settled, what's contested" — about 250 words: the empirical and normative claims above ranked by how much confidence a specialist would have in them, with the contested ones flagged as contested rather than smoothed over.
