Right, I need a hand with a speech and I'd rather not botch it.

I'm 74. I spent about thirty-five years as a protein chemist, most of it on allosteric enzymes — the old argument about whether they flip all at once or one subunit at a time — and for the last four years I've been sitting in on philosophy of science seminars, which my wife says has made me insufferable and which is probably why I've been asked to do this. Next month my old department of biochemistry is being folded into a new Institute for Integrative Systems Biology, and I've been given the after-dinner slot at the symposium to mark it. Seven minutes, roughly.

The room is the problem. There'll be maybe forty of my old colleagues, hard-nosed structural and mechanistic types, who think "emergence" is a word people use when they can't do the chemistry. There'll be thirty-odd systems people who think reductionism was a twentieth-century fetish and are bored of being told to go and find the mechanism. There's a pro-vice-chancellor who personally got the word "emergent" into the institute's title and into the grant application, and is sitting at the top table. And there are spouses, a couple of my grandchildren, and some undergrads — maybe twenty people who are not going to follow any of this and who deserve better than being condescended to or bored for seven minutes.

I don't want diplomatic mush where everybody gets a warm bath and nothing is actually said. But there are things I can't say, and I want the speech built honestly around them rather than fudged. I can't tell the systems people that the word "emergence" is doing no work at all, because I don't think that's true. I can't tell the chemists that emergence is a discovery about what nature is like, because I can't honestly claim that either. I can't say "the whole is more than the sum of its parts," which I don't believe and which would get me quietly laughed at from the back. And I can't use the lazy jab — that emergence is just vitalism with a grant number — because two of the systems people are old friends and it would land as an insult rather than as a joke.

Here's the distinction I've been chewing on, and I want the speech to stand or fall on it. Call the first one *bookkeeping emergence*: the higher-level property is a re-description of a pattern in the goings-on of the parts, you could in principle recompute it from below and get the same answer, and the higher-level vocabulary is essentially a filing system — convenient, sometimes indispensable for us, saying nothing extra about the world. Call the second one *obstruction emergence*: the derivation from below exists in some bloodless sense, but it is not available to any creature like us in any form we could actually use — it would take longer to carry out than the phenomenon lasts, or it needs more precision about the parts than the system will hold still for, or it has to be rebuilt from scratch for every new system and transfers nothing. I know this is close to things the philosophers have said about weak emergence and about derivations that only work in some ideal limit, but I've come at it from the bench and I want to keep my own versions of it.

So: use allostery as the worked example — we have close to complete structural knowledge of the parts and of how they move, and yet the functional description at the level of the whole assembly hasn't gone away and hasn't collapsed into a derivation either. Say which side of my cut it falls on and what follows for what the new institute ought to be doing. Be straight with me about whether the cut is a cut in nature or a cut in us, and what it costs me to admit which. It should end in an actual toast, and I want the toast to be one my distinction licences rather than one that quietly abandons it.

Practical notes: British English throughout, please. Short sentences — it's meant to be heard, not read, and I read slowly now. Explain the idea through the one concrete case, not through jargon; if a fourteen-year-old grandchild can't follow the shape of it, it isn't finished. Nothing that requires me to name a philosopher or cite anything, since I can never remember who said what and I'll just mangle it. And maybe a short note after the speech itself telling me where my distinction is weakest and what I'd be conceding by speaking in these terms — I'd rather find out now than standing up.
