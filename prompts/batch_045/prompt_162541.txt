I would like an analysis of the 2016 United Kingdom referendum on European Union membership as a case study in collective intentionality. Hold these facts fixed: the ballot put a single binary question; roughly 52 per cent voted to leave and 48 per cent to remain; it said nothing about what leaving would consist in — customs arrangements, single-market status and any transition period all unspecified — while the competing versions of leaving (a close regulatory alignment of the Norwegian kind, a bespoke trade agreement, or exit onto default trade terms) were advanced by different and sometimes incompatible parts of the winning side. Afterwards the result was routinely described as the will of the people, that description was used to require legislators who had campaigned against leaving to give effect to it, and the legislature then deadlocked for years over the terms of withdrawal until a general election was fought on completing the process.

Run the mapping in both directions. On the theory side, apply the standard shared-intention conditions — each participant intends that we act together, intends it by way of meshing sub-plans, and stays mutually responsive when those plans break down — together with the plural-subject thought that joint commitment generates member obligations. Ask whether what existed in June 2016 was one collective intention to leave, or only many individual intentions with divergent objects. In particular, test a distinction I have not seen drawn in these terms: a vote may create a collective intention, may merely diagnose one that already exists, or may produce something third — an intention to defer, whose content is that whatever the government subsequently negotiates should be adopted. Which, if any, fits this case, and what would each imply about the mandate claimed afterwards? Relatedly: can a shared intention be content-gappy, an intention to exit with no intention as to what follows, or does an intention that specifies only what it rejects lack an object altogether? Say whether the theory has the resources to distinguish an underdetermined collective intention from no collective intention at all, and whether the later election can supply the missing content retroactively or only replace it.

Then turn it around: what the episode shows about the theory. Does it vindicate the aggregative rival, on which procedures convert plural votes into outcomes without any we at all? Does it expose a gap, namely that institutions act on mandates whose content is filled in later by whoever holds office? Does the plural-subject machinery really license binding legislators who dissented? Where the theory is silent, say so plainly rather than forcing it to speak.
