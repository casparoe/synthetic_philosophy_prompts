**Heritability is a ledger entry, not a verdict**

Most public arguments about heritability are arguments about a number that has already been defined in a way that prevents it from settling them. Narrow-sense heritability, h², is additive genetic variance divided by total phenotypic variance — V_A / V_P — measured in one population, in one environment, at one time. Broad-sense heritability, H², swaps the numerator for all genetic variance, including dominance and epistatic effects. Either way you get a ratio of two variances. That's the whole object.

The first thing to notice is that a ratio of variances is a piece of bookkeeping about a population, not a decomposition of a person. It does not say that 70% of your height came from your genes and 30% from your breakfast, any more than saying that 90% of the variation in a field of corn is genetic tells you that 90% of any particular stalk is genetic. Call this the *ledger illusion*: reading a population-level accounting identity as if it were an individual-level causal split. Once you see it, a lot of confident sentences collapse. A trait can be paradigmatically "genetic" in its development and barely heritable at all — the number of eyes a person has is under tight genetic control, but the variation we actually observe is mostly injury and disease, so the heritability is low. Conversely, a trait can be highly heritable while being entirely environmental in its mechanism, provided the environmental cause is reliably correlated with ancestry, as with a culturally transmitted practice that happens to run in families.

The second point is the one I care most about, and it's the one I think people find genuinely counterintuitive: **heritability is, in a sense, a compliment to the environment.** Because the denominator is total variance, anything that homogenizes the environment mechanically pushes heritability estimates up. Suppose (this is a made-up example) that the Lakeview school district standardizes reading instruction so that every classroom uses the same well-funded curriculum, while the neighboring district lets instruction vary from excellent to nonexistent depending on the building. If you estimate the heritability of reading scores in each district, you should expect it to be higher in Lakeview — even though Lakeview is doing a better and fairer job. The genetic variation didn't change; the environmental noise shrank, so genes explain more of what's left. A rising heritability estimate can therefore be evidence that some equalizing intervention worked. The popular framing treats high heritability as a reason for fatalism about policy. On the definition, it can just as easily be a receipt for policy that already succeeded.

Third, the heritability of a trait within groups carries no information about the cause of a difference between groups. Two bags of seed corn from the same variety: one sown in rich soil, one in sand. Within each bag, height variation is nearly all genetic; between the bags it's nearly all soil. High within-group heritability is perfectly compatible with the gap being 100% environmental, and no value of h² inside either bag will tell you which you're looking at. What you'd need is a comparison across the environments, which is a different study with a different design.

None of this makes heritability useless. It was built for a purpose: predicting how a population responds to selection, via the breeder's equation. Used inside that purpose, it's a fine tool. The trouble is only when the ratio gets dragged into questions it was never shaped to answer.

So here's the reframing I want to try out. Instead of asking "how heritable is this trait?" — which invites the ledger illusion and the fatalism — ask what I'll call the **residual inequality question**: if we standardized the malleable inputs at the best level we could feasibly deliver to everyone, how much of the outcome variation would remain, and how large would it be relative to the gap we started with? That's a question about an intervention and a counterfactual, not about a variance ratio, and it's the question policy is actually asking. It also explains why heritability is so seductive: people reach for it because they want the residual inequality question answered, and h² is the only number lying around.
