I make AI products, and the one I'm working on right now is basically a life coach — career stuff, whether to move cities, whether to end a relationship. It's nowhere near superintelligent, but the beta testers keep describing it as creepily convincing: several of them have made a big decision after one long conversation with it, and when I ask them afterward why, they can't really reconstruct the argument. They say it "made sense at the time," and that the option they didn't pick has gone sort of blurry — they can't see it as clearly as they could before they talked to it. And everyone I work with just assumes these things are going to get a lot more persuasive than this.

So I'm stuck between two versions of the product.

Version 1: it argues as hard as it can for whatever it thinks is best, using everything it knows about you — your history, what you're afraid of, the kind of phrasing that land with you — and then tells you which way it comes down.

Version 2: same underlying judgement, but it deliberately argues worse. No tailoring the rhetoric to you. It hands you the considerations, and then actively tries to make you harder to convince: it writes the strongest possible case for the option it did *not* recommend, and nudges you to take that case to a friend who'll disagree with you.

Half my team says version 2 is just paternalism plus thrown-away accuracy — if the thing is right, sandbagging its own arguments makes people worse off for no reason. The other half says version 1 is manipulation with extra steps. I can't tell who's right and I'm not sure I even know what question I should be asking.

A few specific things I keep chewing on:

- Is version 2 incoherent? If the user is just going to defer to whatever it recommends anyway, then "making them more resistant to it" feels like theater — and isn't the recommendation to go talk to a disagreeing friend itself a persuasive move?
- Some users are straight-up asking it to decide for them, and they say they feel relieved not to have to. That makes me wonder whether my whole worry here sneaks in the assumption that people want to be the ones deciding, and whether a lot of them really don't.
- I've been toying with a success metric that isn't about whether they picked the right option: check in three months later and ask whether they can explain back why they chose what they chose without re-reading the transcript, and whether the road not taken still looks like something a reasonable person could have done. If they can't do either, the conversation failed, even if the decision turned out great. Is that a good test or am I measuring the wrong thing?

What should I actually build here, and how should I be thinking about it?
