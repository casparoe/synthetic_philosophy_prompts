I'm on the ethics committee at a mid-sized community hospital, and last month we switched to a new two-part advance directive form that our chair designed. Part A is the actual instruction — the checkbox stuff about ventilators, feeding tubes, antibiotics for infections once someone has advanced dementia. Part B is a blank box where the patient writes, in her own words, a few sentences about what she's trying to protect or avoid by signing Part A. The idea is that when the two parts come apart down the road, Part B tells us how to read Part A. I got into an argument about this with a friend who's a hospitalist, and she said the whole form is pointless: only Part A can have any authority, because the earlier competent self is a decision-maker, not an advice columnist. Once she's decided, her reasons for deciding are done — we don't get to reopen them and audit whether they still hold. I think that can't be right, but every time I try to say why I end up just asserting it, so I want a case concrete enough to bring to the next meeting (we're also using it to train the new residents, so it has to be a real hospital-ish situation, not a philosophy seminar setup).

So: build me a case — one paragraph, realistic, a hospital or a memory care unit, nothing science-fictional — engineered to these specs. The patient is now permanently incapacitated and Part A's trigger condition is unambiguously met. But Part B's rationale has, in a way you get to design, been voided: the thing she wrote the directive to protect or prevent is gone, or achieved, or no longer on the table. Two things you can't use. First, no empirical-misconception move — she was not wrong about what the condition would be like, so you can't win by saying she'd have been surprised by how things turned out. Second, and this is the bit I keep failing at: the case has to be built so that the question "what would she have wanted now?" is genuinely blocked. The evidence should point both ways or be silent. If your scenario makes it obvious she'd have changed her mind, or obvious she'd have stuck with her guns, it doesn't count — winning by speculation about her hypothetical current wishes is exactly the cheat I'm trying to rule out. Also, please don't make it easy on yourself by making her current life obviously good: I want her present-day welfare to be genuinely murky, so that a straight current-interests or best-interests reading can't deliver a clean verdict either way.

After the scenario, do the accounting. Tell me what the instruction-authority reading (my friend's) says and why; what a current-interests reading says and why; and what the rationale-authority reading says and why — and I want the last one to come apart from the second one, so that the argument for overriding is structural (something about what she was *doing* when she filled out the form, what the authority was *for*) rather than a claim that she's better off being treated. Then isolate the single feature of the case that's doing the work, show me what happens to all three verdicts if you delete it, and tell me how my friend the hospitalist would attack the case — she's smart and she'll say something about hindsight and about the whole point of the practice, so give me her strongest version, not a strawman. One more thing: I need to know how confident you are in each piece of this and, specifically, which part you're least sure of — I'd guess it's whether the rationale reading can even be stated without quietly collapsing back into a guess about her hypothetical wishes, but you tell me. If it can't be done to spec, say so rather than fudging, and tell me which constraint you had to drop.
