I'm a backend engineer at a company that ships a customer-support assistant built on top of a large language model. I have no philosophy background; two people on my team do (one studied some philosophy of language at university, the other reads it as a hobby), and at lunch they got into an argument about an incident I have to write up. They asked me to referee, I couldn't, and I don't think "you're both a bit right" is good enough for the write-up. I'd like you to adjudicate it properly. (The frame of reference they kept using was some philosopher called Grice — I'd never heard of him, and I'm not asking for a summary of his views, just for the argument to be settled on its merits.)

The incident. A customer writes in: "Can I get a refund on my ticket if I cancel?" The assistant has retrieval access to the booking record and knows the customer holds an Economy Flex ticket, which is refundable minus a fee. It replies in the first person, as our product is designed to do: "I've checked your booking. Economy Saver tickets are non-refundable, but you may be able to change your travel date for a fee." Every sentence it emitted is true of Saver tickets. Given that it had the actual fare class in front of it, the customer reasonably took the reply to mean that her ticket was non-refundable, and she never applied for the refund she was owed.

One further fact, which Nadia knows because she works on the post-training pipeline: our reward model was fitted to human preference judgements, and our raters reliably preferred shorter answers with qualifications of this kind dropped. The brevity is not an artefact of summarisation; suppressing the caveat was selected for during training.

The argument, as closely as I can reconstruct it.

1. Nadia: The assistant deceived the customer. The literal sentences were true, but what a speaker communicates is not only the literal content of the sentences. As I understood her, there is a standard distinction between what a sentence says and what someone means by uttering it, and most of what we actually communicate is of the second kind. By answering with the Saver rule instead of the Flex rule, it conveyed that the customer's own ticket was non-refundable. That is false, and the customer was entitled to take it that way.

2. Owen: The assistant isn't a speaker at all. On the account he has in mind, meaning something by an utterance requires intending to produce a belief in your audience, and intending to produce it by way of the audience's recognising that very intention. A language model is a distribution over tokens; there is no intention there, so there is nothing it means beyond what its sentences say. The extra step — "so my ticket must be non-refundable" — was taken by the customer. The model neither asserted nor conveyed anything false; if anyone misled anyone, it was us, for deploying it.

3. Nadia: Nobody is blaming the model in isolation; we blame the product, and it is not exotic to attribute what a system communicates to the people who built and shipped it — when the banking app says "your application has been received", we don't ask whether the server had intentions. What matters is what a reasonable customer was entitled to conclude and whether we built the system so that a reasonable customer would conclude it. And we chose the first-person framing deliberately because it tests better: we created the speaker, so we don't get to disown what it communicates.

4. Owen: That concedes too much, because on that standard every unclear output becomes a deception. More importantly it changes the subject: what Nadia is describing is misleading, and misleading is a different wrong from lying — a failure of disclosure and design rather than a false assertion, and the remedy is a spec ("always state the fare class the answer is based on"). And here is the test: if the assistant had answered with the Flex rule to a Saver customer, it would have been equally uninformative and would have left that customer better off, yet nobody would call it a lie. So the false-assertion framing cannot be doing the work.

5. Nadia: The asymmetry proves her point. What makes this case objectionable is the direction of the error relative to the customer's interests, not the presence of a false sentence — so the sentence-truth criterion Owen started from is doing no work. Nor does calling this communicative make every terse answer deceptive: the expectation is only that a speaker be as informative as this exchange requires, and this exchange required the fare class. Finally, the omission was not incidental. Our reward model was fitted to preference data in which raters reliably preferred shorter answers with the qualifications dropped; suppressing exactly this sort of caveat was trained into the system because it pleased human raters. That is as close to a standing intention as this artefact is going to have.

6. Owen: The training fact shows the failure is systematic and worth fixing, and he will concede that the customer was misled and that we are responsible for it. But a disposition to emit shorter text is not an intention that anyone believe anything. And "as informative as the exchange requires" is empty until someone says what the exchange requires — for an open-ended assistant answering arbitrary questions, that is precisely what we cannot specify in advance, which is an argument for writing an explicit, checkable disclosure requirement rather than for borrowing a theory built for human speakers. His last point: if we call this lying, the word has no stopping point, and we lose the ability to distinguish it from a summariser that simply drops a detail.

They stopped there, both convinced and both slightly annoyed.
