I'm running a 75-minute seminar on Wittgenstein for about 16 first-years. They've read the family-resemblance passages in the Investigations but they keep hearing it as "there's really a definition, it's just a fuzzy one." Design an activity that makes them feel the opposite: they have to extend a made-up category to new cases, and no definitions or rule-statements are allowed -- they can only put cases to a trainer and get a yes or no back.

My idea, see if you can build on it: split the room in half and train each half on different but overlapping sets of examples, arranged so the overlap is a chain (A resembles B resembles C...) rather than a shared core, so the halves agree in the middle and come apart at the ends. Then one student who was never trained has to decide whether the two halves were playing the same game at all. Give me a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money -- I've got index cards and a projector, that's about it -- and don't let the facilitator name the view until after it's over.

Then a debrief that maps the moments that actually happened onto the idea, what will predictably go wrong (someone smuggles in a definition anyway; the halves negotiate a compromise rule and it turns into rule-making; half the room concludes it's all just subjective), and be honest about which part of family resemblance the game can't produce and I'll have to supply in my own words.
