I'm giving a departmental colloquium in philosophy next month — an hour, with roughly twenty minutes of questions afterward — on a paper I've been circulating under the title "Verisimilitude on a Budget." I need a Q&A prep sheet written for the specific room I'll be in, not a defense of the view in the abstract.

Here is the thesis, and the sketch of the argument the objections should target.

Truthlikeness, I argue, is not a two-place relation between a theory and the world. It is a four-place relation between a theory, an agenda of questions, a menu of available inquiries, and a horizon. Fix a logical space of possible worlds and an actual world; take a theory T as a set of worlds (or the proposition it expresses). Let Q be a partition of logical space whose cells are the complete answers to the questions on a field's current agenda. Let M be the menu of observations or experiments the field can actually perform, and let b be a budget: the number of further inquiries available before the results have to be in. Then the truthlikeness of T relative to (Q, M, b) is the expected accuracy, measured over the questions in Q and evaluated at the actual world, of the best policy open to a researcher who starts out accepting T, may spend up to b inquiries drawn from M, and then has to answer every question in Q.

Two consequences I embrace rather than apologize for. First, T can be more truthlike than T* at one horizon and less truthlike at another, and the ordering need not be transitive across horizons; I claim this captures something real about scientific disagreement, since a crude, robust, cheaply repaired theory can be closer to the truth for a field with the resources it actually has than a precise theory whose errors are very expensive to locate and fix, even if the precise one wins at the limit. Second, relevance and error-cost stop being free parameters: a consequence of T matters just in case it bears on some question in Q, and the cost of one of T's errors is just the share of the budget it takes to detect and repair it.

The paper motivates the proposal in three steps: the content-and-consequence approach as Popper set it up, together with the 1974 results (Tichý, Harris, and Miller, arrived at independently) showing that on the natural definitions no false theory can be closer to the truth than another, because strengthening a false theory so as to add true consequences adds false ones too; the likeness approach that measures distance from the actual world within logical space (proposed by Tichý and Hilpinen, developed especially by Niiniluoto and Oddie), where the choice of metric, weighting, and respects of similarity is doing all the work; and the "relevant consequence" approaches (Schurz and Weingartner, Gemes), which I take to have the right instinct but to owe us a principled account of relevance.
