Hi — I teach a second-year philosophy course called "Fair Division" (cross-listed with politics, around 20 students, one 80-minute seminar slot, projector plus printed handouts, and I'd rather not use real money so I pay out in squares of chocolate and raffle tickets for a book voucher). Last week we did the Nash bargaining solution: they can recite the axioms and find the product-maximising point on a chart by eye. Next week I want them to meet the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, but I don't want to write a formula on the board and say "notice it uses the ideal point instead of the product" — I want them to run into the problem before it has a name.

Here's the half-baked idea I've been kicking around since an argument with a colleague in the econ department who thinks the whole KS critique is a technicality. Pairs get a chart of a feasible set plus a disagreement point and negotiate for real (tiny) stakes, in two rounds: first a simple triangle, then the very same triangle with one extra feasible option bolted onto the side on a seperate slip of paper — an option that only looks attractive to the first player, doesn't raise anybody's best case, and is never going to be agreed to. My guess is that the player-2s come out worse in round two even though nothing was taken away and nobody's ideal payoff moved, and that the rule already in their notes does exactly the same thing. When I did the arithmetic on scaled-up charts — triangle (0,0), (20,0), (10,10) against the same triangle with (18,4) added — I got the product-max point sliding from (10,10) to roughly (11.7, 8.75), i.e. player 2 loses about 1.25 units for no reason at all; if those numbers are wrong, or if some other pair of charts makes the effect bigger and cleaner, please redo them and show the working.

Three constraints that matter to me. The two players should be paid in different, non-comparable currencies (chocolate for one, tickets for the other, no exchange rate announced), because the moment both sides are in the same units my students just split the difference down the middle and nothing interesting happens at all. Nobody should hear "monotonicity" or "Kalai–Smorodinsky" until after the last round — ideally they invent "compare how big a fraction of your own best case you're getting" themselves, and only then notice that this rule behaves differently when the menu grows. And I've got one maths-econ double major who will compute the product-max point out loud within ninety seconds, so I need a way to use him rather than fight him. I'd also like the debrief to contain the mirror-image moment: a chart where *their* new proportional rule is the one that does the ugly thing (I think it's the axiom the Nash rule satisfies that this one breaks, but please construct the actual example), so the session doesn't end with everyone convinced the new rule simply wins.

Can you design this for me? Please give me: "Materials and setup" (~150 words), "The charts, with the arithmetic checked" (~250 words), "Minute-by-minute" (~400 words), "Where the idea bites" (~250 words, mapping specific moments in the room onto the formal difference between the two rules), "Debrief" (~300 words), "What will go wrong the first time" (~200 words), and "What the activity can't do" (~100 words). If free negotiation is too unreliable to land the effect, say so and build the session around something that is reliable — e.g. an arbitrator round where the class has to fix a number for a pair they'll never meet and then defend it to the losing side — but keep it something the students do rather than something I say.
