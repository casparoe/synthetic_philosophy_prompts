I'm finishing a PhD in theoretical physics — my thesis work is on coarse-graining and dimensional reduction in large networks of coupled oscillators — so the vocabulary I actually think in runs roughly as follows: microstates and macrovariables, coarse-graining maps, renormalization-group flow, relevant, marginal and irrelevant operators, fixed points and universality, order parameters and effective degrees of freedom, weakly versus strongly emergent levels, dynamical closure (or autonomy) of a level of description, decoherence and branch structure, and the difference between a genuine part–whole relation and a merely many-to-one map between descriptions of the same system. Beyond a first-year survey course I have no philosophy training. A reading group I attend with some philosophers spent two sessions this term on panpsychism and Russellian monism, and I came away both convinced that the combination problem is the real crux of the view and uneasy about how it is posed: several of the formulations seemed to lean on a notion of composition that my vocabulary simply does not contain, and I want to know whether that is a limitation of my vocabulary or a sign that the problem is less well posed than it looks.

So here is what I would like, and I would like it done as a systematic translation rather than as an explanation pitched at a physicist. Take the combination problem in its usual parts — the subject-summing problem, the quality-combination problem, the grain problem, the structural-mismatch worry about the neural correlates, and the reverse or decomposition argument that runs from our own unified consciousness back down to a supposed plurality of micro-subjects — and restate each of them as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I listed above. Then give me the residue report, in three columns of seriousness: which claims translate cleanly into levels, coarse-graining maps and effective degrees of freedom; which translate only if you distort them, with an explicit note of what the distortion is; and which have no expression in that vocabulary at all. I want the translation to be honest, which means I want you to flag, as you go, the places where a commitment of my framework is doing work rather than merely carrying content — for instance, that coarse-graining maps are not part–whole relations, that fundamental physical properties are specified structurally or dispositionally by their role in the dynamics, and that the choice of a level is in practice made pragmatically, by whatever question one is asking.

There are three things I have been turning over that I would specifically like you to stress-test rather than humour. First, call it the direction-of-detail objection: coarse-graining generically throws information away, washing micro-detail out as irrelevant, so that macro-descriptions are thinner than the micro-descriptions they come from — and yet it is the macroscopic phenomenology that is supposed to be the rich one, containing colour and the rest. So either the richness has to be generated at the macro level by new effective degrees of freedom, in which case nothing is being combined and the view has quietly become emergentism, forfeiting whatever advantage panpsychism was supposed to have over brute or strong emergence; or the panpsychist needs micro-phenomenal richness to be preserved or concentrated under coarse-graining, which is a constraint I cannot see any familiar coarse-graining map satisfying. Is that dilemma real, and which horn do defenders actually take? Second, the grain problem looks to me like a scale-relativity problem: the renormalization group offers a continuum of scales and no privileged one, and scales get picked out either by scale separation, by closure of the dynamics at a level, or by the resolution of an observer or instrument. The fix I have been toying with is that consciousness attaches to the level of maximum dynamical closure — the coarsest description that is still closed under its own dynamics — but I cannot tell whether that is a genuine constraint or whether it collapses into something like maximization of integrated information over scales, or into the unpalatable claim that which subject exists is observer-relative. Third, the decoherence analogy: branching never literally fuses many branches into one, it only renders them mutually inaccessible, and I wonder whether the analogous move for subjects — micro-subjects never fuse, there is merely mutual inaccessibility, or one subject per decoherent branch — is available to the panpsychist, and whether it would count as solving the summing problem or as restating it.

Finally, the part I care about most: for whatever does not survive the translation, tell me whether that is because my vocabulary lacks the resources — no slot for intrinsic, non-structural properties, and arguably no slot for subjects at all — or because the original claim was doing less work than its formulation suggests. And tell me what a positive solution would even have to look like in my terms: is a composition rule for subjects supposed to be a map on state spaces, a mereological operation, or something that cannot be expressed as either? If the honest answer is that the residue is entirely on my side of the translation, say so and say what would have to be added to a physics-style framework for the problem to be statable in it. I would rather be told that than be given a tidy restatement that quietly assumes what was in dispute.
