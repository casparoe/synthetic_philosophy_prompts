Last night a friend and I spent several hours arguing about higher-order evidence, and we eventually agreed to stop arguing about labels and instead write down what we think about cases. Her view, roughly: whenever I get evidence that my reasoning on some question is unreliable — a peer disagrees with me, or I learn that I am badly calibrated on questions of that kind, or I learn that I am tired, drunk, or distracted — that is always also a reason to lower my confidence in, or suspend judgment about, the first-order question itself. My view: often it is, but there are cases where the higher-order evidence is itself unreliable in just the way it says I am, and there it cannot do that work without sliding into suspension about everything. We could not find formulations sharp enough to determine whether we really disagree, so I want a classificatory instrument rather than more argument.

What I would like is a decision tree, built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: the questions first, the combinations of answers they permit second, and only then the actual positions located in that space. Take as the entry commitment something we both accept, namely that evidence about the reliability of one's own reasoning can be relevant to what one should believe about the matter at hand; from there, every branch node should be a single question, phrased as a concrete case or a claim, that someone who has never heard the term higher-order evidence could answer in under a minute, with two to four answer options. No branch question may ask a respondent which view they accept, which author they agree with, or how confident they are in some general principle; the point is to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like.

Here are nine candidate branch cases; use eight of them, or merge two if you think a different split does more diagnostic work, and tell me which you dropped and why.

1. Peer disagreement. You and a colleague of equal competence and carefulness, working from the same data, reach different conclusions about whether the data show an effect. Do you move toward the midpoint, suspend judgment entirely, hold your confidence unchanged, or take any confidence in the interval between the two to be permitted?

2. Tainted source. You believe a city's crime rate is rising because you read a run of reports; you then learn the reports were selected for publication by an advocacy campaign that wanted that conclusion. Have you learned something about crime, or have you only lost your basis for a view you still have?

3. Calibration. You know that on hard yes-or-no questions of a certain kind, when you feel eighty-five percent confident you are right about sixty percent of the time. The question you are now considering is of that kind and you feel eighty-five percent confident. Do you reduce to sixty, keep eighty-five and add a second-order sixty, suspend, or hold eighty-five on the grounds that you would defer to a better calibrated version of yourself?

4. Iterated defeat. You learn that you are in a condition — sleep-deprived, medicated, three glasses of wine in — that degrades your reasoning, and that this very assessment is made in that condition. Does the correction apply once and stop, does it apply to everything including itself, does it undermine itself so that nothing changes, or does it reach only domains where you have independent evidence of impairment?

5. The model's disagreement. You ask a language model a factual question on which you have a well-grounded and confident answer; it disagrees. All you know about it is that on a benchmark drawn from the same body of text you have both read it is right about eighty percent of the time, and that when it errs it errs in the same direction as the familiar human bias on that question type. Is this peer disagreement, first-order testimony about the world, higher-order evidence about relying on a shared corpus, or nothing at all because its errors are correlated with yours?

6. Forgotten evidence. Three years ago you read a careful study and formed a seventy percent credence. You now remember the credence and that you judged the study careful, but not its content; today you learn that you have repeatedly misremembered studies as careful when they were not. Do you revert toward your earlier prior, keep seventy percent and add a second-order worry, treat the retained credence as self-standing, or suspend?

7. Uniqueness. For a fixed body of evidence, is there exactly one doxastic attitude that rationality permits, and if so does it depend on anything about the subject beyond the evidence, such as her priors or her costs of inquiry?

8. Akratic combination. Can there be a subject who sincerely believes that p, sincerely believes that her total evidence does not support believing that p, and is fully aware of both at once? If yes, is that combination always a defect, sometimes exactly what rationality requires, a defect for credence but not for full belief, or is the state psychologically impossible so that talk of akrasia is a category error and the real requirement is to investigate, or to manage inquiry?

9. Action. When a first-order attitude and a conflicting second-order attitude coexist, should the attitude that guides action be the first-order one, the second-order one, or a merged one; or is no single combination required?

Constrain the leaves to at most ten, and make sure these appear among them: conciliationism as split-the-difference, conciliationism as suspension, steadfastness or the total-evidence treatment of higher-order evidence, a permissivism-based response, the view that mismatch between first-order and second-order attitudes is always a rational defect, the view that mismatch is sometimes what rationality requires, the involuntarist dissolution, global suspension of a broadly Pyrrhonian kind, and fragmentation. Where a leaf has no established name, mark it unnamed and give it a one-line description. Each leaf should carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Two further things. First, flag at least two pairs of leaves that your tree cannot in principle separate, and say what extra question would be needed to separate them; do not paper over the overlap. Second, list the combinations of answers the eight questions allow that no actual view occupies, and for each say briefly whether nobody is there because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The audience is a reading group of six, half of them formal epistemologists and half ethicists, all comfortable with probabilities and none of them specialists in this literature; assume no library access, and give me no bibliography, no citations, and no suggestions for further reading. Present the result as a tree, or as a numbered list of branch nodes with cross-references if that is clearer, followed by the leaves, the unseparated pairs, and the unoccupied cells.
