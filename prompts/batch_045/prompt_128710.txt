I'm TAing a mixed upper-level seminar — maybe 18 students, about two thirds philosophy majors and the rest cognitive science people who've had one lab course — and next Thursday I've got a 75-minute slot to do Dennett's ladder of kinds of minds: Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian. I do not want to lecture at them for 40 minutes and then do "discussion"; last year someone ran exactly that with 20 Questions and it turned into a lecture anyway. I want them to hit the differences in their own performance first and only get the vocabulary at the end.

The thesis I want them to feel, and this is the bit I care most about because it's my own pet distinction and I've never seen it staged: the tiers don't differ mainly in how good the *filter* is, they differ in what *generates the candidates*. A Skinnerian creature and a Popperian one can be running the same crappy generator; the Popperian one just gets to kill the bad candidates somewhere cheaper than in the world. What makes a Gregorian creature different in kind is that the generator itself has been rebuilt by imported hardware — notations, words, toys — so it proposes hypotheses it would never otherwise have thought of. And the sting: those tools were mostly designed by other creatures for other problems, so the Gregorian animal is running cranes that fit somebody else's environment and it didn't get to choose them.

So: three rounds of a hidden-rule game on the same table. Round 1, they can only act on the world and eat the consequences — no notes, no talk, and each submission costs real time or tokens. Round 2, they may write candidate rules down and cross them out, and can probe cheaply, but only from hypotheses they could have come up with unaided. Round 3, they get a kit: a plastic folder of artifacts and last year's annotated notes, where most of the kit is tuned to the rule we used last year (say a vowel-count chart and syllable wheel) but which also contains one schematic that makes this year's actual rule — maybe something about alphabetical ordering of letters — obvious at a glance. I want the scoring comparable across rounds, and I'd like round 3 to beat round 2 on average despite the misleading notes, because otherwise they'll conclude tools are a trap. If it helps, they could hand the annotated kit to the next table afterwards so you get a whiff of cumulative culture. Also, one student definately already knows the Dennett material and will say so early; tell me how to use them rather than shut them up.

Give me:

- "What they should walk out feeling" — about 100 words
- "Setup and materials" — about 120 words
- "The three rounds, as handed to students" — about 400 words, actual rules and constraints they see, no explanation
- "The hidden rule and the scoring" — about 150 words, including how you tune round 3 to still win
- "Debrief" — about 350 words, mapping specific moments in the game onto each of the four tiers and onto the generator/filter distinction
- "What will go wrong" — about 200 words, with fixes
- "What this can't show" — about 150 words, i.e. what I have to say out loud because the game can't do it
