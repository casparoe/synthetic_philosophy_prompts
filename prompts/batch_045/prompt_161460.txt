I work in freight logistics. I left school at eighteen, and for the past two years I have been reading about self-deception on my own in the evenings: no teacher, no library, no database access, just whatever I can get hold of and the cases I make up with a friend. That friend, Dana, and I meet for coffee most weeks and put concrete cases to each other. Dana reads nothing on the subject at all; her rulings come entirely from the cases themselves. She is stubborn and unusually consistent, and I have started to suspect she is running some rule she has never stated.

What I want from you is a prediction about a case I have not yet put to her. Her rulings so far (numbered so you can refer to them):

1. A school vice principal tells her colleagues for months that the principal's job is as good as hers, after reading the same all-staff email everyone else read announcing the shortlist, which does not include her. Dana: self-deception.
2. A man scrolls his former fiancée's social feed every night and tells his brother he is just keeping up with old friends. Dana: self-deception.
3. A smoker seeks out industry-funded research specifically, reads it carefully, and then sincerely cites it while arguing with his daughter. Dana: self-deception.
4. A woman uses a paid conversation app for mood support; the app only ever reflects warm summaries of her friendships back to her, and she has come to believe she is much liked. She chose it for comfort rather than accuracy, and it has never once occurred to her that it might be flattering her. Dana: self-deception, but she called it the thinnest of the first four.
5. A shop owner says she is certain the business will break even this year, and in the same fortnight quietly updates her résumé and asks a former manager for coffee. Dana: self-deception, and she said this was the plainest case yet.
6. A juror who was shown, before the trial began, a re-edited clip of the incident, and who now sincerely believes the defendant did it. Dana: not self-deception.
7. A man who believes the room is draughty because a stage hypnotist suggested it to him, and who has no stake in the matter either way. Dana: not self-deception.
8. A manager who tells the team he fully supports Denise's promotion, while quietly routing every visible project to someone else and privately thinking she is not ready. Dana: not self-deception; she called that one lying, or cowardice.
9. A patient told by a careful but mistaken doctor that her scan is clear, who believes she is well. Dana: not self-deception.

There is one complication you should weigh. When I once pressed Dana on whether anyone can really lie to herself, she said that the closest thing to a principle she has is that you don't lie to yourself, you just stop looking. Taken strictly, that remark seems to rule out 1 through 5 as well, which she plainly does not want. So part of your job is to say what rule makes her remark and her rulings jointly intelligible, if any does, and what it costs.

The new case. Marla is sixty-two, a retired accountant. Over about eighteen months she has been losing names, missing two appointments she had written down, and getting lost driving home from a place she drives to weekly. She told her sister this is normal ageing. She has not been tested and has declined twice when her doctor raised it. She instructed her doctor in writing to tell her nothing unless the news is good, and she has asked the assistant who handles her post to pass on nothing from the neurology clinic. When her sister asked her directly whether she is avoiding being tested because she is frightened of what it would show, she answered at once and without discomfort: yes, that is exactly what I am doing; I know I am frightened, I know I have arranged things so that I will not find out, and I still think there is nothing wrong with me. If something were wrong I would deal with it. There isn't. And nothing else in her life has moved: she still drives at night, booked a cruise for next spring, renewed her passport, and has asked no one to prepare for anything.

Second case, which I mention because I think it pulls in a different direction. Marla's brother-in-law Ray had the same worries three years ago and dealt with them by handing the whole thing to his adult daughter: she opens every medical letter and tells him nothing unless the news is good. He has not thought about it since. He now believes he is fine, would be shocked and angry to learn what his daughter has been doing, and in three years it has not once crossed his mind that he might be avoiding anything.

Please do the following.

Say which two to four rules are each consistent with rulings 1 through 9, stated sharply enough that they could disagree somewhere. For each, say what it predicts about Marla and about Ray. Give your best guess at Dana's verdict on Marla, with a confidence between 0 and 100 and the reasons you prefer that rule over its nearest rival. Be honest about where the rules underdetermine the answer, and tell me plainly if one of the rules you favour has the consequence that self-deception turns out to be rare or close to impossible, since that is the question I actually care about. Then give me one further case, concrete and short, that I can put to Dana at our next coffee, chosen so that the two strongest rules disagree on it, and tell me what her answer would mean either way. Do not cite, quote, or recommend any books, papers, or authors: I have no way to look them up, and I want the reasoning to come from the cases themselves rather than from anyone's theory.

Keep the prose under nine hundred words. After it, add a table with exactly these columns:

- rule_name: a short label I can use, such as deliberate-arrangement or motivation-only
- rule_statement: one sentence stating what the rule requires for something to count as self-deception
- verdict_marla: YES, NO, or UNDERDETERMINED
- verdict_ray: YES, NO, or UNDERDETERMINED
- verdicts_explained: which of Dana's rulings 1 through 9 this rule gets right, by number
- verdicts_strained: which ruling or rulings this rule has to explain away, by number, including her remark about stopping looking
- tells_apart_from: the one further case that would separate this rule from its strongest rival, in a single sentence
- cost_if_true: one sentence on what is lost or becomes puzzling if this is the rule Dana is running

Please write in American English.
