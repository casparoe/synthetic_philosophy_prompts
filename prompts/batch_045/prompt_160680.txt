I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy or economics, but I help run a small internal prediction market at work, and I've gotten into a running argument with two colleagues on the infrastructure team about how much of a bankroll to stake when the odds are genuinely in your favour. We started from the standard Kelly setup — stake a fraction f of current wealth on a favourable even-money bet, wealth multiplies by (1+f) on a win and (1−f) on a loss, and the growth-optimal fraction is f* = 2p−1 — and I've ended up writing the notes below, because I think the usual story quietly depends on a special feature of the dynamics that disappears the moment you add anything realistic, such as a fixed cost per round. (My day job involves paying per-request fees for compute, so overhead that doesn't scale with your stake feels like the normal case rather than the exception.) Before I post this to our reading group, I'd like a careful critique; the group has no philosophers in it either, so please explain any decision-theoretic vocabulary you use, and don't worry about references or further reading, since I'm not planning to cite anything.

The notes, in condensed form:

1. Baseline. Even-money bet, P(win) = p > 1/2, stake fraction f of current wealth W. Expected log growth per round is g(f) = p·ln(1+f) + (1−p)·ln(1−f), maximised at f* = 2p−1. I take two properties to be doing the real work: additivity (log wealth after T rounds is a sum of independent, identically distributed increments, so the myopic rule that maximises this round's expected log growth is also optimal for every horizon) and scale invariance (the optimal fraction depends only on p and the odds, not on W). I've also seen it claimed that overbetting past roughly twice the Kelly fraction drives g negative, but I've never checked whether that second threshold is exactly 2f* or merely close to it.

2. The perturbation. Add a fixed overhead c charged in currency units every round regardless of stake (fees, gas, API costs): W′ = W ± fW − c, and the log increment becomes ln(1 ± f − c/W). Writing down the myopic first-order condition p/(1+f−c/W) = (1−p)/(1−f−c/W) and solving gives f(W) = (2p−1)(1 − c/W) = f*·(1 − c/W). Two consequences I want to lean on: (i) with overhead you should always bet strictly less than the frictionless Kelly fraction, and the haircut grows as wealth shrinks; (ii) substituting this f back into the growth expression, maximal growth is positive only while c/W stays below some critical value, and once c/W exceeds it no available policy grows — worse, shrinking W pushes you further past the threshold, so there's a self-reinforcing trap that the frictionless model cannot even represent. Note also that abstaining no longer costs nothing (f = 0 gives W′ = W − c), so the option to sit out a round disappears.

3. Where I think this bites. Myopia should now fail: the increment depends on W, so maximising this round's expected log growth need not maximise E[ln W_T]. I want to argue for a specific direction — that the horizon-dependent optimum is more conservative than the myopic rule when W is near the trap, on the grounds that expected log growth as a function of W is increasing and concave in W, so extra dispersion in future wealth is costly and a long-run optimiser should buy less of it than a myopic one does. I'm much less confident about this step than about §2, and it's the part I most want checked.

4. The philosophical punchline I'm gesturing at. "Maximise your long-run growth rate" isn't even well defined once the dynamics aren't multiplicative, yet the choice problem remains perfectly well defined. So the geometric/time-average style of argument used to motivate Kelly needs a generalisation, and I see two: either fall back on maximising E[ln W_T] over a fixed horizon, which is just expected-utility maximisation with a logarithmic utility function — in which case the dynamical motivation for log collapses into a claim about preferences, and the whole "time average versus ensemble average" framing was never the real engine — or define a growth rate for a state-dependent process (something like the almost-sure exponential rate of the process) and maximise that, which restores a time-average notion but can then come apart from maximising E[ln W_T]. My claim is that the first option is a trap that concedes the interesting thesis, and the second is where the real content lies.

What I'd like from you: (a) a check on the technical claims in §2 and §3, especially the first-order condition, the concavity argument, and the "about 2f*" threshold, with corrections worked through rather than asserted; (b) your view on whether the dilemma in §4 is genuine — is there a natural third option, or does the standard view already have a stock answer to overhead that dissolves my problem; (c) whether any of this is actually new, or whether it's a special case of something a reader will recognise immediately as textbook (fractional Kelly, transaction-cost portfolio theory, or similar), because I'd rather not post something with false pretensions to originality; (d) the single weakest link and the strongest objection a defender of the standard growth-optimal line would raise; and (e) a suggestion for one small worked numerical illustration — concrete values of p, c and W, with the resulting fractions under the frictionless rule, the myopic rule, and whatever you think the long-run rule is — that would make the difference vivid to engineers who have never thought about any of this.
