The thesis, briefly, so you know what to attack. Everyone concedes the nonidentity problem makes 'we are harming future generations' shaky: on any realistic emissions path, the specific people alive in 2120 wouldn't have existed under a different path, so the person-affecting harm condition looks like it fails and we're pushed toward something impersonal — total utilitarianism, or a social welfare function where nobody in particular needs a complaint. I want to block that. My move is to attach the duty to a position rather than to a person. Call it the office-holder view (placeholder name, I know it sounds like the title of a law review note). The idea: duties run to the occupants of roles that a continuing practice defines — 'resident of this polity in 2120', 'tenant of a unit in this housing stock in 2060', 'holder of this queue slot' — and an act is pro tanto wrong if it makes whoever fills the slot worse off than the person who would have filled that slot under the alternative act. So compare slot-to-slot, not person-to-person. There's a continuity clause to keep it from over-generating: a slot survives the counterfactual only if the practice constituting it is institutionally continuous across the two scenarios, which is why 'the child you didn't have' isn't a slot in the relevant sense unless some practice was going to assign someone to it. Practical bite, and this is what the economists care about: you get a person-affecting justification for a nontrivial carbon price without going impersonal, and it defuses the 'they'll be richer anyway, so what's the harm' move, because the comparison holds institutions roughly fixed and asks about the slot. But headcount drops out of the wrongness in a way the total utilitarian will hate, and the view gives you no reason to create extra slots. My old advisor says it definately collapses into something like the neutrality view about adding happy people; I don't think it does, but I'm not confident.
