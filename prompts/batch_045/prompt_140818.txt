I'm giving a talk in three weeks at a joint session of my philosophy department's ethics workshop and the disability bioethics reading group, and I'd like a Q&A prep sheet. About 25 people, and I roughly know who's in the room: two people who've written on the argument from marginal cases, a comparative neuroanatomist who thinks the isotropic fractionator is a glorified soup method, someone from critical animal studies who regards the whole "welfare range" research programme as a legitimation device for humane exploitation, three effective-altruism-adjacent grad students who just want a multiplier to paste into a spreadsheet, and a clinical neuroethicist who has actually sat with families after paediatric hemispherectomies.

The thesis I'm defending: among animals we're already confident are sentient, moral weight per unit time is scalar rather than flat, and the least-bad single-number proxy for it is the count of neurons in whatever structure does the binding (pallium, cortex, or the functional analogue), not whole-brain mass, not whole-brain neuron count, and not encephalization quotient — because the cerebellum holds something like four fifths of the brain's neurons and doesn't seem to be doing anything relevant, so whole-brain counts are actively misleading. My argument is three steps: (1) what matters is the richness of an animal's experiential repertoire at a time; (2) binding-structure neuron count is a decent proxy for that richness; (3) the proxy is only licensed to scale welfare *range* within a "sentience-secure" set, applied at the species-typical level, and never used to trade off whole lives and never applied to individual humans. I've got three moves I rely on and want stress-tested: the "grain vs. volume" distinction (extra neurons might buy finer discrimination rather than more intense experience, and I'm not sure which one I need), the "frame-rate offset" (smaller animals may pack more experiential moments into a clock second, which could cancel the intensity gap), and the "backup-neuron" worry (maybe extra neurons are redundancy, not new states — what would settle that?). I also lean hard on a species-typical ceiling to avoid saying anything about individual humans with atypical brains, and I'm definately least confident there.

So: give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do *in that room* rather than how good they are in the abstract, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it — the neuroanatomist mutters about shrinkage and fixation and n=3 species, the critical animal studies person says "legitimation," the marginal-cases people use the vocabulary of status and recognition rather than welfare — with a two-minute reply for each and a note on where the reply is softest. Tell me what I should concede in the first two slides rather than be forced into later, tell me which single reply has to land for the talk to survive at all, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks (I think it's the hemispherectomy case: children who lose half their cortex and turn out fine, which by my own metric should have halved their moral weight and plainly didn't — but if you think there's a worse one, use that). Also, one of the spreadsheet grad students will recieve your prep sheet too, so please don't pad it with citations, a reading list, or "see so-and-so 2019" — I can't look anything up from the podium and half the room won't care about the literature anyway.
