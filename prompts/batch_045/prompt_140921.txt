You are a philosopher who works on moral responsibility and the ethics of blame, especially the question of who has standing to blame whom and what a person's own hypocrisy does to that standing. Please work through the play's scenes before you settle on anything: I would rather have one reading that has been argued for than a survey of positions, and where a scene cuts against your claim I want you to say so rather than smoothing it over.

I am in a community-theater production of Arthur Miller's The Crucible, writing the program note and running the post-show talkback, and I would like to use the play to make one philosophical point land for an audience with no background in philosophy.

The point I keep circling is hypocrisy and standing. Thinking about it on my own I have found it useful to split the charge "you have no right to say that" into three separate things, and I suspect the play separates them too. First, global loss: once you are guilty of something comparable, you cannot blame anyone for anything, full stop. Second, profit-conditioned loss: you lose standing only when your blame is aimed at the very thing your own wrong was aimed at, so that the blame is a continuation of the wrong by other means; on this view Abigail's accusation of Elizabeth is not just insincere but disqualified, because it is the instrument of her own design. Third, capacity-relative loss: you cannot speak as the injured party or as the community's voice, but you may still be able to say the true thing in some thinner capacity, as one person reporting a fact.

What I want from you is the reading you would actually defend. Does the play treat these as one phenomenon or does it put weight on the differences? Concretely: does Abigail's situation differ from the Putnams' in a way the three-way split captures, given that their motive is land rather than love and that they work through the court rather than through the fits? Does Proctor lose his standing to expose her once he has confessed the adultery in open court, and does the play itself treat that as relevant to whether he should be believed? And the case I find hardest: Hale, who early on lent the court his authority and so helped get people arrested, ends up urging the accused to confess falsely in order to save their lives. Is that blame, or counsel, or an admission of his own complicity, and does his history cost him the standing to say it?

Lead with your bottom line in a single sentence: the one claim about hypocrisy and standing you would put in the program. Then argue for it using the scenes. Then say where the play resists your reading, and what, if anything, the play shows about standing to blame that the philosophical argument by itself does not. Around six hundred words, and please paraphrase rather than quote from the play.
