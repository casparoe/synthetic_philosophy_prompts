I'm in a philosophy of technology reading group this term and we keep going in circles about whether artifacts have politics — and by that I don't mean whether their designers had politics, or whether people happen to use them for political ends, but whether the thing itself can be political in the way the classic case is supposed to show: a parkway built with overpasses too low for buses to pass under, so that people without cars (in the version I was told, mostly poor and mostly Black beachgoers) simply can't reach the beach. My roommate, who builds warehouse robots, says that's obviously just a story about the intentions of whoever built the road plus the transit system around it, and the concrete is innocent; I think there's something to the idea that a design can carry authority or coercion in it even once the intentions have faded, but I can't say what, and apparently historians now argue about whether the low-bridge story is even true as told — which is part of why I'd rather have criteria than a verdict.

So before we read any more of this literature, I want the spec: what does an account of the politics of artifacts have to deliver to be worth taking seriously? The list of things such a theory has to handle, with the case behind each, and your view on which are genuine desiderata and which are just the hobbyhorses of one camp (my supervisor, for instance, insists that any adequate account must be able to say whether a given artifact is unjust, not merely political — is that a requirement or a prejudice?). Cases I want covered: the overpasses, where both the intent and the facts are contested; machines whose political effects nobody chose and nobody can now undo, like a harvesting machine that dissolved an entire class of farm jobs, or a file format everyone adopted and that it's now ruinously expensive to leave — I've started calling these ratchets, where the politics seems to live in an asymmetry: cheap to adopt, expensive to reverse, and nobody ever signed off on the asymmetry; the same artifact used for opposite political purposes by two different groups, like an encrypted messenger used by dissidents in one country and by a cartel in another; technologies that seem to require a political arrangement in a strong sense, of the sort people gesture at when they say you can't have nuclear weapons without a centralized secretive state, versus ones that look flexible all the way down; and the boring case — the brick, the bridge with normal clearance, the paperclip — because whatever the account is, it shouldn't end up calling literally every object political, or it has said nothing.

Two more things I want flagged. First, the distinctions people keep collapsing, and which of those collapses are fatal: having a politics versus having political effects, the politics of the artifact versus the politics of whoever makes, owns or regulates it, being political versus being inherently or essentially so, and whether the notion doing the work is coercion, authority, or mere differential advantage. Second, if some subset of your desiderata can't be satisfied together — it looks to me like handling unintended structural politics pushes toward saying almost everything has a politics, which is exactly the overgeneration I want to block — say so, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories. Give me exactly eight numbered items, no intro and no closing: items 1 through 7 are the desiderata, each a sentence or two with the motivating case and a note on whether it's non-negotiable or a preference of one camp; item 8 is the pair you think can't be jointly met, plus the one requirement that knocks out the most theories. Don't bother scoring anyone's actual theory against the list, we'll do that ourselves in the group.
