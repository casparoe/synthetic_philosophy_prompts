I write the programme notes for a small chamber-music series, and I have started to worry about something in how those notes work on an audience. I have a practical stake in it, but what I want from you is identification rather than advice: I want to know whether what I have noticed has a name, and, more importantly, exactly where its edges fall.

Three cases.

(i) A friend whose ear I trust tells me that a film is hollow and self-satisfied. I watch it, and I find it hollow and self-satisfied. But I cannot afterwards tell whether I judged that or whether I was handed the verdict in advance and spent the running time assembling confirmation. When someone tells me the train leaves at six I can check at the platform; here the thing that would have served as the check seems to have been pre-empted, and I cannot recover what I would have thought if I had known nothing.

(ii) The same friend tells me a novel is devastating. I read it, and I am unmoved. I do not conclude that she was wrong. I conclude that I read it badly, and I consider rereading it. Told that it was raining and then shown a dry street, I would simply withdraw the belief. The oddity here is where the failure gets allocated, and that it gets allocated to me rather than to the claim.

(iii) Yet description, as opposed to verdict, seems innocent to me. A note saying that a piece is a passacaglia built over a descending bass line does not feel as if it takes anything away; if anything it makes the listening better and more fully mine. A note saying that the same piece is one of the great works of the century feels as if it takes something away. I would like to know whether that contrast is the real boundary of the phenomenon or only a special case of it.

What I am asking about is not, I think, merely the familiar thought that second-hand judgments are unreliable, or that you have to encounter a thing for yourself. That slogan does not distinguish (i) from (ii), and it says nothing about (iii). The thing I keep circling seems to have three features: the verdict I am given cannot be traced back and undone; when experience and verdict conflict, the verdict is preserved and my experience is impugned; and descriptive content behaves differently from evaluative content.

I have no access to a university library, so lists of authors and papers are of no use to me. Tell me what the thing is, not where I would read about it. If there is an established concept here, I want to know what it is and how it is standardly formulated. If there is only something nearby, I want to know which of the nearby things fits best and why the others do not. If there is nothing, say so plainly and coin a term, defining it tightly.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no preamble and nothing after the fifth.

1. Whether what I have described has an established name, and, if not, whether anything close to it does.
2. The nearest established concepts, which I expect to be two or three, and what each of them actually claims.
3. How my three cases, and especially (ii), differ from each of those concepts.
4. If nothing established fits: a coined term and a definition precise enough to separate my cases from ordinary factual testimony on one side and from mere suggestion or persuasion on the other.
5. Two boundary cases of your own choosing: the hardest case you would still count in, and the nearest case you would count out, with your reason in each case.
