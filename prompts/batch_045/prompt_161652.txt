so i've got this friend — LW-adjacent, ex-history PhD, runs a tiny group chat where we argue about war guilt — and he's got a long track record of verdicts on "does the nation, as a collective, bear responsibility for this war, and who bears it primarily." his calls so far:

- germany 1914: responsible, and primary. he's a Fischer guy; he thinks the german leadership treated the july crisis as a window and that the sleepwalker framing, where everyone just stumbled in, is a cop-out
- austria-hungary 1914: responsible but not primary. he says the ultimatum to serbia wasn't blameworthy in itself — any great power of that era would have done something like it after an assassination — but using it as the trigger for a continental war was
- russia's full mobilization, july 1914: not responsible. once the german plan was on rails he thinks they had no real alternative
- france 1914: not responsible. dragged in by an alliance signed years earlier, and honoring it wasn't a choice you could refuse without strategic catastrophe
- britain 1914: not responsible for the outbreak; entering over belgian neutrality was a later and separate call, and a justified one
- the war-guilt clause at versailles: basically correct as a verdict about germany, whatever you think of what it was used to justify
- germany 1939: responsible, primary, not close
- US 2003: responsible, and "they were honestly wrong about the WMDs" is no excuse in his book, though it does make it a lesser thing than 1939

i've never managed to reverse-engineer the rule he's actually running. as far as i can tell at least three fit every call above: (a) blame tracks whether the people in the room had an open alternative at the moment of decision that didn't cost them everything; (b) blame tracks who took the first step that made war near-certain while knowing it would; (c) blame tracks deviation from what great powers of the era openly did. i've also got a fourth i half-invented and he's never endorsed — call it counterfactual ownership: you own the outcome if you built the machinery that made it the default, even if at the moment of decision nobody could have done otherwise. that one seems to fit his calls too, which is annoying.

so i built a case to try to split them. it's hypothetical, near-future, and i'm bringing it to the chat next week:

two nuclear powers, A and C, in a crisis over A's client B. a decade earlier both militaries moved to automated early warning plus standing mobilization timetables that their civilian leaderships never really revisited. when C mobilizes, A's plan requires striking through neutral D within 72 hours; A's generals tell A's civilians the clock can't be paused, and that's true. C's plan is also on rails, but C's generals tell C's civilians it can't be paused when in fact a five-day delay was available; the generals would rather fight now than later and the civilians have no particular reason to doubt them. A strikes first because its rail is shorter. C's counterstrike is automated. roughly ten million die and nobody wanted that scale.

what does he say — A primary, C primary, both equally, both somewhat, or nobody-as-a-nation — and which rule is he running? i care much more about the prediction and the reconstruction than about who's really to blame, so please don't just give me your own verdict dressed up as his. lay out what each of the candidate rules predicts about this case, give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, tell me whether "the nation" here is supposed to include the people who wrote the timetables ten years earlier (same institution, different individuals), and name the further case — the variant you'd actually want to bring him — that would tell the rules apart. and be explicit about how confident you are in each moving part, and single out the one bit you're least sure of, whether that's the prediction, the reconstruction of his rule, or some judgement about the scenario itself.
