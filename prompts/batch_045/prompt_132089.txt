For the last two years I have been the executor of my father's papers, and for the last six months a limited series about the industrial disaster he worked on in the 1970s has been in post-production. He appears under his own name for something like eleven minutes of screen time. In those minutes he is given a line the transcripts show he never said, he stays silent at a meeting where the record shows he raised the alarm, and a marriage that lasted thirty-eight years is compressed into one explanatory scene whose purpose is to motivate a decision he made for professional reasons. The producers have asked me to consult on a possible second season. My brother wants me to demand the scene be cut. My sister says a drama is not a deposition and that I am confusing my father with my father's reputation. My family has roughly one conversation left before this stops being a disagreement and becomes a rupture, and I would like to have thought it through before I spend it.

My father's notebooks and letters go to a university archive next spring under a twenty-five-year seal. So for the next two decades, for almost everyone who encounters him at all, this series will not compete with the record; it will be the record. That is the feature of the case I keep coming back to, and it is why I want the specification before I decide anything: what would an account of what we owe the real dead, when their lives are dramatized, have to get right to be good enough to decide a case like this one? Not a survey of the field and not a reading list, and I do not need the positions named after anyone.

The material I think the list has to handle, each with the case behind it:

The triple asymmetry: my father cannot be compensated, cannot correct the record, and cannot refuse. Whether the wrong, if there is one, is something done to him or only something done to the living who remember him, and whether those two tracks ever come apart — my brother argues as though it is entirely about us, my sister as though it is entirely about him, and neither has said why the difference should matter.

Identification: at what point does the character stop being my father? Compressed timelines, composite figures, invented scenes, and the special case where an invented scene is the one the archive most supports. I want to know whether that last case is the hard one or the easy one.

Whose norms settle what a fitting depiction is: his, as a man of a particular time, class, and profession, who would have found some of this flattering and some of it unimaginable, or ours, now.

The audience side, which I think is where the real weight is. The more a production signals research — real names, a credited consultant, photographs of the actual people under the titles — the more it borrows an epistemic authority it is not answerable to; and once it exists it overwrites the thinner and harder-to-reach archival memory, so that later audiences inherit the dramatization as their only access and cannot check it. I have started calling this the second death, but I cannot tell whether it is a genuine moral factor or a metaphor doing the work of an argument, and I would like that settled rather than assumed.

The distinctions I am not sure are real: between being remembered falsely and being remembered as having done wrong; between damage to a reputation and damage to a memory; between the person who made himself public and the private man pulled into somebody else's story, which is what my father was.

Remedies: a disclaimer, a based-on-real-events caption, an accompanying documentary, a right of reply deposited in the archive alongside the papers. Whether any of these discharge anything at all, or whether they only ever operate on the living.

And the conflict structure: whatever is owed here has to be weighable against what the makers are owed by way of expressive freedom and against what audiences are owed by way of understanding, and I want to know whether that is a genuine three-way conflict or whether one of the three is not a moral consideration of the same kind.

For each requirement, give me the case that motivates it and mark it as either non-negotiable or a preference of one temperament rather than another. Flag any pair you think cannot be jointly satisfied — my own suspicion is that respect for his anticipated wishes and fidelity to what the archive now supports pull apart, and that the duty to him and the epistemic duty to audiences pull apart — since if some subset is unsatisfiable that is itself the result I most need. Then, briefly, tell me which single requirement excludes the most of the things I keep hearing at the dinner table: that nothing can be owed because the dead are not harmed, that the estate's consent settles it, that artistic licence settles it, that only the famous are fair game, that truth is owed to the audience and never to the subject. Finally, say what would change the analysis if the dramatization were one among many rather than effectively the only record, and whether his being a minor figure in someone else's story, rather than its subject, should make his case easier or harder.
