The topic is van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin problem, in the version we used in seminar. A soldier is dropped by parachute into a square territory divided into a friendly half and an enemy half, each of which is subdivided into a headquarters-company region and a second-company region. Write B for friendly, R for enemy, H for headquarters-company, S for second-company. Her prior over the four regions is 3/8 for B∧H, 1/8 for B∧S, 1/8 for R∧H, 3/8 for R∧S, so that P(B) = 1/2, P(H | B) = 3/4, and P(H | R) = 1/4. Over a radio she hears: "If you are in enemy territory, you are in the headquarters-company region" — call this conditional M1. Treated as a proposition of her own algebra, M1 has prior probability 5/8, and conditionalizing on it takes P(B) from 1/2 to 4/5: good news, because the conditional rules out the large enemy/second-company cell.

Here is my half-formed idea. Every treatment I have seen hands Judy the conditional as flat, certain evidence. But real reception is noisy, and I suspect that once reception is uncertain the standard rules either stop applying or come apart in a way nothing in the seminar settles. Suppose the message might have been either M1 or M2 = "if you are in enemy territory, you are in the second-company region," and she would split 0.9 / 0.1 over which was broadcast. Three things I think I can show, and that I want the proposal to build on:

First, the candidate messages do not partition her possibility space. M1 and M2 overlap exactly in B and together cover everything, so for any distribution P(M1) + P(M2) = 1 + P(B). So the natural Jeffrey-style move — impose P*(M1) = 0.9 and P*(M2) = 0.1 and otherwise keep the prior's shape — is not merely unmotivated, it is catastrophic: the two constraints together force P*(B) = 0, i.e. certainty of being behind enemy lines, on the strength of a message that is good news on either reading. Jeffrey's rule has nothing to bite on here, because the uncertainty is not uncertainty about a partition of the space the hypothesis lives in.

Second, the two models of the noise that do make sense give different numbers. If she averages the two updates, she gets P*(B) = 0.9 × (4/5) + 0.1 × (4/7) ≈ 0.78, and note that this does not deliver P*(M1) = 0.9 — it delivers about 0.96, so the rule and the intuition about what the 0.9 was a confidence in come apart. If instead she heard M1 distinctly but distrusts the source (accurate with probability 0.9, otherwise uninformative), the natural single-constraint update gives P*(M1) = 0.9 + 0.1 × (5/8) = 0.9625 and, preserving ratios inside and outside M1, P*(B) = 0.77. Close, but not the same, and I don't know whether the gap is an artifact of these numbers or systematic.

Third, I can't tell whether the "average the updates" rule is Bayesian conditioning in any joint space containing both a message variable and a location variable, given that in B both candidate conditionals are true and so the story doesn't say which would have been broadcast. That underdetermination in the overlap region looks to me like the real source of the trouble, and it is exactly where I think the paper has to do its work.
