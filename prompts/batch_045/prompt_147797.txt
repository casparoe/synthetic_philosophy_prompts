My therapist has been doing this thing with me for a couple of months now and I can't stop picking at it.

Background: I was in a bad car crash last year, and afterwards I started having the same nightmare over and over — not every night, but often enough that I was basically avoiding going to sleep. The treatment is rescripting / imagery rehearsal: I write the nightmare down, I change the ending to something harmless (in mine, the car never arrives), and then I rehearse the new version for ten minutes a day while wide awake. There's also a morning sleep diary where I rate how intense the nightmare was, 0 to 10. It's apparently a standard treatment for trauma nightmares and it has mostly worked, which is the annoying part — it works, so I can't just wave it off.

What I keep getting stuck on is how much every step of it treats the nightmare as something that happened to me. The handout calls it a "target memory" (or "target image," I don't have it in front of me), and the nurse filed it in my chart under the same heading as the crash itself — so my medical record now lists two things that happened to me, one of which happened while I was asleep and, as far as anyone can tell, did not involve a car. The rationale she gave me was that we're not touching the memory of the crash, we're changing the dream — but they're sitting in the same folder. And rescripting seems to need the nightmare to have had an ending, at some time, that I underwent and that I can now go back and edit. If I make up a scary story and change it's ending, nobody thinks I've changed anything except the story.

But then: I told her about a different dream where I did something awful — hurt someone — and she was very firm that this wasn't something I'd done, wasn't evidence about me, wasn't a memory of anything. So the dream is something I suffer but not something I do, something that can be a target memory but not a source of evidence, something whose ending is open for editing.

She mentioned offhand that philosophers argue about whether dreams are experiences at all. I think one of the names might have been Malcolm? And she described another view where the dream's content doesn't get fixed until you wake up and report it, something about a recording — but I'm pretty sure I'm mangling it.

So: what does this whole treatment practice presuppose about dreams? Does it strictly require that dreams are experiences you undergo while asleep, or would the "no" view and the "content only gets fixed on waking" view license exactly the same clinic? Is anything in the practice only coherent on one of them — the diary ratings, the chart-coding, the idea of going back and changing an ending? And if a clinic decided dreams aren't experiences, what would they actually have to do differently: would any of the treatment change, or just the way they talk about it?
