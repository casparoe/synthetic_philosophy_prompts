My seminar this term pairs classical American pragmatism with a short unit on contemporary normative epistemology, and I have managed to talk myself into a corner in the term paper I am drafting. We spent the first half of the course on James's essay The Will to Believe, and we then turned to the modern puzzle about reasons of the wrong kind: the observation that a demon's offer to reward you for believing that p, or its threat if you fail to, can be a perfectly good reason to want to have that belief, and yet seems structurally incapable of being the reason for which you hold it, whereas evidence for p apparently can. My draft contains a confident one-line claim that James belongs with the pragmatists about reasons for belief, that is, that he simply rejects the wrong-kind complaint. My supervisor and two people in my study group all pushed back on this, and I no longer think the line survives contact with the details of James's own text.

What I would like from you is a careful reconstruction of what James himself would have said had this puzzle been put to him in its current form. Please do not simply summarise the essay for me; I have read it, and I have the terminology. I want you to work with it. James builds the argument out of a set of distinctions: hypotheses that are live or dead for a given thinker, with liveness measured by willingness to act; an option as a decision between two hypotheses; options that are living (both hypotheses live), forced (no possibility of not choosing), and momentous (unique stake, significant, not reversible); a genuine option as one that is at once living, forced and momentous; and then the thesis itself, which is restricted to genuine options that cannot by their nature be settled on intellectual grounds, on the further ground that leaving the question open is itself a passional decision carrying the same risk of losing the truth. He also argues that our willing nature cannot revive hypotheses that are already dead for us, which matters for how much doxastic voluntarism he is actually committed to. I want you to run the contemporary cases through this machinery and tell me precisely where James draws the line, and by which condition.

Three cases in particular. First, the standard wrong-kind case: the demon rewards you for believing something the available evidence decisively disconfirms, say that the sky is green. Does James rule this out, and if so, is he ruling it out because the option is not living for you, or because the intellect can settle it, or because belief here is not available to the will at all? Note that the contemporary diagnosis is different in shape: the incentive is a fine reason to want the belief but the wrong kind of thing to believe for. I want to know whether James reaches a similar verdict by a different route. Second, the case his essay was written for: a momentous, forced, living option where the evidence is genuinely silent, of the sort Pascal's wager was meant to address. Here his conditions are met, and my question is whether he should be read as saying that the pragmatic consideration is a reason for which one may believe, or as saying something weaker and different, namely that one is permitted to let one's passional nature settle a question that the intellect will not, where the permission is not itself a reason of any kind. Third, and this is the part I find most interesting, the self-verifying cases he dwells on: confidence in one's own ability to accomplish something difficult, trust in another person's goodwill of the kind that can help create the goodwill, social and commercial credit of the sort that only exists if people believe in it. In these cases the pragmatic consideration looks as though it is also truth-relevant, because my coming to believe that p can raise the probability that p. So: would James say that here the pragmatic reason has become a reason of the right kind after all, or that the right/wrong kind distinction simply has no grip on such cases, or that the distinction itself should be redrawn in terms of whether a consideration bears on the truth of the target proposition rather than in terms of where the consideration comes from? Does momentousness, for him, do any work that the contemporary discussion has no analogue for, and would he think the puzzle generalises to attitudes other than belief, such as admiration or love?

Please be explicit about the anachronism throughout: James has no vocabulary of normative versus motivating reasons, no taxonomy of doxastic attitudes, and no label for the wrong-kind distinction, so I want you to flag which parts of the position you are attributing to him are genuinely recoverable from his text and which are extrapolation, and to say what the extrapolation rests on. Where you think the position is underdetermined by what he actually wrote, say so rather than smoothing it over. A sustained answer of roughly 700 to 900 words would be ideal, and finish by telling me whether the one-line reading in my draft can be defended in any form, or whether I should cut it entirely.
