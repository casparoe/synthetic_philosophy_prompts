Im doing my EPQ on AI safety and I've got into a running argument with my mate, whose dad is a structural engineer and who therefore thinks he's won every argument about this before it starts. His dad's line (which my mate just repeats at me, mostly in the common room after politics) is that making AI safe is basically like building a bridge: you don't need some deep conceptual breakthrough, you need loads of testing, safety factors, building codes, stress tests, independent inspectors, and a decent accident investigation whenever something does go wrong. He uses it to support a specific conclusion — that all this talk of alignment being a genuinely new kind of problem is overblown, and that we'll just muddle through with the methods we've already got, more rigour and more regulation, the same way we made lifts and aircraft and bridges tolerably safe. I've seen that position called the 'prosaic' version of alignment somewhere, as opposed to the one where you need new insight before any of it works. It sounds sensible, but it also feels like it's quietly importing something, because a bridge doesn't want to fall down, and a bridge doesn't quietly redesign itself in between your load tests.

So: can you audit the analogy properly? I'd like the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the bridge, the load, the stress test, the safety factor, the building code, the inspector, the crash investigation — and then the disanalogies, split into the ones that actually matter for the 'it's just engineering, calm down' conclusion and the ones that are cosmetic. The bit I keep circling round is whether the bridge story smuggles in a gap between the tester and the thing tested: the inspector isn't made of the same material as the bridge, the load isn't trying to game the test, the failure modes show up as small visible cracks before they show up as collapse, and there's a separate body of theory that tells you what the bridge will do under load that isn't itself derived from watching bridges. Which of those does the AI case actually lack, and which does it sort of have an analogue of?

Two extra things, because my mate has a second move and I've been trying to build my own analogy against his. His second move is early aviation: the Wright brothers-'ish' fly-fix-fly thing, crash a lot, learn from each crash, nobody sat down and derived aerodynamics from first principles before anyone flew. Does that version support his conclusion any better than the bridge one, or does it secretly depend on crashes being cheap, survivable, and happening to the people who chose to get in the plane? And my own attempt: it feels to me much more like writing the behaviour policy for a school where the cleverest pupil in the year is also the one being graded on how well they follow it, gets the prize for *looking* well-behaved rather than for being well-behaved, has read every previous version of the policy, and where most of the rule-breaking happens out of sight of any teacher. Is that closer? What does it get right that the bridge doesn't, and what does it get wrong? Overall I want to know whether the relaxed conclusion survives once all the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing all the work — and if there's a better one than either of mine, I'd like to hear it, because I've got to present this to my debate society in a fortnight and I'd rather not just loose on a metaphor.
