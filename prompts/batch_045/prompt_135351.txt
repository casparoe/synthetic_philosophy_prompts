I host a small, fairly technical podcast on nineteenth-century philosophy, and in two weeks I'm recording an episode on Schopenhauer's philosophy of music. My guest is a philosopher who has just published a book on the subject (I'll anonymise her and the book for the recording, so please don't worry about attributing anything to a real person). Her reading is what I'd call kinematic: where Schopenhauer argues that music is not a copy of any Platonic Idea at all but an immediate copy of the will itself, she thinks we should hear this as the claim that the shape music takes in time — tension, delay, acceleration, frustration, gratification, resolution, and the stretching of these over a passage — is formally the same as the shape of purposive striving, so that music's privileged relation to the will is an isomorphism between two patterns of directedness rather than a piece of metaphysics. Her reliable escape hatch, whenever the metaphysics becomes uncomfortable, is to say that the will-talk is really a description of the phenomenology and that the phenomenology is where the real claim lives. I want questions that make that escape hatch progressively harder to open, not questions that let her restate the book's thesis.

Draft eight to ten questions, each with the answer she is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that answer, pressing on the following. First: the exception music poses to his own account of the other arts as cognition of Ideas by a will-less subject of pure knowing — and, more sharply, why the exception is supposed to be music specifically, given that he already allows architecture to present the lowest grades of the will's objectification, the Ideas of gravity, cohesion and rigidity, which is arguably halfway to the will already. Second: whether an essentially successive, divided, temporal artifact can really be a copy of a will that is supposed to lie outside time and outside the principle of individuation, and whether her isomorphism story dissolves this puzzle or just relocates it. Third: his part-by-part mapping of the voices of the harmony onto the grades of the will's objectification, the bass to the lowest or inorganic grades and the melody to human life and its striving — whether the mapping does any work, what it predicts about a piece that omits a voice, and what it says about a fugue or any dense polyphony where several independent voices are equally singable. Fourth: how the isomorphism claim differs from the familiar view that music sounds the way emotion feels and so expresses it, a view that needs no will and no thing-in-itself at all. Fifth: his ranking of instrumental music above vocal music and his insistence that in opera the poetry must be subordinate to the music, which on her own account should look puzzling, since if music's content is the form of directed action then a text supplying what the striving is about would seem to add rather than detract. Sixth: the tension between music's status as the highest art and his account of aesthetic experience as a temporary release from servitude to the will, given that music is the art most entangled with the body — marching, dancing, the compulsion to keep time. Seventh: what the theory implies about music that doesn't behave like his repertoire — a sustained drone, rhythmically static music, a piece without tonal resolution — whether the account is quietly indexed to early-nineteenth-century tonal practice, and whether that is fatal to a claim that was meant to be about the will as such.

Close with one question asking her to name a finding, phenomenological or empirical, that would count against the view — something she would regard as a cost rather than a redescription — and suggest how I should handle it if she answers that nothing could. Assume my listeners know the broad shape of Book III of The World as Will and Representation but not the secondary literature, so keep each question self-contained and concrete, anchored in an actual feature of music or an actual claim of Schopenhauer's, rather than in the vocabulary of the debate.
