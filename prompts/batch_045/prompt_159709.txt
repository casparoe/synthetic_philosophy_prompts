I'm putting together a lecture for my grad seminar on causation and explanation in the social sciences (philosophy department, but half the room is from psych and education), and I've got into an email argument with a colleague in behaviour genetics that I thought would be easy to settle and now isn't. To make one move precise I invented a statistic: call the *gain heritability* of an intervention the narrow-sense heritability of the change score, i.e. h²(Δ) = V_A(Δ)/V_P(Δ) in the population that actually received the intervention. Suppose a reading programme produces a mean gain of 8 points in some population and h²(Δ) = 0.6 there. Her argument, as near as I can render it:

(1) h² is a ratio of variances, computed for a defined population under a defined distribution of environments; it partitions variance, not any particular difference.
(2) If 60% of the variance of Δ is additive-genetic, then 60% of any given child's gain is due to their genes and the remaining 40% is due to everything else.
(3) The mean gain is just the average of the individual gains, so at most 0.4 × 8 = 3.2 of the 8 points can be credited to the programme; the other 4.8 is genes.
(4) Generalising: as h²(Δ) goes to 1, the programme cannot be responsible for any part of the mean gain.
(5) The same reasoning applied to a trait rather than a gain says: if a trait is highly heritable within each of two groups, then a mean gap between those groups — kids in an under-resourced district versus a well-resourced one — is largely genetic, so equalising resources shouldn't be expected to close it, whatever the within-group numbers happen to be.
(6) But (4) is absurd on its face. Phenylketonuria is about as genetic as a trait gets and is managed by diet; mean height has moved substantially with nutrition; average IQ scores rose a great deal over the twentieth century in ways nobody thinks reflect genetic change. So any argument that shows no intervention can move the mean of a heritable trait has proved far too much.

I want to keep (1) and (6), and my instinct is to drop (2) — but I'm honestly not sure (2) is where it breaks, and its not even clear to me whether the argument is invalid or valid-with-one-premise-I-dislike. So: name the step you'd reject, say why it looked innocent, and tell me what rejecting it costs — in particular, is (2) salvageable *within* a fixed environment in a way that (3) and (5) are not, and if so what exactly has to be true for the move from a variance decomposition to a claim about a mean difference to be licensed (random assignment of genotypes to environments, no gene-environment covariance, additivity, whatever), and how close those conditions ever get to holding in human populations. Also price the alternative: what would I be committing to if I instead kept (2)–(5) and gave up the standard "heritability does not mean immutability" line, and is there any legitimate statistic that does the work people want a "genetic share of the gap" to do, or is that quantity just not identified from within-group heritability no matter what you assume?

Give me your confidence level on each part of the diagnosis, and if the whole thing as I've set it up rests on a confusion — including if my gain-heritability gadget is nonsense or if I've strawmanned my colleague — say that plainly instead of handing me a polite diagnosis. Don't cite anything; I can chase references myself, and I'd rather not put a guessed-at paper on a slide in front of twenty grad students.
