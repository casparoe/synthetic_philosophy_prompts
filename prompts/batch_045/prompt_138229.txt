I would be grateful for your assessment of a repair I have been working on for the truth-conditional treatment of indicative conditionals in the light of Lewis's triviality results.

Here is how I am setting things up. Let P be an agent's credence function over a Boolean algebra of propositions, and let → be an indicative conditional connective: whenever A and C are propositions, A→C is itself an element of the algebra, so that it can be negated, conjoined with other propositions, and embedded in further conditionals, and P(A→C) is well defined. Adams's thesis — the probabilistic form of the Ramsey test — requires that P(A→C) = P(C|A) for every such pair. Lewis's results, as I understand them, show that this requirement cannot be combined with the rest of the setup unless P is trivial: if the equation holds throughout an algebra closed under →, then every proposition is probabilistically independent of every other, and hence, taking C = A, every proposition of nonzero probability has probability 1. The package consisting of (i) classical Boolean propositions, (ii) a genuine conditional connective, (iii) the Ramsey-test equation, and (iv) non-extremal credence is thus inconsistent, and the familiar responses are either to keep truth conditions and give up the equation, or to keep the equation and deny that conditionals express propositions at all.

My difficulty concerns how much this really shows. The conclusion is often reported as the claim that there is no proposition whose probability equals the corresponding conditional probability. But read pair by pair, that cannot be what has been established. Fix a single pair A, C. The number P(C|A) is perfectly well behaved, and if the algebra is rich enough — suppose it is atomless, so that for every real r in [0,1] there is a proposition of probability r — then there is certainly some proposition X with P(X) = P(C|A). In the special case where A and C are probabilistically independent the choice is immediate: take X = C, since then P(C|A) = P(C). The argument's leverage therefore appears to come entirely from demanding that one single, uniform operation deliver the right number for every pair at once, inside an algebra that is closed under that operation.

That suggests the following move. Let "if A then C" express a proposition, but let the proposition it expresses be fixed pair by pair, by stipulation: for each A, C, select some element X_{A,C} of the algebra whose probability is P(C|A), and identify it with the content of the conditional. No single connective is then being asked to satisfy the equation everywhere; on my reading, the triviality derivations proceed by applying the equation to pairs in which a conditional is itself a constituent, and nothing in the present proposal requires those applications to be well behaved. The Ramsey-test equation holds by construction, conditionals have truth conditions and standard Boolean behaviour, and credences may remain as non-extremal as one likes.

Before writing this up I would be grateful for your judgement on three points.

First, is the technical claim correct? Does stipulating the content of each conditional separately place the proposal beyond the reach of Lewis's arguments? I also vaguely recall that there are strengthened versions of the result which derive triviality from comparatively few instances of the equation, or from its holding only approximately; would those tell against it?

Second, I have not given any rule for selecting X_{A,C} beyond its having the right probability, so the account does not determine the content of "if A then C" from the contents of A and C in any systematic way. Is that a merely presentational gap which could in principle be filled in, or is something important lost when the contents are stipulated rather than composed?

Third, if the move is sound, why would it not be regarded as a satisfactory response to the claim that triviality forces a choice between truth conditions and the Ramsey test? It seems to me the obvious reply, and I would like to know what I am missing.
