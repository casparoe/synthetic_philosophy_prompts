I'm taking this weird interdisciplinary seminar on early modern skepticism and cultural relativism, and we're slowly going through Montaigne's essay Of Cannibals (the late-1500s text; I'm reading it in the old Cotton translation). I got totally stuck on the very first paragraph, which is this business about King Pyrrhus and the Romans. Here's the passage I mean, from the opening of that chapter:

When King Pyrrhus invaded Italy, having viewed and considered the order
of the army the Romans sent out to meet him; "I know not," said he,
"what kind of barbarians" (for so the Greeks called all other nations)
"these may be; but the disposition of this army that I see has nothing of
barbarism in it."—[Plutarch, Life of Pyrrhus, c. 8.]—As much said the
Greeks of that which Flaminius brought into their country; and Philip,
beholding from an eminence the order and distribution of the Roman camp
formed in his kingdom by Publius Sulpicius Galba, spake to the same
effect.  By which it appears how cautious men ought to be of taking
things upon trust from vulgar opinion, and that we are to judge by the
eye of reason, and not from common report.

My study group and I had a kind of dumb but oddly gripping argument about what Montaigne is actually doing here. One friend insisted it's basically a lexical-history point: the Greeks used barbarian just as a relational marker for foreigners or non-Greek speakers, not as a substantive insult, and Montaigne is reminding us of that before he turns to the New World. I thought it was more of a conceptual or argumentative pivot: the interesting move is the gap between the label barbarians (which Pyrrhus repeats as the vulgar term) and the property barbarism (which he says the Roman army's disposition completely lacks). So barbarism can't just mean non-Greek or even not our custom—it has to be some property like disorder, cruelty, irrationality, or a defect of reason, and the point is that the people we casually call barbarians may turn out to have less of that property than we do. (We started calling this the Pyrrhus pivot: the step where a term of outsider-disparagement gets detached from the thing it is ordinarily aimed at and then potentially reattached to the speaker's own group.)
