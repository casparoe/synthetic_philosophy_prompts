My friend and I spent about two hours arguing about Memento last night and neither of us budged, so I want a third opinion. He thinks it's just a film about memory being unreliable; I said that's too shallow, and that what it actually dramatizes is what happens to someone who has to run his whole life on knowledge sources he can never audit. Read it through Nyāya epistemology and tell me who's closer.

Concretely, I want two things worked out. First: Leonard's system of polaroids, notes and tattoos. The notes function as testimony, but the testifier is a past self he can't cross-examine, and the only evidence that the testifier is reliable is more writing from the same unreliable source. So is that a fifth pramāṇa, a distinct kind of self-authored testimony from a self you can't question, or does Nyāya have to fold it into śabda and then disqualify it because the speaker is defective, or at best unverifiable? And remember Nyāya is parataḥ prāmāṇya about this: validity gets established from outside the cognition, by inference from the reliability of whatever produced it or by whether acting on it works out. Leonard's setup seems designed so that the outside check never arrives, and when evidence of failure does show up he destroys it. Does that mean the honest Nyāya verdict is that he basically never has knowledge, or that he has too much of it?

Second, the Sammy Jankis stuff. I think the film is secretly a study of upamāna: Leonard comes to understand his own condition by comparison with a case he's told is merely similar, and the gut-punch is that the analogue was never other than the thing itself. Is that a legitimate use of upamāna, or am I forcing it?
