Our reading group spent last night on the "safe Pareto improvement" argument for existential risk reduction, and Dan pushed it somewhere I think it obviously breaks but I can't put my finger on the step. We're using the term to mean what our chapter says: a change where everyone is at least as well off in expectation, and someone is better off. What makes it "safe," on this usage, is supposed to be that you can recommend it from any reasonable axiology, because there's nothing to trade off. Here's the version I wrote up:

(1) A safe Pareto improvement is a change such that every person is at least as well off in expectation under it than under the alternative, and at least one person is better off. Scope clause: "every person" means everyone who exists, or would exist, under any of the outcomes we're choosing between — otherwise the principle is blind to exactly the cases it exists for, since the people in a surviving future don't exist yet and the people who die in a catastrophe don't exist in the surviving branch.

(2) If a change is a safe Pareto improvement, no contested axiology is needed to recommend it: no population ethics, no interpersonal comparisons, no social discount rate, no repugnant conclusion, no utilitarian fanaticism. There's no trade-off to adjudicate because nobody loses.

(3) Reducing existential risk is a safe Pareto improvement. Everyone alive now faces a lower chance of dying prematurely, and everyone who would live in a future that gets to happen — a trillion of them, or a billion trillion, the number doesn't matter — gets in expectation a share of a future that otherwise wouldn't occur.

(4) So longtermism's headline conclusion rests on about the least controversial principle in welfare economics.

(5) Improvement is transitive and insensitive to magnitude: if A is an improvement over B and B over C then A is over C, and nothing in (1)–(2) lets a small gain anywhere outweigh the fact that everyone is better off.

(6) Then combine (3) and (5): the gain from an x-risk intervention scales with the number of future people, which is astronomical. So an intervention that lowers extinction risk by one-in-a-billion does more good, on this accounting, than all near-term spending on malaria or education put together, and since (2) says there are no knobs — no discount rate, no utility ceiling, no aggregation to bound — there's nothing to balance it against. Everything goes to x-risk; near-term work is permissible only as x-risk reduction.

(7) And here's where I think it dissolves. Any large-scale policy changes who gets conceived and when, so the people who'd have existed under the alternative don't exist under ours and vice versa. If existence is a benefit, then (3)'s gain for future people is really a gain of creating people — and then any policy whose population is larger or better-off than the alternative's is a safe Pareto improvement over it, including policies that are awful for the people who end up in them, because the people who'd have been better off under the alternative never exist to lose anything.

(8) So from an allegedly axiology-free premise we get both that we owe everything to x-risk reduction and that "safe Pareto improvement" is a label you can stick on nearly any large-scale policy.

(8) is unacceptable and I think something earlier is doing the work. Three of us have picked three different steps and I want an umpire rather than a vote. I'd most like to keep (4), and I'll give up (6) if I must, but I'd rather know what I'm paying. Please don't just tell me it depends on population ethics — I know it does, I want the step.
