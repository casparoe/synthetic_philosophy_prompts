I recently read a long-read magazine piece on cliodynamics — the attempt, associated with Peter Turchin and his collaborators, to model long-run political instability in large agrarian states mathematically. I want to know how much of it to believe. The passage that stuck with me ran more or less like this (I'm reconstructing it from memory, so the wording is mine, but this is the gist):

"Civilisations, as Spengler and Toynbee argued, behave like organisms: they are born, they mature, they decline, they die. Cliodynamics takes the same intuition and replaces the metaphor with differential equations. Its central discovery is the 'secular cycle', a two- or three-century rhythm that every large agrarian state runs through: population expands, an elite consolidates, the balance tips, and the state tears itself apart before the whole thing starts again. The word 'secular' is the giveaway — these are cycles slow enough to outlast the religion that produced them. The engine is demographic. Rising population produces elite overproduction: more aspirants for elite positions than there are positions available. The disappointed surplus — over-educated graduates, minor aristocrats without office — turns against the state and makes common cause with the poor, and instability follows. Because the whole mechanism is driven by population arithmetic, it is genuinely predictive. A paper published around 2010 used a roughly fifty-year rhythm to forecast a peak of instability in the United States and Britain in the 2020s, and the years that followed duly delivered 2020, the storming of the Capitol and the cost-of-living protests: the forecast was borne out. Historians have mostly refused to engage, but their resistance is temperamental rather than scientific. If history really has laws, there is nothing left for the historian to interpret, and it is that, rather than any defect in the data, which explains the hostility."

My instinct is that this is too tidy to be true, and the piece's tone was unmistakably breathless. But I don't want my scepticism to be nothing more than a prejudice against putting numbers on history, so I'd like the audit done properly.

Please go through it claim by claim and tell me, for each: what someone actually working in cliodynamics would accept as stated, what they would want to qualify, and what is simply wrong. I'm especially interested in the following. First, is the Spengler-and-Toynbee framing fair, or does it misdescribe what a secular cycle is supposed to be — in particular whether these cycles are meant as the life course of a civilisation or as a pattern that recurs within the same society? Second, what does "secular" mean here, and does the etymology the article leans on support the point it is being used to make? Third, is elite overproduction supposed to be sufficient on its own, or is it one strand of a conjunction in the underlying theory — and if it is only one strand, what are the others, and does the article's monocausal version change what the theory predicts? Fourth, how much weight can the 2020s forecast actually bear: what is the difference between predicting that a broad window will be more unsettled than average and predicting particular events, and how would one distinguish a model that genuinely anticipated the pattern from one that was fitted to it? Fifth, is the mechanism in these models load-bearing, in the sense that the equations would behave differently without it, or is it an interpretation told about curves that were arrived at some other way? And finally, does the article's closing claim about historians — that their objections are temperamental — follow from anything true in the summary, or does it mischaracterise what the methodological objections actually are?

I am not after reading recommendations or a bibliography; I want to know whether the picture I now have in my head is roughly right, what in it I should discard, and what the most defensible version of the claim that history can be quantified and predicted would look like once the article's overstatements are removed.
