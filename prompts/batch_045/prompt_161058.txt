My reading group has spent the whole term on Kuhn, and I'm the one closing the final session, which is on incommensurability. I'd rather end on a poem than on a summary, and I've got it into my head that the form should be a sestina: six stanzas of six lines with the six end-words rotating, then the three-line envoi gathering all six one last time. Last night, over a long and slightly heated dinner, a friend pressed me on why anyone should take the thesis seriously at all: either the failure of comparability is total, in which case Priestley and Lavoisier were not really disagreeing with each other and the history books are unintelligible, or it is so local that it collapses into perfectly ordinary meaning change, no more mysterious than the fact that cool now means something it didn't used to mean. I tried to answer that the second horn is too quick: on the later version of the view, the kind I take to be the defensible one, what changes is not the sense of an isolated word but the place of a kind-term inside a taxonomy whose kind-terms are not permitted to overlap in reference unless one contains the other — so that redubbing one term forces a redistribution of its neighbours, and you do not get to change just one thing. That, I said, is why it isn't merely cool: the word's inferential surroundings travel with it.

My friend's reply, which I've been chewing on since, was that once the community settles the new definitions, the old word is simply dead — it stops doing any work at all, and whatever residue is just nostalgia. I think that's wrong, and I've started calling what's left semantic drag: the retained word keeps quietly performing its old inferential role in the habits of people who would now define it the new way, so that a sentence in the new language can be assented to partly because it is still being half-heard in the old one, and a new theory can look like it explains something largely because its users have not fully stopped meaning the predecessor claim. I don't know whether there is an established name for this and I don't want references for it; I want the poem to make it something a reader can feel rather than something I assert.

So here is what I'm after. Take six end-words from the oxygen side of the transition — the shift from phlogiston talk and dephlogisticated air to the new chemistry — and let them split into two kinds. Four of them should be words that come through the change more or less intact, the shared quantitative and procedural vocabulary both sides use: weighing, measuring, the vessel, the flame, that sort of thing. Two of them should be the kind-terms whose sense and whose neighbours are reorganized: air and earth, or calx, whatever you find most workable. The point of the form is that the rotation should do the philosophical work by itself: as the words come back in their new positions across the six stanzas, a reader should feel the four standing still and the two sliding into new jobs, without the poem ever announcing which is which. The envoi is where I want the question settled, because it is the situation a working scientist is actually in — having to use the new taxonomy while the old one is still audible to her: can all six words be got into three lines in a single true sentence, or does the sentence equivocate? If semantic drag is real, I think the envoi has to equivocate and be beautiful anyway. It should be accurate enough that someone who has read the 1962 book and the later papers would say the poem has the thesis right rather than just naming it, and it should still work as a poem for someone in the room who has read nothing. Give me the poem itself and nothing else — plain text, no title, no preface, no explanatory note afterwards, no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, just the thirty-nine lines.
