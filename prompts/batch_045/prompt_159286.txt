I'm running a philosophy-of-mind discussion night for some first-years and we're screening Arrival (the 2016 Villeneuve one, from Ted Chiang's novella) next week, and I really want to avoid the thing every Arrival discussion turns into — the free will and determinism take. So: read the film through the language of thought hypothesis instead, and keep the two readings separate. If your answer drifts into "but can she change the future," you've lost the thread.

Where I'm at. Louise learns Heptapod B, a written system that isn't speech written down, and the premise is that mastering it reorganises her mind so that she experiences time all at once and can remember forward instead of backward. On its face that's a Whorfian story: acquire a new public code, get a new mind. Fodor's picture is supposed to block exactly that move — thinking is computation over Mentalese, an innately given system of syntactically structured mental representations, and English or Mandarin or whatever is just an input/output format bolted onto it. So the question I want argued, not hedged: does the film actually contradict LOT, or is there a reading on which it's fine?

The distinction I've been kicking around and want you to actually use: call it notational restructuring versus representational restructuring. A new notation can change what you can do with an unchanged representational system — Arabic numerals against Roman ones, a good diagram, a formal notation that lets you see an inference you couldn't before — without touching the medium of thought at all. Representational restructuring is the stronger thing the Whorfian reading needs: the medium itself gets rebuilt. Which is the film giving Louise? And if you go with the notational version, does the movie still get what it wants dramatically, or does it quietly turn into a story about an extremely good notation?

Please deal with these rather than skating past them:

- Heptapod B has no phonology, it's writing that doesn't transcribe speech. I keep going back and forth on which way this cuts: no phonology makes it less like a natural language and more like a formalism, which pushes toward the notational reading; but it's also the device the film uses to claim that thought itself changed. Resolve it or say why it doesn't resolve.
- Fodor's line that you can't really acquire a brand-new concept, that learning isn't hypothesis-testing over concepts you lack. Louise looks like a counterexample. Is she, or is the better reading that she gains access to something already there?
- The productivity and systematicity argument for LOT. The aliens presumably think in something with composition but without sequence. Does that tell you anything about whether Mentalese's syntax has to look the way Fodor says it does?
- And a thing I can't settle: can one head contain two languages of thought? If Louise ends up with Heptapod B as a second medium alongside her original Mentalese, is that coherent on LOT, or does the hypothesis quietly assume uniqueness per thinker? Say which, and why.
- Where the reading breaks. Be honest: the film uses language loosely, its linguistics is not something a linguist would sign off on, and its premise may really be about a new perceptual modality rather than a new medium of thought. Tell me where pushing the LOT lens distorts the movie, and where the movie simply isn't asking the question. Also, if you invoke Sapir-Whorf, say which version you mean — the strong determinist one or the weak relativistic one — because the film only looks interesting if it's the strong one.

End with what the film adds, if anything: what does the movie show about language and thought that the Fodor-versus-connectionism arguments don't? I want to close the session with something better than "and that's why Whorf was wrong."

Aim for maybe 800-1000 words of actual argument rather than a plot summary, and no bibliography or citations — I want your reading, not a reading list.
