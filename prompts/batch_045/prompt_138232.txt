You are a philosopher of physics who has taught hands-on sessions for years and who cares about the difference between students hearing a result and running into it themselves. Please think the design through carefully before answering: brainstorm at least three genuinely different mechanics for the session, and then give me the one you would actually run, plus a sentence on why you rejected each of the others.

I teach a twelve-week undergraduate seminar in philosophy of physics. This is the third meeting: 75 minutes, nominally 14 students, though attendance will be patchy because it is the week before midterms, so the design has to survive ten or sixteen people rather than exactly fourteen. About four of them have had a GR course, the rest have only special relativity, and none of them have met the hole argument. I want them to feel its force: that a theory without fixed background structure, combined with taking the points of the manifold to be individuals, seems to leave what happens inside a matter-free region undetermined by everything outside it — and then to feel the pull of the response that the two models are not two worlds at all. No lecture up front. They should not hear the words diffeomorphism, hole argument, or Leibniz equivalence until after they have collided with the problem; use your own neutral vocabulary until then, and tell me which word you are using for which thing. The room is the bad one: fixed tiered seating, narrow writing ledges, no lab benches, a projector but no whiteboard at the front. My starting idea for the mechanism is physical rather than drawn — a square of stretchy fabric clamped in an embroidery hoop, boundary pinned, interior deformed, so a team literally drags the events around inside a hole while everything outside stays put. But I have a specific worry that I want addressed rather than designed around: the deformation visibly changes the distances between marks on the fabric, whereas in the argument the dragged-along metric is supposed to preserve every metrical relation and only the assignment of those relations to manifold points is supposed to differ. Tell me whether that is precisely the feature the activity cannot reproduce, how the debrief should handle it, and how to stop it from teaching the wrong lesson in the meantime.

I also want a comparison across teams, because there are two conditions I suspect the argument quietly depends on and I would rather the students discover them than be told. Split the group so that teams get different setups: for some, the hole is empty of matter and the coordinate grid is drawn on the fabric so that it is dragged along with the deformation; for others, the same hole but with a dust cloud of marked matter-events inside it; for others, an empty hole but a fixed, non-dynamical grid printed on paper under the fabric and not dragged; and so on, in whatever pattern you think is most revealing. Each team builds a twin model from the original and then the teams try to catch each other: reporters who are allowed to report only coincidences — meetings of worldlines, orderings along them, readings exchanged at meetings — write up an observation report from their own model, and the receiving team must say which of the two twins produced it. My expectation is that some teams can discriminate and some cannot, and that the pattern across teams is what does the teaching; tell me whether the pattern will actually come out that way and what to do if it does not. Then give me the rest: a materials list from cheap sources plus a pen-and-paper fallback that works on a narrow ledge; the rules and the roles, including someone whose job is to police the pinned boundary; a minute-by-minute timeline; the exact wording of the reveal and of the fork the students are then put to; a debrief that maps moments in the session onto steps of the argument, including the distinction — easy to blur, I think — between the twins being empirically indistinguishable, their really being the same physical situation, and the indeterminism claim proper; small stakes or a scoring scheme that makes failing to discriminate feel like a discovery rather than a trick; and an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, since I am fairly confident someone will say it is only a change of coordinates and someone will stretch the hoop. Please don't pad the answer with suggested further reading — I have the readings; I need something I can run on Thursday. Finally, state how confident you are that the session as designed produces the target confusion in a room like mine, and single out the one step — of the argument or of your design — that you are least sure about.
