I need Q&A prep for a talk I'm giving in three weeks, and I've been staring at my slides too long to trust my own judgment about what's going to kill me in the question period.

The setting: a small interdisciplinary workshop on rationality and computation, around 25 people, 40 minutes plus 20 minutes of questions. The room breaks down roughly like this:

- two formal epistemologists / decision theorists in the Bayesian tradition, who think "bounded rationality" is mostly a modelling convenience and that normative claims belong to the ideal theory and get approximated downward afterwards;
- two people from philosophy of cognitive science who work in the resource-rationality / computational-rationality line — they think the mind is close to optimally adapted to its computational budget, and they know the worst-case vs average-case vs smoothed-analysis distinction cold;
- three or four political philosophers plus a couple of legal theorists who came because the workshop advertised "computation and justice" — they care about structural injustice, choice architecture, and whose option sets get shaped by whom;
- one emeritus philosopher of action who has written on "ought implies can" and will ask about it no matter what I say.

My thesis in one line: computational hardness results (NP-hardness and friends) are evidence about our models of agency rather than about the agents themselves, and once you notice that the stream of instances an agent actually faces is partly produced by that agent's own earlier choices and by institutions other people designed, intractability stops being a constraint on what to demand and becomes something someone can be responsible for. I lean on Simon's scissors analogy and push it one step: the environment blade isn't given, it's built, so there's a further question about who shaped it and for whom. I've been calling the resulting thing "imposed hardness," which is a placeholder name and I'm happy to be told it's a bad one.

Rough shape of the argument, so you have something to aim at:

1. Intractability is representation-relative and worst-case. "The agent's decision problem is NP-hard" is a claim about a problem family plus an encoding, not about a particular Tuesday morning. So a hardness result can't be read straight off as "this agent couldn't have done better."

2. What you'd actually need is the instance distribution — and for agents embedded in a social world that distribution is endogenous: it's a product of the agent's own past commitments, of the menu somebody else put in front of them, of institutional design. My examples are a public benefits system where working out which combination of entitlements you're eligible for is a combinatorial mess, a retirement plan with a couple of hundred funds and interacting contribution rules, and a hospital rota system where the space of acceptable schedules explodes.

3. So the normative question isn't only "is this requirement tractable for this agent" but "could somebody reasonably have arranged things so that this instance was easy" — and if yes, and if they had reason to, there's a criticism available that doesn't reduce to anyone's outcomes being worse or anyone's rights being violated, because everyone still has all the same options.

I know step 3 is where I'm thinnest and I'd rather you hammer it than be polite about it.

What I want: the objections I'll actually get from this specific room, ordered by likelihood times damage, each phrased the way that subgroup would really phrase it — the Bayesians should sound like Bayesians, the resource-rationality people should sound like people who've read the smoothed-analysis literature, the political philosophers should sound like they're smelling a structural-injustice argument in disguise.
