Here's the half-formed thing. Nearly everyone writes about the duty to remember as if remembering were free and the only hard part were getting the story right. But remembering is upkeep: it costs money, attention, curricula, institutional real estate, and it crowds out whatever else that budget could have gone to. And meanwhile the actual custodians of most of the things we say we must never forget aren't people with any living relation to what happened — they're archives, mandatory curricula, search indexes, and now models trained on more or less everything. Margalit ties duties of memory to something like thick relations and doubts they extend to humanity at large; Rieff goes the other way and makes a case for forgetting, especially for communities coming out of their own violence, on the grounds that remembering is what keeps the cycle going. Neither of them, as far as I can tell, is writing about a world where the record is permanent, searchable and nearly free, and where the remembering is done by systems that stand in no relation to anybody.

My current stab at the question: is a duty to remember discharged by the continued availability of an accurate record, or does it require that somebody actually recall — and if it requires recall, what happens to the duty when the only recallers left are institutions or machines? And the flip side: if forgetting is sometimes owed, and nothing can really be destroyed anymore, does the duty to forget become a duty to make something unreachable or unqueryable rather than a duty to erase it?
