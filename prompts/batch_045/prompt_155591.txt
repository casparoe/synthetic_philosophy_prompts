I'm writing a novel in which the narrator — a translator working from Greek — is handed a ring that does what the Lydian shepherd's ring does in the challenge Glaucon puts to Socrates, only more so: whatever he does while wearing it is not merely unseen but untraceable, so that nothing he does can be held against him. Late in the book he writes a sonnet about it, and I want that sonnet to be printable as his own composition: a poem first and an argument second, but accurate enough that the philosopher who reads my drafts won't wince. Please write it in American English, fourteen lines, loose iambic pentameter, no archaic diction a twentieth-century translator would not use, and no apparatus — the form has to carry the thinking.

The structure I have in mind: the two quatrains of the octave should hold Glaucon's pair of extreme cases side by side as a mirror — one man just and reputed unjust, the other unjust and reputed just — so that the reader sees reputation, not character, being traded. The turn at line nine should be the point I have not managed to get into prose: the ring does not merely hide the wearer, it unbinds him. Someone who can never be found out cannot give his word, cannot be trusted, cannot be loved for anything he did unseen; impunity therefore carries a price the bargain never counts, and it costs him every good that requires his being believed. Let the closing couplet name what is left of the challenge once that price is subtracted — whether the question of why one should be moral survives when the audience is gone and the reward of being seen as moral is not simply withheld but made unavailable in principle — and leave it open rather than settling it.
