**Question.** A team of anthropologists announce the results of a thirty-year survey of 312 small-scale societies. They report that nearly all of the variation in what a society treats as morally forbidden is predicted by a single measurable variable, which they call the cooperation index: the extent to which that society's food production depends on people who are not kin sharing labour and dividing up the harvest. Roughly, every society in the sample forbids whatever would most damage that form of cooperation.

Suppose the result held up. Would it tell us anything about what we really ought to do? In your answer: (a) explain the is-ought gap and the naturalistic fallacy, and say whether they are the same mistake; (b) say whether either of them shows that findings of this kind are irrelevant to ethics; (c) say what, if anything, would have to be added to the results before they could support a conclusion about what we ought to do.

**Student's answer.** Hume's is-ought gap is the observation that writers on morals slide from claims about how things are to claims about how they ought to be without ever explaining the transition: from premises containing no ought you cannot validly derive a conclusion that does. Any argument that gets an ought out has to bring in a normative premise along the way — promises ought to be kept, say — and once you state it explicitly you can see that the real work was being done by that premise all along. The underlying reason for this, for Hume, is his view that reason is only the slave of the passions: reason on its own can never move anyone to act, so no amount of factual reasoning can deliver a conclusion about what to do.

Moore's naturalistic fallacy is basically the same point put another way, that it is a mistake to try to extract an evaluative conclusion from purely descriptive premises. His open-question argument is meant to establish it: for any natural property you care to name — pleasure, whatever is favoured by evolution, whatever a survey of societies picks out — it always remains an open question whether that property is good, so no such account of what good is can be right. That is why the fallacy is called naturalistic. Ethical naturalism is the doctrine that you can derive an ought from an is, and the open-question argument is what refutes it.

Applied to the survey, then, the is-ought gap settles the matter. The researchers' results are entirely descriptive, so they cannot entail anything about what we really ought to do. And it is worth being clear that they cannot even be evidence for any moral conclusion, because any bridge premise you attach is exactly what is in dispute in the first place, and nothing merely factual could ever settle which bridge premise to accept. Descriptive facts are, morally speaking, inert. The only work an empirical finding can do in ethics is, once our moral principles are already in hand, to tell us which particular actions fall under them.

What would have to be added, then, is a normative premise, something like one ought to do whatever sustains the form of cooperation one's society depends on. But since that premise cannot itself be reached from the survey, and since any further ought needed to support it would face the very same problem, the survey tells us nothing whatsoever about what we ought to do, and no empirical finding ever could. That, I take it, is the whole point of the gap: morality is autonomous from science, and moral disagreement is never a disagreement about matters of fact that further investigation could clear up.
