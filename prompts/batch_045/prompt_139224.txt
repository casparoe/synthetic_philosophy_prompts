I'm a clinical epidemiologist, and twice a year I run a ninety-minute workshop for hospital mortality-and-morbidity review committees: around fifteen clinicians, no probability training beyond risk ratios and p-values. The one idea I want them to walk out with is the classical Condorcet jury theorem, because the working assumption in these rooms is that a verdict endorsed by eight people who read the same chart is more likely to be right than one endorsed by three, and that the size of the committee is itself doing the epistemic work.

Plan how I should explain it; don't just explain it to me. Tell me what has to be in place before the claim is even intelligible, which single case should carry the whole thing and why it should not be a courtroom jury, where in the sequence to defuse each mistake I expect, one question I can ask at the end that would show it landed rather than being nodded at, and what I should openly decline to cover at this level.

Two things I want handled carefully. First, my reviewers fail the theorem's independence condition, but not primarily because they talk: they all form their views from one shared written record, so their errors have a common cause even when nobody speaks. I want the sequence to separate non-communication from independence in the relevant sense, and to mark where deliberation genuinely helps — pooling evidence the members separately hold — as opposed to manufacturing the correlation the theorem excludes. Second, many of these reviews ask whether a death was avoidable under a standard of care rather than what happened, and I suspect the theorem has no purchase there; tell me where that limitation goes in the sequence and whether it is a footnote or load-bearing.

The misconceptions I expect: that the theorem vindicates majority rule as such; that adding members always improves accuracy however weak they are; that what is needed is each member better than chance rather than the group's aggregate; that this is the same result as the intransitive-majorities problem also associated with Condorcet; and, worst, that the theorem shows our committee was right, when at most it licenses a conditional whose assumptions we cannot check from inside the room.

Give it to me as one ordered plan of under 600 words, naming the carrying example in the first sentence and tagging the point at which each misconception gets headed off.
