so I finally read Hofstadter's thing about variations on a theme being the crux of creativity — the knob-twiddling picture, where nobody actually leaps out into empty space, you just have a space you've internalised really deeply and you turn a handful of dials very precisely inside it, so "originality" is about how well-aimed the variation is and not about how far out it lands. (I'm probably mangling it but you get the idea: the space is inherited and shared, the creative act is a specific high-fidelity move within it.)

The thing that got me thinking is music copyright, which seems built on almost the opposite picture in some places and weirdly compatible with it in others. The bits I half-know, roughly:

- courts keep saying chord progressions, stock riffs, genre conventions, that kind of thing aren't protectable — the building blocks are free, only the particular expression is owned
- but there have been huge verdicts where a song was found to infringe an older one over its overall groove/feel/production, with no shared melody or lyric — i.e. over exactly the stuff that looks most like "settings of the knobs" rather than a copied tune
- with sampling, even a tiny unlicensed slice of someone's actual recording seems to be infringement, so the space is free in the abstract but fenced the moment anyone walked on that bit of it
- independent creation is a complete defence: if you really produced the same tune without ever hearing the other one, you're fine. So what's protected is causal history, not the shape of the output.
- in jazz the written melody is property, the improvised solo basically isn't, and the entire tradition is quoting other people's tunes inside a shared space

So: what theory of creativity does this bundle actually presuppose? My guess is that it's mostly romantic authorship — discrete works, individual originators, originality as novelty — and that Hofstadter's picture would be flatly incompatible with it. But I'm not sure the law strictly *needs* the romantic picture. Like, maybe the "building blocks are free" rule and the independent-creation defence are in better shape under a knob-twiddling view than under a genius view (if everyone is turning dials in the same inherited space, convergence is exactly what you'd predict), and it's only the groove/feel verdicts and the sampling rule that secretly assume creation is made of discrete ownable atoms.

Can you (1) reconstruct the implicit theory as cleanly as you can, (2) separate what the doctrine strictly presupposes from what it merely sits nicely with, (3) say whether the pieces are consistent with each other — especially "the space is free" vs "any fragment of a recording is owned" — and (4) tell me what would actually have to change if we rebuilt music copyright around the variation-in-a-space model instead. I'm imagining something like a "thickness of the space" standard, where protection tracks how much a work moves the space rather than how much it borrows from it, and where the shared space gets paid through some kind of pool instead of being either free or fenced — but tell me if that's confused, and say what a real version would look like and what it would break: who'd get paid, what happens to genres, whether it rewards or punishes people who work deep inside a strong tradition, whether novelty even remains the right thing to be measuring.
