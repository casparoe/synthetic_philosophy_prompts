I've been on my city's zoning board for about six years now and I keep feeling like the rules we apply only make sense against some background theory I can't quite name. I'd rather not get into which city it is, so here's the shape of it, in my own words.

Most of the city is capped at about three stories by a map drawn back in the 1960s. Buildings that were legal when they went up can stay forever even though they break every current rule, and if one burns down or gets taken out by a storm the owner can rebuild it more or less as it was with no review at all; if the same owner tore it down on purpose, the replacement goes through the whole process. At the same time, we do force every building to retrofit for earthquakes and fire safety, so it isn't that existing buildings are simply left alone.

Anything ten units or bigger triggers a design hearing. Anyone who owns or rents within about 300 feet can speak, and can appeal if they don't like the outcome; nobody else has that standing. The standard we're told to apply is whether the project fits the existing streetscape. There is no hearing, and no appeal, about what happens if we say no. Also, because the trigger is ten units, we see a definate stream of nine-unit proposals, and sometimes three or four of them on the same block in a couple of years.

New projects at that size also have to set aside some units at below-market rents and pay a per-unit fee toward parks, schools and transit. Existing buildings never pay any of that. The below-market units get allocated by lottery, and people who already live or work in the city get priority in it. Meanwhile the city has an official target for how many homes it wants built each year, and it misses it every year, and nothing happens to anybody when it does.

So what I want is the theory underneath the package. It looks to me like it treats the people already here, and the buildings already here, as having a kind of claim that someone who might move here one day simply doesn't have. But it also looks like it treats *change* as the thing that needs justifying rather than the state of the world, which is what the fire rule and the grandfathering are both doing. Are those two different pictures bolted together, or one picture, and if they conflict, which one is doing the real work? Does a set of rules like this strictly presuppose any particular answer to who has a right to the city, or would several quite different answers license the exact same rules? I'd like you to seperate what the practice really commits us to from what it merely sits comfortably with, and to say where it's genuinely underdetermined.

Then the second half: suppose we swapped in the opposite picture, where the people who would otherwise move here count the same as the people already here, and where a harm is judged by how things end up rather than by who changed them. What would actually have to change in the day to day mechanics of a board like mine? Concrete changes, please, not a manifesto.

Please answer in American English, and I'm not a philosopher, so keep the jargon down and explain it where you can't.
