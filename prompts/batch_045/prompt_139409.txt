hey — I'm a data engineer. I live in SQL, schema design, entity resolution across instances, migrations, that sort of thing. I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group (my partner's, I have no background in it) and they got onto Kripke: rigid designators, the necessary a posteriori, all of that. I have this hunch that the whole debate is basically primary keys versus views, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's an insight or whether I'm flattening something important.

So: do the translation as carefully and as unsympathetically as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use — schemas, rows, primary keys versus descriptive queries and materialised views, instances of a database, constraints and integrity checks, and the difference between what you can read off the DDL without looking at a single row and what you have to go inspect the data to find out. Translate, one at a time:

1. the descriptivist picture — a name like "Hesperus" gets its referent via a description (the brightest thing visible in the evening sky, say), so what it picks out depends on how things happen to be;
2. rigid designation — the name picks out the same object with respect to every counterfactual situation, not just the actual one;
3. the argument that if two rigid designators each pick out something and it's the same something, then the identity has to hold in every counterfactual situation too;
4. the necessary a posteriori — that identity is necessary, and yet you have to go out and look to find out whether it's true;
5. the mirror-image case, the contingent a priori — the metre bar in Paris, where someone who fixes the reference by pointing at it can know its length without measuring anything, even though the bar could easily have been a different length.

For each: what carries over cleanly, what only carries over if I squint or tweak it, and what I simply cannot say in my vocabulary at all. Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. What's left over, and is the leftover my problem (schemas-and-instances just can't represent it) or theirs (the distinction was never as crisp as it gets made out to be)?

Two places where I suspect the translation is cheating — tell me if I'm right: (a) cross-instance identity. For me, whether row 7 in instance A is "the same row" as row 9 in instance B is exactly what I decide when I design a key, so I suspect the translation is quietly answering a question that Kripke gets to treat as primitive. (b) "possible instance" versus "possible world". My instances are things that exist and that I could in principle query; his worlds are counterfactual and most of them never get materialised. Does that difference do any real work, or is it idle?

And here's the case from my actual job that started this. We have a table of astronomical objects with UUID primary keys, and separately a view defined as *the brightest object visible in the evening sky*, which happens to return the row with one particular UUID. A colleague says the view is just another name for that row. I think Kripke would say no, but I can't explain why in language that would survive a standup. Related: last year we did a migration where every UUID got remapped to a new one, and half the team is now convinced that means the rows "aren't really the same objects any more". I think that's confused and I can't say why, and I have a feeling it's the same confusion as in (a) — but if it's a different confusion, say so.

Oh, and one more: is there anything in a setup like mine that could even *be* a contingent a priori truth, or is that one never going to show up in a database?

If my hunch that this is all keys versus views is shallow, say so and tell me exactly where it goes shallow.
