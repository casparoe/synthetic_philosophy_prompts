**Draft: what the Sleeping Beauty argument is really about**

Setup, for anyone skimming: on Sunday a fair coin is flipped. Heads means Beauty is woken Monday only; Tails means she's woken Monday and Tuesday, with a memory-erasing drug in between so that each waking is subjectively indistinguishable from the others and she's never told what day it is. Question: what should her credence in Tails be when she finds herself awake? Halfers say 1/2, thirders say 1/3.

The standard story is that this is a fight about anthropics — about which self-locating principle you accept, the one that says you should reason as if you were randomly sampled from the actual observers versus the one that says possible observers too. I think that framing is wrong, or at least doesn't do the work people think it does. Here's the case that convinced me.

*The Two Wards.* Ada and Bea are two volunteers in identical windowless rooms. Both are fully briefed on the protocol; neither can tell which room she's in; they can't communicate. The coin: on Heads, exactly one of them is woken, and which one is decided by a further fair coin whose outcome neither of them ever learns; on Tails, both are woken. Otherwise the same question, same drug. Note what's been removed: no repeated wakings of a single person, no memory erasure, no "is Monday-Beauty the same agent as Tuesday-Beauty." The self-locating uncertainty is over *who you are*, not *when you are*. And structurally it's nearly identical to the original: two wakings on Tails, one on Heads.

Now the thing I want to hang the essay on: in this case both styles of anthropic reasoning give 2/3. The SIA-flavored route weights branches by how many wakings they contain — 1/2 × 2 against 1/2 × 1, normalized to 2/3. The SSA-flavored route treats your evidence as "I'm one of the subjects who is awake," notes that P(awake | Tails) = 1 while P(awake | Heads) = 1/2, and gets (1 × 1/2)/(1/2 + 1/4) = 2/3. And crucially, the classic halfer move — on waking I've learned nothing I didn't already know, so I stay at the objective chance of 1/2 — is simply unavailable here, because "I am awake" *is* new information and it does favor Tails. So if the 1/2-versus-1/3 gap were really driven by the choice of anthropic principle, this case shouldn't be able to come out at 2/3. My conclusion: the gap in the original problem needs an extra premise that has nothing to do with anthropics, something about whether the temporally distinct wakings of one continuing subject are the kind of thing that can be "this one" — whether erasing someone's memory splits her into two chances to be her. The original problem is under-described in a way the Two Wards case isn't, and people are quietly filling in the description differently. That's the thesis.

*Where I get nervous.* Consider the Lottery Schedule: same as the original, except that on Tails the number of wakings is itself drawn by a fair coin — one waking with probability 1/2, two with probability 1/2 — and Beauty knows the mechanism but not the draw. If you count wakings in the long run, four runs give you two Heads-wakings and three Tails-wakings, so 3/5. The general recipe, when Heads always yields exactly one waking and the Tails count averages k and Beauty can observe nothing correlated with the count, is P(Tails) = k/(1 + k) — which gives 1/2 at k = 1, 2/3 at k = 2, 3/5 at k = 1.5, and also implies she should end up 2/3 confident the schedule came up "two." But this recipe is a ratio of *expected* counts, and it inherits everything fragile about expectations. Run a St. Petersburg-style schedule — on Tails, 2^n wakings with probability 2^-n — and the expectation is infinite, so the recipe returns something like certainty, while the long-run frequency in any number of trials anyone could actually run is dominated by absurdly rare mega-runs and looks nothing like it. I genuinely don't know whether that's a reductio of the whole frequency-based approach or just a reminder that "a randomly selected waking" isn't well defined without a cutoff.

*And a puzzle I can't settle at all.* Wakings have durations. Suppose on Heads she gets a single 100-minute waking, and on Tails she gets 100 separate one-minute wakings, so total conscious time is the same either way. Counting wakings gives 100/101; counting subject-minutes gives 1/2. My suspicion is that the thirder answer is quietly importing a choice of measure over conscious time, and I have no idea what principled rule would pick one.
