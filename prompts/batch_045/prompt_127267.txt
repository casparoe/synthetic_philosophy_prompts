I work as a freight dispatcher, I left school at seventeen, and for about four years now I have been reading philosophy on my own, with no classes and nobody to check me against. I take part in a small online slow-reading group that works through a page or two of Pascal's *Pensées* a week; most of the members are in the same position I am.

This week we reached the fragments on justice. The line that keeps getting repeated, in the form our group has fallen into using, is roughly this: justice without force is helpless, force without justice is tyrannical. (I have noticed that different English editions word the first half differently, and that people in the group slide between force, power, and might without noticing.) A member I will call D. used the line to close a discussion from the previous week. Another member, M., had been fined for taking part in a sit-in at the council offices over a housing decision she believes is unjust, and she asked whether she ought to pay the fine or refuse it. D. said the line settles it: nobody can actually settle what justice requires, force is what in fact decides, so whatever the authorities have settled is what we treat as justice, and M.'s conscience is only her own will wearing a moral costume. Pay the fine.

I do not believe one sentence can be doing all of that, and I suspect D. is relying on a slide between claims I cannot yet pull apart. I am not asking what the author meant, and I do not want anything about editions, commentaries, or the surrounding text that I would have to look up: I have no library access and could not check it. I want the sentence itself taken apart.

Here are the readings I have managed to distinguish so far, with my own labels. Please correct them, collapse any that are not really distinct, and add the ones I have missed.

1. The enforcement reading: a just rule that no one is able to make anyone follow produces no compliance. A remark about how the world works, close to a platitude.
2. The not-really-a-rule reading: a norm with no sanction behind it is not a law at all, or does not bind; coercion is part of what law is.
3. The we-cannot-tell reading: disputes about justice are never closed by argument, so the outcome that force produces is the only workable stand-in for justice, and we should treat the settlement as just.
4. The whatever-wins-is-just reading: there is no standard beyond what the powerful manage to establish, so force does not track justice but makes it.
5. The naked-force-has-no-authority reading: coercion that answers to no standard of justice does not bind, it merely overpowers, and the sentence is a condemnation rather than a quietist shrug.
6. The order-is-precious reading: even where a rule is unjust, having disputes closed at all is a great good, and that gives a reason to comply which has nothing to do with the justice of the rule.
7. The do-not-reopen-it reading: you may think a decision wrong, but you may not substitute your own judgment for the settlement; if you want it changed, use the authorized routes.

My own attempt at a combination, which I cannot tell is genuinely different from 3, 6 and 7 or only a restatement of them: call it the Settlement Principle — the fact that a dispute has been closed by someone able to close it gives you a reason to comply and a reason not to reopen it, but gives you no reason whatever to believe the settlement just.

There are also six questions I cannot settle, and I suspect the whole disagreement turns on them:

(a) Helpless how? Does it mean that no one will comply, or that no obligation is created, or that the thing is not law, or only that it usually fails? Does D. need the strongest of these, and is the strongest of them true?
(b) Is justice the same word in the same sense in the two halves? If it means whatever force establishes, the second half seems to say nothing at all, since force without justice would then be impossible. If it is an independent standard, the first half becomes a contingent claim about effectiveness that might be false. Can the sentence hold both at once, or does its force depend on sliding between them?
(c) Whose force? Force is always somebody's: the bylaw, the court that upholds it, the legislature, the protest that blocks the entrance, the crowd that occupies the office. The sentence needs a specification it does not contain. And if the answer is whoever wins, does justice become retroactive, so that a successful rebellion was just all along and a failed one was not? Does any reading escape that without quietly smuggling in an independent standard?
(d) Does nobody can settle what justice requires mean there is nothing to settle, or that we cannot find out, or merely that we keep disagreeing? And whichever it means, does the step to so treat the settlement as authoritative follow, or does it need an extra premise about the value of peace or the standing of an established order?
(e) What would treating as just even be: acting as if it were just, believing it just, or refraining from reopening it? D. seems to need at least the first and the third, and I suspect the argument supports at most the first.
(f) Does the sentence apply to itself? If what settles things is force, then D.'s using it to end the conversation is itself a small exercise of force, someone's say-so prevailing. Is that a real difficulty for the deflationary readings or only a debating trick? I genuinely cannot tell.

Please lay the answer out as a numbered menu of readings, mine as corrected plus any additions, and for each give: the precise claim in a sentence or two; what kind of claim it is (about how the world works, about what our words mean, or about what we owe); whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive but probably false, or incoherent; and whether it would actually deliver D.'s advice to M. or M.'s refusal instead. I have a suspicion that the second half of the sentence can be read as a licence for resistance rather than compliance, and I would like that either confirmed or killed. Then answer the six questions briefly, and say which reading you think people in D.'s position are usually reaching for, since that matters to me more than the most charitable reading available. Please do not cite or recommend any further reading; I could not get hold of it.

Finish with one line, in exactly this form and with nothing after it, where N is the number of the reading from your menu that you think is the most defensible thing to mean by the sentence: the one that is neither trivial nor false and that does as much of the work people want from it as anything can.

\answer{N}
