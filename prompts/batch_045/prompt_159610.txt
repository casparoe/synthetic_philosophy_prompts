i help run a pub philosophy thing once a month and next month the topic is time. last time someone brought up relativity and whether the future is already sitting there waiting for us and it went nowhere, everyone just repeating that everything is relative and nobody able to say what actually follows. i've been reading about the Rietdijk-Putnam argument on my own since then and i think i get the shape of it: if which distant events count as happening now depends on how fast and which way you're moving, and if that can't be a fact about what is real, then nothing gets to be specially real and it's all one block. i want to open the night with a poem that does that argument in its bones instead of asserting it.

so: a sestina. 39 lines, six stanzas of six plus the three-line envoi, unrhymed, roughly ten syllables a line but don't let the meter wreck the physics. the six end-words have to be six ordinary concrete events, things like a kettle or a door or a hand, not words like event or moment.

here's the bit i care about, and i worked it out with a bit of paper so please take it seriously: in the standard sestina rotation every single pair of end-words gets swapped at some point over the six stanzas. that means if any two of your six events can causally affect each other, some stanza will put them in an order no observer could ever get. so all six have to be pairwise outside each other's light cones, and the clean way to guarantee that is to put all six on one flat now-slice in some frame, six places at the same instant, spread out across more than one dimension, and then let every stanza be a different observer moving fast in a different direction. no pair may be timelike or lightlike separated, and the poem should make it feel like these six things are each happening in total ignorance of the others.

the envoi is the payoff: all six words together, two to a line, no order left to argue about. that has to land the inference from they disagree about the order to none of these is more real than the others, and it shouldn't just announce it. show why someone takes that step, the thought that reality can't depend on how fast somebody happens to be walking, and let a listener who's never heard of relativity feel the floor move. please keep spacetime, frame, spacelike and block universe out of the verse itself.

two accuracy things, because i'd like an actual physicist in the room not to wince. first, don't let it slide into being about what anybody can see or has information about: this isn't observation lag, it's simultaneity after you've already corrected for light travel time, and no signal could ever settle the disagreement. second, be honest about scale: if the events are in the same room you'd need speeds nobody has, so either put them light-years apart, the walking-toward-a-distant-galaxy trick where a stroll shifts their now by days, or give your observers a real fraction of light speed. say which you picked.

after the poem, a short prose note, not verse: the six events with rough places and times, which frame each stanza is in as a direction and rough speed, and a check that each stanza's ordering really could come from some direction of motion. if one of them can't, move the events around or swap two end-words until it can, and tell me if you had to. if you think the argument's last step is bad, write the poem as if it works and put your objection in the note.

cheers, and keep it readable aloud, half the room will be on their third pint.
