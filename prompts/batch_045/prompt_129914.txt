My understanding of Augustine is this: a lie is not merely saying something false. The heart of lying is a kind of inner dividedness. Augustine says that a person lies when they have one thing in their mind and they utter, or otherwise signify, another thing, and they do this with the will to deceive — that is, they want their audience to accept the proposition they utter rather than the one they actually have in mind. So the wrong-making feature is a willed gap between inner thought and outer utterance, aimed at getting someone to believe the utterance. I am paraphrasing rather than quoting, and I may be missing nuance, but I hope this is close enough to his view.

Now here is the argument I built. I have numbered the steps so we can argue about them precisely.

(1) According to Augustine, a speaker S lies if and only if: (a) S has some proposition P in mind, in the sense of believing or opining that P; (b) S utters or signifies a different proposition Q; and (c) S does this with the will that the audience come to believe Q, or, more broadly, accept Q as what S really thinks.

(2) Map this onto a modern LLM assistant. Let what the model has in mind be the proposition expressed by its highest-probability continuation, its argmax completion, at the moment of generation. Let what the model utters be the sentence it actually emits after sampling, decoding, or whatever generation procedure is used.

(3) In ordinary factual question-and-answer chat, the product is designed so that the user is supposed to accept the assistant’s declarative output as the assistant’s assertion, that is, as something the assistant puts forward as true. So condition (c) is satisfied for ordinary assistant outputs.

(4) Therefore, in ordinary chat, whenever an LLM emits a declarative sentence Q but its argmax mind is not-Q, the LLM lies by Augustine’s definition, assuming the other conditions hold, and in ordinary chat they do.

(5) But now consider cases that are not ordinary factual assertions: the assistant is asked to write a short historical-fiction scene, to roleplay a fictional character, to translate into French the sentence The Earth is flat, or to tell a joke. In all of these cases, the model’s argmax mind is, let us suppose, the mundane truth, for example that the Earth is not flat or that the fictional character does not exist, while the emitted sentence expresses some other proposition, such as a French sentence asserting that the Earth is flat or a line of dialogue in which the character says something false.

(6) By (1), (2), and (5), these fiction, roleplay, translation, and joke cases are also lies, because the model has one proposition in mind and emits another, and, by the product design, the user is at least supposed to accept the emitted sentence as the content of the story, the character’s speech, the translation, or the joke.

(7) So Augustine’s definition, plus the natural engineering mapping in (2), implies that a huge amount of clearly non-lying speech — fiction, roleplay, translation of false statements, jokes, and probably most hypothetical reasoning — is actually lying.

(8) That cannot be right. Augustine himself sets jokes aside, because the joker’s manner shows there is no will to deceive. And nobody seriously thinks that writing a novel, translating a sentence, or playing a role is lying in the moral sense.
