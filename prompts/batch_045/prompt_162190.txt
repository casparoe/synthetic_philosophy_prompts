I am a philosopher giving a twenty-minute paper at a two-hour seminar next month, and I want a question-period preparation sheet, not a defence of the view.

The paper: I accept the substance of the Fischer thesis as it is usually stated — in his 1961 book Griff nach der Weltmacht Fischer argued from the imperial archives that Germany's leaders deliberately used the July Crisis to bring about a war of expansion — but I argue that the verdict it licenses is a verdict about a government, not about a nation. My positive claim is an authorisation test: a political community can be held responsible for what its government did only where it had (i) workable channels through which it could have learned that the decision was being taken, (ii) institutional means of contesting it or withholding consent, and (iii) a genuine opportunity to use them. Imperial Germany in July 1914 fails that test: a chancellor answerable to the Kaiser rather than to a Reichstag that could not remove him, foreign policy and the army largely outside parliamentary control, a Prussian Landtag elected under the three-class franchise, and emergency arrangements that would have suspended ordinary political contestation the moment mobilisation was ordered. So the war-guilt clause and the reparations bill visited on the German nation were a moral category error — and I close by arguing that this still shapes what a state can honestly apologise for today.

The room: five diplomatic historians of Imperial Germany, one of whom dismisses what she calls the Fischer orthodoxy and another of whom works on the German right's reception of Versailles; two philosophers who write on collective and corporate moral responsibility; and one international-relations theorist who holds that intentions are epiphenomenal to alliance structure and mobilisation timetables. I am a philosopher working from secondary sources, and I will be out of my depth on archival detail.

What I want, in British English: the objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, phrased in the vocabulary of whoever raises them — the historians', the collective-responsibility philosophers', the structural realist's — each with a two-minute reply I could give aloud, a note on where that reply is weak, a statement of what I should concede before anyone asks, and the single reply that has to land if the paper is to survive at all. Include the objection I am most likely to be hoping nobody raises, with an honest note on why I cannot answer it well; my own guess is that the realist will point out that applying my test symmetrically dissolves blame for Russia, Britain and France as well, and that I have no principled way to stop it, but tell me if you think the real one is different. Please give me no reading: no citations, bibliographies or archival references — I do not need them, and a model without web access could only invent them.
