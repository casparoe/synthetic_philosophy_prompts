**The wasp that couldn't stop starting over**

Most tellings go like this. A female digger wasp paralyses prey, drops it near the entrance of her burrow, goes inside to inspect the nest, comes back out, and drags the prey in. But if, while she's inside, someone moves the prey a few inches away, she comes out, finds it, drags it back to the entrance — and then goes in to inspect the nest *again*, leaving the prey outside again. So the prey can be moved again, and again; reportedly the cycle can be repeated many times over, some accounts say more or less indefinitely. The usual moral: behavior that looks purposive and adaptable can really be a rigid tape, and what looks like deliberation in us might just be a much longer tape. Hofstadter and Dennett put the wasp to work this way decades ago, and it's been a stock intuition pump in free-will arguments ever since.

I want to argue that the pump does much less work than people think, and that the interesting lesson is nearly the opposite of the usual one.

**1. The surprise isn't the repetition.** A loop that re-runs is just a loop; nothing philosophically loaded there. What's genuinely odd is that the wasp seems to treat a *fact about the world* (prey sits at the threshold) as if it were an *output of her own routine*. Call it achievement conflation: she can't represent "prey at threshold" as a condition that holds for reasons she might want to know about, only as something her routine brings about. So when the condition breaks, the only repair available to her is to re-run the routine that produces it. Her failure isn't behavioral, it's ontological — she has no slot for "something interfered."

**2. But maybe that's not failure at all.** Suppose she has no causal model of disturbance. Prey can be displaced by wind, by ants, by a rival, by an experimenter's forceps. If you can't tell which, then a policy of *re-establish the invariant before continuing* looks pretty sensible: inspection is cheap, dragging in a prey that has been tampered with is expensive, and re-running the check is idempotent — doing it twice is no worse than doing it once. On this reading sphexishness isn't a bug but a reset protocol: a robustness strategy available to an agent that can't do counterfactual bookkeeping. And notice we do the same thing. Walk back into the room you came from to remember what you came for: that's us exploiting the world as memory because we can't read our own goal stack. The wasp isn't less flexible than we thought; she's optimizing under information poverty.

**3. A better question than "free or machine?"** Ask instead about absorbance: how many independent perturbations can a routine absorb before it re-enters at step one, and how much of the routine gets thrown away when it does? Human routines have huge absorbance in familiar domains, and spectacular failures of it elsewhere — re-checking the stove, reloading a page that hasn't changed, re-running an argument in the shower. Nothing categorical seperates us from the wasp here. What seperates us is a family of decorrelation mechanisms: noticing that the world changed, asking *why* it changed, and re-appraising the plan in that light.

**4. So, free will.** Two conclusions, one negative and one I'm less sure about. (a) The wasp can't support incompatibilism: the supposedly damning feature — mechanically re-running — is perfectly compatible with the behavior being locally optimal, and being a deterministic optimizer doesn't make you unfree in any sense the incompatibilist actually needs. (b) But the case does undercut a voluntarist picture on which agency consists in the capacity to step in from outside the routine. If agency is graded re-appraisal capacity, it's an empirical, trainable, partly situative property rather than a status. And then "could have done otherwise" should mean something like: the agent *had*, and could have deployed, a mechanism that would have re-appraised the situation. Which is a thing we can partly lose — to fatigue, grief, addiction, panic.
