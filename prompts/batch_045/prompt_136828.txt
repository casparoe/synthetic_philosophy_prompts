A popular book I picked up for a long flight — one of those the-universe-is-made-of-numbers shelf books, written by a competent science journalist rather than a physicist — has a chapter that leans hard on Wigner's essay about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics. The passage that stuck with me runs roughly like this: "Wigner's point was that the laws of physics are written in the language of mathematics, and that the mathematics physicists reach for is nearly always already on the shelf: whole formalisms developed generations earlier by pure mathematicians who had no interest in applications whatsoever — complex numbers, non-Euclidean geometry, group theory — and then nature turns out to obey them to ten decimal places. That is the miracle, and there is no naturalistic explanation for it, since the mathematics was not built to fit the physics. It follows that the universe is at bottom a mathematical structure and that physics is really just mathematics applied to one particular object. It also follows that the final theory, when we find it, will be a set of equations so compelling that no alternative could have been written down — otherwise the effectiveness would not be unreasonable at all, merely lucky." The book then spends two pages dismissing the obvious deflationary answer — that we tally the hits and forget the misses, and that the mathematics we happen to have is itself shaped by the spatial, temporal, object-sized world our brains evolved in — as an evasion that explains nothing, and concludes that Wigner's essay is best read as an argument for mathematical Platonism.

My problem is that I've now read the essay twice and can't reconstruct where Wigner's actual claim stops and the journalist's begins. I'm fairly sure the universe-is-made-of-mathematics bit is the book's own addition; my half-memory is that Wigner is more careful, that his puzzle is specifically about the formulation of laws rather than about what the world is made of, and that he somewhere says he has no explanation to offer and isn't certain the question is even well posed. So I'd like a sentence-by-sentence audit rather than a general verdict: what would someone who has actually thought about the essay and its reception accept in that passage, what is defensible compression, what is a subtle distortion, and is anything simply wrong? In particular: would a Platonist reading be one Wigner himself would have endorsed, and is the claim that no alternative formalism could have been written down doing more work than it can bear?

A further wrinkle, because it is where my reading group got stuck. One of us proposed a way of testing the thesis: if Wigner's puzzle is real, there should be an asymmetry in the historical record — the needed formalism should generally be *ahead* of the physics, sitting there waiting, developed for internal mathematical reasons before anyone knew it was needed. Call it the lead-time test. Another member pointed out that the record is mixed at best: Fourier built his series to solve the heat equation, Newton more or less invented calculus because mechanics needed it, delta functions and distributions were rigged up after quantum mechanics demanded them, and Feynman's path integrals are still not rigorous in the way their admirers would like. A third replied that this may all be beside the point, because the puzzle isn't about who got there first but about why a formalism invented for one purpose keeps turning out to be exactly right for a phenomenon nobody had in mind — matrices for Heisenberg, Riemannian geometry for gravitation — and that a mixed lead-time record doesn't obviously touch that. So please also tell me whether the lead-time test is a fair test of the thesis the book attributes to Wigner, and whether that test together with the deflationary selection story the book waves away actually dissolves the puzzle or only relocates it. Commit to a definite overall judgment on the passage — is it usable, or should I stop repeating it to people? — with your reasons, and name the single move in it that misleads most.
