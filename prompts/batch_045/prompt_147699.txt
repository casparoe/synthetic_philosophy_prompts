I run a 90-minute session for a graduate reading group on collective rationality and social epistemology — about twenty people, mostly philosophy and public-policy students with a few from computer science, none of whom will have read anything on the topic beforehand. I want a runnable activity that makes them feel the unilateralist's curse from the inside, and I want the design to carry the point rather than the debrief: they should not hear the name, or any statement of the thesis, until after the last round.

The structure I have in mind is a repeated card game. Each round the world is in one of two hidden states; each participant gets a private, independent, imperfect signal of which state it is; and any participant may unilaterally launch an action whose gains and losses are shared by everyone. If nobody launches, everyone gets the status quo. Give me the actual payoff numbers, the signal accuracy, the number of rounds, and show the arithmetic, under these constraints:

- A single decider acting alone on a favourable private signal should be barely right to launch — expected value positive but small — so that nobody in the room is being reckless or irrational.
- The same game with twenty people under an anyone-may-launch rule should come out clearly worse for everyone than never launching.
- The damage should grow with the size of the group, and participants should be able to see why: the chance that someone gets a favourable signal when the world is good saturates almost immediately as deciders are added, while the chance that someone gets a falsely favourable signal when the world is bad keeps climbing. If your numbers do not make that asymmetry visible within the number of rounds we can fit, say what to change and consider running identical parameters once with groups of four and once with twenty.
- Payoffs are points, not money. Tell me what the points should be worth and how to keep individual scorekeeping from undermining the fact that the stakes are shared.

I also want two short variant rounds, your choice of which two, each with a note on what it is meant to reveal:

1. One participant is privately designated as better informed — they see two independent signals, or a signal with higher accuracy. The question I want on the table is whether the obvious remedy, defer unless there is broad agreement, simply throws away the best evidence in the room.
2. The default flips: the action happens unless someone unilaterally vetoes it. Does the same logic push toward paralysis, and what does that suggest about whether the standard remedy is quietly biased toward the status quo?
3. All signals in a round come from a single shared draw, so that errors are perfectly correlated. My guess is that the curse disappears; I want participants to feel that it is the independence of the mistakes, not their size, that does the work.

Practicalities: no discussion before or during the sealed decisions in the core rounds, and I need a way to enforce that with twenty people in a room without it feeling like a compliance exercise. Give me a minute-by-minute timeline, the materials, and a scoring sheet.

Then the debrief: which moments in the session map onto which part of the structure, and what I should be able to say in five minutes once they have seen the pattern. I want them to arrive at the fix themselves as a matter of where the threshold sits — launching only when at least k of N favour it, with k scaled to group size — rather than as a matter of exhorting people to be more careful, and then to see what follows about when adding a decision-maker makes a group worse off.

Finally, be honest about three things. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: I expect one participant who never launches on principle and one who launches every time, and I do not know how to handle either. Which features of the real-world cases the game cannot reproduce — the very small probabilities against catastrophic magnitudes, the fact that the stakes themselves are contested rather than given, the career and reputational incentives attached to being the one who acted — and how much that costs. And one thing about the curse that the activity will leave participants with a false or misleading impression of, which I will have to correct out loud.
