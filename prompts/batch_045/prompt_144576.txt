I take part in a small monthly "theme and variations" exchange online. Someone posts a short theme, each of us submits one transformation of it, and a friend of mine — the only one of us with real training — writes a few lines on every entry and files it as either "creative" or "knob-twiddling." Her vocabulary comes from a cognitive-science book she read years ago: a style is a space, the dimensions of that space are "knobs," ordinary work turns the knobs that are already there, and genuinely creative work changes which knobs there are — she calls that "re-racking." Here are her verdicts so far:

- A Baroque dance theme restated as a bebop head, the melodic intervals preserved exactly but the harmonic and rhythmic language entirely new: creative.
- The same theme kept in its own style but pushed hard on every surface setting at once — double tempo, minor mode, heavy ornamentation, extreme register: knob-twiddling.
- The theme's pitches kept note for note and reharmonized so drastically that the tune now sounds like a different melody altogether: creative.
- The theme subjected to a constraint imported from somewhere else entirely — every phrase must be a palindrome — the first time anyone in the exchange has tried such a thing: creative.
- A later entry applying that same palindrome constraint to the same theme, with a different-sounding result: knob-twiddling.
- One extreme composite of familiar operations — retrograde, compressed to half length, recast in 7/8 — very far from the theme, but describable in a single line: knob-twiddling.

The entry I want to ask about is next month's. Someone is submitting not one variation but a cycle of thirty-two, in which each variation differs from the one before by exactly one familiar knob-turn: mode, metre, tempo, register, a single chord substitution. No single link in the chain is the sort of thing she would call creative. But the chain is ordered so that it walks steadily out of the theme's home style and arrives, in the last four bars, at a style none of us has ever used. The entrant's note says only: "I didn't add a knob. I just walked."

What will she call it, and why? I'm less interested in the verdict than in the rule she is running. I can think of at least three that fit her six verdicts equally well and that come apart here: one turning on whether a new knob has actually been posited, one on whether the theme has been re-identified as this rather than that — its intervals, its pitches, its durations, its scaffolding — and one on whether the result was findable by ordinary taste working the old knobs. Tell me which of these her record best supports, what each predicts about the cycle, and give me your own best guess with the reason for preferring it. Then tell me which single further submission would most cleanly separate the rules: the same thirty-two variations scrambled out of order, a second and different walk to the same destination, or a walk that leaves home and returns.
