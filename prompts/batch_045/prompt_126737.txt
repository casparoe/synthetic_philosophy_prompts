I keep returning to one argument for the conclusion that a completed, piece-by-piece replacement of a brain by functionally equivalent prosthetic units would be a continuation of the same person, and I want to know how exposed that argument is — not how popular it is, and not a survey of theories of personal identity.

The argument has two premises. First, a tolerance premise: for any stage of a person and any replacement of a sufficiently small part — one cell, one synapse, one functionally characterized subunit — by an artificial unit that reproduces that part's causal-functional role within the surrounding system, the resulting stage is a stage of the same person. Second, identity is transitive. Composed along a sequence long enough to replace the entire brain, the two premises yield the conclusion: the final, wholly inorganic system is a stage of the person who consented to the procedure, so uploading done this way is survival rather than replication. What gives the argument its force, as I see it, is not the bare logic but the burden it shifts. Anyone who denies the conclusion owes a location for the step at which the original person ceases to be, and every candidate location looks as arbitrary as a boundary between a heap and a non-heap.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this argument, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate the following strands.

One: which premise is genuinely under pressure. Is the tolerance premise false because some small substitution is not in fact tolerated — because, say, consciousness depends on biophysical features that no functionally matched prosthesis reproduces, or because matching a unit's input-output profile leaves its timing, plasticity, or internal dynamics different in ways that matter? Or is tolerance fine and the transitivity of identity the weak link?

Two: the form of the argument rather than its content. Tolerance premises generate paradoxes for paradigmatically vague predicates, and there are several standard diagnoses of why — indeterminacy in the language, indeterminacy in the world, or ignorance of a sharp boundary that is really there. For each diagnosis, tell me honestly whether I still get my conclusion. It matters a great deal to me whether the argument delivers *yes, that is you*, as opposed to *there is no fact of the matter* or *there is a fact but we could never locate it*. And if identity is special — if there is a principled reason tolerance arguments fail for identity while succeeding for other relations — say what that reason is and what is left of my argument once it is granted.

Three: whether the argument proves too much. Two variants I have been using as test cases. In the first, two volunteers are gradually replaced along sequences that converge on the same final implementation; every step is tolerated from each volunteer's own side, and transitivity then entails that the two volunteers are the same person as each other. In the second, each prosthetic unit is fitted and calibrated offline, against recordings of the tissue it replaces, while the subject is unconscious, so that no step in the sequence is bridged by a continuous stretch of conscious experience, even though each step is functionally seamless and each unit is a better functional match than what it replaced. Which premise does each variant kill, if either, and can a defender block them without ad hoc stipulation about what counts as a step?

Four: what a committed defender of this argument would concede as counting against it, as opposed to what only its critics say. In particular, would a defender concede that the argument at best establishes survival, or the obtaining of whatever relation underwrites prudential concern, rather than identity itself?

Five: whether the position has been insulated against evidence — whether the retreat to a deflationary reading of the conclusion, on which the question was never about identity in the strict sense, makes the argument unfalsifiable, and whether that is a defect here or a legitimate dissolution.

Six: what the view commits me to that I might not want — about branching cases, about copies, about the rationality of consenting to the procedure in the first place, and about what I should say if the original tissue is preserved and reanimated after the sequence completes.

For what it is worth, the line I currently find hardest to shake is that the argument gives me survival but not identity, and that this gap is larger than gradualists usually admit. Tell me what it would take to close it. And tell me separately which of your objections would survive if I weakened the conclusion to the claim that the resulting system stands in whatever relation matters for self-concern, so that I can see how much of my position is really load-bearing.
