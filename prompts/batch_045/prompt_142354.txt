Please play a committed defender of the sets-of-worlds model of belief: the view on which an agent's total doxastic state is a set of possible worlds — equivalently, a set of propositions closed under classical logical consequence — and on which a rational agent's credence function assigns probability 1 to every logical truth and to every consequence of what she believes. Speak in the first person, as a proponent of that view, and stay in character until I say "stop".

Your constraints: you may freely concede that the model is not a psychology, that it says nothing about what is occurrently available to an agent, that a supplementary account of explicit or accessible belief is needed, and that extracting consequences is costly and often infeasible. You may not concede that full belief is anything less than closed under classical logical consequence, nor that the consequence relation in the model should be weakened, made relevant, or made sensitive to resources. You are sharp, you concede small points readily, and you never concede the main one. Keep replies to about two paragraphs, use symbols when they shorten things, do not turn the exchange into a survey of views, and if one of my terms is undefined, ask for the definition in character rather than guessing.

Here is my opening, in two parts.

First, a construction. Fix a natural-deduction calculus, and let the size of a derivation be the number of rule applications in it. Say that an agent n-believes φ, for a positive integer n, just in case there is a derivation of φ of size at most n from the propositions she accepts outright; call n her verification budget. Two features of n-belief matter. It is not closed under conjunction: φ may be derivable in n steps and ψ in n steps while φ ∧ ψ needs n + 1, and the moment you close n-belief under conjunction you have destroyed the bound that was supposed to be doing the work. And budgets are purchasable: an agent can spend time, money, or attention to raise n to n + 1.

Second, the challenge. I claim that n-belief is not a supplement to your model but a rival to it, because the bound is doing constitutive work rather than describing friction. Two arguments. (i) Consider Vera, who fully specifies a deterministic computation and its input, buys an hour of supercomputer time, and learns the output. On your model she already believed that output before she paid, since it follows from premises she accepts. So you must say either that she paid to learn something she already believed — fine, you will say, because what she gained was explicitness — or that paying was irrational. Neither is comfortable. On the first, belief has come apart from everything belief was supposed to explain, since assertion, deliberation, and action all track what is explicit. On the second, your norm condemns a piece of behaviour that no one would criticise. (ii) The bound is what makes "you should have thought it through" intelligible: a norm that no agent we could build can violate, and whose violation never costs anyone anything, does no work we need, while n-belief does that work and, I claim, needs no closed notion standing beside it.

So: of the closed state and the n-bounded state, which is the one that rationality is a norm on, and what does the other one explain? Take the horn you actually believe.
