So I'm on this little committee at my university that helps the housing office redo the web form where incoming first-years pick their meal plan, and at the same time I'm taking a philosophy course on autonomy and well-being this term, which has made me completely overthink what should have been a boring admin task. Here's the setup. There are basically two plans: the "flex" plan, which is cheaper and dumps a big pile of points into your account that you can spend anywhere, including the late-night places that sell milkshakes and mozzarella sticks; and the "residential" plan, which costs a bit more, gets you into the dining halls for actual meals, and gives you fewer snack points. About 80% of first-years pick flex. Then, totally reliably, by around November a lot of them have burned through their points, don't want to spend their own money, and end up eating oddly or skipping meals; the housing office gets a pile of complaints, and every year the survey they send out has a big chunk of those same students saying they wish they'd gone with the other plan. So we're trying to fix it, and we've narrowed it to three ideas: (1) make the residential plan the one that's already ticked when the page loads, so choosing flex takes an extra click; (2) leave the order alone but put a single line on the page saying how many first-years run out of points before Thanksgiving and roughly how much they end up spending out of pocket; (3) let people choose flex normally, but have the confirmation email show up two days later with a link, so there's a beat where they can change their mind.

Everyone on the committee agrees all three would move some people over to residential, and that none of them really inconveniences anyone who genuinely wants flex — you can still get it in about ten seconds in every version. The fight is about whether that even matters. My roommate, who isn't in the class, says the whole thing is condescending and that we obviously think we know better than these students what's good for them, and I notice I can't really deny that. But a guy in my seminar says that's confused: we aren't substituting our judgment about what a good life or a good semester looks like, we're just helping students get the thing they'd have picked for themselves if they'd been thinking about November while filling out a form in July — and he says that difference is the whole ballgame. Someone else said that doesn't rescue any of it, because what's objectionable isn't whose judgment wins but the fact that you're steering someone by a route that doesn't go through their own reasoning at all, and that on that score option (1) might actually be the least bad, since at least it isn't pretending to be informing anyone. A fourth person said none of this is even a moral question, because there's no way to put two options on a page without some order and some wording and some amount of clicking, so we might as well pick the version that works.

I have a short paper on this due in like ten days and I genuinely dont know where I land. Help me sort it out: is there a real moral difference between the three options, or are they all the same thing wearing different hats — and whichever way that goes, which one should we actually do? I'd especially like to know what to say to my roommate, since her objection is the one I feel the pull of but can neither answer nor shake. Please don't just tell me to survey the students or A/B test it or run a pilot — I know that's the sensible practicalmove, and it isn't what I'm asking.
