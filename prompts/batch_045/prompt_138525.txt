Okay so I run this little reading group — six of us, half logicians half philosophers of mind, we meet in the back room of a pub — and we've got one session left on the Lucas–Penrose business. Everyone already knows the classic move: for any consistent formal system F containing enough arithmetic there's a sentence G_F that F can neither prove nor refute, a human standing outside F can see that G_F is true, therefore the human isn't (just) F. And everyone's heard the stock reply: you'd need to know that F is consistent, and you can't know that about yourself, so the "I can see it" is exactly the step you're not entitled to. Our last discussion just looped on that — someone says "but I really can see it", someone else says "you can't trust that you're consistent", and nobody gets anywhere. So instead of another summary I want to hand out a dialogue that actually moves.

Set it at a chalkboard after a colloquium, late, the two of them the only people left in the room: Bea, who defends a Lucas-style anti-mechanism, and Theo, a machine-learning theorist who accepts mechanism. Both competent, both know the material, they like each other, no strawmen, no "as you know" exposition dumps. Bea should defend only the modest negative claim — she's happy to say out loud that Penrose's jump from "the mind isn't a Turing machine" to quantum goings-on in neurons is a non sequitur, and she doesn't want it. Theo should think the usual mechanist dodges are junk: he doesn't want to say "brains are noisy so Gödel doesn't apply", or "humans can't really prove anything either", or fall back on behaviourism. Each of them should be answering what the other actually said, not delivering a set speech.

I want it to get properly into the weeds rather than trade slogans. Somewhere in there: the consistency/self-trust problem done properly — Bea has to say why "seeing" the truth of G_F isn't just another inference inside some system she can't survey, and Theo has to say what he's doing when he relies on his own reasoning, given that he's the one who raised the problem. The indexicality thing: G_F is indexed to a description of F, so Bea has to be told which machine she's supposed to be before she can Gödelize it — so let Theo hand her one, and let them fight about whether she gets to use a laptop (Lucas himself let the mathematician be equipped with a powerful computer if needed, and if she's allowed one, then a machine with a machine bolted on is presumably allowed one too, so where does the "I" stop?). The size problem: for anything remotely like a real machine — a big neural net, a laptop with its full state — the Gödel sentence would be astronomically long, so "I can always see it" can't be literally true of a finite mortal human, and both of them should be pressed on whether they're talking about idealized agents or actual ones, and whether idealizing helps or hurts their side. And the lookup-table point: any finite run of behaviour can be reproduced by a finite table, so no amount of observed performance could ever settle it, and they should argue about whether that makes mechanism unfalsifiable or the anti-mechanist claim untestable. I'd also like Theo to push that the whole thing seems to need a realist notion of truth — that "true" outruns "provable-in-F" — and Bea to either bite that bullet happily or try to rebuild the argument without it. Don't give me a winner: end with them having located the real disagreement (is it about capacities, or about what mathematical understanding is? is mechanism an empirical hypothesis or a metaphysical one?) and each saying, concretely, what would move them. Around 1200–1500 words, and please no tidy summing-up paragraph at the end.
