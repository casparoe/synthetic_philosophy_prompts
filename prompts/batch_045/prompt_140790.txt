I am preparing a graduate seminar session on meaning and computation, and I have been reading the closing chapter of a recent popular book on machine intelligence, which gives what looks like a brisk statement of the symbol grounding problem. I have lent my copy out, so what follows is my reconstruction of the passage rather than a verbatim transcription. Before I put any of it on a slide, I want to know how much of it I can trust.

The reconstructed passage runs roughly like this:

The symbols inside a computer program have no meaning at all. A program is a formal system: it shuffles symbols according to rules that mention only the shapes of those symbols, and the only way a symbol can ever acquire meaning is by being causally hooked up to the thing it stands for — a thermometer's column of mercury means temperature because temperature makes it rise, and my word dog means dogs because dogs cause me to use the word. If a symbol's meaning is fixed instead by other symbols — a dictionary entry, a word explained by further words — then the whole system floats free of the world, and nothing inside it is about anything. That is the symbol grounding problem, and it is the same point the Chinese Room argument makes: syntax alone never delivers semantics. It follows that a system trained only on text cannot mean anything by its words, however much text you feed it. The remedy is to give the system sensors and effectors, so that its elementary symbols are caused by the things they represent; its remaining symbols then inherit their meaning by being defined in terms of those elementary ones, and the problem is solved. And that is why, in arguments about whether an AI system could be conscious, grounding rather than scale is the thing that should worry us.

My own suspicion is that the causal part is the shaky bit, especially the thermometer example, and I have a vague sense that the final sentence changes the subject. But I do not trust either instinct, and I cannot tell which claims a philosopher who presses this argument would actually defend.
