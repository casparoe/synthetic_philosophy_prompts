I'm prepping a graduate seminar on the extended mind for next term and I want to use my colleague Nadia as a running foil, because she has extremely firm verdicts on individual cases but gets visibly annoyed when anyone asks her to state her criterion. I've wrote out her rulings from a workshop we ran last spring as best I remember them: Otto's notebook — yes, the belief is genuinely partly in the notebook; Inga's ordinary biological memory — yes, boringly; someone playing Tetris by rotating the pieces with a console button rather than in their head — yes, "the system rotates the zoid, not her thumbs"; a man with dense amnesia who uses a notebook but only opens it when his carer hands it over and says check the book — no; a blind colleague's long cane — yes, part of her perceptual apparatus; a whiteboard in a shared kitchen where four flatmates all write and read the shopping list — no, "that's the flat's memory, not anybody's"; a diary she wrote herself, carried everywhere and trusted without checking — yes; an expert abacus user who no longer attends to the bead positions — yes; a phone photo archive she scrolls through when she wants to recall a holiday — no, the pictures have to be interpreted; a memory prosthesis implanted in the skull that does all its computation on the chip — yes, "though only because it's in your head, which is the uninteresting direction"; and, oddly, a man with amnesia who simply asks his wife whenever he needs a fact and never checks her answers — yes, on the grounds that she is reliably there and he doesn't scrutinise her.

The case I want to put to her next is Ruth. Ruth has dense amnesia after a stroke and uses a cueing prosthesis: a phone app plus a small unit in her bag, which — without being asked — vibrates and displays a short sentence ("the blue mug is in the cupboard left of the sink", "that's your neighbour Dan") at moments chosen by a model trained on other amnesic patients' data plus her own first six weeks of use. She never wrote, reviewed or endorsed any of the entries, has no idea how the model picks its moments, and never checks the object-location prompts, though she does sometimes double-check people's names with her daughter. When she has no signal the app falls back to a crude on-device model that is right about a quarter of the time, and she has stopped consulting it altogether in the cellar of her building, where there's no reception. For object locations she acts on the prompt at once and would say, if asked, "I remembered where the mug was."

So: what rule is Nadia actually running, and what other rules fit her track record just as well? I can see at least three live candidates — a parity or functional-role rule (the gadget does what memory does: reliably there, easy to consult, trusted, not scrutinised), a constitution-versus-mere-causal-coupling rule (her implant verdict suggests she cares that the processing is inside the head, or that the states involved have the right sort of content, and the model here sits partly on someone else's server), and something like an ownership or single-subject rule (the whiteboard verdict suggests the resource has to be *hers* in some stronger sense, and this one is shaped by other patients' data). Tell me what each predicts for Ruth, give me your best guess at her verdict with the reasons for preferring it, say whether you think she should also be read as attributing a *belief* about the mug to Ruth or only a memory-ish state, and name the one further case I should put to her to separate whichever rules survive. Note that when really pressed she says there may be no fact of the matter and it's just a decision about how to talk — so say plainly if you think the right move is to refuse the question rather than answer it. Also flag how confident you are in each thing you claim and single out the claim you're least sure of. A few hundred words of prose rather than bullet points, ideally something I could paste into my notes, and in British English please.
