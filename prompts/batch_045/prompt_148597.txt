I'm giving a twenty-five-minute internal talk next Thursday to our lab's model safety review — roughly ten minutes of it Q&A — and I want a prep sheet for the questions rather than a defense of the view. Assume a smart, time-pressured, mildly hostile audience that does not want to hear the word "deception" redefined.

The proposal I'm defending is a definition of deception for AI systems, meant to replace the working definition in our evaluation suite. Here it is compactly. Deception is fixed by what I call the Counterfactual Reliance Condition: an output is deceptive just in case (1) it is selected so as to make some proposition more credible to the intended user than the system's own epistemic state warrants, (2) the surface form of the output and the interface it appears in invite the user to rely on it as answerable to the truth, and (3) under a counterfactual intervention on the training objective — same query, same context — the system would be measurably less likely to produce that output if the objective penalized the user's ending up with a false belief. Literal falsity, on this account, is neither necessary (a hedged, literally true, sycophantic reply to someone with a bad plan can deceive) nor sufficient (an explicitly framed fictional role-play does not). The part I expect to be most contested: I want to classify confident confabulation as a failure of epistemic honesty rather than as a case of deception, on the grounds that there is no represented truth for the output to be answerable to — the system is indifferent to the truth, not set against it. I'll also introduce a distinction between epistemic honesty (outputs track the system's own uncertainty) and reliance honesty (the system does not engineer the user's epistemic position), argue that the two come apart in both directions, and conclude that any single scalar honesty score is the wrong target for the eval suite.

The room: three alignment engineers who built the current suite and are attached to it; one interpretability researcher who will say that the system's own epistemic state is either not well defined or not recoverable from what we can actually read off the activations; a philosopher of language we hired last year, who will press on assertion, implicature, and whether deception without assertion is coherent at all; our compliance counsel, who thinks in consumer-protection terms and wants to know what would survive a regulator reading our evals; and a product manager whose objection is false positives — every clause I add to the definition is a clause that can flag a shipped feature.

What I want: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, in the order they will come, each phrased the way that person would phrase it, but ranked by how much damage it does to the proposal rather than by how philosophically interesting it is. For each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which objection I should concede in the talk itself rather than in Q&A, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Flag anywhere my epistemic/reliance distinction is doing less work than I think it is.

Finally, tell me what would change your own assessment. If you are wrong about which objection is most damaging, or about which clause of the definition is load-bearing, what is the consideration or argument that would show it — and where is your reasoning most likely to break down?
