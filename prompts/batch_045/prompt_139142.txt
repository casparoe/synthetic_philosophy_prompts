My old supervisor died in March and I have to give the memorial talk at the department gathering — about six minutes, spoken, half the room her colleagues and ex-students, half her sisters and kids and neighbours who never read a word of what she wrote.

Her thing, which she went on about for thirty years, was that the standard picture puts uptake in the wrong place: not a felicity condition hanging outside the act but half of it. On her view a promise, a bet, an apology, even a thank-you, is co-authored — the person it's addressed to has to answer, and their answer is partly what makes it the act it is. She had a name for the ones that don't get an answer: utterances like a promise made to someone who can't hear it anymore. She called them orphan performatives — perfectly formed, apparently doing something, and in fact unfinished.

So I'm stuck, and not just rhetorically. Her old students will hear every "we'll finish your book" and "the seminar will go on" as me standing up in her memory and producing precisely the half-an-act she spent her career saying wasn't one. Her sisters, reasonably, need what I say to actually do something, not just describe somebody impressive. I think what's left is that the living can still answer each other, so whatever gets done has to get done sideways, among us, and the part aimed at her has to stay openly unfinished rather than dressed up as complete — but I'm not sure that's right, or that it's enough for the people in the room who aren't philosophers. Write it, and tell me if you think I've got the shape wrong. Don't have me promise her anything, and don't turn it into a tour of speech act theory.
