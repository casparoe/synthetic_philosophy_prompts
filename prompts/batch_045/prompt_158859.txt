ok so "sunk costs are sunk" (or "don't throw good money after bad") has become the conversation-ender in my friend group and I want to know what its actually claiming.

Where I keep meeting it: Im three years into a PhD im not sure about, and every time I try to talk it through I get cut off with some version of the line — the years are gone either way, the only question is whats ahead, staying because of what Ive already put in is "the sunk cost fallacy" and ipso facto irrational. It's doing a lot of work in that argument: its being used to establish that no consideration having to do with my past investment can count at all, and therefore that the only remaining question is a forward-looking cost-benefit one.

So can you lay out the distinct precise theses that one-liner could be expressing, as a menu rather than picking one and explaining it? I want the trivial ones separated from the substantive ones from the false ones, and I want to know which reading would actually get my friends their conclusion and which readings are compatible with me staying. Some axes I suspect matter but cant organize: is the claim about reasons (past expenditure gives you no reason), about outcomes (acting on it makes you worse off), about the psychology (the pull is explained by something debunking like loss aversion or waste aversion, so it isnt a real reason), or about which agent at which time is supposed to be doing the deliberating (the person who paid vs the person deciding now)? And is there any reading on which its straightforwardly false rather than just narrow?

Three things I keep coming back to that I cant place on the menu, please tell me for each whether its a genuine separate reading, a version of one of the others, or just a confusion:

1. Metabolized costs. Some of what I "spent" isn't gone, it seems to have turned into me — skills, a committee, a topic I know cold, options I didnt have before. Is this just the boring point that the cost was never fully sunk, or is there something more interesting going on (something about the cost having created the thing I'd be valuing, so that the investment and the object aren't separable)?

2. Past investment as evidence. That I put three years in, that my department put funding in, that other people bet on me — isn't that evidence about how good the thing is going to be, in a way that has nothing to do with wanting the past back? Is that a legitimate reasoning pattern that merely looks like the fallacy, or does the fallacy charge cover it?

3. Honoring sunk costs as a policy. Being the kind of person who finishes what they start seems to pay: people trust you with things, you trust yourself, you can make commitments credibly later. So a standing rule of finishing could be instrumentally rational even in a case where finishing this one thing loses. Is that a fourth reading, or does it collapse into the outcomes reading, or is it a different kind of rationality claim altogether (something about rules and self-governance rather than about this decision)?
