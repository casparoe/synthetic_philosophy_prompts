I've been going back and forth on whether I could have originated from gametes other than the ones I actually came from, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts on particular cases add up to. I mean the question de re: not "could there have been someone very much like me," but "could I have been that person."

Here is where my gut lands. A child conceived from my mother's egg and a stranger's sperm, then raised exactly as I was raised, in my room, under my name, is not me. A child conceived from the very same egg and sperm but fertilized by injection in a dish rather than in a fallopian tube is me without question. A child from that same egg and a sperm whose genome differs from the actual one at a single base position is me. A child from the same parents whose genome is a different recombination of their chromosomes — my actual brother's genome — is my brother, not me. A child from the same egg and sperm whose mitochondrial DNA comes from a donor egg, with the nuclear genome unchanged, is me. And if the zygote that became me had split and both halves had been carried to term, the other twin is not me — though I cannot say which of the two I would have been, or why.

Two things about this pattern bother me. First, I am tempted by the thought that what is essential is the genome rather than the gametes; that handles the mechanics and mitochondria cases and explains why my brother is not me. But it seems to say that my hypothetical identical twin and I are the same person, or at least that I could have been him, which I reject — and it is silent about which half of a split zygote I would be. Second, crossover points during meiosis sit at positions that vary continuously along the chromosome, so there is a long chain of genomes, each differing from the next by a single base pair of crossover placement, running from my actual genome to a genome that is a different recombination of my parents' chromosomes. If a one-base shift preserves me, and identity is transitive along such a chain, I ought to accept that I could have had my brother's genome — which I do not accept.

Please extract from these verdicts a principle stated precisely enough to be applied, tell me plainly which of my verdicts a defensible account has to overturn and which of the two worries above is the real problem, and then say what the account predicts for a case I have not listed: a child born from an embryo that was split in a laboratory and of which only one half was transferred, and separately, someone created a hundred years from now from a preserved copy of my nuclear genome.
