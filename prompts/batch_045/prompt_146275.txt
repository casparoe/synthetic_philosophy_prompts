A popular book on metaphysics I read recently has a short section on dispositions — fragility, solubility, and the like. It is the only thing I've read on the topic, so I have no way of telling how much of it to trust. Here is roughly what it says (this is my own paraphrase from memory, not the book's exact wording):

"A disposition is just a matter of how the thing would behave: the glass is fragile if and only if it would break if it were struck, the sugar is soluble if and only if it would dissolve if it were dropped in water. That simple conditional analysis runs into two kinds of counterexample. A fink is a mechanism that switches the disposition off when the stimulus arrives — the standard case is a live wire attached to a device that cuts the current the instant a conductor touches it, so that the wire is live and yet would not conduct if touched, precisely because being touched destroys the very property that would have done the work. A mask is a condition that leaves the disposition in place but hides its manifestation from us: a fragile glass so thoroughly padded that the blow is absorbed before it can do any damage, so that the glass is struck, remains fragile, and does not break. Finks and masks are really the same problem in different costume — in both, the object has the disposition, the stimulus occurs, and nothing happens. And that is the end of dispositions as real properties. Of the two, the fink is the fatal one; a mask can be brushed aside by adding a suitable ceteris paribus clause to the conditional. Either way, a fragile glass is just one we expect to break, and once finks and masks are on the table we cannot even say that much. Dispositional language is a convenient way of packaging our behavioural expectations, not the name of anything in the object."

Two things make me suspicious, though I can't say where the errors are. The padded glass doesn't feel like the same kind of case as the wired-up wire, and the book's insistence that they are is the part I trust least. And the conclusion seems too strong: the failure of the conditional analysis is being treated as a refutation of dispositions themselves, and I have a vague sense that some philosophers think these cases push in the opposite direction — toward taking dispositions more seriously, not less — though I couldn't explain why.
