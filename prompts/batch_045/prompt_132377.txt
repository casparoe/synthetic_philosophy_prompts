Here is the thing. I am on a small committee rewriting the unannounced inspection policy where I work (we inspect workplaces for safety compliance, a few hundred sites a year), and I keep having the feeling that our policy is doing something impossible — and also that it obviously is not, because we did it about three hundred times last year.

Concretely, this is what we do:

- At the start of the year every site gets a letter saying it will recieve exactly one inspection during the year, and that the inspection will be unannounced.
- The window is the calender year, except that we cut out December, and specifically the last two weeks, so that the visit is genuinely a surprise. Somebody put that in the policy years ago and nobody has questioned it.
- The day is not random in any formal sense. The regional scheduler picks a day that suits the inspector and tries to spread visits out geographically. There is no randomiser anywhere in the process.
- If word gets out in advance — a manager hears from another site that the inspector is in the area — the visit is pushed and rescheduled, on the grounds that it would not be a real inspection any more: they would have had time to tidy up.
- After the fact, we grade the visit partly on how the site responded in the moment: whether records were produced on the spot, whether the place looked like a normal working day. And the official point of the whole regime is deterrence — you do not know when we are coming, so you comply all year.

The philosophers version of this says the announcement is somehow in trouble: there cannot be a surprise on the last day of the window, so not on the second-to-last, and so on down. Our policy looks like living proof that either that argument is wrong or that we are all confused about what we are doing.

My hunch is that the practice only needs something like you cannot be certain enough to change your behaviour, and that the trouble starts the moment you read surprise as an all-or-nothing, knowledge-requiring property. But I cannot shake two other things. One: we deliberately cut the last fortnight out of the window, which looks like conceding the backwards induction while conceding only one step of it. Two: our letter literally says there will be one visit; if the philosophers are right that no such announcement can be true, or known, or rationally believed, then we have been posting a defective sentence to three hundred people a year and it seems to work beautifully.

So: what does this practice presuppose? Specifically —

- What view of surprise, or of knowledge, certainty, rational belief or predictability, does the policy only make sense under, and what does it quietly rule out? Does it need the inspectees uncertainty to be about which day rather than whether? Does it need surprise to be a matter of degree — how many hours of forewarning — rather than a yes/no, given that plenty of inspection regimes define no notice as a minimum notice period measured in hours?
- Does it need the event to be objectively chancy? Ours is not chancy at all and nobody involved thinks that is a problem. What does that tell you?
- Is the practice internally consistent? We exclude the end of the window, we use will rather than may, and we abort the visit when word gets out. Do those three bits presuppose the same thing or different things?
- What does the practice strictly need, as opposed to what it merely fits well with? I suspect several different theories would license it, and I want to know which parts of the policy are load-bearing and which are decoration.
- And what would we have to change if we took the literature seriously — if we decided surprise requires genuine chance, or that the announcement is self-referential and so defective, or that all that matters is the inspectees ability to prepare? Would we have to switch to you may be inspected, or publish a random draw, or shorten the window, or stop grading the response in the moment?

Please do not just summarise the debate about the paradox for me. Point the argument at the actual practice and tell me whether the practice is incoherent, or whether the paradox is a problem about a sentence rather than about anything anybody does.
