I'm the senior registrar at a regional museum — me and two others — and questions about whether something is still the same object land on my desk maybe twice a week, always sideways: is this the thing we insured, is this the thing the lender gets back, does the label reading 'original' have to come down, can we open a second accession number for the material we took out of it, does the object we promised to return still exist. The stakes are unglamorous but real — insurance valuations, loan contracts, our accreditation, and at the moment two return claims from source communities — and I get about half an hour a case, there's no philosopher on staff, and whatever I write has to survive both the collections committee and an auditor who will definately not accept "the conservator pointed at it" as a criterion.

I've been reading round the statue and the clay business in my own time — the move where you say the statue is constituted by the clay but isn't identical with it, so you've got two things in one place with the same mass and the same weight, one of which survives being squashed and the other doesn't, plus that business about which sortal governs which object you've got — and I half get it, but I can't hand it to my team, and our catalogue lets a single object and its material carry separate accession numbers, which means the distinction is already half-built into our database without anyone having decided what it means. A mate who does loss adjusting laughed when I described the problem and said it's exactly the "substantial transformation" question the customs people argue about, which made me think we're probably reinventing something badly.

So please turn this into a decision procedure my team can actually run in half an hour on a real case: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a named person because it's a question of what we owe rather than what's there. It has to cope with subtraction and accretion as well as replacement (we swapped a lens between two accessioned microscopes in the 1990s and nobody wrote it down), with cases where the matter is tracked separately from the object, and with cases where the only accessioned thing is a set of instructions or a digital file rather than a lump of anything. Mark clearly the points where judgement can't be squeezed out, and gloss any term of art in one line, because I'm not going to get my team to read metaphysics at the weekend. Then run it on three cases: (1) a bronze figure where roughly sixty per cent of the metal is modern infill and the original corroded fragments sit in a tray with their own number — one object or two, which one travels on loan, and what do we tell the insurer; (2) a work by a living artist consisting of a block of wax the artist instructs us to melt and recast annually from the same wax — the artist says the 2026 casting is the accessioned object, our insurer says it isn't; (3) a carved house post a source community has always treated as persisting through the replacement of every plank, which our records describe as a 1972 reconstruction with original elements and which the community wants returned as the thing taken in 1904. I'd like (3) to come out as the same object, so if your procedure won't give me that, say so plainly and tell me who has to decide instead. Finish by being honest about what the procedure cannot do — in particular whether it can tell me whether there's a fact of the matter here at all, or only what we should write on the form.
