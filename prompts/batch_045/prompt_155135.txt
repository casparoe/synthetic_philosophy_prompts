I teach a 13-person third-year seminar on distributive justice, two-hour session. We've done Rawls and a couple of weeks of expected utility theory, so they're fine reading a vNM utility table and comparing lotteries. I want a classroom game that makes them feel the bite of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem without naming it, or utilitarianism, up front: I want them to run into the fact that once you require (a) the social ranking to respect unanimity and (b) the social ranking itself to behave like an individual's ranking under uncertainty (transitive, and satisfying independence), you get pushed into ranking prospects by a weighted sum of individual utilities — and that most of the alternatives they find intuitive up front (prioritarian weighting, anything leximin-ish, plain "help the worst off") quietly stop being available.

What I need: persona cards with stated utility tables, a deck of lottery cards over who ends up in which outcome, a rule that forces groups of 3-4 to produce one group ranking, small stakes (chips counting toward participation, no money), and a timeline for about 90 minutes. The part I most want help with: I'd like a second round in which I secretly change one thing — same numbers, but I announce that each persona's scale is in it's own arbitrary units, so levels can't be compared across people — and they discover that the ranking they were proud of in round one now depends on a labeling choice nobody made on purpose. I don't know how to stage that so it lands rather than just confusing them.

Then a debrief mapping moments in the game onto the premises of the result, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect one group to just vote; I expect someone to flatly refuse the independence requirement; I expect someone to try to average money instead of utility), and an honest note on what the game cannot stage — I assume it can't give them the proof, and it can't give them the equal-weights step that gets you from "some weighted sum" to averaging. Please also be clear about which claims you're building the session on are settled math and which are contested interpretation; I really don't want to teach a contested reading as if it were textbook.
