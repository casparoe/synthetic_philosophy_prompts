yo so im in a philosophy elective and we just did this thing my teacher called the paradox of fiction — how can we genuinely get sad or scared about people we know never existed. She gave us like three families of answer: one where were not really feeling it, were just playing along / making believe; one where the feelings are totally real but emotions only need thoughts or imaginings to be about something, not beliefs, so theres no conflict; and one where we kind of half-trick ourselves into forgetting its fiction while were in it. I understand the setup but the abstract version bores me, and I keep thinking about something way more specific that feels like it should be evidence: spoilers.

Everyone treats spoilers as seriously harmful. People have ended friendships over them, subreddits have whole mod systems for them, streaming sites blur thumbnails, and someone who spoils a movie out loud gets treated like they broke something of yours. But the more I think about it the weirder it looks:

- The same people who scream about spoilers rewatch their favorite shows constantly and say it hits *harder* the second time, and then go watch an hour-long explainer video about it. So knowing what happens isnt always the problem.
- Knowing the ending up front sometimes makes it worse, not better — everyone knows how Romeo and Juliet or Titanic end and those scenes land harder *because* you know. So its not that uncertainty is required for the feeling.
- It isnt only about fiction: people who taped a game dont want the score spoiled either, and that really happened. But nobody spoiler-warns the news, and true crime fans happily consume stories whose ending they already know.
- People get mad about spoilers for stuff they had no plan to watch, like the spoil ruins a future experience they havent even decided to have.

So: what does the spoiler norm actually presuppose? Not just about politeness, but about what our feelings toward fiction are and about whats supposed to be valuable about engaging with a story. Reconstruct the implicit theory — what view of fictional emotion, and of what fiction is *for*, does the practice only make sense under? Does it strictly require one of the three answers my teacher gave, or would several of them license it, meaning the practice is underdetermined? And where does it seem to presuppose inconsistent things, given the rewatch and dramatic-irony cases?

Be concrete: what is the thing a spoiler is supposed to deprive me of — a feeling, a kind of experience, a piece of knowledge — and is it strange to think you can be deprived of a genuine emotion about something that never happened? Does the norm give either side a real argument in the paradox-of-fiction debate, or is it neutral? And if we took the "were only playing along" answer seriously, would spoiler outrage be irrational, or could that view explain the norm some other way? Finally, what would actually have to change in how people treat spoilers if we swapped in a different theory of what fictional feelings are?
