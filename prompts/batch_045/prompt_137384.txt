I am trying to work out how much of Bayesian epistemology is genuinely unsettled by Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals, and I have talked myself into a conclusion I am fairly sure is wrong. I would be grateful if you could locate the mistake.

Let P be an agent's credence function over an algebra of propositions, and suppose that for any A and C in that algebra the indicative conditional A → C is itself a member of it. (I use the arrow neutrally, for whatever proposition a conditional is supposed to express — not for the material conditional.) My reasoning runs as follows.

(1) The triviality results show that the Equation P(A → C) = P(C|A) cannot be sustained: if it holds throughout a suitably rich algebra, P is forced into triviality — on the usual presentation, every proposition turns out probabilistically independent of every other — which no genuine credence function can be. And the argument is essentially algebraic; it appears to assume nothing substantive about the semantics of *if*, only that the conditional is the sort of thing that has a probability.

(2) But the Equation just is the Ramsey–Adams thesis: that one's confidence in *if A, C* ought to be one's confidence in C under the supposition that A. That thesis, rather than any particular semantics, is what makes conditional probability the standard by which ordinary conditionals are assessed.

(3) So the results do not merely refute one candidate semantics for *if*. They refute the suppositional account of how conditionals are to be evaluated, and with it the Ramsey test as a constraint on theorizing about conditionals.

(4) And the damage then spreads. To conditionalize on A is precisely to adopt P(C|A) as one's new credence in C; that is, Bayesian update is the Equation applied pointwise across the algebra. If the Equation is untenable, then a central pillar of Bayesian epistemology rests on an identification that Lewis refuted — which cannot be right, given how readily Bayesians invoke conditionalization.

My own diagnosis is that the only honest way out is to conclude that indicative conditionals have no truth conditions at all, so that the results effectively settle the truth-conditional debate in favour of the non-propositionalists, and the probabilistic framework has to be rebuilt around conditional credence rather than credence in conditionals.

Where does this go wrong? In particular: should the results be read as ruling out the Equation for each individual pair A, C, or only as ruling out its holding universally over a rich algebra? Does the proof really carry no semantic commitments, given that it treats A → C as a proposition in the same Boolean algebra and P as a classical probability function over it? And is there any sense in which conditionalization — a rule for revising credences by ratios of unconditional credences — is implicated by an argument about the probability of a conditional?

Relatedly, how should I understand the two maneuvers that suggest themselves as escapes: treating conditional probability as primitive (a two-place function rather than a ratio), and assigning conditionals non-classical semantic values, along the lines of conditional events or of worlds paired with a selected antecedent-world? Do these evade the results, or merely relocate their cost?
