I'm a senior doing an independent history research project on the surviving records of a mill town near where I live — the poor-relief ledger, the overseers' accounts, the parish burial register, the hiring book from one of the mills — and I keep running into the same problem in a form I can't resolve. My mentor gave me a rule she calls the Ledger Principle: a gap in a ledger is a fact about whoever kept the ledger, about their interests, their categories, and what they were required or inclined to write down, and never a fact about the world the ledger describes. I have been using it in my paper to argue that we cannot conclude much about the women who worked in the mills, since the records enter them only as the widow or daughter of some male householder, or by first name alone, and never as workers in their own right. But when I wrote the argument out in steps, it seemed to destroy itself:

(1) A silence in an archive is evidence only about the record-keeper's interests, categories, and capacity — never about the people or events the archive fails to mention.
(2) The town's surviving records were produced by overseers, rate collectors, and mill agents whose business was property, poor relief, and wage labor, and who had no institutional reason to record anything else.
(3) So nothing about the people they had no reason to record — their numbers, their literacy, whether they organized, what their lives were like — can be inferred from those records.
(4) So a historian who reads off the archive that those people were few, illiterate, or politically inert has made an invalid inference: she has taken the shape of the record for the shape of the world.
(5) But my own conclusion is that these people were systematically unrecorded, and that the silences are products of who held the pen and whom the pen was for. That conclusion is also a claim about the world, drawn from the same archive: it says something about the record-keepers and about the people who are missing.
(6) So if (1) holds without restriction, I cannot assert the thing I want to assert — nor can anyone else. On the strict reading we would have to say that we know nothing whatever about the people in the gaps, not even that they were gapped, which is absurd.
(7) Therefore (1) must be given up or restricted. But every restriction I try either lets back in exactly the inferences I used (1) to block in step (4), or turns it into an empty slogan.

My friend says this is just the streetlight effect — everyone looks where the light is — and that I am overthinking it. I think that misses something, because the light here was pointed deliberately by people with an interest in where it went, and it is that interest, not merely the light, that I want to make claims about. I also can't see where to put a case like the burial register: the parish was legally required to list everyone in it, so a name missing from it really does seem to tell me something about the world, and my mentor insists that this is compatible with (1) — I can't see how. If I have to choose, I would rather keep something close to (1) than accept that the archive can be read straight, but I want to know what keeping it costs, and whether one of my other steps is the weak one rather than (1) itself.

Please answer as a philosopher of history and epistemologist who works on evidence, testimony, and the politics of records. Work through the steps one at a time and think carefully before committing yourself — I would rather have a slow, careful diagnosis than a quick one. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence: which step fails, or, if none fails, which premise you would give up and why. Then give the reasoning, say why the step or premise looked fine, and price the alternatives — in particular what each way out commits me to about the burial-register case and about the claim in (4). Roughly 700 to 900 words, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
