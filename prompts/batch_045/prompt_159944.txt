I have a fifty-minute seminar next week on the frame problem, for first-years in a combined philosophy-and-computing degree. They have had exactly one prior lecture on classical planning — actions specified as preconditions and effects over a state description — and no formal logic beyond truth tables. Assume I know the material and do not explain the frame problem to me; what I need is a plan for getting it across. Four things in particular.

The order. Give me the sequence of moves and, for each, what has to be in place before it can land. I keep losing the thread at the transition between the two things that get called by this name: the technical one, where representing an action's effects in logic seems to oblige you to write out, for every action and every condition it leaves alone, that the condition does not change; and the broader one, about how a system with very many beliefs works out which of them bear on what it is doing now. My instinct is to spend the first twenty minutes on the technical problem and then ask why solving it leaves the second untouched, but I am not sure the second problem is even visible to them unless they have already felt a proposed fix fail. Tell me what must come first, and what, if anything, must be deliberately withheld until later.

The carrying example. One scenario should do the whole session's work, and I want it to meet a constraint I have not seen imposed anywhere: the same scenario must survive a change of goal. It should contain at least one consequence of the action that is far-flung but relevant, one that is close at hand and utterly irrelevant, and — this is the part I care about — one whose relevance flips when I change what the agent has been told to want, so that I can run the identical vignette three times with three different goals and let relevance itself be the variable. Propose such a scenario, and say what it must have and, as importantly, what it must lack. I had meant to use the familiar story of the robot that retrieves a battery from a room, where the battery sits on a wagon alongside a bomb, and I would be glad to be told why that is the wrong vehicle here, or what would have to be stripped out of it.

The misconceptions. Here are the ones I expect: that this is a problem of computational cost, which more speed or memory would dissolve; that it was solved by the late 1980s and so there is nothing left to discuss; that it is the same as the qualification problem, or as the ramification problem; that relevance is a property a belief has on its own rather than something it has relative to a task and an agent; and that the takeaway is a vague holism, the thought that everything bears on everything. For each, tell me the exact point in the sequence at which to forestall it — I suspect some must be blocked before they can form while at least one is better provoked and then dismantled. Then give me the one or two misconceptions I have got wrong or missed, since my list comes from last year's cohort and may be idiosyncratic.

Two further questions, folded in. First, whether to give five minutes to the temporal-projection puzzle in which a gun is loaded, an interval passes, and the trigger is then pulled — the Yale shooting case, which showed that the minimal-change fix is not always right — or whether that detour pulls attention back into the formalism and away from the problem I want them to feel; I suspect it is a trap for me rather than for them. Second, some philosophers have recruited the frame problem into an argument that central cognition cannot be computational at all, on the grounds that whether a belief bears on a conclusion is sensitive to anything and everything. Is that a legitimate extension worth a closing minute, or a different argument wearing the same name, and if I do raise it, what has to be true of the preceding forty-five minutes for it to be more than a slogan?

Finally, a check and an omission. I want one question to put to them near the end whose answers would show they have the distinction between the two problems and not merely the vocabulary. I was going to hand out three short vignettes and ask which instantiates the frame problem, which instantiates one of its siblings, and which is only an expensive calculation, with at least one deliberately borderline case; tell me whether that will discriminate, and if not, what will. And tell me what to refuse to explain at this level, and how to make the refusal feel principled rather than evasive.

Please do not suggest readings, sources, or citations: the course bibliography is fixed and I do not want to smuggle in new ones.
