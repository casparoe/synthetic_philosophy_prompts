My flatmate is three years into a DPhil on the moral equality of combatants — she's basically a McMahan person and gets properly annoyed if anyone says she's just parroting him — and she thinks Walzer's equality thesis is dead but won't say what replaces it. We have this kitchen-table game, usually after the pub, where I make up cases and she gives verdicts, always as two separate answers: may this person be killed (assume the necessity and discrimination conditions are met so we don't argue about those), and is it murder when he does the killing. Her verdicts so far, from my notes: (1) volunteer on the unjust side who knows the war is a war of conquest — may be killed, and yes it's murder; (2) conscript on the unjust side, drafted at gunpoint, no independent news, sincerely convinced he's repelling an invasion — may not be killed if he can be disabled or captured instead, and on the murder question she went slippery and would only say he's not to blame for any of it, she wouldn't say whether the killings themselves were wrong; (3) volunteer on the unjust side who suspected the war was aggressive and deliberately looked away — may be killed, it's murder, but "less murder" than case 1, her words; (4) volunteer on the just side who fights purely for the signing bonus and would fight for whoever pays — may not be killed, not murder; (5) civilian software engineer who voluntarily writes the targeting code for the unjust side's missiles, knowing exactly what it's for — may be killed; (6) farmer selling potatoes to the unjust army at market rate, knowing they feed the front — may not be killed.

Tonight's case, which I've been saving for weeks: a volunteer in the army of the country that is in fact fighting a just defensive war. The enemy runs a really good disinformation campaign and his own side's information hygiene is so bad that the forgeries are the most credible evidence available to him, so he's as confident as any reasonable person could be that his side started the war — and here's the bit I like, he's half right, his government did sign the gas deals, the war is just the same because the other lot invaded first. He doesn't care about the cause either way; he joined to keep an eye on his younger brother, who's still in the platoon, he keeps fighting to keep him alive, and he kills two advancing enemy soldiers in a firefight. Same two questions: may he be killed, and is it murder.

I don't so much want the right answer — I've got my own half-baked view and I lost the arguement about case 3 anyway — as the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit all six of those verdicts just as well, what each of them predicts for tonight, your best guess with the reason for preferring it, and the one further case I should spring on her tomorrow to tell the rules apart. Oh, and write it in British English, she's one of those people who complains about "defense" with an s.
