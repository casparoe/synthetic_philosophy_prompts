I am taking an upper-level seminar on rational choice, and we recently spent a session on the Death in Damascus case; I have been arguing about it with some friends in my study group, and I realize I do not know whether the summary we were given is reliable. The handout we were given is very sympathetic to evidential decision theory, and my friend, who is something of a partisan for EDT, repeated its line and summarized the case and its upshot for me roughly like this:

In Death in Damascus, you meet Death, who tells you he will come for you tomorrow. You can either stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo. Death is a perfect predictor and has already written down where he will find you, so if you stay, he will be in Damascus and you will die there; if you flee, he will be in Aleppo and you will die there. Since you will die either way, the only difference is the inconvenience and expense of travelling, so it is obviously rational to stay in Damascus and save the trip. Evidential decision theory gets this right: it says you should stay, because fleeing is strong evidence that Death is in Aleppo, which is just as fatal as staying, plus travel costs. Causal decision theory, by contrast, tells you to flee, because it notices that your choice cannot causally affect where Death already is, so if there is even a small chance that Death is in Damascus, fleeing raises your chance of survival. The case is therefore a refutation of causal decision theory: the recommendation of causal decision theory is not only murderous but unstable, since if you believe you will stay it tells you to flee, and if you believe you will flee it tells you to stay, so a CDT agent can never make up her mind. The upshot is that only evidential decision theory survives the case: rational agents should treat their own acts as evidence and choose the act with the best news value, doing what they would least dislike learning they will do, even when the news is that they are about to die.

I am suspicious of the tone of the handout, and the claim that even a small chance of Death being in Damascus makes fleeing safer struck me as possibly backwards, but I do not know enough decision theory to say where the summary goes wrong.
