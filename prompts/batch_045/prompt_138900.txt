I teach a first-year seminar on persons, and next term I want to pair two texts that raise the same question from opposite directions: whether a mind can belong to a thing made of many smaller things. The pairing is Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary from the "Ant Fugue" dialogue in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* — an ant colony that converses, holds opinions, and is treated by her friend as someone — and Orson Scott Card's *Ender's Game*, where an insectoid species is organized around thinking queens whose workers act as the cells of a single body. I have taught each separately and never got the pairing to work, so I am asking for the reading I cannot produce: one that takes the colony case seriously as a metaphysical possibility and takes the novel seriously as a novel, without reducing either text to an illustration of the other.

The specific question I want answered is whether a Formic colony is an Aunt Hillary at all, or whether it is the exact inverse of one. My own suspicion, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that it is the inverse, and that the difference can be made out along three axes I have been using in lectures:

The first is what I call the architecture of the parts. Aunt Hillary's parts are themselves agents with small mental lives of their own, and her beliefs — that the Anteater is a friend, say — are had by no ant; a brain's parts are not agents at all; a Formic worker, as the novel gives it to us, is an agent that nonetheless has no mental life of its own, an organism that behaves purposively while belonging wholly to another's purposes. I want you to say which of these three architectures actually supports the claim that the whole has a mind, and whether the middle case is really the model the other two are being read against.

The second is the grain of a mind. Aunt Hillary's contributions are assembled out of thousands of slow, spatially spread movements, and her thought, if she has any, moves at a pace at which nothing human could converse with her. Does the timescale of a system's transitions matter to whether there is something it is like to be it, or is that merely an obstacle to our ever recognizing her as a subject? This matters more for the Formics: their queens are said to share thought across any distance, which would make the colony not slow but instantaneous, and I want to know whether that feature helps or hurts the case for a colony mind.

The third is death and division. If a colony is fused with another, or if its queen dies and the workers fall inert, has one subject ceased, or many, or none? I want this applied to the novel's decisive action — Ender's use of the Molecular Disruption Device against the Formic homeworld — and to the cocooned queen he carries away at the end: on the view the reading defends, what exactly did Ender do, and does the novel's moral horror depend on an answer it has not earned?

There is one further thread I cannot place, and it may be where the novel is most interesting philosophically. As I read it, the tragedy turns on the Formics' inability to grasp that each human being is a separate center of will, so that the war is fought over a failure of mind-attribution in the direction we rarely discuss: not under-attributing mind to an alien system, but being constitutionally unable to attribute it to an individual. If that is right, the novel would be dramatizing something the Aunt Hillary case only gestures at — that the difficulty is symmetrical, and that a genuinely collective mind might be as blind to us as we are to it. Tell me whether the text supports this, and whether the novel's resolution, in which understanding the enemy becomes a kind of redemption, is a philosophical answer or only a consoling one.

Where the novel resists the reading, say so plainly: I suspect it may cheat by giving the hive a single queen who speaks in a recognizably individual voice, since the plot needs the aliens to be morally legible. I would also be grateful if you flagged any point where your account depends on a detail of Card's text, or of the later books, that you cannot be confident of, since my students will have only the novel in front of them.
