I’m reading the opening chapter of Tolstoy’s What Is Art? (1897), and I’m stuck on this bit:

“A false and antiquated view of life is supported by means of art, and is inseparably linked to some manifestations of art which we enjoy and prize. If the false view of life be destroyed this art will cease to appear valuable. Is it best to screen the error for the sake of preserving the art? Or should the art be sacrificed for the sake of truthfulness?”

I get the surface dilemma, but I want the deeper pay-off for Tolstoy’s own theory. Heres the wrinkle I’m chewing on: suppose a work is extremly infectious in his sense — it really does make an audience feel exactly what the author felt — but the feeling is wrapped up in a false view of life (say, a nationalist or superstitious picture of reality). Once the audience is undeceived about that picture, the work doesnt just look morally suspect to Tolstoy; he seems to say it literally stops being valuable as art. So is he committed to the claim that successful infection alone cant make something art unless the transmitted feeling fits the true religious perception of the age, or is he only saying that the work loses a kind of honorific value while remaining art in the thin, infectious sense? Where in the passage, and in the machinery of infection, sincerity, and the religious perception of the age that he builds later, do we decide between those two readings? Does his own question about screening the error betray a worry that truthfulness and artistic value can actually come apart in practice, even if his official theory says they cant? I dont need citations or a summary of the whole book — just help me see how this early dilemma cashes out in his technical vocabulary, and maybe tell me which reading makes the rest of the book least contradictory.
