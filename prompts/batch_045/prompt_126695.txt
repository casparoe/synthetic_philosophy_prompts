Two people in my decision-theory reading group got into it after class last week in the grad lounge and I've been turning it over since, so let me just lay it out. The setup was the Allais pair: Problem 1 is a sure $1M against a gamble that pays $5M with 10%, $1M with 89%, and nothing with 1%; Problem 2 is an 11% shot at $1M against a 10% shot at $5M (nothing otherwise). Most people take the sure million first and the $5M gamble second, which expected utility can't accommodate, and both of them agreed on the reason why: work it through and each comparison reduces to the very same inequality, 0.11·u($1M) versus 0.10·u($5M) + 0.01·u($0), so no utility function whatever — concave, ruin-averse, any of it — can produce that pattern. The argument started after that. (1) Marta said the Allais preferences are therefore just an inconsistency, and that independence isn't a taste but a requirement of rationality, which she tried to show by redescribing the choice as sequential: spin a wheel first that decides whether you land in the 89% "common" branch or the 11% tail, announce the result, and then notice that in both problems the only live choice left is the same one — a sure $1M against a 10/11 shot at $5M — since the common branch pays $1M under both options in Problem 1 and nothing under both options in Problem 2. So your preference in the tail had better not depend on which problem you're in, and that's consequentialism, and consequentialism is independence. (2) Rob said that redescription isn't neutral: she's added a stage and an announcement, and the claim that the branch you didn't land in can't matter is exactly what's in dispute. Someone can perfectly coherently care about having had a real shot at $5M, or about not having a wheel decide something they could have settled outright; refusing consequentialism isn't a contradiction, it's a different view of what a decision problem even is. (3) Marta granted that but said independence is really just separability across states — EU needs the contribution of each state not to depend on what the other states pay — and that once you drop it you get preferences that shift with how the uncertainty is described and resolved, which leaves you open to a money pump as the tree unfolds. (4) Rob said the pump only works on someone who re-optimizes at every node and forgets their own plan; a resolute chooser who picks a plan up front and doesn't revisit it is immune, and resoluteness is an available normative ideal too, just not the same one. He added that the pattern isn't only about certainty anyway: shrink both probabilities by a common factor — a 25% shot at $3,000 versus a 20% shot at $4,000, say — and people flip the same way with nothing certain anywhere. (5) Marta replied that you can't just stipulate resoluteness, because the plan has to be fixed at a point where you don't know which branch you're in, and consequentialism is the discipline that makes plans stable across branches; and she said the effect is supposed to mostly evaporate when you swap the sure million for a 99% chance of a million, which she took as the signature of a framing artifact rather than a real anti-independence preference. (6) Rob said shrinking isn't vanishing, that the 99% version also changes the worst case and the spread so it isn't a clean test of certainty, and that what her side really owes is an argument that outcomes in branches that don't obtain are normatively inert — which is the actual commitment, and it's the one that disappointment and regret theories deny. (7) Marta came back sharp here: state-dependent utility doesn't help him, because it's still additive across states and still cancels on the common branch; to get Allais you need genuine non-separability, meaning your ranking of what happens in the tail depends on what would have happened in a branch you're not in. She conceded this isn't incoherent — you can keep transitivity and respect for stochastic dominance and still fan out your indifference curves — but said the cost is exactly the dynamic instability from (3). (8) Rob said fine, he'll pay that, and proposed a tiebreaker experiment: run the pair under a no-feedback condition where the forgone option's outcome is never revealed, and again with the money going to a stranger, and see whether the pattern survives.

Then say who's more right overall, whether (8) would actually settle anything if they ran it, and what both of them are missing.
