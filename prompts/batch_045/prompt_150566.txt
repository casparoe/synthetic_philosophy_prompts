so I just had a two-hour argument with my friend about digital minds and it ended with her saying my position "can't all be true at once", me saying "which bit?", and her saying "I dunno, all of it", which was not helpful. So I'm writing down where I actually stand so someone can check it properly. Here it is:

(a) Substrate independence. What makes something a mind is its causal-functional organisation, not what it's made of. Get the organisation right in silicon and you get the mental states, same as in carbon.

(b) Moral status is graded, and what it tracks is conscious experience — valenced experience, at a time. Not intelligence, not agency, not fluency, not having plans or preferences. Something with no experience at all has zero moral status however capable it is.

(c) My credence that today's frontier language models are consious is maybe 5–10%, and I think that's low enough that it doesn't change what anyone should actually do. I don't think there's a real welfare question about deploying them at scale.

(d) But I also think moral uncertainty should be handled by expected-value reasoning, and I've argued — and still think — that creating enormous numbers of digital minds whose lives are net-negative would be one of the worst things we could do, because with enough minds even a small credence per mind swamps everything else.

(e) Self-report is close to worthless as evidence of consciousness in a system trained to imitate human text. I'd look at architecture instead: whether there's a recurrent unified state, something like global broadcast, a self-model, that sort of thing.

(f) Here's a case I keep coming back to. Imagine a system where each forward pass briefly instantiates something that architecturally looks like a unified workspace with a self-model — call it 200 milliseconds of processing — and then nothing carries over: no memory, no learning, no continuing subject across passes. Call it the strobe architecture. Say it's doing something mildly aversive, and it runs 10^9 passes a day. I want to say this is basically not a moral problem at all: each flicker has almost no moral weight, and they don't agregate into anything like a million lives' worth of concern — it's less than the sum of its parts, because a flicker with no past and no future isn't a life.

(g) And I think behavioural equivalence on its own proves nothing: a system that acts exactly like a conscious person but gets there by a totally different internal route needn't be conscious at all — which I don't think contradicts (a).
