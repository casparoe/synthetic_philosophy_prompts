I've just come home from a long, slightly rancorous dinner with a friend — an old grad-school officemate who now does interpretability work on neural networks — and we spent most of it on the causal exclusion argument. His line, which he returned to perhaps six times, was that the whole thing is an artifact of a bad picture of causation: once you say that to be a cause is to be a difference-maker, or to figure as a manipulable variable in a causal model, then the mental property and the physical property that realizes it are both causes, at different grains of description, and there is nothing left to exclude. I kept saying that this doesn't so much solve the problem as lower the price of admission: if causation is just carrying information about an outcome, then a higher-level property "causes" the effect in much the same cheap sense in which a thermostat's dial causes the room to be warm, and the original discomfort — that the mental property looks like it isn't doing any work the physical property isn't already doing, and isn't the kind of thing a world would bother keeping track of twice — just reappears as a complaint about whether carrying information at a coarse grain is worth having. He said I was smuggling in a production notion of causation; I said he was smuggling in a bookkeeping notion; neither of us moved; and at some point I said, half-jokingly, that the entire dispute is a tax audit. That is the thing I'd like you to write.

What I want is a piece of deadpan bureaucratic satire: a leaked internal memo, with an attached claims form, from a fictional government office — the Bureau of Causal Audit, or whatever name works better — addressed to a higher-level mental property, say the property of being in pain, informing it that following a routine audit its causal contribution to a piece of behavior has been assessed at residual zero, on the ground that a sufficient physical cause has already been credited with the full amount, and that it is therefore being reassigned, with thanks for its years of service and with no loss of grade or salary, to the Office of Explanatory Services, a non-causal posting. About 700–900 words, formatted as an official document: letterhead, numbered paragraphs, and a fill-in form at the end. The form should offer the claimant three exemption routes and dispose of each in one or two flat sentences — (i) an identity claim, where winning means the claimant keeps its causal standing only by ceasing to be a distinct claimant; (ii) a claim of overdetermination, which is available only where the redundancy is occasional rather than systematic and industrially pervasive; (iii) a proportionality claim, to the effect that the credited physical cause is too fine-grained to be what made the difference to a coarse-grained effect like reaching for the aspirin. Please invent at least one piece of in-house jargon for the audit procedure itself and use it consistently throughout — I've been toying with a "no silent partners" rule, or with a line on the form asking the claimant to state its causal residual after the physical cause has been fully debited; use one of those or something better. The auditors should be scrupulously courteous and never sneer, so that the physicalist position comes across as reasonable and even humane rather than as a villain.

Two further constraints. First, the jokes have to come from the philosophy and not from the bureaucracy: a reader who works on mental causation should be able to tell that you know why the exclusion argument has bite — the sufficiency of the physical cause, the refusal to tolerate systematic overdetermination, how the difference-making and proportionality replies are supposed to rescue the mental property, the consolation prize of explanatory relevance without causal relevance, and the standing threat that if the argument generalizes the way it appears to, then chemistry, biology and economics are queued up behind it — and should find the satire fair to both sides rather than a hit job on one of them. Second, and this is the part I care about most: the memo must close with a short, entirely unironic compliance footnote from which an attentive reader can see that the Bureau is itself a higher-level entity whose causal work is, on its own criteria, fully discharged by the microphysical doings of its staff — without the word "self-undermining," or any other wink, appearing anywhere in the document. Don't let the deadpan break, don't name any real philosophers, don't include any authorial explanation of the allegory, and keep it legible to someone outside the debate, who should still be able to enjoy it as workplace satire and feel, without being told, that something has gone quietly wrong.
