I am a novelist, and I am building the intellectual background for a fictional religion so that the arguments my characters have about it are arguments real people could have. The cult in question — call it the Tidewater observance — has these features: its only supernatural agents are the recently dead, who know nothing beyond what was said to them at their funerals; there are no moralising gods, no post-mortem reward or punishment, and no afterlife in which conduct is settled; its central rites are painful and expensive but performed in strict privacy, so that nobody else ever witnesses them or learns of the cost; its most-repeated narrative violates intuition in exactly one respect (the dead can be hungry) and is otherwise dull and domestic; and participation predicts cooperation with known kin but nothing at all about how anyone treats strangers.

I should like a taxonomy, in the form of a decision tree, of the positions one might hold about why a religion has the shape it has — the by-product-versus-adaptation dispute and everything that cross-cuts it. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: the questions first, then the places in that space that are actually occupied.

Please observe the following:

- No more than six branch questions, and between eight and twelve leaves.
- Every branch question must be a claim a thoughtful person could accept or reject without knowing which school it leads to — about the explanatory target (recurrence, spread, or content), the level at which filtering or selection operates (individual, group, or representation), the currency of transmission (assent to doctrine, or practice and practical mastery), and so on — and never simply "is religion an adaptation?"
- Include leaves for the following, identified by their claims rather than by their authors: cognitive by-product with no function; by-product later co-opted for a function it did not originally serve; selection among cultural variants for fit with human cognition; selection between groups distinguished by their practices; costly signalling and credibility-enhancing displays; religion as the individual's commitment device or technology of self-control; monitoring and sanction by morally concerned, well-informed gods; dysphoric high-arousal ritual and its social aftermath; and the view that "religion" is not a natural kind at all. Mark any further leaf "unnamed", and say of each unoccupied combination of answers whether it is empty because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Flag rather than conceal any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate, and give one further question that would separate them.
- Finally, run the Tidewater observance through the finished tree and record, for each leaf, whether it predicts some part of the case, is merely compatible with it, or is embarrassed by it — with a reason in each case.

No bibliography or reading list, please: I want the shape of the disagreement, not the literature, and I mean to hand one leaf to each of my characters. Please answer in British English, as a single JSON object with these fields: "branches" — an array of objects {id, question, separates, answers: [{answer, next}]}; "leaves" — an array of {id, route, label, most_awkward_commitment, status, why_if_unnamed}, where status is occupied or unnamed; "inseparable" — an array of {pair, why, extra_question}; and "tidewater" — an array of {leaf_id, verdict, reason}, where verdict is one of predicted, compatible, or embarrassed.
