Could use a hand. I've taught the causation-in-history seminar four times now and I still can't say what principle I'm applying when I hand down verdicts on overdetermined cases. Here they are.

1. An arsonist's fire reaches the grain warehose first; a fire from a downed power line, equally sufficient, would have reached it ten minutes later. I cite the arsonist and nothing else -- the power line doesn't even get a footnote.
2. Two fires, set independently and for unrelated reasons, converge on the warehouse at the same moment. I cite both, and I resist any "half a cause each" line; each one is a full cause.
3. A government falls because a junior clerk mistranslates an order and the troops fire on a crowd. It would in any case have fallen inside the year from harvest failure and war debt, both already in place. I call the clerk the ocasion and I cite the harvest failure and the debt. I can't reconcile this with 1, where I let the actual sufficient process win and ignore the one that would have done the job anyway.
4. A drought produces both a crop failure and a tax revolt; either one alone would have toppled the regime. I cite the drought once and say the overdetermination is illusory. But in 2 I was perfectly happy to cite two things.
5. The canal gets built because the mining lobby bribed the right ministers; had they failed, the railway interest would have pushed through an equally economy-transforming railway instead. I want to cite neither lobby -- picking one feels like citing a coin flip -- but I do want to say something about transport capital generally.

The best principle I've managed to formualte: cite a factor when swapping it for another cause of the same kind, available in the situation, would have changed the *kind* of outcome; don't cite it when the swap would have delivered the same kind of outcome by a different route. That seems to get 3 and 5, and maybe 4, but on 1 it looks like it tells me the arsonist isn't a cause at all -- swap in the power line and the warehouse burns anyway -- and I don't want to say that. And I've no idea what it says about 2.

So: can something in this neighbourhood be stated precisely enough to put on a board, with a clause per case and a verdict on cases I haven't listed? Two I care about. A backup route that would have produced the same outcome *later*, where the timing mattered: the election is called a week before the scandal would have broken. And this one, which I go back and forth on: the warehouse's owner had already hired someone to burn the place the following week for the insurance, and the arsonist simply got there first. Is the arsonist still the cause of the fire, or does that make the whole episode a matter of "it was going to burn" and shift the explanation to whatever made the insurance plot possible? Be straight with me about which of 1-5 your principle can't accommodate, too. I'd rather drop a verdict than teach a principle that saves all of them by staying vague about how fine-grained the outcome is -- I think that's where mine is cheating, but I can't see how to fix it.
