So I'm in this kind of effective-altruism-adjacent reading group, and the other night someone used 'one thought too many' on me and I haven't been able to shake it. The setup: my friend's dad, who isn't any kind of philosopher, paid out of pocket for his daughter's surgery instead of giving the money to a top-rated malaria charity. I said something like 'sure, but he should at least be able to say what makes saving his kid beat saving several strangers' and this guy just goes 'no — that's exactly one thought too many.' The phrase obviously did a lot of work for him there, but I can't tell what work it was doing.

What I want is a menu of the precise claims that slogan could be standing for, with a verdict on each — platitude, substantive and defensible, or false — and, for each, whether it would actually get him his conclusion (that the dad needs no standing consequentialist permission slip for his partiality).

A few things I keep tripping over and want you to sort into those readings rather than answer separately. First: is the complaint about what has to pass through the agent's head at the moment of action, or about what has to be true for the action to be right? I genuinely can't tell whether he's making a point about deliberation or about the structure of justification. Second: is it really about integrity and 'ground projects' at all, or is it mostly about love and special relationships — i.e. are the dad-type case and the classic case of the guy who turns down the weapons-research job doing the same argumentative work, or is one of them carrying the other? Third, and this is the one I care most about: is the objection supposed to be specific to consequentialism, or would it land just as hard on the Kantian who has to check a maxim for universalizability, or on the Aristotelian who has to get the 'right reason' right? Our group is full of people who take the thought-too-many line against utilitarians on Saturday and then spend Sunday auditing their own motives in the name of virtue, and I want to know if that's incoherent or if there's a reading that lets them off.

Oh, and one more: isn't there a version of the case where the thought isn't literally occurrent, because the agent was raised or trained to just do the right thing without working it out — say a doctor who triages smoothly because she drilled the protocol for years? If so, doesn't the objection only ever mark a failure of habituation rather than anything about the theory? Say which of your readings that move kills outright and which ones survive it untouched.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up.
