I've been working through a popular history of medieval philosophy of the sort written to correct the impression that nothing happened between antiquity and Descartes, and its chapter on Islamic thought contains a reconstruction of al-Ghazālī's occasionalism that I can't quite evaluate. I've reproduced the passage below roughly as I remember it — I can't vouch for the wording, but I think I've kept the substance:

Al-Ghazālī's target was the Greek-inspired idea that causes necessitate their effects by their very natures: fire burns cotton not because God wills it on that occasion, but because fiery stuff just is the sort of thing that burns. Against this he argued that there is no contradiction in supposing that cotton should touch fire and not burn, or that a man should be sated without eating, and that a claim whose denial is coherently conceivable cannot be a necessary truth. So far this is just Hume six centuries early, and Hume reached the same conclusion: we observe constant conjunction and never a productive power, and the necessity we read into nature is a habit of our own minds. Where al-Ghazālī adds God, he adds very little — God merely supplies, once and for all, the pattern that the Humean accepts as brute. Properly understood, then, occasionalism is barely a metaphysical thesis at all. Al-Ghazālī's word for cause is an agent's word: a cause is what brings something about. Since only God brings anything about, saying that fire is not a cause is mostly a point about the correct application of a term rather than a discovery about the world. None of this threatens natural science. Al-Ghazālī never denied that cotton in contact with fire burns, and burns every time; he denied only that it burns by a necessity visible in the fire itself. Inquiry in the Islamic world carried on regardless, and the story that occasionalism strangled it is a modern myth.

I am inclined to think the chapter is right that the decline-of-science narrative is overblown, and I'm therefore worried that I'm accepting the whole passage because it flatters a prior I already hold. So I'd like a claim-by-claim audit: what a specialist would accept outright, what they'd accept as a defensible simplification for a general reader, what they'd wince at, and — this matters to me — which of the chapter's framings a sympathetic occasionalist would welcome as a friendly restatement rather than resist as a distortion. Please also say where the author appears to correct one error by overcorrecting into another.

The specific points I most want adjudicated:

First, the reduction of occasionalism to Humeanism plus a theological gloss. Is a regularity grounded in a single divine agent's stable will really the same thing as a regularity taken as brute, or does that gloss smuggle in exactly the productive source the Humean denies? Relatedly, is the conceivability argument doing the work the chapter assigns it, and is al-Ghazālī's positive account of how we know the regularity holds — something about God's custom and about His not being a deceiver — a genuine answer to the problem of induction, or does it either collapse into the Humean's shrug or relocate the question into theology?

Second, the deflationary claim that the dispute with the philosophers was largely verbal. I have a vague sense that al-Ghazālī elsewhere allows that God creates effects at or through the contact of the cause, which sounds weaker than the chapter's version, and that his occasionalism is tied to a broader atomism and to a view about human action that the chapter never mentions. If either is right, the verbal-dispute reading looks like an overstatement.

Third, counterfactuals and laws. On the chapter's reading, what is supposed to ground the truth of "if this cotton had touched fire, it would have burned"? I've heard occasionalism described instead as a kind of agent-causal or voluntarist picture, on which the only genuine explanations are personal ones and laws of nature are decrees rather than descriptions — which, if correct, would make it a rival to both Humeanism and dispositionalist views of powers rather than a variant of the first. Is that a better reading, and does it make the "no obstacle to science" verdict more secure or less?

Fourth, take the author's own example seriously: how, on occasionalist premises, should a well-attested case of fire failing to burn update one's confidence in the regularity — and is that any different from how it should update it on Humean premises? If the two answers differ, that difference seems like the cleanest test of whether the chapter's reduction has lost something.

Finally, and most importantly: does the chapter's closing conclusion — that occasionalism was no obstacle to science and that blaming it for any stagnation is myth — actually follow from the parts of the summary that survive correction, or was it resting on the misrepresentations? I'd also welcome a note on whether comparing him to Hume is even the most informative comparison, or whether a later occasionalist motivated by a quite different problem would make the structure clearer.
