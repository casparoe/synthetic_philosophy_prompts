You are a professional philosopher of mind with a working knowledge of the debate over whether dreams are experiences (Malcolm's conceptual attack on the very idea, Dennett's empirical-minded reply, and the sleep-laboratory work they were arguing about). Please think carefully and take your time: before answering, sketch your own map of the dialectic, then work through my commitments one at a time rather than reacting to the list as a whole.

Over the past few months this has stopped being a classroom topic for me. A change of medication left me with dreams far more vivid than any I had before, I began keeping a dream journal, and then I started having occasional episodes in which I knew, while the dream was still unfolding, that I was dreaming. Trying to say what is actually going on there has pushed me into the following set of commitments, which I would like audited before I try to defend them to anyone else:

1. Malcolm's criterion argument gets something importantly right: a dream report cannot be checked against the experience it purports to report, and the tie between the report and the dream is conceptual rather than evidential.
2. But that insight is a point about our concepts, not a discovery about sleep, so whether there is experience during sleep remains an open empirical question.
3. Because it is empirical, the sleep-lab evidence is exactly what we need: awakenings from a specific physiological sleep state yield dream reports far more often than awakenings elsewhere, giving us an independent, well-correlated marker of dreaming.
4. The lucid-dreaming evidence settles the matter: trained subjects can carry out an agreed signalling manoeuvre from inside a dream, during a sleep state that is independently recorded, so there is experience during sleep, full stop.
5. Dreaming is nonetheless not perception. It is a species of imagination or mental imagery, and nothing in a dream has the presence, or the directedness onto an external world, that genuinely seeing something has.
6. The sleeper's own report is the last word on whether they were dreaming; no neural recording can overturn a sincere first-person avowal.
7. For the same reason, someone who reports never dreaming should be taken at face value: they are reporting dreamless sleep.
8. I also find attractive Dennett's own positive fallback about composition rather than playback, that what we get on waking is something like a reconstruction staged at the moment of waking rather than the replay of a recording laid down during the night.

Is this a consistent package? I want an audit, not a survey of the literature: tell me which commitments genuinely conflict, which only look as though they do, and for each real conflict what would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set. Rank the tensions by severity, and say explicitly which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why. Please do not assume I already know where the trouble lies, and please do not assume in advance which commitment I would be willing to abandon.
