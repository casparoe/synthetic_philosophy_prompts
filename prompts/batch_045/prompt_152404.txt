I keep bumping into the same odd patchwork around personal information and I cant tell whether it adds up to a view of privacy or just to a pile of habits.

First: the pop-up. A site puts a banner in front of you, you click the button, and after that pretty much anything done with what you generate is treated as fine — bundled, sold on, fed into ad targeting. One click seems to do an enormous amount of normative work.

Second: de-identification. Take the name and a few fields off a medical or location dataset and it stops being anybody's personal information; researchers and companies pass it around without asking anyone. But when somebody re-identifies a person inside a released dataset, we treat that as a serious wrong even if nothing was done with the information and nothing bad happened to the person.

Third: handing it over. What your bank knows about your spending, or which numbers your phone dialled, gets treated as not really private anymore — you gave it to a company. Yet a letter you hand to the postal service keeps about the strongest protection there is, and the record of where your phone has been is now treated as especially sensitive even though a company holds that too.

Fourth, and this one I'm half inventing (I may have read about a pilot somewhere, or I may have dreamt it): a hospital where any clinician can open any patient's full chart with no need-to-know check, every open is logged with the viewer's name and role, patients get a monthly list of who looked at them, and the only thing actually forbidden is passing what you saw to anyone outside the care team.

So what does this bundle presuppose about what privacy is and why it matters? I don't want the textbook tour of the rival accounts, I want the reconstruction: what the practices taken together commit us to, and which piece is doing the presuming (the pop-up seems to presuppose that privacy is control and that consent transforms the situation; de-identification seems to presuppose that it's really about identifiability; the third seems to presuppose something about the norms attached to a role or a medium). Is the bundle actually consistent, or is one of the pieces the outlier that a coherent theory would have to give up? Which accounts does it quietly rule out — the one that says privacy talk is just property and bodily-rights talk in disguise, the one that says the issue is whether a flow fits the norms of the context and consent is neither necessary nor sufficient, or the one that says privacy matters because intimacy and relationships need selective disclosure? Concretely: if we swapped in one of those, what would the first three look like — what would have to stop, what would have to start? And in the hospital one, is transparency doing the same job that restriction would do, or a different job?

Last thing: is the value being assumed here instrumental only (discrimination, manipulation, chilling) or is something non-instrumental doing work in there, and where is the practice anyway underdetermined, i.e. which bits would several different theories all happily license?
