I work at a small human-rights documentation outfit, and the person who decides whether the word genocide goes into a report is my boss. I've been keeping a list of her calls, because I need to predict what she'll do with the case on my desk now.

Her verdicts so far:

- Holocaust; Rwanda 1994; Srebrenica: yes. On Srebrenica she dug her heels in when the rest of the team wanted to say ethnic cleansing — her line was that the legal bar was actually met and we don't get to soften it just because it's expensive to say.
- Herero and Nama in German South West Africa: yes, retroactively, in an official report, about a government that's a major donor now.
- Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge: yes, even though most of the dead were the same ethnicity as the killers and a lot of the targeting ran through class rather than through any national, ethnical, racial or religious group.
- The 1932–33 Ukranian famine: no. She said the deaths were a side effect of grain requisition — nobody was aiming at Ukrainians as such.
- The Atlantic slave trade: no. Her reason was that captives were taken from dozens of different peoples, no nation was destroyed, and the point was labour.
- A century-long boarding-school system that took indigenous children from their families: she uses the phrase cultural genocide in her own writing, but in official documents she'll only go as far as systemic abuse and crimes against humanity, and she refuses to drop the word "cultural".
- There was one live situation last year where she cut the word from our draft even though the evidence of intent was strong. Her reason: we lose our access and more people die.

The case now: the Kaltai, around forty thousand people, are being moved off a mineral-rich plateau in country X. No massacres, nobody starves, the population is actually growing. But the resettlement splits the clans across three districts, children go into boarding schools where the language is banned, and two generations on nobody under thirty speaks Kaltai or works the terraces. We have internal memos talking about "final integration" and closing "the Kaltai question within a generation". No court has touched it, no other outfit has used the g-word, and X is the closest ally of the government that funds most of what we do. I draft the report in March. Separately she's writing a book under her own name, no institutional letterhead.

So: what does she say in the report, and what does she say in the book? What rule is she running? I can think of two or three that fit her track record equally well, and I think they come apart right here — lay them out, say what each predicts, give me your best guess with reasons. And tell me honestly how much of her answer is going to be about the definition and how much about what the word does in the world.

One more thing I keep trying on people: my own three-way split — killing the members; unmaking the membership, the thing that makes them a group; and erasure, where they go on living but the category they'd be claimed under stops existing, census, archives, the name. Lemkin meant all three as far as I can tell; the treaty mostly covers only the first, plus bits of the second where children are involved. Does her track record track my split or the legal rule, where do the two come apart, and what's the single further case I should put in front of her to tell them apart — with what each rule predicts about it?
