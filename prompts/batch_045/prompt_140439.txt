I am writing a novel whose central institution is the Court of Revision of a fictional republic, and I want its constitutional doctrine to hold together under pressure rather than merely sound plausible.

The Charter is amendable by two-thirds of the Chamber with the concurrence of two-thirds of the cantonal councils. Four precedents matter. In 1902 the legislature used that formula to lower the formula itself to three-fifths; the Court upheld it, holding that the amending power may alter the terms of its own exercise, including by relaxing them. In 1931 the legislature, under the ordinary formula and with no referendum, enacted that the Charter might thereafter be amended only by unanimous resolution of every cantonal council together with a two-thirds national referendum; the Court let the accompanying substance stand but severed the entrenchment, on the ground that one two-thirds vote cannot place the whole Charter beyond the reach of every later two-thirds vote. In 1968 the legislature made the franchise article amendable only with a referendum, and although the new article did not so require, submitted its own amendment to that referendum and carried it; the Court upheld the entrenchment in full. In 1994 a convention summoned under the Charter's convention article replaced the Charter outright, amending formula included, and the Court upheld that too, on the ground that the convention speaks for the constituent power and answers to none of the rules it displaces.

The case now before the Court is the Continuity Amendment, passed under the ordinary formula with no referendum. It provides that any future amendment touching the amending article or the franchise article must additionally be approved at a referendum held not less than twelve months after first passage; that this requirement governs any amendment repealing or relaxing it; that it shall take effect upon promulgation and shall not apply to its own adoption; and it pointedly leaves the convention article out of its list.

What I need is the holding my protagonist should draft. Which rules fit these four precedents equally well — a symmetry rule, on which the amending power must accept for itself the hurdle it imposes on its successors, versus a flat rule, on which validity is tested only by the procedure in force at adoption and a commencement clause declining to apply a new rule to the act enacting it is ordinary drafting, subject only to the outer limit that some operative route of amendment survives — and what does each say here? Which precedent is the true analogue, 1968 or 1931, and does the deliberate omission of the convention article settle that? The cooling-off period means this amendment could not have cleared its own hurdle even in principle at the moment of its adoption: does that impossibility excuse it, or is it the strongest objection to it? Give me a best guess with the reasons for preferring it, tell me what principle the Court is committing itself to either way, and name the further case — a re-passage after twelve months with the referendum carried, an immediate referendum with a deemed-compliance clause, something else — that would actually discriminate between the two rules.
