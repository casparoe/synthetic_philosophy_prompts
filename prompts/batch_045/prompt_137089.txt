I work on response-dependent accounts of aesthetic value and am trying to extend Hume's standard of taste past the cases his 1757 essay actually settles. Set out the verdicts I'm working from. In the story he borrows from Don Quixote, the two connoisseurs who taste leather and iron in the hogshead of wine are vindicated against the crowd that calls it excellent. A reader who ranks Ogilby above Milton, or Bunyan above Addison, is not credited with a different taste but charged with a defective one. Beauty is located in the sentiment the object excites rather than in the object itself, and the persistence of Homer's reputation across two millennia and many nations is offered as evidence that we have hit on genuinely general rules of composition. The competent judge is described in five parts — delicacy of taste, practice on the same art, comparison within a genre and period, freedom from personal prejudice, good sense — and the palate of a man in a fever gets no vote. Finally, some residual variation is conceded to be blameless rather than defective.

The case I can't settle. A canvas in the manner of a Dutch Golden Age painter has been attributed to him for a century, and every judge satisfying those five conditions reports real pleasure after repeated viewings under favorable circumstances. Technical analysis then shows it was painted last year by a generative model trained on the painter's complete output. The judges are told; the surface is unchanged in every visible respect; their reports now describe it as inert and derivative.

I can see at least three rules that fit the earlier verdicts equally well. On one, beauty is a disposition to please qualified judges under favorable conditions, and the question is whether knowledge of provenance belongs in the circumstances that must be favorable — so that the post-announcement verdict is the authoritative one, or so that the collapse is itself a prejudice of the sort the standard was meant to exclude. On a second, the joint verdict of true judges is constitutive, and two verdicts from equally qualified judges in different epistemic situations mean the standard simply runs out and there is no fact of the matter. On a third, the standard tracks pleasure taken in the right kind of thing, so that derivation is disqualifying regardless of how the surface responds.

Which of these best fits Hume's actual verdicts, what does each predict about the canvas, and what is your best guess with the reason for preferring it? I also suspect his text never settles whether the standard is meant to constitute beauty or merely to evidence it — say whether that ambiguity is doing any work here. Last: name the case that would most sharply tell these rules apart, and tell me why.
