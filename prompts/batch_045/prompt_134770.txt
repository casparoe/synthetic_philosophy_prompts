Hey — I'm in a philosophy of religion seminar and we're three weeks into Molinism and middle knowledge, and I've noticed my gut verdicts on cases dont add up to anything coherent. Hoping you can find the principle, or tell me which verdict to drop.

Quick setup as I understand it, so we're on the same page: before God decrees anything, God knows for every possible creature and every complete circumstance-set it could be in what it would freely do — counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, call them CFs. God then picks which circumstances to actualize and gets the outcome without causally determining anyone. I get why that's attractive. What I cant get straight is which CFs are true in the first place, and whether every true one is equally available for God to lean on.

My verdicts:

1. Plain case. Claire in C1: offered money to quietly bury a colleague's mistake, easy to get away with, no one obviously hurt. She'd freely refuse. Verdict: true, and God can use it. No hesitation.

2. Frankfurt-ish case. Dan in C2 signs the form, and he does it freely, but a hidden mechanism would have forced his hand if he'd hesitated. I want to say "in C2 Dan would freely sign" is true — he really does sign freely — but I also want to say God gets no providential leverage from a CF like this one. It's true but second-rate somehow. Is that even coherent, or am I smuggling in two properties where the theory has one?

3. Brittle case. Eve would freely refuse in C3, but in C3', identical except the coffee in the room is tea instead, she'd accept. Verdict: the CF about C3 is still true. But I feel queasy about God actualizing C3 precisely to get the refusal — that reads as cherry-picking the circumstances rather than respecting her freedom. I dont want to say the CF is false, though.

4. Unsettled agent. Gina in C4 has never faced anything remotely like this, has no settled policy, and her reasons are exactly balanced. Ask me whether she'd freely refuse and I want to say: no fact of the matter. Not true, not false.

5. Disjunctive. Ines in C5 would freely take either of two options, and nothing about her settles which. I'm happy to say she'd freely do-A-or-B; not happy to say she'd freely do A.

6. The spite case my prof gave us. In C6, Hal is reliably told that God's middle knowledge contains "Hal would freely accept." Hal, being Hal, would freely refuse out of pure contrarianism. I think that CF is true, and I also think a batch of CFs like this can't all be true-and-known together, which is the bit that makes my head hurt.

7. Merely possible people. Jael is never created. "If Jael were in C7 she'd freely refuse" — true, no problem at all.

So: 3 and 4 look alike in the respect that seems to matter (nothing about the agent or the situation robustly fixes what happens), and I give them opposite verdicts. My own tentative rule was that a CF is true iff the agent would decide that way throughout the nearest C-worlds, where "nearest" tracks causal similarity rather than matching C down to the tiny details — which would make 3 indeterminate like 4, and would hand me 5's "A or B" for free. But it also looks like it makes 2 false, since Dan doesn't sign in the nearby C-worlds where the mechanism isn't lurking, and I dont want that.

What I want: a principle, or two or three rival candidates, stated precisely, that tells me which CFs are true, whether all true ones are equally usable for providence, and what that last distinction even consists in. Lead with the bottom line in your very first sentence — the principle, or the news that nothing single and clean saves all my verdicts — then show your work: clause by clause with the case that forces each clause, then your verdicts on cases I haven't listed (I especially want to know what your principle says about CFs concerning what God himself would freely do, and about a pair of agents whose CFs are entangled so that no circumstance-set satisfies both at once), then an honest list of which of my verdicts your principle overturns, and which one you'd give up yourself if you had to drop exactly one.
