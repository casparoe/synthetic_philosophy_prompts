I am helping a colleague who maintains a benchmark of Bongard problems — the visual induction puzzles in which six figures on the left instantiate a rule, six figures on the right instantiate its complement, and the solver has to recover the rule from those twelve examples alone. Her benchmark scores submitted rules as correct or incorrect, and over the past year she has had to make a series of judgment calls about what counts as having recovered the rule. There is a submission in front of her now, she has asked me to argue it through with her, and I would like the reasoning rather than just a verdict.

Her record so far. In each case the submission separated the twelve correctly, so the ruling is never about fit to the sample alone.

1. *B-04.* Left: six closed outlines — circle, square, irregular blob, five-pointed star outline, pentagon, ellipse — each with a single dot inside. Right: the same six outline shapes, each with a single dot outside. Submission: "On the left the dot is inside the closed outline; on the right it is outside." Accepted.

2. *B-09.* Left: six figures, each two closed outlines that do not touch and are of the same shape (two circles, two squares, two triangles, and so on). Right: six figures, each two closed outlines of different shapes. Submission: "On the left the two outlines have the same shape; on the right they do not." Accepted.

3. *B-26.* Left: six figures, each two closed outlines that overlap, part of each lying inside the other. Right: six figures, each two closed outlines wholly separate from one another. Submission: "On the left the two outlines have at least one point in common and neither one contains the other; on the right they have no point in common." Accepted, with the note that the second clause is redundant but harmless.

4. *B-11.* Left: six closed outlines containing one, three or five dots. Right: six closed outlines containing two, four or six dots. Submission: "On the left the outline contains one dot, or three dots, or five dots; on the right it contains two, four or six." Rejected: "this is a list of the numbers that happen to turn up in the sample; it would put a seventh dot on the wrong side."

5. *B-14.* Left: six open curves — an arc, a wave, a zigzag, and so on. Right: six closed loops. Submission: "Every figure on the left is drawn with a dashed stroke and every figure on the right with a solid stroke," which is true of the twelve as printed. Rejected: "that is a fact about how the page was printed, not about the figure; my generator randomises stroke style."

6. *B-18.* Left: six figures, each two straight segments that cross. Right: six figures, each two straight segments that are parallel and so do not meet. Submission: "On the left the segments are not parallel; on the right they are parallel." Rejected: "non-parallel segments need not meet; this works only because the twelve happen not to include a pair too short to reach each other."

The pending case, *BP-31.* Left: six figures, each a single simple closed outline — circle, square, ellipse, five-pointed star outline, irregular blob, crescent outline — with one dot clearly inside it: near the centre in some, close to the boundary in others, and in one case up in a narrow point of the star. Right: six figures with the same six outlines and one dot clearly outside: far away in some, just outside in others, and in two cases sitting in the mouth of the crescent or in the bay between two points of the star, so that the outline curls around the dot on three sides. No dot lies on an outline. Submission: "On the left, a straight ray drawn from the dot out to the edge of the frame crosses the outline an odd number of times, whatever direction you pick (ignoring directions that graze a corner or run along a straight side); on the right it crosses an even number of times." The submitting lab's cover note argues that this is to be preferred to the plain inside/outside wording, because it can be checked mechanically without appealing to any notion of a region.

Assume, as I do, that the submission is in fact extensionally correct over the twelve, and very likely over every figure of this kind. What I want from you: the two or three criteria that fit all six of her earlier rulings equally well, stated precisely enough that they can disagree; what each predicts about BP-31; your best guess at her ruling, or at the ruling she should give if she wants to be consistent with herself, with the closest analog among her precedents identified and the reasons for preferring it; whether the parenthetical about grazing corners and straight sides does any work in your prediction; and the further submission — to BP-31 or to a deliberately altered variant of it — that would best tell the surviving criteria apart. As far as I can tell she has never before been asked to rule on a submission that is equivalent to the rule she had in mind but stated in a much more roundabout way; if that is what makes this case new, say so, and say what her ruling would then commit her benchmark to measuring.
