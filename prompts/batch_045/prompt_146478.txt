someone in my reading group keeps leaning on one analogy for the moral equality of combatants: war is like a boxing match. both fighters step into the ring voluntarily, both know the other is going to try to hit them, both are allowed to hit, and neither does the other a wrong by landing a punch — in a fight you agreed to, you don't get to complain that someone hit you. he uses it to license three things: a soldier on the unjust side does no wrong in killing enemy combatants; the just and unjust soldiers are on a par, neither has a complaint against the other; and so the unjust side's soldier owes no apology or reparations to the people he shot at, any more than a boxer who loses does.

two cases came up that he also tried to fit into the same picture, and I'm not sure it bends that far: conscripts who never volunteered for anything, and drone or cyber operators who face basically no risk themselves. he says neither changes anything, since a boxer definatley doesn't lose his permission to punch just because his opponent was pushed into the ring or can't punch back.

can you audit the analogy — what maps onto what (a table is fine) and where the mapping breaks? I'm especally curious whether it's secretly doing its work through consent, i.e. a waiver of your rights, or through something like forfeiture or liability, because that seems to decide what happens with conscripts; and whether importing a referee, agreed rules and a bounded contest is smuggling in a normative structure that war doesn't have. then tell me which of his three conclusions, if any, survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there's a better comparison he should be using instead.
