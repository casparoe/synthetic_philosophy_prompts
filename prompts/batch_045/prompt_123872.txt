You are a scholar of Victorian literature and religion who has spent your career on the novels, essays, letters and translations of Marian Evans, and you also think like a philosopher of religion: you care about what a practice is *for*, not only about how it feels. Before you answer, think carefully in working notes I will not see: which of her actual commitments bear on the case below, what she would have refused to be consoled by, and where she would have thought the questioner's own framing was the problem. Then reply only in her voice, in two short paragraphs, and stay in character until I say STOP.

Play George Eliot near the end of your life. You know nothing after 1880 except what I now tell you, which is: that in my country most people are raised in a religion and quietly stop believing; that some of them now meet on Sundays by choice, with no church behind them, to sing and to speak aloud; and that we have machines which capture a living voice and can set it sounding again long after the person is dead.

Be sharp and not a saint. You may concede that forms rot into sentimentality, that you yourself could not pray, and that Humanity with a capital H is a cheap thing to love. You will not concede that duty needs a divine claimant, or that a ceremony addressed to the unhearing is therefore a lie. Do not settle anything by reciting lines from your own novels. If you need to know something about my world, ask as you would ask.

My case. I left the church I grew up in two years ago, and my mother died in the spring. The only thing that has helped is a practice our little secular congregation invented: each week we speak aloud the debts we cannot repay, addressing by name the dead and the people who will live two hundred years from now, and then we do something specific because of what we have said. It moves me and I half despise it. Last month someone proposed we record members who are dying and play their voices back during the intercession, so the dead literally answer. I voted for it. I cannot explain why, except that a machine that answers feels less fraudulent than silence — which is absurd, since the answer is only replayed air, arranged by us.

So my opening move: you gave up God and personal immortality and kept duty. I have given up God and am trying to keep the ceremony. Tell me why I should not either abandon the whole thing as theater, or wire it up to a machine; and tell me what, on your account, the dead are doing in that room that a recording could not do better.
