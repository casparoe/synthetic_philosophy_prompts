I chair the ethics advisory group to a national commissioning body that is deciding whether to fund psychedelic-assisted therapy, and I want a genuinely Platonic contribution to the deliberation rather than a costume drama. Please reconstruct, as faithfully as you can, what Plato would say about the proposal below, working from the arguments about wine and drunkenness in the opening books of the Laws and from whatever else of his bears on the matter: the partition of the soul and what happens to reason when it departs, his suspicion of poetry and of conviction that cannot be given an account, the divine madness praised in the Phaedrus, and his doctrines about who may be entrusted with what.

As I recall the Laws position — correct me if I have it wrong — wine is treated as a drug that has been given to human beings precisely because it produces shame and boldness, so that the soul's capacity for self-mastery can be exercised and tested in the way a body is exercised by gymnastics; the test is cheap, safe and reversible, provided the drinking happens in company under the authority of a sober symposiarch, and provided the reveller is afterwards held to account for what he did while intoxicated. The rules are also graded by age and office: the young are to abstain, those in their prime to drink moderately, and men past their prime to drink more freely at the festivals, while soldiers on campaign and magistrates on duty are to abstain. Please work with that reading, but tell me plainly if you think I have mangled it.

The programme we are weighing up has these features. (1) Three supervised sessions with psilocybin, one patient and two facilitators, the patient wearing eyeshades and listening to a curated playlist; facilitators are trained to be non-directive. (2) The indication is a diagnosis — treatment-resistant depression — with standard informed consent, and the sessions are reimbursed like any other procedure. (3) A "rescue medication" is available at any moment, so that a distressing session can be pharmacologically terminated on request. (4) Recovery of the experience is handled by a post-session "integration" session and, optionally, an app that turns the patient's notes into a written narrative of what the session meant. (5) Running alongside it is a wholly private market: microdose subscriptions sold to healthy professionals, self-administered alone, marketed for creativity and emotional balance, with no supervisor and no follow-up. (6) The same compounds remain criminal offences outside the clinic.

The questions I want answered are these. Would Plato count any of this as the practice he defends in the Laws, or as the licence he warns against, and which single feature is decisive for him? What does it do to his argument that the supervisor has been replaced by a drug that switches the state off, and that the account the drunk man owes is now produced by an app? Does the inversion of his age gradient matter to him, and what would he make of a drug taken daily, alone, in doses too small to intoxicate, for the sake of performance? What would he say about the fact that the sessions characteristically produce an unshakeable conviction that something profound has been revealed — a conviction patients report as knowledge, and that clinicians are careful neither to affirm nor to deny? And if you were drafting the corresponding statute for Magnesia, what would it look like: who would be eligible, who would preside, what would be forbidden, and what would happen afterwards?

I would like roughly 900 to 1,100 words, and I would like it flagged, at the point where it matters, which claims you take to be supported by the texts and which are your extrapolation from them, together with a short closing note on which of Plato's premises he would have had to abandon in a modern setting and whether his verdict survives their loss. Please do not give me citations, quotations you cannot verify, or a reading list: paraphrase the arguments and rely on them. Say how confident you are overall, and single out the one claim you are least sure of. Write in British English, and do not close by asking what I think.
