I'm taking a graduate seminar on the political economy of international investment law, and I keep feeling that our material is much older than we pretend it is. Two institutions keep coming up. The first is the standard clause in investment treaties and trade agreements that lets a foreign company haul a host government before a private arbitral tribunal and claim damages if a new law — a mining ban, plain-packaging rules for cigarettes, a minimum-wage increase, a change to licensing — cuts into the profits the company expected when it invested: three arbitrators, usually commercial lawyers who rotate between acting as counsel and as decision-makers, rule on the legality of a sovereign's legislation, and their award is enforceable against the state, while the state cannot bring a claim against the company under the same clause. The second is the family of special economic zones and proposed "charter cities" in which a host state hands over the making and enforcing of rules inside a territory — land rules, labour rules, sometimes courts — to a foreign or private developer for decades at a time. I've started thinking of both as the return of the chartered company: a body that does business under a grant of privilege and also exercises what look like sovereign powers.

Suppose Adam Smith were in the seminar room. Someone has explained both institutions to him, along with the facts that today's firms mostly are not state-chartered monopolies with their own armies and that the governments being sued are often elected ones; someone has also walked him through the modern antitrust consensus, in which reviewers ask almost exclusively whether a practice raises prices or restricts output. Give me your reconstruction of his reaction, drawing on what he actually argued about the chartered companies, the monopoly of colonial trade, and the empire across the Atlantic, and on his claim that a country needs little more than peace, low taxes, and reasonably impartial courts to become prosperous. I want the reconstruction rather than a summary of the Wealth of Nations: how he would name what these institutions are, which of his commitments they offend (privilege granted at the public's expense, government captured by the interests of merchants and manufacturers, the constitutional unworkability of rule at a distance) and which of them they might actually serve (secure property, restraint on arbitrary rulers, the disciplining of governments that expropriate). Two further questions matter most to me. First, my own proposed fix: would it satisfy him if the clause were made reciprocal — if the state could bring claims against investors, and if workers, communities, or domestic firms could bring claims before the same tribunals — or is the delegation of adjudication over public law to private arbitrators the objectionable part, so that symmetrising it changes nothing? Second, my suspicion that he would find the modern consumer-welfare lens nearly useless, since what he objected to in monopoly was often the privilege and the corruption of government rather than the price, and a reviewer who asks only about prices would wave through arrangements he would have thought poison.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no concluding summary: (1) how he would characterise these institutions; (2) which of his commitments they offend and which they serve; (3) whether the reciprocity fix settles his objection; (4) how he would regard the consumer-welfare-only approach to market power; and (5) where the extrapolation from eighteenth-century chartered companies genuinely breaks down, and what would change his mind.
