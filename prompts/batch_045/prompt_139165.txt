I am writing a short critical essay for a philosophy of biology module, and I would like to read Andrew Niccol's 1997 film Gattaca through the dispute about whether DNA is literally a code: whether the genome is a text, a message, or a program specifying the organism, or whether talk of genetic information is at most a convenient façon de parler that persists because of the explanatory and rhetorical work it does. I need a reading, not a literature review; please argue from the film and the positions themselves and do not cite anyone.

To fix the target, grade the code claim into three commitments, and tell me which ones the film's world actually needs. First, the cipher claim: that there is a genuinely arbitrary mapping from codon triplets to amino acids, discovered in the 1960s, and read by a molecular machinery that is itself partly encoded in the same molecule — so that the reader is, awkwardly, part of the message. Second, the program or blueprint claim: that the genome contains instructions for, or a specification of, the developed organism. Third, the actuarial or dossier claim: that a genome is a legible file of risk figures and probable endpoints, readable in seconds by anyone with the right instrument. My suspicion, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that Gattaca is routinely criticised as a genetic-determinist film when in fact almost nothing in its social order requires the second commitment: employers and insurers in the film read probabilities, expected lifespans and risk thresholds, not destinies. If that is right, then the interesting question is not whether genes are a code but what institutions have to build in order to make a genome legible at all — the scanners, the registries, the job categories, the daily rituals of sample provision — and the useful distinction is between genetic essentialism, a view about what someone is, and something like genetic audit, a practice of governance in which a body is treated as an auditable document. I want you to press the thought that legibility is an institutional achievement rather than a molecular property, and to consider whether the code metaphor survives philosophical criticism precisely because institutions find it administratively useful, in something like the way a credit score makes a financial history legible without anyone believing the score is the person.

Work the reading through particular scenes rather than the plot in general: the instantaneous profile read from a stray follicle at the door; Vincent's daily scrubbing and the paraphernalia of borrowed hair, blood and urine; the borrowed-ladder arrangement with the swimmer who took silver and then threw himself in front of a car; the two games of chicken in the sea with his engineered brother, and what the second one reveals about how the first was won; the co-worker who is valid but carries a heart that disqualifies her; the physician at the final pre-launch test who quietly falsifies the result because of his own son, who was selected for everything and turned out to be less than promised; the two lifetimes of stored samples, the envelope, and the medal taken into the incinerator; and the closing narration's assertion that no gene accounts for the human spirit.

Be faithful in both directions. Say where the film is subtler than the standard critique of it, and say where it resists or betrays the reading you want to give it: whether Vincent's triumph actually challenges the practice or only his own verdict within it, whether the appeal to a spirit beyond coding concedes the code picture everywhere except in one mysterious corner, and whether a film whose title spells its own name out of the four letters of the nucleobase alphabet has really earned the right to criticise the metaphor. Close, if you can, by saying what the film adds to the philosophical argument that the argument alone does not supply.
