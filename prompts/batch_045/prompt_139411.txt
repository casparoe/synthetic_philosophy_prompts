I am writing a seminar paper on Rousseau's *Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre* (1758) for a postgraduate reading group, and I have tried to reconstruct the argument I find in it as a single chain of reasoning. Reconstructed, it now seems to me to prove far more than either I or Rousseau could accept, and I cannot locate the step that is doing the damage.

The argument, as I have it:

(1) An amusement is to be assessed not by its content but by the sentiments it habituates in those who enjoy it. It is good if the sentiments it exercises are ones the polity needs and that carry over into action; it is bad if the sentiment is spent within the amusement itself.

(2) The theatre is bad in the second way. The pity and terror it excites are discharged in the theatre, and the discharge takes the place of the action: one leaves having wept over sufferings that are not present and having done nothing about the sufferings that are.

(3) What makes that discharge corrupting is not that the stage is fictional, since fiction can instruct; it is that the sentiment's object is particular, complete and given — this suffering, already finished, set before me, with nothing left for me to do about it.

(4) What a republic should therefore cultivate is sentiment whose object is the whole, exercised in common; and the institution proper to it is the open-air festival, in which the citizens are at once performers and spectators.

(5) But the sentiments of the festival are particular and complete in just the way (3) describes. The decorated veteran, the bride, the mother of many children are particular objects, and the emotion they arouse is exhausted in the moment of arousal.

(6) Nor can passivity supply the difference. The festivals are described in terms of citizens watching one another, and the mutual visibility of a small republic is a standing form of spectatorship; the Geneva Rousseau defends is one in which everyone is perpetually before everyone else's eyes.

(7) So if (3) is what is doing the work, the festival is corrupting in exactly the way the theatre is; and if (3) is not doing the work, then (2) contains no principled objection to a theatre in Geneva at all, and the issue collapses back into d'Alembert's proposal.

(8) The reasoning also reaches beyond the festival. It would condemn the novel — including Rousseau's own, written to move readers to tears in private — and it would condemn private love and friendship, which direct sentiment at particulars as well. Nobody, and least of all Rousseau, accepts that.

(9) Therefore the Letter's argument is either self-refuting or it proves vastly more than it can want to prove.

I would like to keep (1), (2) and (4): the negative verdict on the theatre, the positive recommendation of festivals, and the claim that what matters about an amusement is the sentiment it habituates rather than its content all seem to me right. I cannot accept (9). My supervisor's suggestion is that the trouble is entirely at (3), and that the distinction the Letter needs but never states is between sentiments we consume and sentiments we contribute — the spectator pays to be moved, whereas the citizen brings the feeling with him and joins it to others'. I do not find this settles anything, for reasons I would rather you identified than I did.

Please tell me:

Which single step you would reject, and why that step looks sound enough to have carried me past it. What rejecting it costs me elsewhere in Rousseau — in particular, what happens to his treatment of the actor's profession, to his attack on comedy, and to the case for censorship and for the general will. Whether my supervisor's "consumed versus contributed" distinction is the same point Rousseau is making under another description, or a different and better one. And, as a test of whatever diagnosis you offer: a national ceremony watched by millions on screens, with the same sentiments of belonging that a festival elicits, is on your account a festival or a theatre, and why.

Assume I know the Letter and do not summarise it. Answer in British English, in no more than 900 words, and name the step you reject in your first sentence.
