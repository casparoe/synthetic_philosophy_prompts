Two friends of mine have been going at it for about a year about who should get to have a say in a given political decision, and I volunteered — should of said no — to help them turn the argument into a written thing. One of them holds the all-affected-interests view: you have a claim to a say in a decision just in case, and to the degree that, the decision affects your interests. The other holds the all-subjected view: what matters is that you are subject to the coercive power of the body making the decision. They have agreed in principle to an adversarial collaboration and I am meant to produce the draft they can both react to. Will you write it?

What I need in it:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section — the things both of them could actually sign. I think at least some of these belong there, but you decide and add your own: that a boundary principle is supposed to tell us who has a claim to a say, not who gets to rule; that both views agree on the easy cases, like long-term legal residents and small children represented by trustees, and only really come apart at the margins; that any such principle seems to face a circularity, because you need some prior account of which decisions are this bodys to make and which are collective at all; that neither principle by itself fixes what level of government should decide a given thing; and that stakes come in degrees while having a vote is binary. Where a claimed agreement is only apparent, say so and say why.

2. A disagreements section, as a table, where every disagreement is stated twice — once in wording the affected-side would accept for its own view, once in wording the subjected-side would accept for its own — with the source side labelled. If you think a row is really just a verbal dispute or a difference of emphasis dressed up as a substantive one, flag it as such rather than pretending it is deep.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. My guesses about what belongs here: whether coercion or stakes is the right currency in the first place; whether the relevant stakes are stakes in this particular decision or in the whole package of things the polity does over time; whether a very slight degree of affectedness generates any real claim, or whether the affected view quietly collapses into everyone belongs to every demos, and if so whether that is a reductio or a fine result; whether these principles are about legitimacy, so that a demos that excludes wrongly simply lacks authority, or only about justice in how the lines get drawn; and questions of timing, like people who show up after the vote, children born later, and people who can exit only at ruinous cost. Cut or add freely.

4. For each crux, a case both of them would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance of the answer, like a prediction register. Please use concrete cases and not the textbook ones, because they have both read all of those and just trade intuitions about them. Here is what I have in mind, roughly, but fix them so they actually isolate something:

- A city of about 380,000 puts a combined transit and zoning package on the ballot every two years, voted by registered city residents. Roughly 70,000 people commute in each day from four unincorporated towns; their commute and costs swing with what the city decides, and they pay a city parking levy that city inspectors enforce against their cars. The city's stormwater rules also determine whether a township 40 km downstream floods, and those residents never enter the city at all.
- A county of 90,000 votes to host a hazardous waste repository with a 150 year design life. Nearby counties, a town across a nearby border, and people not yet born all have big stakes; nobody outside the county is subject to the county's coercion.
- A large trade bloc sets a product safety and data standard for its internal market, and because of the market's size it becomes the de facto global standard, so a factory worker in a country outside the bloc has her working conditions set by a rule that in no sense applies to her.
- A tourist on a nine day visit is fully subject to local law, including fines and arrest, and is barely affected by anything the city decides; separately, someone held in pre trial detention for eighteen months and then released without charge.
- A homeowner in a one industry town whose house would sell for less than it would cost to move. She can leave, but only at ruinous cost, and she says therefore she cannot.
- The same question, say water abstraction, could be decided by a city, a river basin authority, a state, or a treaty body, and everyone agrees the answer matters more than who counts as a member. Also: if membership follows affectedness, then someone with thin stakes in lots of places belongs to every demos at once, and I want to know whether that is a problem or just how it goes.

One more thing I keep wondering about and want you to take a position on: maybe the disagreement is not really about inclusion at all but about weight. If you let the size of someone's stake set their voting weight instead of setting the boundary, does either principle secretly push you toward that, and does the fight dissolve or just move? I have been calling it the graduated demos fallback.

End with a short section on what would actually move this debate, where each side writes down what would count as evidence against its own view, and tell me straight if you think part of the year-long argument was about words. For style: my friends are not writing a paper, one of them keeps saying the other's view is just a sticker for whoever has power, and the other keeps saying the affected view is unworkable because affectedness is infinite — feel free to say when one of them is being unfair. Format it however makes it usable: numbered agreed claims, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes with their cases and predictions.
