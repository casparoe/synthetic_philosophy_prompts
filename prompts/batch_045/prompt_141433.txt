In my LessWrong-ish reading group we keep circling back to Carroll's tortoise, and one member has a move he thinks ends the discussion: rules of inference are just like the rules of a game, chess say. Modus ponens isn't the kind of thing that can be justified or unjustified, it's constitutive of the arguing game, so asking why you should accept it is on a par with asking whether it's legal to play chess by the rules of chess. On that picture Achilles' blunder was treating Z as one more premise to be handed over, when really it's a move he's already licensed to make — and the upshot he draws is that the whole justification-of-deduction literature is chasing a pseudo-problem and we should drop it.

I'm not sold, and I'd rather the analogy got audited than just accepted. So: lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the pieces, the board, a legal move, a player, winning, cheating, the rulebook. Then the places it breaks, and please separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the use he's making of it from the ones that are just decoration.

Two worries of my own, which you can take or leave. First, you can write the rules of chess down as premises too: a stubborn chess player can grant that the rules permit the bishop move, and that they permit it, and still sit there without moving the bishop — and I'd of thought the constitutive or conventional status of a rule is exactly the thing that doesn't explain why stating it fails to help a creature that won't apply it. Second, chess rules look like arbitrary conventions we can step outside of and evaluate from somewhere else (is this a good game, does it serve our purposes), and I can't tell whether there's any equivalent of stepping outside here, or what it would even look like if there were. Relatedly, compliance with a chess rule seems checkable without interpreting anything, whereas whether an inference instantiates a rule looks like it depends on what the rule means — say whether that's a real disanalogy or something the chess side has too.

Then the part I actually care about: does the no-pseudo-problem conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the game analogy quietly carrying it the whole time? If it doesn't survive in full, what's the strongest nearby claim the analogy does support, and is there a better thing to compare rules of inference to?
