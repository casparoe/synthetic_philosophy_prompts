I'm finishing a PhD chapter on the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory, and I've reached the point where the standard example — the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion as evidence for general relativity — strikes me as doing far less work than the literature assumes it does. Every treatment trots it out for a paragraph and then moves on to formalism. What I want instead is a real mapping of the episode through the main responses to the problem, and then an honest account of what the episode does to those responses.

Take the following as given, since I don't want the answer to hinge on anything you can't check: Le Verrier flagged the discrepancy in 1859, at roughly 38 arcseconds per century in his re-analysis of Mercury transits, later revised to something like 43 by Newcomb in 1882; it remained the outstanding quantitative failure of Newtonian celestial mechanics for half a century, and the leading response was to postulate unseen matter, above all the intra-Mercurial planet Vulcan, until photographic eclipse searches in the early 1900s effectively closed off that avenue; Einstein's field equations of November 1915 reproduced the anomalous value, and the 1919 eclipse expeditions to Príncipe and Sobral measured light bending at roughly the relativistic figure rather than the half-value a Newtonian treatment yields, one of the two Sobral instruments giving a discordant result that was set aside on grounds of systematic error. (One further wrinkle I am not certain of, so flag it rather than lean on it if I have it wrong: I believe the earlier Entwurf theory Einstein developed with Grossmann had failed to reproduce the anomaly, commonly given as somewhere around 18 arcseconds per century.)

The mapping I want is this. For each of the main strategies say what the evidence E is, what the hypothesis H is, whose credences are being modelled — Einstein in November 1915, the astronomical community in 1919, or a physicist appraising the theory now — and what, on that view, the confirmation consisted in: the counterfactual-credence or ur-prior move, of the sort usually associated with Hosiasson-Lindenbaum and pressed hard by Earman; the Garber-style move on which what one learns is not the datum but the entailment claim, plus the logical-omniscience objection to it; Glymour's bootstrapping treatment; and, as a contrast class, the non-Bayesian options — the use-novelty or novel-prediction requirement of Zahar and Worrall, and something like Mayo's severity. Then turn it round and test them. Three complications seem to me to matter and to be under-discussed. First, E is not a raw datum but a residual computed under auxiliaries — planetary masses, solar oblateness, perturbation theory — so the entailment relations these solutions rely on are approximate and auxiliaries-laden rather than clean. Second, the datum had already been spent as evidence for a rival that was subsequently abandoned: is oldness a property of the datum, or of its dialectical history, and does evidence get recharged when the theory it was supporting dies? Third, Einstein was constructing his theory partly under the constraint of reproducing the anomaly, so the fit was in some sense engineered; but if the earlier Entwurf theory failed the same test, the anomaly also discriminated between live candidates of his own, which is not what an engineered fit usually does. And then there is the comparison that interests me most: the perihelion result was old but clean and sharply discriminating, while the eclipse result was novel but messy. My sense is that the perihelion did more actual confirmatory work among physicists than the eclipse did, which sits badly with views that make novelty of prediction the mark of genuine evidence — but I can't tell whether that intuition indicts those views or just reflects how the story got told.

Please take a definite position: tell me which strategy or combination best handles the episode, and give me your reasons rather than a survey of equally weighted options. Where you think the literature settles a question, say so; where you are going beyond it, mark that too. If any of my historical claims are wrong, say which and say whether it changes the analysis. Keep the whole thing to 700 words or fewer, and write in British English.
