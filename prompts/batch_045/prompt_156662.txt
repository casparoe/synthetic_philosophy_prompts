I'm in a second-year undergraduate seminar on Plato, and this week we're working through Republic VI and VII — the sun, the divided line, and the cave. I'd like a joke I can read out at the end of my tutorial presentation: under about 150 words, set-up and punchline, short enough to say aloud, and aimed at people who have actually done the reading rather than at a general audience.

Here is the constraint that makes it hard, and the bit I actually care about. I don't want the standard cave gag — that the shadows aren't real, that philosophers are unemployable, that the prisoners are like people on their phones. I want the joke built on a specific asymmetry in Plato's epistemology that we went over in seminar: getting out of the cave and getting all the way up are not the same achievement, because the ascent mapped by the divided line has more than one stage. Below dialectic there is the condition Plato assigns to mathematicians, who work from starting-points they cannot yet justify and lean on visible diagrams, and whom he describes as dreaming about being rather than awake to it. So the figure I want at the centre of the joke is someone who has genuinely been released, has genuinely stopped taking shadows for the real thing, and is now outside in the daylight doing geometry on what he can see — investigating the sun by way of a drawn figure and an undefended hypothesis — and who therefore still cannot give an account of the Form of the Good even though he is standing right in front of the light. Some of the comedy should come from his confidence, and from the return: when he goes back down, the apparatus he has acquired leaves him worse at reading shadows than he was before, worse than everyone around him at the one task they are all good at, and for a reason that isn't stupidity on anybody's part.

Two things to steer clear of: no punning on the word form (printed form, being in good form, and so on), and no punchline that only amounts to the observation that academics are useless. The laugh has to come out of the structural point, not out of a general nerd joke wearing a toga.

Give me the joke first, then one section titled What the joke is banking on, roughly 150 to 200 words, in which you name the feature of the divided line and of the cave story that the punchline depends on, and flag honestly any place where you had to bend Plato to make the joke work.
