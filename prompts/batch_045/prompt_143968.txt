I'm a PhD student in intellectual history finishing a dissertation on how political communities commemorate military defeat, and I keep running into a configuration in my sources that I don't have a word for. I need one, because I'm building a typology for a chapter and I can't keep proceeding by listing instances.

The pattern: later generations use a defeat to do two things at once that seem to pull in opposite directions. First, the loss is treated as evidence of the losers' devotion. They fought on, they suffered, they were beaten, and the costliness of that is part of what licenses the honoring — a cause that had won cheaply would not do the same commemorative work. Second, the very same loss is used to detach the commemorators from the content of what was fought for. Because the side lost, the cause was never implemented, so it can be inherited as pure commitment — willingness to die for X — without anyone having to say what X would have looked like had it won, or whom it would have cost.

Three cases I keep circling. The Confederate material: an idiom that praises the dead for having fought and lost, paired with the claim that what they fought for was not really what the war was about — so that honoring them commits the speaker to nothing about what a Confederate victory would have meant for the people it held in slavery. The Kosovo cycle: a ruler's death on the field remembered as a deliberate preference for a heavenly over an earthly kingdom, devotion certified by death, with the defeat's actual political aftermath left almost entirely out of what later generations take from it. Masada: a collective suicide at the end of a siege turned into an emblem of national resolve, where what is being emulated seems to be not the fighters' cause but the bare fact that they had one.

And then Dunkirk, which I suspect is a boundary case rather than an instance: there the objective is restated after the fact so that the outcome counts as an achievement, and the devotion half seems to drop out. I want to know whether that is a different genus or simply my second condition failing.

What I've tried: "moral victory" names only the first half. Cognitive dissonance and sour-grape reasoning are too generic — they don't explain why the loss is monumentalized rather than quietly dropped, and they predict that the goal will be downgraded, whereas here the goal is often upgraded in the very act of being abandoned. The familiar line about history being written by the victors cuts the other way, since these are all cases of the defeated holding the pen. I've been writing "the martyr's alibi" in my notes, which I dislike because it prejudges that the detachment is the point.

So: is this one phenomenon or two that merely travel together? Does it have an established name in the memory-studies or political-theory literature, and if the fit with whatever is established is imperfect, I want the carving rather than the label — which nearby concepts are closest, how my description differs from each, and which fits best. If nothing fits, coin a term, define it, give me a diagnostic test I can apply to a speech or a monument, and give me a near-miss case that fails the test. In particular I need to know which conditions are doing the work: must the losers actually have suffered; must the cause be one the commemorators would now disavow; does it survive in lost battles inside wars that were ultimately won, or in non-national cases like a dissolved institution or an abandoned scientific program.
