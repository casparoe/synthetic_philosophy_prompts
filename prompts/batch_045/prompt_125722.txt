Our reading group has been grinding through Wittgenstein's On Certainty for about two months, and the pair of us who keep talking over eachother are me — I think the remarks about propositions that are exempt from doubt, that doubts and questions turn on, are pointing at something genuinely special: framework commitments that aren't beliefs in the ordinary sense, aren't known, and can't be rationally assessed at all, because they're what makes assessment possible — and Priya, who thinks he's just describing extremely well-entrenched ordinary empirical propositions that we do know, on the basis of perception, testimony and inference to the best explanation, and that are revisable in principle like anything else. We've agreed to stop going round in circles and do this as a proper adversarial collaboration, and I've volunteered to draft the joint document, so please write it for me.

What I want is, first, a numbered section of claims we can both actually sign, and I mean ones that aren't vacuous: that there's a distinguishable class of propositions with a distinctive functional role (not normally doubted, not normally supported by evidence, and such that doubting one tends to wreck other propositions' evidential relations rather than merely adding a false belief); that the regress of reasons does in practice bottom out somewhere; that propositions migrate in and out of this status over time — we've started calling this hinge-drift, and our stock examples are things like the claim that smoking causes lung cancer or that the earth is immensely old, which look like they began as contested hypotheses and ended up as assumptions that awkward data has to be explained away rather than as things the data could refute; that this migration is at least prima facie awkward for both of us and neither of us gets to just claim it; and that Moore's hand-raising gesture is baffling rather than a proof. Add or drop as you think fit, and tell me if any of mine are secretly slanted. Then a disagreements table with columns for: the point at issue; the claim in wording Priya would accept as hers; the claim in wording I'd accept as mine; the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of us; one concrete case, argument or piece of evidence both of us would agree in advance bears on that crux; and each of our predictions about what it'll show, written down before the fact. Candidate cruxes I have in mind: whether the class is picked out by a role a proposition plays in a given inquiry or by what kind of proposition it is; whether the regress stopping is a principled stopping point or a practical one; whether doubt about a hinge is unintelligible or merely fantastically implausible; whether hinges get installed by training or by testimony; and whether arithmetic and logic belong to the same phenomenon at all.

For at least two cruxes please use cases we invented rather than the usual ones. First, the interview study: you ask a few hundred people to justify the propositions that they have hands, that the earth existed long before they were born, and that nobody has walked on Mars, then ask what would ever get them to give each one up; you code whether they cite evidence, cite something that already presupposes the target, or say the question doesn't make sense. What would each of us predict, and would we both accept in advance that the results bear on the crux? Second, the valley case: an isolated community for whom the claim that the river has always run east past the meeting house plays the hinge role in how they describe the landscape, plan planting and tell stories; then a landslide reroutes it, and for months their own observation reports are unintelligible to them. Is what eventually happens rational belief-revision in the face of evidence, a shift in practice amounting to something like conversion, or something neither of us has a category for — and again, both predictions up front. I also suspect a decent chunk of our fight is verbal, over belief, know, arational and evidence: say which bits you think are and precisely why, and don't smooth over the bits that aren't. Finish with a short section where each side writes down what it would count as evidence against itself. Keep it to a page of dense prose plus the table if you can — its going to be read out loud at the final session, and I'd much rather it was something both of us could put our names to than a tidy summary that quietly assumes one side's framework. No citations or reading list please, we've got the primary text and nobody's looking anything up.
