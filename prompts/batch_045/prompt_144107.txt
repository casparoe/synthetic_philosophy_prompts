The question (20 marks; 6 for (a), 7 for (b), 7 for (c)):

(a) Set out the argument from illusion as a valid argument, in numbered premises, in its strongest form. Identify which premise carries the argument's weight, and say why a defender would regard that premise as the strongest link rather than the weakest.

(b) State the metaphysical disjunctivist's objection to that argument. Say exactly what the disjunctivist denies, and say what the disjunctivist can concede without giving the view up. Be careful to state whether disjunctivism, as a thesis about the nature of perceptual experience, by itself delivers anything about knowledge or about the reasons a subject has for her beliefs.

(c) The Replay Room. S is shown a red apple for two seconds. Unbeknownst to S, a device records the entire proximal neural sequence that the apple's action produced in S's visual system during the first second, and then, during the second second, with the apple removed and the room dark, induces in S that very sequence. Stipulate: (i) S's experience during the second second presents itself as present-tense perception of the apple, with no felt memory, no sense of familiarity and no sense of a gap, and is introspectively indistinguishable from her experience during the first second; (ii) S's total neural state during the second second is exactly what it would have been had the apple remained; (iii) the apple is nonetheless genuinely part of the causal explanation of the second experience, via the recording. Is S's experience during the second second a perception, a hallucination, or neither? Does the case raise a difficulty for the disjunctivist that the ordinary hallucination case does not, or is it simply a hallucination with an unusual causal history? Explain.

The answer (candidate 0417):

(a) P1. In illusion and hallucination we are aware of something. P2. What we are aware of in such cases cannot be a mind-independent material object: there is no bent stick, no pink wall, no rat where the drinker sees one. P3. So in the bad cases we are aware of a mind-dependent item, a sense-datum. P4. A veridical perception can be subjectively exactly like a hallucination: from the inside the subject cannot tell which is which. P5. If two experiences are exactly alike from the inside, they are experiences of the same kind, and experiences of the same kind have the same kind of object. C1. So in veridical perception too we are aware of a sense-datum. C2. So we are never directly aware of mind-independent objects; the material world, if there is one, is known only by inference from sense-data. The premise carrying the weight is P4, the indistinguishability premise: it is what lets the argument generalise from the bad cases to the good ones, and it is the strongest link because it rests on the first-person point that no amount of attention or reflection reveals a difference between seeing the stick and hallucinating it.

(b) The disjunctivist denies P5. She holds that perception and hallucination are not two species of a single genus; rather, in the good case the mind-independent object itself is a constituent of the experience — the experience is a relation in which the object figures — whereas in the bad case there is only a sensory or hallucinatory state with no object. Since the two are not states of the same kind at all, the generalisation in P5 fails and the argument never reaches C1. Moreover, because her whole point is that the good case is partly constituted by the object, the disjunctivist must deny P4 as well: if the object is literally a constituent of the experience in the good case and absent in the bad one, the two experiences cannot be exactly alike from the inside, and she is committed to saying that the subject can at least in principle notice which case she is in. This is also what makes the view epistemologically attractive. The reason for which the subject believes that there is an apple — her experience of the apple — is a factive reason, one that logically entails that the apple is there, and so that very reason is unavailable in the bad case, where she has at best a justified true belief. The disjunctivist thereby blocks the sceptical conclusion in C2 without having to maintain that hallucination is impossible.

(c) The Replay Room is simply a hallucination, and it adds nothing. During the second second the apple is not there, so the experience has no object and therefore belongs to the bad disjunct. Whether the object once caused a recording of the subject's neural activity is irrelevant to whether she is now perceiving: causal history is not constitution, and the disjunctivist's claim was never that any old causal connection to an object makes for perception. The case is a hallucination with an unusual causal history and raises exactly the difficulty that hallucination always raises, no more.
