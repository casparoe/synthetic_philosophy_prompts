I run a monthly methods club at the health economics consultancy where I work: about a dozen analysts, most with an MPH or a health-economics master's, all of them fluent in QALYs, Markov models, cost-effectiveness acceptability curves and probabilistic sensitivity analysis, but none of them with any formal social choice theory. Next month we are doing a session on equity weighting, and I have one fifty-minute slot this week to get Harsanyi's aggregation theorem in front of them first. I don't need the result explained to me; I need a plan for explaining it to them.

The part I most want help with is the sequence. My draft is: define von Neumann–Morgenstern utility, state the Pareto condition, state the conclusion that social welfare is a weighted sum of individual expected utilities, and then say that this is why utilitarian cost-benefit analysis has a claim on us. I'm fairly confident that last move is where I go wrong, but I can't say exactly how: I suspect the weights are doing more work than I will be admitting, and that using the word utilitarian in front of this group will have them nodding at something they would reject if they saw the premises written out. There are also two things I'd rather the plan be honest about than paper over. I have seen the theorem stated with only unanimity-of-indifference as the Pareto premise and also with full unanimous preference, and I can't tell whether the stronger premise is what rules out negative weights or whether it does some other work entirely. And I keep encountering what looks like a second Harsanyi argument built around an impartial observer who imagines an equal chance of turning out to be anyone; I don't know whether that is the same result in different packaging or a genuinely different argument with different inputs, and if it is the latter I'd like to flag the difference in one sentence rather than let the group conflate them.

So please give me the order the components have to go in, with what depends on what. My own instinct is that the distinction between ranking sure social states and ranking lotteries over social states has to be in place before the theorem is even stated, or the whole ex ante versus ex post business will be invisible to them, but tell me where it belongs and what breaks if it arrives later. Then the carrying example: I want one case to do the work of the whole session, ideally in our idiom, say two candidates for a single remaining dose or a single program slot, and I want you to say what such a case must contain, whether that means a coin-flip option, or asymmetric stakes, or enough structure that we can actually compute the sums by hand at the whiteboard, and also what it must not contain, because my worry is that if the stakes are monetary my analysts will silently read utility as willingness to pay, and if the number or the identity of the people varies across options we will be arguing about population ethics by minute ten and never get back. On misconceptions, here is my list and I want yours: that the theorem vindicates the cost-effectiveness analyses they already run; that QALYs are the utility functions being summed, so that adding them across patients is licensed by it; and that summing implies equal concern for everyone. One colleague who has read a little Sen will at some point announce that utilitarianism is indifferent to distribution, and I want that to land as something the group can watch fall out of the result rather than as a slogan, so tell me where in the sequence to place it. Relatedly, I have been told that the theorem cannot even represent a strict social preference for flipping a fair coin over handing the good to one identified person, and that this is at the heart of the standard objection to it; I cannot reconstruct the argument and would need it in a form I can put on a whiteboard in two minutes. Finally, give me one question to put to them at the end that will show me whether the idea has actually landed rather than merely been endured, and an explicit list of what I should refuse to go into at this level and say out loud that I am refusing; I assume the proof itself is on that list, but tell me what else.
