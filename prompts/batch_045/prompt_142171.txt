In the science and philosophy of cultural evolution, the same argument has been running for roughly twenty-five years without, as far as I can tell, anyone changing sides. On one side there is the meme-style picture: culture is a second hereditary system, cultural items spread by being copied, and cumulative selection over heritable variation is what explains the design-like fit between those items and the environments — including the psychological environments — they inhabit. On the other side there is the cultural-attraction, or reconstructive-transmission, picture: transmission is reconstruction rather than copying, the same variants keep reappearing because shared features of human minds, bodies and ecologies bend each act of reconstruction back toward the same region of possibility-space, and so there is no second replicator and cultural selection is at best a loose metaphor for a sorting process with no cumulative reach of its own.

What puzzles me is that the two camps now routinely cite the same data — near-identical motifs recurring in populations with no contact, the drift and rationalization observed in serial-reproduction chains, the near-perfect fidelity of digital copying — and read them in opposite directions.

My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that the disagreement is not really about population-level facts, because the two mechanisms are close to indistinguishable at that level: in a simple toy model, lossy transmission plus a shared attractor settles the population into the same equilibrium distribution as faithful copying plus selection among variants, and the two differ only in what happens under intervention — perturb the hosts' psychology and the attractor view moves, perturb the copied content and the selection view moves. If that is right, the live issue is which causal structure we are licensed to cite, and the observations the field keeps producing are simply not of the discriminating kind. But I can also see a reading on which this is a verbal dispute: fidelity in particular seems to mean both token-to-token copying accuracy and stability of the population-level distribution, and I suspect much of the cross-talk rides on that.

Give me a diagnosis with reasons. Is this verbal, substantive, two questions under one heading, or an empirical question stalled because the available data cannot discriminate the hypotheses? Where exactly does my indistinguishability claim fail, if it does? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a measurement, a perturbation result, a formal result about identifiability, a distinction we should adopt, or an abandonment of the original question? Finally: is there a version of the memeticist's claim that no possible finding could overturn, and if so is that fatal to it, or is it a sign that the claim is doing methodological rather than empirical work?
