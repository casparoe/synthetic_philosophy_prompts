I should say at the outset that I have no formal training in philosophy. What got me onto this question is a habit I have kept for about eight years: every evening I write a single number in a notebook for how good the day was, on a scale from -5 to +5. The number is easy to write on some days and nearly meaningless on others. A day of steady, uneventful physical comfort and a day containing one absorbing, exhilarating stretch in the middle do not feel as though they are being measured by the same instrument, and yet the notebook insists on one scale. I am trying to work out whether that discomfort is my own imprecision or a real feature of what I am trying to measure.

The view I am worried about is the one that says how well a stretch of life goes is entirely a matter of how much pleasure it contains, net of pain. That view needs there to be such a thing as how much pleasure a given experience contains, and it needs any two experiences to be comparable in that respect. The worry I keep circling is that pleasures do not seem to share a felt quality in common: the pleasure of a cold drink when you are very thirsty, of a punchline landing, of a knot in your shoulders releasing, of being deep in work and losing track of time — those do not feel like one stuff in different amounts. So perhaps the question which of these contains more pleasure has no answer.

What I would like from you is one carefully built example, not a survey of positions. Specifically:

Give me a pair of ordinary experiences, both of which I would grant are pleasures without argument, and both of which I can check against my own memory, such that the natural ways of settling which contains more pleasure disagree with each other. I have in mind four such ways: the re-run test (if you could have only one of them again, each at its own natural length, which would you choose — and note whether your answer changes if both are stretched or compressed to the same duration); the right-now test (which would you rather be in the middle of at this moment, setting aside what it leads to); the keep-going test (which carries the stronger felt pull not to stop); and the trade test (if each had to be bought with hours of mild tedium, which commands the higher price). Please construct the case so that at least two of these rank the two experiences oppositely, and describe the mechanism in the experience that makes them come apart, so that I can see why the result would generalize to similar pairs rather than being a trick of that one example.

I would like the case to avoid the easy explanations. It should not be a plain trade-off between intensity and duration. It should not be a case where some value other than pleasure — health, achievement, morality, what other people think — is quietly doing the work. It should not be the kind of case where we simply do not know the answer but a better instrument would tell us. And it should not depend on the person having changed between the two experiences; ideally they are ones a single person could have on the same day.

My own hunch is that something like the difference between a pleasure you are absorbed in and a pleasure that is really a relief or a background comfort is doing the work, but I do not trust this hunch, and I would rather have an example that stands on its own than one rigged to fit my diagnosis. If the real explanation is different, say so. If you think I have been conflating two separate questions, separate them and then answer both.

After the example, I would like your analysis of what it shows. Is the failure of comparison in your case best described as a fact we cannot get at, as no fact at all, or as one phrase standing for several legitimate but distinct quantities — and what would someone defending each of those descriptions say about your example? What further difference in the case, or what kind of further information, would move the matter, and in which direction? And what does this do to the larger claim that how well a life goes is a matter of pleasure: does your example show that comparisons of welfare are themselves indeterminate, or only that one way of measuring them is, and does whatever is true of two experiences carry over to whole days and lives? I am not asking for names, dates or references — I cannot chase them up, and they would not help me. I would rather have one example worked through over a few hundred words of setup and a few hundred of analysis than a broad treatment of several.
