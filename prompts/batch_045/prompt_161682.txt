I teach a six-week module on rational choice for second-year PPE undergraduates — sixteen students, philosophers and economists in roughly equal measure, one ninety-minute session a week — and I want to give next week's session to the Allais paradox. I do not want to repeat what I did last year, which was to put the two standard pairs of gambles on the board, take a show of hands, point out that the modal combination (the safe option in the first problem, the risky one in the second) is inconsistent with expected utility, and move on. That landed as a card trick, and the students wrote it off as other people's innumeracy. So I would like a properly designed activity — a game with rules and tickets, or a staged choice with real (small) stakes — in which they run into the conflict themselves before anyone in the room says the words independence axiom or expected utility, and in which the conflict costs them something, even if only in pride or in their chance of winning the prize I can actually afford. The department will not reimburse cash, but I can spend the price of a couple of pizzas on chocolate, cafeteria vouchers and one decent hardback, and I am happy to run the whole thing for points that convert into entries in a single raffle. What they should leave with is not the moral that people are irrational, but the felt pull of both sides at once: that the pair of choices they want to make is genuinely attractive to them while also being, on a description they have already accepted, incoherent.

I had a long argument about this on Sunday with two friends, and I want the design to answer them rather than to have me adjudicate in front of the room. The first, an economics PhD student, says the standard result is nothing but a certainty effect: degrade the certain outcome to a ninety-nine per cent chance of that same outcome, rescale the other probabilities proportionally, and the effect allegedly disappears — so what the paradox shows is that the word 'certain' does something psychological to people, not that any axiom is in trouble. The second, a philosopher, says the paradox only bites if you also grant that compound lotteries may be collapsed into simple ones: if the shared branch of the options is resolved in a first stage and the choice only matters in the remaining cases, she says, the offending pair can be redescribed so that nothing violates independence, and any activity that presents the options as flat lists of outcomes-and-probabilities is simply helping itself to that assumption. So I want the activity structured so that both escape routes show up in what the students do rather than in what I say afterwards — a near-certainty variant run alongside the original, and a staging of the randomisation in which the first draw is performed and seen, and the second draw happens only in the surviving branch, so that the compound-lottery exit is either closed or made vividly costly to take. If you think one of those two cannot be done in ninety minutes with sixteen people, tell me which to drop and why.

There is also a market stage I half remember from a seminar years ago: after everyone has committed to a ticket, open a few minutes of trading — students buy and sell each other's tickets for points, or I act as banker and offer to buy particular ones back — on the theory that an inconsistent pair of preferences can be converted into a certain loss. My philosopher friend says a genuine money pump requires the same person to face the choice repeatedly under a sequential resolution of uncertainty, and that a one-shot ticket market cannot deliver one; I cannot tell whether she is protecting the view or stating a fact. Please tell me honestly whether that stage earns its fifteen minutes, and if it does, what it should look like and what I should be watching for in the trading.

Then give me the debrief: which moments in the room map onto which parts of the formal idea, including how I should handle — or whether I should draw at all — the distinction my friend insists is real between independence as it appears in the von Neumann–Morgenstern setting and Savage's sure-thing principle, which I am not confident I can explain to nineteen-year-olds without confusing them. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: the two or three students who have heard of the paradox and pick the 'rational' pair out of spite, the ones who say they don't care about chocolate, the ones who ask why we are using play money when real stakes would settle it, the ones who treat the whole thing as a puzzle about which answer I want. No reading list and no citations — I am not asking you to source anything, and I know you cannot look things up. Do tell me how confident you are in the claims you make, in particular the empirical ones about how large the effect is under real but small stakes and whether it survives the near-certainty variant, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
