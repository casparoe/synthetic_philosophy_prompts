You are an analytic political philosopher with a strong taste for conceptual diagnosis and for the sociology of knowledge: the kind of person who asks, before answering a question, what work the question's central term is doing. Please think carefully and begin with an open-ended brainstorming phase — list, before you commit to anything, at least four or five candidate accounts of why the dispute I describe below refuses to move, including candidates I have not proposed, and only then rank them, defend one, and set out its consequences. Give me a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of positions, and no bibliography, reading list, or citations — I want your own argument.

I sit on a small working group — two political theorists, a historian of ideas, and a comparative sociologist — hired by a European foundation to produce a short position paper on what, if anything, is meant by "Western values." We are six meetings in and have stalled completely, and what frustrates me is that the stall does not look like a factual gap. My colleagues on the "realist" side hold that the phrase names a genuine cultural-historical kind: a co-instantiated bundle of commitments — something like the priority of individual conscience, negative liberty, the rule of law, toleration, and a standing fallibilism about authority — with a traceable genealogy and a real causal role in why certain institutional arrangements took hold where they did. My colleagues on the "deflationist" side think the phrase is a polemical boundary-marker: its content is filled in by whatever adversary is currently in view, its genealogy is retrofitted, and its extension slides opportunistically (Catholic Poland, Orthodox Greece, secular Turkey, Japan, post-colonial Latin America) with the geopolitical needs of whoever is speaking. What makes this feel like a pseudo-dispute is that when we actually write down a list of values, both sides endorse almost the same items; the realists concede that every item on the list can be found outside Europe, and the deflationists concede that no other region instantiates the whole bundle in quite the same configuration. So the fight is not over content but over two conditions I have started calling the "bundle condition" (is the co-instantiation of these items, rather than the items themselves, what does the identifying work?) and the "ancestry condition" versus the "residence condition" (does a value belong to the West in virtue of the historical lineage that produced it, or in virtue of being currently professed and instituted there?), and these two conditions famously come apart in the cases above.

I have three hypotheses and I want you to test them against each other rather than accept them. First: this is really a difference about direction of fit — the realists are treating "Western values" as what I'd call a microscope concept, whose job is to track a real kind and whose vagueness is therefore a defect, while the deflationists are treating it as a flag concept, whose job is to assemble and rally a coalition and whose vagueness is therefore a feature; neither side's arguments even address the other's criterion of adequacy. Second: there is a hidden empirical question doing the work — whether these commitments really do cluster in cross-national survey data, and whether the boundary of the cluster falls where speakers want it to — but the choice of which items to include and how to weight them is itself partly a moral and political choice, so the empirical question quietly dissolves back into a normative one and no dataset can end the argument. Third: it is a bedrock disagreement about the point of political concepts at all — whether they are to be assessed as instruments of explanation or as instruments of democratic contestation — and bedrock disagreements are not arguable because each side's standard of assessment is what is in dispute. My own suspicion is that the first and third are the same hypothesis at different depths, and that the second is a decoy.

So: which is it, or what is it instead? Tell me whether this is a verbal disagreement, a substantive one, two groups answering different questions under a single heading, a disguised empirical question, or a sign that the question itself is ill-posed rather than merely hard. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me concretely what would count as progress: a distinction we should adopt, a change of question, a methodological move, an empirical result that would actually bite — or, if you think no progress should be expected, argue for that and tell me what the honest thing for our working group to do is.
