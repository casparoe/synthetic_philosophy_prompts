I teach a first-year seminar on collective decision making — 19 students, one 80-minute slot, and a room with moveable chairs, index cards, a whiteboard and nothing else (no projector I trust, no laptops, no phones if I can help it).

I want a game that makes them *feel* the Condorcet jury result instead of being told it. The thing I fail at every year is this: they leave thinking the theorem says "more people = smarter." What I actually want them to feel is that the punch comes from the votes being independent *conditional on the state of the world* and from each one being better than a coin toss — and that headcount is doing much less work than they think, and can even hurt.

So the mechanics have to be arranged so they run into it. Here's roughly what I have in mind; please fix it wherever it's broken.

- A repeated secret-ballot round game: each student gets a private clue card about which of two states is actual (guilty/innocent, left/right, whatever carries best), with a stated per-person reliability of about 0.6. Tell me exactly how to generate and shuffle the cards so that the reliability really is what I claim and I'm not fooling myself into a rigged demo.
- A mid-game twist where I quietly break independence. Options I've been turning over: a public "expert briefing" that a random subset of them also saw, a round where open deliberation is allowed before the vote, or reading out three students' votes before everyone else marks their card. Pick whichever breaks it most reliably and say why the other two are worse.
- A forced choice at some point between one big group of mediocre voters and a tiny group of near-experts — I was thinking 19 at 0.6 versus 3 at 0.9, but use whatever numbers make the gap land hardest. Their gut will say big group.

What I need from you:

1. The card/signal generation, and the per-round procedure. Rounds have to be fast — around 90 seconds each — so we get enough repetitions that the frequencies actually show up rather than one lucky majority. Tell me how to keep that pace and how many rounds I can realistically get through, and what the group should believe at the end of each block of rounds (i.e. the order the rounds come in).
2. A payoff scheme in points, no money. It should make them want the group to be right (common interest), while not rewarding people for just sounding confident or for copying their neighbour. If those two pull against each other, say so and tell me which to sacrifice.
3. Where the 80 minutes go, minute by minute.
4. A debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific assumptions — independence, competence above a half, and common interest — and that names the theorem only at the end. No lecture up front at all.
5. What will predictably go wrong. I can predict two myself: they'll whisper their clues to each other in the first block even when told not to, and somebody will try to meta-game the points. Give me the rest, and how to handle each.
6. An honest note on what the game can't show. I assume the asymptotics are unreachable with 19 people and one session, so tell me exactly what I have to supply myself at the end, and how little I can get away with saying while still being straight with them.

Two more things. First, I'd like a rough number for what majority accuracy I should actually expect in each block, so I don't misread a bad run as a failed design. Second, near the end I want them to be able to say, in their own words, why *adding* a voter can make the group worse — so tell me where in the sequence that has to be set up, and whether it's better staged with one voter who's reliably slightly wrong or with a whole block of correlated ones.
