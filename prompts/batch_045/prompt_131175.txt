So I'm putting together a handout for my third-year seminar on Madhyamaka, and I think I've finally worked out why the discussion keeps going round in circles: everyone in the room says "empty" and means a different thing by it. We finished the first chapter of the MMK last week (the bit where all four ways something could arise — from itself, from something else, from both, from neither — get set aside), plus the material where emptiness gets turned back on itself, plus that famous verse in which he says he has no thesis of his own. What I want from you is a decision tree that takes someone from that shared starting point to one of the readings, and I want it built out of the logical space rather than out of a list of school names: give me the questions first, lay out which combinations of answers are available, and only then drop the actual readings into the cells they occupy. And give each leaf one line on the most awkward thing it commits you to — that's what makes the handout useful, the students see the price before they pick.

Starting commitment, to fix it: everybody in the room already grants that Nāgārjuna isn't a plain nihilist who thinks nothing at all exists. Don't spend a branch on that.

Five or six branch questions at most, and each one has to be phrased so that a student who's done intro logic and one Indian philosophy module can answer it without already knowing which reading it leads to. Two distinctions I want doing most of the work, the first of them near the top: what the tetralemma is even about — the world, what can be coherently said or asserted, or what can be coherently conceived — and how the negation works, i.e. whether denying a corner cancels the predicate and leaves something in its place, or merely declines to endorse it without committing to anything in its place. Call those paryudāsa and prasajya in a margin note if you like, but the tree itself has to say it in plain English or it turns into a Sanskrit vocabulary test.

Cases I want built into the tree rather than buried: the regress question — is emptiness itself empty, and does that worry you — and the no-thesis question: if someone presses you on whether the claim that everything is empty is itself a claim with content, what do you say. Those two separate a lot of readings, so give them real branch nodes.

Leaves: eight to ten, and these all have to be in there somewhere — the reading on which he only ever draws consequences out of the opponent's own commitments and never asserts anything in his own voice; the reading on which emptiness can be established by a properly formed inference of his own and is a thesis in that sense; a dialetheist reading on which at least one opposed pair of corners is really both true; a reading on which the four corners are semantic values in a logic with gaps and gluts (true, false, both, neither — something along the lines of first-degree entailment) rather than four things somebody is asserting; a therapeutic reading on which the whole device is there to dissolve the question rather than answer it; and, if you think it's genuinely a separate cell, a reading on which emptiness is only a claim about our concepts and their failure to cut anywhere. Anything left over, mark unnamed. Don't label leaves with school names — describe each by what it commits you to, and if you want to gesture at the later doxographical labels do it in parentheses, since the Indian authors didn't sort themselves that way.
