I am writing a dissertation chapter in the philosophy of science on Hacking's looping effects, and specifically on the question of when a psychiatric category is epistemically compromised by the fact that it loops — when the feedback from classification to the classified should count against the category, and when it is merely the normal life of a human kind. My problem is that my own verdicts on cases do not line up with any distinction I can state cleanly, and the distinctions I try either over-generate or collapse. I would like to set the verdicts out and ask what principle, if any, they add up to, and which of them I should abandon.

One preliminary request: please treat the empirical shape of each case as stipulated. I am reasonably confident of them as description, but I do not want the argument to rest on the details being exactly right, and I am not asking for checks on the literature or for figures I could not verify.

The cases, with my verdicts:

1. Autism spectrum conditions. Recorded prevalence has risen steeply over roughly three decades; the standard explanation mixes broadened criteria, diagnostic substitution and better ascertainment; autistic self-advocates have changed how the condition is described in the manuals and in the clinic; and large numbers of adults now identify themselves as autistic on the basis of online questionnaires, sometimes without a clinical assessment. My verdict: the expansion was largely legitimate, the kind is better characterised now than it was, and the loop has been benign or good. Not discredited.

2. Paediatric bipolar disorder in the United States. A steep rise in diagnoses over roughly two decades, with chronic, non-episodic irritability read as mania, followed by heavy prescribing of mood stabilisers and antipsychotics to children; the manuals eventually responded by adding a separate category for severe, persistent irritability in youth, developed largely to recapture children who had been given bipolar labels without episodes of mania or hypomania. My verdict: bad looping; the category as then applied to children was substantially an artefact and should not have been used that way. Discredited as applied.

3. Dissociative identity disorder in the 1980s and 1990s. Small numbers of cases before, then a large increase, with presentations that clustered around clinicians who looked for them, and with the recovered-memory practices of the period in the background. My verdict: largely iatrogenic, discrediting.

4. The category of female sexual desire and arousal difficulties, as revised and promoted with substantial commercial involvement by parties with a pharmacological indication in view. My verdict: discrediting.

5. Adult ADHD. A large rise in adult diagnoses, stimulant marketing, diagnostic criteria that were written for children, and now a great deal of self-diagnosis through short-form video and online screening tools. My verdict: not discrediting. I think this is mostly recruitment of people whose impairments were real and previously unlabelled, plus some genuine worsening from an attention-demanding environment, and I am untroubled by the commercial involvement here in a way that I am not in case 4.

6. A stylised case, close to things reported during the pandemic but which I want to stipulate rather than assert. A video platform ranks content by engagement; clips of young women displaying sudden, complex, tic-like movements propagate; within months, clinics in several cities report adolescent girls presenting with abrupt-onset tic-like behaviours that differ in form from typical tic disorders, that are far more common in girls than the usual sex ratio would predict, and that often remit when the young person is removed from the platform. Nobody is diagnosed before the behaviour appears; no clinician applies a concept to these girls; the platform does not classify anyone, it ranks clips. My verdict: this is looping, and I want to say so — but I do not know whether to call it discrediting. The suffering is real, the mechanism is not the patients' awareness of being classified under a kind, and the classifier is not a person or an institution with a concept at all.

7. The replacement of gender identity disorder by gender dysphoria in the current DSM, a change driven substantially by the objections, arguments and self-understandings of trans people themselves, and by the aim of removing a pathological label from a way of being while retaining access to care. My verdict: looping-driven change, and an improvement; not discrediting.

8. Myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome: a long fight with clinicians, names and diagnostic criteria changed under patient pressure, decades of contested status. My verdict: an indifferent kind obstructed by bad nosology; the activist reshaping does not discredit it.

Two pairs are, I think, the ones doing the damage. Cases 2 and 4 on the one hand, and 1 and 5 on the other, all involve a rapid expansion of a contested category under pressure from interested parties, including industry; I condemn two and exonerate two. And cases 3 and 7 are both cases of a category being reshaped by the people classified under it, with the presentations of the classified shifting accordingly; I treat one as an artefact and the other as an improvement.

My own tentative principle, which I do not think survives, is a self-confirmation test: a category is discredited by looping when the classification is itself among the causes of its own criteria being met, and when no line of evidence independent of the criteria — heritability, mechanism, stability across settings and observers, or response to an intervention whose efficacy does not depend on the patient's knowing the category — supports the kind. This runs into at least three problems. It threatens to discredit case 7, where the loop runs through the classified people's own claims about what relieves their distress rather than through criteria that anyone can satisfy at will. It has nothing to say about case 6, where there is no concept being communicated and no one is classified, so that the awareness Hacking's account turns on is awareness of exemplars and of behavioural affordances rather than of a kind-term applied to oneself. And it is silent about the direction of the loop: some loops make the classified worse, some make them better, some make them different but no worse, and my verdicts track that in a way the test does not capture.

So what I want is this. First, a principle — or, if the honest answer is that no single principle will do, a small set of candidates with the trade-offs between them stated — precise enough to be applied, given clause by clause with the case that motivates each clause. I would like the distinction I have gestured at, between looping that runs through a kind-concept communicated by a classifier and looping that runs through exemplars, models or affordances without one, to be either made to carry normative weight or shown to be beside the point. Second, the verdicts of your principle on cases I have not listed: prolonged grief disorder, recently added to the main diagnostic manuals; self-diagnosed autism in adult women; misophonia, currently a proposed rather than an established category; and contested post-viral illness categories of the sort that appear after pandemics. Third, a plain statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot reproduce, and what it would cost me, theoretically, to give them up rather than complicate the principle. And fourth, whether the resulting account is a development of Hacking's machinery — the interactive and indifferent distinction, making up people, the looping of kinds and of persons — or a departure from it. If a design rule for diagnostic manuals falls out of all this, say what it is; if none does, say that too.
