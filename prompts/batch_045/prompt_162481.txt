Last night I had a three-hour argument with my friend (she does 19th-century France) about whether germs, climate and volcanoes can be historical actors, and I ended up committing myself to a position I now have to defend in public: next month I'm giving a department colloquium on it. My thesis, roughly: non-human causes should get the role of actor in historical explanation — not agent, they have no intentions — when they meet two conditions. First, leverage: small differences in the cause move the outcomes more than comparable differences in any named human decision in the record. Second, deliberative invisibility: the people living through it could not have taken it into account, even though its effects were already landing on them. The bit I keep pushing is that the second condition is what saves this from environmental determinism — the claim isn't that climate or a pathogen decided the outcome, it's that this is where human deliberation was running blind, so the counterfactual worth caring about isn't what if they'd chosen differently but something about the gap between what was happening and what could be thought at the time. My cases are Tambora and the year without a summer, the cholera pandemics, and the Little Ice Age.

The audience is a mid-sized history department: mostly social and cultural historians of modern Europe, several of whom are allergic to anything that smells like the big Diamond book; one historical-climate person who I think will say I'm reinventing her subfield badly; one Marxist who'll say everything is mediated through class and property; and a senior theorist who thinks actor is just a metaphor doing no work.
