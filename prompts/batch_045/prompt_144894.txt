I'm a PhD candidate in philosophy working on formal epistemology and the foundations of probability, and I've been asked to contribute a short satirical piece to our department's blog. I want it to be funny to someone who has actually thought about Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference, and still legible to a general reader, so I'd like you to write it as a parody of a specific documentary genre rather than as free-standing comedy.

Please write a Federal Register-style Notice of Proposed Rulemaking — docket number, "Background," numbered regulatory sections in the mandatory "shall" voice, a definitions section, at least one worked example, a compliance-cost subsection, and a closing "Request for Comments" — issued by a fictional body I'll call the Interagency Council for Uncertainty Quantification, proposing to require that federal agencies, whenever they lack empirical evidence about a quantity, assign it a probability distribution by the principle of indifference rather than declining to quantify. The target of the satire should be the practice, and the idea behind it: that "we have no information" is itself a determinate input to the probability calculus, and that a rulebook can make the resulting numbers non-arbitrary.

The jokes have to come from getting the material right. In particular:

- Build the centerpiece around the chord problem: a chord drawn at random in a circle, the probability that it exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and the three classical procedures — random endpoints on the circumference, random point along a radius with the chord drawn perpendicular to it, random midpoint in the disc — yielding 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 respectively. Say why they differ in a way a specialist would accept (the different weight each method gives to chords near or at diameters; the different measures over the space of chords), and note that the family of admissible answers is not just these three.
- Escalate the absurdity the way the real problem escalates: an advance-registration requirement for one's choice of variable; an "Approved Parametrization Registry" with a safe-harbor entry; a consulting industry that helps applicants select the variable that produces the answer they want; then a Second-Order Indifference Clause instructing agencies that, absent information about which parametrization to use, they shall be indifferent among those on the registry — followed, without any change of tone, by the discovery that the resulting mixture depends on how the registry is indexed or grouped.
- Include at least one quotation from a fictional commenter — a compliance officer, a registered variable-selection consultant, a member of the Council — and at least one invented official with a title.
- Let the closing Request for Comments do some of the work: five or six questions for commenters that a formal epistemologist would recognize as the live disputes, asked in the voice of regulators who think they are housekeeping details.
- The straight face must hold. No winking, no "of course this is absurd," no punchline at the expense of civil servants as such. A reader should be able to tell what is being ironized from the content alone. What I do not want is the piece collapsing into "probability is subjective so anything goes," or into a general sneer at Bayesianism; the specific claim being sent up is that ignorance can be regulated into a number, and the moral that should be visible underneath is that the missing input is a specification of the selection mechanism, not a principle.

Length around 900 words. If you want a second worked example, the wine-and-water case (the same mixture described by a ratio or by a proportion, with indifference applied to each) is the natural one, but either show the arithmetic explicitly and get it right or keep the numbers out of it — a wrong number will lose the readers I care about most. Please don't lean on any citations, bibliographies, or named historical attributions beyond what a well-read generalist would know; describe the invariance-based reply to Bertrand rather than citing it.

After the piece, add a short note of about 150 words, headed "Author's note," doing three things: (i) state plainly what is settled mathematics here — that the three procedures give different answers, and why — as against (ii) what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, including whether invariance or maximum-entropy considerations pick out one of the three as the uniquely well-posed answer, whether such reasoning itself presupposes a choice of coordinates or of the relevant group, and whether the principle of indifference has any legitimate domain at all; and (iii) flag where the satire takes liberties or presses a contested reading harder than the state of the argument allows. I'd especially like the piece to be fair to the invariance side rather than quietly assuming it fails, so tell me if the version you've written is doing that.
