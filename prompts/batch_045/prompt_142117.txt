I am a novelist, four books in, and for the first time I am writing one set entirely in the past: a close-third-person novel about one family in San Francisco through the second wave of the 1918 influenza pandemic — the mask ordinance, the league that formed to repeal it, the arguments about whether compulsory gauze did anything, a laundress who keeps a door-to-door tally of the sick households on her block, and a short present-day frame narrated by a great-granddaughter who has spent ten years digitising the family's papers. My editor loves the draft and keeps praising it for putting the characters "on the right side of history," and the more she says it the more I suspect that is precisely the failure. My partner is an epidemiologist and will say things like "they'd have saved thousands of lives if they'd just listened," which I now hear as the vice in miniature. The trouble is that what I have is a temper, not a test: I can feel when a scene is congratulating us for how far we have come, or when I have handed a character a motive they could only have had in our vocabulary, or when I have excused them by saying they "couldn't have known" — which I've started to suspect is itself a condescending way of refusing to take their actual reasons seriously — but I can't reliably catch it in my own prose, and my fact-checking historian won't catch it either, because she checks whether the streetcar line existed and not whether my plot treats a side as having lost because it was benighted rather than because it was outvoted, outbid, or unlucky.

So please take this seriously and carefully, and work as if you were both a philosopher or historically-minded theorist who knows what is actually at stake in the complaint about whig history and a developmental editor who has to be useful by Friday. Start by brainstorming — ten minutes, in your head — the distinct ways whiggishness can get into a work of fiction specifically, and only then condense that into something I can run: a numbered decision procedure I can apply to a single scene in ten to fifteen minutes during a revision pass, with no theory vocabulary in the steps themselves. For each step, tell me what question to ask, what counts as a pass, what counts as a fail, what counts as "rewrite this line," where the procedure stops with a verdict, and where it has to stop and hand the question to me because it is a question about what my book is for. I need it to keep separate the things I keep conflating — narrating as if a later outcome were the point of earlier action, judging past people by present standards, and the mirror-image vice of romanticising the period as a lost golden age — and I need an actual test for anachronistic *concepts*, not just anachronistic objects, since my characters keep wanting to say things like "the science" and "prejudice" in words that flatten what they meant. Assume I have no training in any of this, that I will run it on about forty scenes, and that the output has to survive being read by my editor.

Then show me it working. Run it on three scenes from the draft, in detail: (1) the meeting where the anti-mask characters are given the strongest version of their case — that the gauze is useless, that the ordinance is enforced against the poor and waved through for the rich, that compulsion in a republic in wartime is itself the harm — where I cannot tell whether writing them well is honest or is apologetics; (2) the deathbed scene in which an opponent recants and concedes that the doctors were right, which is the scene my editor singles out and which I would be genuinely unhappy to lose, so if your procedure condemns it I want to be told so plainly; and (3) the frame chapters, where the great-granddaughter calls her ancestor "a forerunner of modern epidemiology" on the strength of that household tally, and I can't work out whether that is a legitimate claim about influence or just retroactive ennoblement of a dead end. Finally, be straight with me about where the procedure misfires: what kinds of scenes it will score wrongly, what it cannot tell me at all, and which of its verdicts I should treat as a prompt to think rather than an instruction to cut.
