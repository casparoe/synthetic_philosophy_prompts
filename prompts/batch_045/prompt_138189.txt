Before answering, brainstorm two or three ways of sequencing the material, say briefly which one you are choosing and why, and only then give me the plan itself. Please write in American English.

I am a high-school junior and I run my school's history club; in two weeks I have to lead a twenty-five-minute session for ninth-graders on whether nations are old or recent. I do not need the explanation — I need a plan for giving it.

The thing I want them to walk out holding is not that nations were invented and therefore are not real. It is a three-way distinction: a nation's reported age (what its own origin story claims), the age of its materials (language, calendar, costume, epic, religion), and the age of its arrangement (when those materials were assembled into a single national package and handed to everyone at once). The claim that nations are modern is, as I understand it, only a claim about the third, and the first two can be very old while the third is recent; I have been calling that gap borrowed age and want to use it as the spine of the session.

So: what has to be established before that distinction is even intelligible to a fourteen-year-old, and what comes after it? Which single example should carry it, and what must that example have and lack — I am thinking it needs a findable seam, a point we can point to where the practice was standardized, and it needs to be genuinely load-bearing for how people identify now, but it should be neither so recent that everyone already knows it was made up nor so trivial that nobody cares. If the kilt-and-tartan case is the obvious pick, I would rather avoid it; tell me whether that is a mistake.

Then the misconceptions, in the order you would expect them to surface, and where exactly in the sequence to defuse each: that invented means fake and therefore illegitimate; that what was made up once can be unmade whenever we like; that some identifiable person or committee designed it; that before nations existed people had no collective attachments at all.

Two more things: should the two labels they may have half-heard — invented tradition and imagined community — go on the board at the beginning or only at the end, and why? And give me one question I can ask at the finish that would show me they have grasped the three-age distinction rather than just the slogan, plus one thing I should openly decline to get into at this level. No reading lists and nothing I would have to look up, please.
