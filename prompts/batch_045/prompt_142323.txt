I'm in the final year of a combined law-and-philosophy programme, and for our seminar on liability for autonomous systems each of us has to bring in a short "discussion object" — something creative that still does real argumentative work — to hand out before we discuss responsibility gaps. Mine is going to be a piece of fake regulation, and I'd like you to write it with me.

The form: an excerpt from a statutory instrument, written in the deadpan, clause-numbered register of actual civil-service drafting (definitions, duties, a schedule, an illustrative worked example), plus a short prefatory "Explanatory Note" in the voice of the ministry official who drafted it. Call it something like the Autonomous Systems (Continuity of Accountability) Regulations, made in the late 2030s by a jurisdiction of your choosing; around 700–800 words total. The instrument should operationalise a principle the seminar keeps circling: that when no one could have foreseen or prevented what the system did, the law's answer is not to prevent but to allocate — to make sure that blame always has somewhere to land. I want the satire to come out of the drafting itself rather than out of jokes. So, for instance: a definition of "meaningful human control" that is technically satisfied by an operator who has no real chance of intervening (control is meaningful if the operator could have averted the outcome by some action "available" to them, where availability is defined without regard to human reaction time or to what the interface actually permits); a registry of Registered Responsibility Bearers — ordinary employees who contract to absorb liability for fleets of systems they never touch, subject to a maximum ratio of machines per person and a fitness test consisting only of being reachable and insurable; a default apportionment formula for the case that interests me most, where the harm was produced not by one opaque system but by the interaction of two well-behaved ones built by rival firms from overlapping training data, so that no single designer's choices explain the outcome and the formula instead splits blame by something arbitrary but administrable, such as each firm's share of the relevant model market over a lookback window; and a residual clause for harms with no determinate author, under which the unsatisfied "moral remainder" is discharged by official ceremony or by purchasing certificates. The reductio I want to land, without ever being stated outright, is the closing provision: that once a completed attribution statement has been issued and served, a responsible agent shall be conclusively deemed to have existed for the purposes of retributive justice.

Two constraints, if you can respect them. First, my tutor thinks the whole responsibility-gap framing is overblown, so please don't let the piece rest on the systems being mysteriously unpredictable or malevolent — keep the technology boring and let the unease come entirely from the paperwork and from how reasonable each individual clause sounds. Second, I'd like the worked example to be concrete rather than abstract: something like a collision between a delivery drone and an automated ambulance at an intersection, both functioning exactly as specified, with the incident report dutifully working through the Regulations and reaching a result that is procedurally flawless and substantively absurd. Include a couple of invented officeholders or form numbers to make it feel lived-in, and keep the whole thing legible to a classmate who hasn't done the reading.
