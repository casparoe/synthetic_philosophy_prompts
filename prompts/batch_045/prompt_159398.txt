I build controllers for warehouse robots that operate across shifts in which the environment itself changes — floor friction, aisle layout, how much human traffic cuts through a given corridor. My working vocabulary is that of robust and distributionally robust control, or robust MDPs if you prefer: for each (state, action) pair I specify a set of plausible next-state distributions, and I optimize the worst case over that set with a robust Bellman recursion. That recursion is valid exactly because my sets are rectangular — the adversary picks a kernel for each (s,a) independently, and what comes out is a stationary policy I can execute without re-planning. Where I need a single distribution rather than a set, I fall back on a Bayesian model: a prior over environment parameters, a posterior, a posterior-predictive or Bayes-optimal policy. Disagreement across an ensemble gives me epistemic uncertainty, which I spend on exploration bonuses, and I distinguish that from aleatoric noise, which I simply integrate over. I have no philosophy training, and I know the Ellsberg case only from a blog post: an urn holds 30 red balls and 60 balls that are blue or green in an unknown proportion; people bet on red over blue and on blue-or-green over red-or-green, and no single assignment of probabilities to the colours fits both preferences at once.

Please work in three parts, and do not pad the answer with references or historical attributions — I need the translation, not the literature.

**1. Translation.** Restate, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I gave you: (a) the choice pattern itself; (b) the worst-case-over-a-set-of-distributions explanation of it; (c) the second-order explanation, which keeps a probability distribution over which distribution is correct and then adds a separate, separately tunable attitude toward how spread out that distribution is. For each piece, say which of my objects it maps onto — uncertainty sets, rectangularity, the robust Bellman recursion, priors over parameters, epistemic versus aleatoric uncertainty, exploration bonuses — and be explicit about which mappings are exact equivalences and which are only suggestive.

**2. The case I actually care about.** I tried to reproduce the pattern by giving my controller a *non*-rectangular uncertainty set: one unknown "urn composition" parameter shared by the whole episode, so the adversary commits to a single kernel once and is stuck with it. This is, I take it, what makes the Ellsberg urn an urn rather than a per-state coin, and the controller now shows the target asymmetry. But it also does something I cannot classify. Policies that are optimal under the ex-ante worst case over the shared parameter are not the policies the controller prefers once it is partway through an episode and part of the uncertainty is resolved: re-planning reverses the plan it committed to, and the robust Bellman recursion I use for rectangular sets no longer returns the right value. Diagnose this in my terms. Is the reversal (i) an artifact of my having updated the uncertainty set incorrectly, (ii) a genuine property of worst-case reasoning over shared parameters — in which case it is not a bug in my code but a property of the objective — or (iii) evidence that there is no single thing called ambiguity aversion that a controller could implement at all? Say which, and if the answer is (i) or (ii), give me the form of the fix — the correct update rule, or a state augmentation, or the relaxation I should be using — without turning this into a survey.

**3. Residue report.** Tell me what in the philosophical debate has no counterpart in my vocabulary whatsoever. Two things in particular. First, whether my epistemic/aleatoric distinction is really doing the same work as their risk/ambiguity distinction, given that my version of it is model-relative and shrinks as data comes in. Second, and more important: my framework has no word for "rational." It has only better or worse return on the deployments I actually care about, which is itself an average over a distribution I do not know. Does the normative question — whether the Ellsberg pattern is a violation of rationality, or merely another preference an agent is allowed to have — disappear entirely under this translation, or does it return disguised as a choice I am already making without noticing it? Where the translation fails, tell me honestly whether that is because my vocabulary is too thin to state what the philosophers are asking, or because their question was less well defined than it looks.
