I'm putting together a lecture on the dollar auction and escalation of commitment — you know the setup, the top two bidders both pay, only the top one takes the prize, so a war that started over a dollar ends with somebody paying several dollars for it. I want one case that runs the other way, and I'm being annoyingly specific about how it gets built.

Construct a scenario in which all of the following are true at once.

1. Two parties, A and B, contesting a single prize, with irrevocable outlays and the loser forfeiting whatever they put in. Structurally a dollar auction, but set somewhere ordinary: two departments fighting over a shared hire, two firms after the same patent, a lawsuit neither side wants to settle, whatever you like. No contraptions, no science fiction. One paragraph.
2. A has already sunk a substantial amount. B has sunk nothing. Everything else about their situations is symmetric.
3. It is genuinely rational, forward-looking, for A to put in more — and not rational for B to enter now. Not rational given that A is too deep in to think straight; rational on the numbers, and A can see that it is rational on the numbers.
4. A nevertheless wishes A had never entered. The trap is intact: A's total position is negative and was always going to be. A is digging further into a hole, and digging further is the best move available.
5. The A/B difference must not come from the sunk cost doing any work as a sunk cost. So I don't want any of: loss aversion or the feeling of waste; pride or face-saving; reputation or credibility in a repeated interaction or in front of any audience beyond B; or the earlier spending having produced some real asset, partial deliverable, or option. What I do want is a difference in what each side can rationally expect the other to do next, and I want that difference to trace back to what has become common knowledge about A's outlays and has not become common knowledge about B's. Note the asymmetry is not that A knows something about the world that B doesn't — B has seen everything A has seen. It is that B knows something about A that A doesn't know B knows about A... you get the idea, only it has to actually be load-bearing.

Then show the case meets the specs: lay out the payoffs (numbers if you can, structure of the decision if you can't), say what A does and why, what B does and why, and check clause 4 explicitly rather than asserting it.

Then the part I actually care about. Name the single feature doing the work. Show what happens if you remove it — I expect the case collapses back into either a plain dollar auction or a plain sunk-cost fallacy, so tell me which and why. Then give me the strongest objection available to someone who holds that sunk costs are never reasons for anything: my guess is they'll say the case cheats by smuggling in a third element, the fact that somebody is watching, instead of showing that the expenditure itself matters. Give me your reply to that, and then tell me honestly whether the reply is solid enough to put in front of second-years or whether all the case really shows is that sunk costs matter when there is an audience.

Point of the exercise, so you know what counts as cheating: I want to argue that never let sunk costs guide you is too crude without conceding anything to the claim that sunk costs as such ever matter. If the case only works by stipulating the verdicts rather than deriving them from the payoffs, it's useless to me.

Keep the whole thing under about 600 words. And if the spec as stated is incoherent, or satisfiable only by stipulation, say which clause to drop instead of papering over it — that would definately be worse than telling me I have overconstrained you.
