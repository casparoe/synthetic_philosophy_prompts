I am a novelist, and a long-form essay I read recently in a literary magazine — part of a series on "post-truth fiction" — summarised pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism roughly as follows:

"The two theories come to much the same thing: whether someone counts as knowing depends on how much is at stake. Contextualists hold that the word 'knows' shifts its meaning with the conversational context, so that raising the stakes raises the standard — a woman who knew the bank would be open on Saturday morning ceases to know once a missed payment will cost her the house. Pragmatic encroachment adds that the operative stakes are those of whoever is doing the attributing, which means knowledge is less a fact about the subject than a status we confer on people when their evidence is good enough for our purposes. The view has accordingly been refuted by its central objection — that it makes what a person knows depend on who happens to be talking about them — and few philosophers defend it any longer."

I suspect this is unfair, and I am fairly confident about the "much the same thing" part, since I have seen the two views listed separately, but I cannot locate the rest. Could you go through the passage and tell me what a defender of each view would accept, what is a legitimate simplification for a general reader, and what is simply wrong or overstated — and, in particular, whether the essay's conclusion that the position has been refuted survives once the summary is corrected?

The reason I care is that I wanted to press one of these views into service in the novel I am drafting. It is narrated in the first person by a woman recalling a death she may have caused, and at a crucial point she says of her sister, "she knew the letter was a forgery." I want the reader to feel that line turn false as the stakes for the sister inside the story rise, even though nothing whatever is at stake for the narrator or for the reader. I have been calling this "diegetic encroachment": the proposal that, in fiction, a knowledge attribution answers to the practical situation of the character it is about rather than to that of the speaker, the audience, or the author. Does anything in contextualism or pragmatic encroachment actually support that extension, is it a coherent thesis in its own right, or does it rest on a confusion that the corrected version of the theories exposes?
