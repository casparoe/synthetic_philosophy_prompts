I sit on the grants committee of a small family foundation. For three decades the payout has gone to near-term causes — vaccination, deworming, secondary school scholarships — and this autumn we are being asked to move roughly a third of it into longtermist funding. I volunteered to read into the philosophical literature before we vote and, because I was reading anyway, to act as second marker for a colleague's third-year seminar on the ethics of the long-run future. The essay question and one submitted answer are reproduced below. I would like an independent and reasonably severe reading of the answer; my own judgement is not to be trusted, since I have no training in this area and the prose is persuasive.

Please do three things. First, assign a mark out of 25, with a sentence on where that places the answer in the range. Second, give point-by-point comments keyed to the structure of the question — parts (a), (b) and (c) — flagging both what the student gets right and, specifically, anything that reads as correct but is not; where a step is defensible but under-defended, say so rather than marking it wrong. Third, say in two or three sentences what a first-class answer to (c) would have had to establish.

Question (25 marks)

Strong longtermism, as we have encountered it this term, has two components: an axiological component, to the effect that the value of what we do now is determined, to a very large extent, by the effects of our actions on the very long-run future; and a deontic component, to the effect that we ought to be doing considerably more than we currently are to make that future go well.

A critic — call her the levelling critic — argues that the first component cannot support the second. Her argument runs as follows. Our best models of how a present intervention propagates into the very long-run future are so uncertain that once credence is spread across the full range of mechanisms by which the intervention might matter, the expected long-run value of every serious candidate intervention comes out roughly the same. If that is right, the long-run term in our decision problem is approximately a constant across the options: it cancels. But a term that cancels cannot tell us to do one thing rather than another. Nor, she adds, can the longtermist retreat to the thought that some very small probability of a very large effect is enough: for every mechanism by which an intervention might improve the long run there is a broadly similar mechanism by which it might worsen it, and nothing in the axiological component tells us which family of mechanisms is more probable.

Write an essay in which you:

(a) set out the levelling argument in the strongest form you can, state precisely which component of strong longtermism it targets, and explain the difference between saying that the long-run term is roughly constant across the options and saying that long-run effects have no value;

(b) assess whether the claim that the stakes are astronomical is by itself an adequate reply. You may use the following illustration: intervention A certainly improves the near-term welfare of one million people by a given amount; intervention B has some probability p of improving the very long-run future by the equivalent of ten billion such improvements, and otherwise does nothing;

(c) consider whether the case for strong longtermism survives if we hold the axiological component fixed but adopt a person-affecting axiology of population ethics rather than a totalist one — for instance the asymmetry, on which a person can be made better or worse off by how their life goes once they exist, but bringing a person into existence with a life worth living benefits nobody. Be explicit about what such a view can and cannot say about the badness of human extinction.

Submitted answer

Strong longtermism (SLT) is best understood as two claims. The axiological claim is that the very long-run future contains, in expectation, astronomically more value than the near term: there may be trillions of future people, and the near-term effects of anything we do now are a rounding error by comparison. It is because the future is where the value is that the effects of our actions on the long run determine, to a very large extent, how good those actions are. The deontic claim follows directly: we ought always to act so as to maximize the expected value of the very long-run future. On this view the near-term benefits of an intervention drop out of the picture except insofar as they feed into long-run outcomes. The comparative formulation — we should be doing more — is best read as a maximising instruction in polite dress; taken literally it is too weak to be worth defending.

The levelling critic is best read as attacking the bridge between the components rather than the axiology itself. For the sake of argument she grants that long-run effects determine most of the value of what we do, and argues that this cannot tell us what to do. The distinction the question invites is important here: a term that is constant across the options is not a term with no value. It can be enormous and still fail to discriminate between options. That is why her argument is an epistemic objection about our leverage on the future, not a claim about the value of the future. She has two lines of support. The first is that our models of long-run causal propagation are too diffuse for the long-run terms of different options to differ in any estimable way. The second is that where we do have some grip, the mechanisms by which an intervention might improve the long run are roughly matched by mechanisms by which it might worsen it, so the expected long-run effect hovers near zero. If either line holds, the long-run term cannot do the work that the deontic component requires of it.

Is the appeal to astronomical stakes an adequate reply on its own? No, and no careful longtermist should say otherwise. The size of the future's value is one factor in a product; the other is the difference our action makes to it. So the view needs a leverage premise: some probability that what we do now affects the trajectory of the long run. But that premise is close to trivially satisfied. Take the illustration. A's certain benefit is 10^6 units; B's expected benefit is p times 10^10; the two are equal at p = 10^-4. So a one-in-ten-thousand chance of a large long-run effect already matches a certain benefit to a million people, and B wins at any p above that threshold. More generally, since there is no upper bound on how many future people there might be, it is enough that the probability of our affecting the trajectory be non-zero: for any non-zero p, some finite number of future people is large enough that p multiplied by the value at stake exceeds the value of any near-term benefit. This is also why the critic's symmetry point does no work. Symmetric mechanisms cancel in expectation only where the magnitudes on either side are equal, and here the magnitudes are astronomical. Long-run considerations therefore dominate the decision problem even before we ask how likely it is that our action makes a difference.

Finally, suppose we keep the axiology but adopt a person-affecting view of population ethics — the asymmetry, on which a person can be made better or worse off by how their life goes once they exist, but on which bringing a person into existence with a life worth living benefits nobody, though it can harm them if the life is not worth living. It is sometimes said that this deflates the longtermist case, on the grounds that if value is person-affecting then extinction harms nobody: those who would have existed are not made worse off by not being created. I think this is a misreading of the asymmetry. Extinction would deprive all the people who would otherwise have existed of the good lives they would have led, and being deprived of a good life is bad for the person deprived of it. What the asymmetry denies is that there is a reason to create happy people; it does not deny that there is a reason not to take away a good life that someone would otherwise have had. So the person-affecting restriction costs the longtermist very little: the astronomical figure survives, and with it both components of SLT. There is a residual question about the loss to people alive now, through the frustration of their projects and desires, but that is a small part of the loss and in any case not the part the longtermist is trading on.

The levelling critic has therefore identified a genuine constraint — the longtermist needs a leverage premise — but it is a constraint that is met almost automatically. What remains of her objection is a general scepticism about acting under uncertainty that nobody should accept. Strong longtermism survives intact, in both components and on either axiology.
