I've been reading the rationalist corner of the internet for roughly fifteen years, and gun policy is one of the very few political topics where I think the blogs genuinely moved me off my starting position without my being able to point to the single argument that did it. My local group is doing a series in which each member writes up their position on one contested policy area and the others audit it for internal coherence before the person defends it in person. Mine is gun control. Below is the package as I'd actually defend it, in the terms I'd defend it in. I'd like the audit before I present rather than after.

Where I've landed:

(1) The only defensible case for widespread private firearm ownership is the self-defense interest, and it is weightiest for people the police will not reliably protect. I treat that as a right: the state bears the burden of justification for any prohibition, and the bare fact that the aggregate would go better under some restriction is not by itself a justification.

(2) I reject soft paternalism toward competent adults. That a product can be misused, or that owning it is statistically risky for the owner, is not a reason to restrict access to it — that logic would also license banning alcohol, motorcycles, and backyard pools. On this basis I oppose feature-based bans on semiautomatic rifles, discretionary licensing schemes under which an official decides who has shown sufficient cause to carry, taxes or fees on purchase, and registration.

(3) On the empirics, I hold that the honest reading of the social science supports three things and not a fourth: more guns means more firearm suicide, and that is not merely confounding; the presence of a gun makes a given violent encounter considerably more likely to be lethal; the evidence does not establish that general civilian gun prevalence raises homicide rates; and the very large survey-based estimates of defensive gun uses that circulate online should not be credited — most of the incidents counted are not cases in which the gun was needed.

(4) I own a handgun anyway, and would defend the right to.

(5) The restrictions I do support: universal background checks on transfer, extreme risk protection orders including short ex parte emergency orders with a prompt adversarial hearing, a waiting period of roughly ten days, mandatory safe storage in the home, and lifetime prohibition for anyone convicted of a violent misdemeanor.

(6) Institutionally, I want firearm policy written as provisional — every provision sunsets after five years, new provisions take effect on a staggered or randomized rollout across jurisdictions so the effects can actually be measured instead of inferred from the same confounded cross-sectional comparisons, and administration sits with an arm's-length technical body rather than with legislatures trading in symbolism. Gun policy should be one of the places where we generate evidence rather than re-litigate it.

(7) On the constitutional question: I think the original-public-meaning case for an individual self-defense right is roughly correct as history, but I am otherwise a living constitutionalist, I would defend essentially this same package in a country whose constitution said nothing about arms, and I take original meaning to carry little weight on its own. I am not leaning on the Heller-style reading; I mention it only because several people have accused me of invoking it selectively.

What I want from you: treat the set as an object and tell me which pairs actually pull against each other and how badly, ordered by severity rather than listed as equal, and for each real tension say exactly what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the package. I also want to know which of the conflicts that look like conflicts dissolve once the commitments are stated carefully, and why, since I'd rather present five items than discover in the Q&A that I was holding two versions of the same principle. If there is one revision that cleans up more than any other, name it. Please don't spend the answer on the observation that the empirical literature is contested — I've said what I take it to support, and I'd rather you grant that and trace what it commits me to. I have a nagging sense that something here is doing more work than it can bear and that at least one of the apparent conflicts is not real, but I can't locate either.
