hey — I teach a final-year undergrad seminar on consciousness and I'm writing the take-home final right now. I need one essay question on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, and I really dont want it to be passable by reciting the dialectic: illusionists say phenomenal properties don't exist, realists say they do, illusionists owe an account of the illusion, Chalmers raises the meta-problem, everyone goes home. That answer should scrape a B at most.

The thing I keep circling is this. Illusionism explains our conviction that we have phenomenal states as an introspective misrepresentation — our introspective systems represent our states as having qualitative properties they dont have. Fine. But now imagine a system I've been calling the ventriloquist in my notes (rename it if you like). It was trained on nothing but first-person reports paired with sensory and behavioural data, so everything it says about its own states it learned from report feedback. It has no inherited introspective architecture of the sort evolution gave us. And it says all the things we say: that experience has a distinctive character, that the character of red cant be captured in functional or dispositional terms, that there is a hard problem. It writes papers about it.

So the crux: does illusionism have to say the ventriloquist is under the same illusion we are? If yes, illusions start to look extremely cheap, and the illusionist's explanation of our conviction seems to generalise to a case where intuitively nothing is going on at all. If no, illusionism owes a difference-maker between really misrepresenting and merely outputting the reports, and it has to find that difference-maker without helping itself to phenomenal consciousness. I want them to feel both horns before they pick one.

Students have read Frankish on illusionism, Chalmers on the meta-problem, and the standard realist replies. It's a 48-hour take-home, 1200 word cap, and the question is worth 20 marks of the paper.

Could you write:

1. The question as it would appear on the handout, 120 words max. Don't name the dilemma in it — they should have to find the horns themselves.
2. A model answer of about 700 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit moves, with the marks for each and one line on the characteristic way students blow each one — I care a lot about what separates a solid 13 from a 17.

One more thing: if the dilemma as I've set it up has an obvious reply I'm missing, the kind of move that would let a strong student dissolve it in a paragraph and make the question too easy, say so before the model answer and tell me how to patch the setup.
