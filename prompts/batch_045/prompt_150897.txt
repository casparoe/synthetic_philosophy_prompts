My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about when it's OK for a scientific model to rest on assumptions you know are false, and I've come away less sure than when I started, which is annoying.

He's an economist, and he went full Friedman on me: the realism of the assumptions is never the test, the only test is whether the model's predictions hold up, and a wildly unrealistic assumption is actually a feature because it's what buys you a simple, general theory with a wide domain. That's the F-twist, as far as I can tell, and he thinks it settles everything. I pushed back with what I took to be the obvious rival: a false assumption is fine only if it's the kind of falsehood you can undo. You zero out or idealize away some factor, and separately you can say, at least roughly, what happens as you put it back. Friction and air resistance on the inclined plane: fine, because we know how to add them and we know roughly when they start to matter. Call it the de-idealization or isolation test.

By hour three I'd also talked myself into a distinction neither of us could place, and I want to know whether it's doing real work or is just a fancy way of redrawing the line I already liked. Not every false assumption is a case of leaving a factor out. Some say a factor is absent when it isn't there to be absented — the frictionless plane, an infinite population, no mutation. But some describe a factor that definitely is there in a way that's just wrong: the representative agent standing in for a whole distribution of heterogeneous agents, or rational expectations standing in for whatever people actually do. And some replace a mechanism with an equilibrium condition: markets clear, price equals marginal cost, everybody meets everybody. Absence, misdescription, and closure-by-fiat, if you want labels.

The cases we went through, since he kept demanding examples: point-mass molecules with no intermolecular forces in the ideal gas law; the frictionless plane and the massless string; infinite population, random mating and no selection in Hardy-Weinberg, where the whole point is that it's a null baseline to detect selection against rather than a prediction about anything at all; a single representative agent and a continuum of agents in macro; complete markets and rational expectations in the DSGE models central banks were running before 2008, most of which didn't really have a banking sector in them; and fitness just equals payoff in evolutionary game theory. We also fought about Ptolemy, because I pointed out that epicycles predicted the planets better than anything else for a millenium, and he said Friedman meant novel, out-of-sample prediction, not tuning a model after the fact to fit what you already knew.

So please compare the two accounts head to head — his predictivism and my de-idealization test. Which gets the right verdict on those cases, and where does each one go quiet or come out wrong? The things I most want: is my test actually independent of his, or does it collapse into "the assumption is fine if the predictions survive de-idealizing," which is just his test with extra steps? Does his test have anything whatsoever to say about a null model like Hardy-Weinberg, where nobody was trying to predict? Is whatever makes the missing banking sector feel different from the frictionless plane something either account can capture, or is the difference only that one of them had consequences we cared about? Can predictivism tell apart a model that predicts well because it's onto something and one that's been retrofitted to the data, and does the novel-prediction repair actually save it or does it make the criterion so strict that it convicts half of macro and half of population genetics? And should I keep my three-way distinction or drop it? Say explicitly where each account is silent, rather than merely weak.

Also, he thinks we're arguing past each other because the two accounts answer different questions — his about whether to accept a model, mine about whether you can use it to explain or intervene. If you think that's right, say so, but bear in mind he'll call it a dodge if you do, and I half agree with him. And give me a verdict on one case we never got to: the homogeneous mixing assumption in standard SIR compartmental models, everyone equally likely to meet everyone, which is obviously false and which the herd immunity thresholds come straight out of.
