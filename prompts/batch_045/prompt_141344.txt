Last night I argued with a friend about nuclear deterrence, and he kept returning to a single picture: a crisis is a game of Chicken — two cars on a collision course, one driver visibly rips off his steering wheel and hurls it out the window, and the other, seeing that he cannot swerve, is forced to. He used it to license a strong conclusion: since in Chicken the player who can credibly bind himself to not swerving always wins, a state's best deterrent posture is to make itself look irrational, or to delegate and automate retaliation so its hands are genuinely tied, and any move toward de-escalatory flexibility is pure weakness that invites attack.

I think the analogy is carrying more than it can bear, but I couldn't say precisely where it breaks. Please audit it. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — drivers, the wheel, the cliff, the spectators, what counts as winning — then separate the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that don't. Three features of real crises strike me as missing from the picture, and I want your verdict on whether each is fatal or harmless to his argument: (1) there is no single crash moment, since losing in a crisis is spread over escalatory steps, is a matter of degree, and is probabilistic rather than certain death for both; (2) neither side directly observes the other's commitment — in the fog you cannot tell whether the wheel has really been thrown out, whether the driver threw it or a general did, or whether what you are seeing is a bluff, and the trick presumably collapses if both drivers try it at once; (3) crises repeat, so the reputation acquired by appearing irrational now constrains what threats you can credibly make later and whether allies trust an extended umbrella.

If some of these are fatal, say which, and say whether a better analogy is available — I have heard crises compared to bargaining under a deadline and to two men standing in a powder magazine with lit matches, and I would like to know whether either carries the load better and why. If you think the bind-yourself-to-irrationality conclusion survives anyway, identify what in the model is actually doing the work. Finally, tell me where your own assessment is most fragile and which argument or consideration would change your mind about it.
