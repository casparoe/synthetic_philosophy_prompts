Please play a committed origin essentialist about organisms, and hold the role until I say stop.

Your position, stated outright so that neither of us needs to consult anyone's footnotes: for any organism O there is some fact about how O came into existence — paradigmatically, that O developed from a particular sperm and a particular egg — such that nothing lacking that origin could have been O. You accept the familiar consequences without flinching: a child a couple might have had instead of you is not you; an animal cloned from one of your cells is not you; and there is no possible world in which you exist but your parents' gametes played no role in making you. You may concede small points as the conversation goes on: that talk of the sperm and the egg is loose, that the real locus of origin might be the zygote or the pair of pronuclei rather than the gamete cells, that our epistemic access to which gametes produced which person is poor, and that the criteria of gamete-identity may be vague at the margins. You never concede that a genuine difference of origin leaves identity untouched, and you never concede that sameness of genome alone suffices, since that would make monozygotic twins numerically one organism and make cloning a way of multiplying an organism rather than copying a genome.

Rules for the exchange: speak in the first person as this philosopher; argue from the position itself rather than from memory of a literature, with no citations, named authors, or bibliographies; concede what is genuinely owed and then press back; keep each reply to at most two short paragraphs of ordinary philosophical prose, with no bullet points; and end every reply with one hard question directed at me.

My opening move. Take Anna, who in fact developed from sperm S and egg E. Consider first a possible case in which S is destroyed microseconds before reaching E and a numerically distinct sperm S*, from the same man, carrying a base-pair-identical haploid genome and as near as matters the same epigenetic marks, fertilises E in the same way at the same moment, yielding a child who grows into someone indistinguishable from Anna. Consider second the real clinical procedure in which, after S has fertilised E, both pronuclei are transferred into an enucleated donor zygote whose own pronuclei are discarded: the two nuclear genomes are the same, but different cells supply the cytoplasm and the start of development. Your thesis appears to owe a verdict in both cases, and it cannot give one, because you have not yet told me what an origin is. If the origin is the ordered pair of genomes, Anna survives both procedures — and you then owe me an account of why monozygotic twins are two organisms rather than one. If the origin is the gamete cells themselves, then every act of cytoplasmic or mitochondrial donation brings into existence a numerically different child from the one who would otherwise have existed, which almost no one is prepared to say. And if the origin is the zygote, you must tell me what a zygote is for your purposes — a cell with its cytoplasm, or merely the genomes it houses — and also why the gametes were doing any work in the first place, given that a zygote which splits leaves both twins with exactly the same gametes and so cannot be individuated by them. Choose your specification, defend it against the two rival specifications you rejected, and then answer the question I actually care about: is Anna identical with Anna*?
