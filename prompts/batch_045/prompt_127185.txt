I spent my career as an experimental condensed-matter physicist and have only recently begun sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group. They have asked me to present a short statement of where I stand on reduction and emergence, and before I do I would like to know whether the commitments below are jointly tenable. Several members have told me the set is inconsistent, but none has said which part I should abandon, and I cannot see it myself.

(1) Everything is physical: any two situations that agree in their complete microphysical description agree in every respect, and there are no fundamental forces or influences beyond those physics already posits. In particular I reject any downward causation that would add a new fundamental interaction to the inventory.

(2) Emergence is nonetheless real and not merely epistemic: at higher levels there appear regularities, patterns and quantities that are genuine discoveries about nature, not artefacts of our limited computing power or of our being too lazy to work through the details.

(3) The special sciences are methodologically autonomous and often irreplaceably so. One cannot get from the Standard Model to cell biology or to market behaviour, and in practice the explanatory traffic frequently runs from the top down.

(4) Higher-level explanations are good precisely because they are insensitive to the microphysical detail, since the same higher-level law covers an enormous variety of underlying constitutions. Temperature applies to gases, to solids and to radiation, and cannot be identified with any single microphysical quantity; that is what makes a thermodynamics possible at all. In my own field, systems with entirely different micro-constitutions behave identically near a critical point, and a full specification of the molecules would explain nothing about it.

(5) We are entitled to believe in whatever earns its keep in our best explanations, and the posits of the higher-level sciences earn their keep exactly as much as any unobservable entity in physics does.

(6) Where reduction succeeds, it succeeds by exhibiting a mechanism: one explains the behaviour of the whole by showing what the parts are doing and how they are arranged.

Leave consciousness out of it; I want to know whether the package holds up for ordinary special-science cases.

Which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly? Please rank the real tensions by severity, say for each what I would have to give up and what that would cost me, and name the single revision that would tidy up the most. Also flag any apparent conflict that dissolves on inspection, and tell me why the objection fails, so that I can answer it when it is raised.
