My wife and I are halfway through our first IVF cycle, and our clinic has begun offering polygenic embryo screening: for each blastocyst it will report risk scores for things like type 2 diabetes and coronary artery disease, and — unless we opt out — a single composite ranking meant to guide which embryo gets transferred first. We have to decide this week whether to look at the ranking at all. I have argued myself into a position I now have to defend out loud: that using the ranking is not merely permissible but is something we owe whichever child we end up having, and that the familiar liberal-eugenics objections miss their target because they are aimed at a population-level practice while ours is a closed-cohort choice among the handful of possible children this one cycle could produce. I defend a deliberately narrow version of the procreative-beneficence argument: within-cohort ranking for health-related traits is permissible even when the score is partly tracking traits that are socially mediated, because what we are selecting is a genome, not a life plan.

Next week I present this for twenty minutes to the clinic's ethics advisory committee, which is drafting guidance on whether to offer polygenic screening at all. In the room: two reproductive endocrinologists who will want effect sizes and think the scores are being oversold to patients; a clinical ethicist who reads embryo selection through the disability-rights critique and cares about the message the practice expresses; a health economist who will ask what this costs per quality-adjusted life year and who gets access to it; and a patient representative who has been through six cycles and will ask what any of this means for the embryos that get frozen indefinitely or discarded.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet for the fifteen minutes after the talk: the seven or eight objections I will actually get from that room — not the ones I would get in a philosophy seminar — ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased in that objector's own vocabulary, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the first two slides. Name the single reply that has to land for the position to survive the meeting. And include the objection I am quietly hoping nobody asks — I suspect it concerns the fact that most of the variance these scores explain is variance between families rather than within one, which is precisely the variance my closed-cohort framing does not get to use.
