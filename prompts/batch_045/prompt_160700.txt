I am writing the position section of a paper on moral responsibility and alternative possibilities, and before I commit to the package in print I would like an audit of whether it is actually stable. The commitments below are listed roughly in the order I arrived at them rather than in order of confidence, and I do not myself know where the problem is, if there is one.

(1) I accept the standard verdict on Frankfurt cases: an agent who acts exactly as he wants to act, and whose action is in no way produced by the counterfactual intervener standing by, is morally responsible and blameworthy for it even though — precisely because of that intervener — he could not have done otherwise. I take this to refute the principle of alternative possibilities.

(2) I reject the flicker-of-freedom reply. The alternatives that survive in such a case — that the agent could have tried to do otherwise, or willed otherwise, or that the prior sign on which the intervener was keyed might simply not have occurred — are too thin to bear any weight. My reason is the robustness thought: an alternative possibility can ground an attribution of responsibility only if it is the sort of thing that could explain why the agent is responsible, and a flicker is not.

(3) What does the work, I think, is the actual sequence: the agent is responsible when the mechanism issuing in his action is moderately responsive to moral and prudential reasons and is a mechanism he has made his own. I am therefore a semicompatibilist — regulative control is not required for moral responsibility, and causal determinism in the actual sequence is not a threat to it.

(4) I also accept, and have used in argument, the claim that the intervener is irrelevant: since he never intervenes, he plays no causal role in the production of the action, and the Frankfurt case is, in every respect that bears on responsibility, just an ordinary intentional wrongdoing. This, I have assumed, is what makes the intuition in (1) trustworthy rather than an artifact of a strange example.

(5) Separately, I find the four-case manipulation argument genuinely forceful: the argument that runs from a Frankfurt-style case, through a case in which the agent's reasons-responsive mechanism is installed by neuroscientists who program him from birth, to a case of ordinary causal determinism, and claims that the only difference between the first and the last is the presence of a counterfactual intervener — a difference that makes no difference to the agent's capacities or to how his action was actually produced. I am therefore inclined to think the intuition in the Frankfurt case is explained by an irrelevant feature and so cannot bear the weight (1) puts on it.

(6) I have also come to think that the dilemma objection to Frankfurt cases is basically correct: if the case is built around a prior sign and the actual sequence is indeterministic, some alternative always remains, whereas if the impossibility of doing otherwise is built into the actual sequence instead, the case is quietly helping itself to determinism. I do not find this troubling, since I accept compatibilism anyway. But I notice that I still cite Frankfurt cases as the consideration that ought to move someone who rejects compatibilism, and I cannot quite say why it should.

(7) I hold that ought implies can, as a constraint on obligation: I cannot be morally required to do what I am unable to do.

(8) Finally, I hold a history condition of a familiar kind: an agent whose reasons-responsive mechanism was installed in him by neuroscientists, or by a childhood of systematic brainwashing, is not morally responsible for what he does on that mechanism's basis — even if, at the moment of action, everything specified in (3) is satisfied.

What I would like from you:

First, tell me which of these are in genuine tension, ordered by severity rather than listed as if equally bad, and for each say precisely which claim would have to be abandoned or qualified and what that qualification costs me elsewhere in the set. I want the mechanism of the conflict — which commitment is doing what, and why it cannot be doing both things at once — not a summary of the surrounding literature.

Second, tell me which pairs only appear to be in tension and dissolve once the right distinction is drawn, and say whether the dissolution depends on my reading a claim one way rather than another.

Third, tell me whether any single revision would clean up more than one of the genuine tensions, and which is the least costly overall revision.

And in particular: can (3) as I have stated it deliver the verdict in (8) at all, or is (8) an additional condition presented in the vocabulary of (3)? I am not asking for references or suggestions for further reading.
