I'm putting together a short "where I stand" document for a grad reading group on personal identity and the self — we've done Dennett's center-of-narrative-gravity stuff, Parfit's reductionism, Galen Strawson's anti-narrativity paper, and some of the narrative self-constitution literature (I think the reality/articulation constraints are Schechtman, but I may be mixing up names, so don't hold me to the attributions). The group is mostly Parfitians plus one guy who keeps interjecting "but what about the phenomenology", and I'd like someone to stress-test my set of views before I present it on Thursday. I'm writing this on the train back from my sister's, so its rough.

Here's roughly where I've landed. (1) Dennett-style: the self is a center of narrative gravity, an abstractum rather than an extra ingredient in the world, with no inner narrator sitting in a Cartesian theater; I'm happy to be called an anti-realist about the self as long as that isn't eliminativism, since abstractions can be real patterns. (2) Parfit-style: identity over time isn't what matters, and in fission-type cases there's no further fact, so "did I survive?" can be empty rather than having a determinate answer — and I also think taking this on board should loosen my grip on my own future, less self-concern and more impartiality. (3) Strawson-style: narrativity is neither psychologically universal (episodic people are real, not confused) nor ethically required, and pressuring people into a coherent life story can be genuinely harmful. (4) But I also want a strong reality constraint on self-narration: I can be wrong about my own past, a self-narrative can be false, and there's a fact of the matter about whether some episode belongs to my life whether or not anyone ever articulates it. (5) I'm a Humean about reasons: no external reasons, nothing normative binds me except through my own desires and projects. (6) Methodologically, I want the metaphysics of the self to be normatively inert — I get suspicious whenever someone extracts an ethical conclusion from a claim about what persons are. (7) Two case verdicts: someone who sincerely but falsely rewrites their autobiography hasn't thereby made it true that they had that past, and I'd call that a kind of self-betrayal even when the new story integrates them better and they're happier for it; and in a case I made up — a woman who lost most of her childhood memory in an accident and rebuilt a childhood out of family stories that later turn out to be mostly invented — I want to say her self-narrative is straightforwardly false rather than that it quietly defined a different self. (8) Finally, a bit of my own gadgetry: the narratives that fix a self don't have to be told by anyone, not even silently to oneself — "unvoiced narratives", I've been calling them, ways of living that are narrative-shaped without being narrated. I mainly use this for infants and animals, not for the adult human cases.

What I'd like is an audit rather than a lecture: go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and for each real tension what would have to give and what that costs me. Please rank them by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, flag anything where one of my stated principles can't actually deliver the verdict I want in (7), and then say which single revision would clean up the most mess. Don't assume I already know which commitment is the weak one — I don't.
