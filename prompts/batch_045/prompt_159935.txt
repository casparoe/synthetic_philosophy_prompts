Next Thursday I'm presenting a chapter of my thesis to the work-in-progress seminar here, and I want to walk in with an actual prep sheet instead of vibes. Can you draft one?

The thesis I'm defending: Thomson's lamp has no answer, but not for the reason people usually give. The story isn't describing an impossible task and it isn't self-contradictory; it's radically underdetermining, in roughly the way a differential equation without boundary conditions is. Call it extension-pluralism: the flip story fixes the lamp's state at every time in the half-open interval and fixes nothing at the limit point; any assignment of a state at the limit point is an equally eligible completion of the very same story, and there's no further fact — not even an unknowable or brute one — about which completion is the right one. I'm arguing this beats the two rivals: the "the description is inconsistent, so no supertask can be performed" answer, and the "there is a determinate terminal fact, we just can't derive it" answer.

I lean on two cases of my own. First, the pre-loaded lamp: the lamp has already been flipped at −1, −1/2, −1/4, … and is not flipped at or after t = 0; you walk in at t = 0 and look at it. Pluralism says the same thing here as in the forward case — nothing settles what you're looking at — and I take that as a virtue, because it suggests the underdetermination isn't about limit points or about finishing an infinite sequence but about the algebra of the operation: an involution applied infinitely many times has no determinate value. Second, the reset-switch lamp: same supertask, except that at the limit point the switch doesn't toggle, it sets the lamp to off. Now the terminal state is determined, and I claim that supports pluralism rather than killing it.

The room: five or six metaphysicians who work on persistence, vagueness and grounding and who think "no fact of the matter" is a heavy-duty posit that owes us a story about what makes it true; two philosophers of physics, one of whom has published on supertasks in classical mechanics and thinks they're physically possible, the other of whom thinks this whole literature ignores that real switches have finite bandwidth; and one philosopher of mathematics who will want everything stated in terms of partial functions, extensions, order types and convergence, and who'll get annoyed if I use "limit" loosely. Our early modern person attends everything and will ask whether any of this is Leibniz's problem about the infinite.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each of these people would actually phrase them — the metaphysician's version of "that's just epistemic", the physicist's "you're predicting genuine indeterminism inside a deterministic model, and anyway no real switch does this", the mathematician's "bivalence plus your pluralism is just inconsistency in a costume" — with a ninety-second reply for each, one thing I should concede inside each reply, and a note on where the reply is softest. Tell me which ones have real force and which I can wave off, which single reply has to land for the chapter to survive, and where I should just say "that's a paper of its own." Everybody in the room knows the literature, so no summaries and no citations — I only need things I can say out loud standing up. And include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks; my guess is it's something about grounding the terminal state in the lamp's earlier states, but I'd rather be wrong here than surprised in the room.
