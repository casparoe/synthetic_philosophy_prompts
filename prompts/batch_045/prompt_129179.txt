I have been asked to give a single ninety-minute evening session on the direction of time and the thermodynamic arrow to a reading group of about twenty scientifically literate adults — retired engineers, a few physicians, several software people — who last month read a popular-science chapter on entropy. They arrived at three beliefs that I have to work with rather than around: that entropy means disorder; that the early universe was hot, dense, and small, and that this is why the past had low entropy; and that the arrow of time is in some way a consequence of the expansion of the universe. Nobody there has studied statistical mechanics formally, but all of them can follow a simple combinatorial count, and all of them will notice, and resent, any gesture that they suspect is concealing an equation.

What I want from you is not the explanation but a plan for delivering it. Please give me:

The dependency structure. Which distinctions have to be in place before the second law can even be stated in the form that does philosophical work, and in what order must the remaining pieces follow? I want the ordering defended in terms of what depends on what — for instance, whether the macro/micro distinction can be introduced before the separation between typicality and dynamics is on the table, and whether there is any step whose placement looks natural but is actually optional.

The one example that carries the session. State what it has to have and what it has to lack: it must be visualizable without apparatus, it must let me draw the distinction between what is improbable and what is impossible, it must not invite the disorder gloss, and it must survive being reused at three different points in the argument rather than being spent in the first ten minutes. Tell me why the two obvious candidates — gas spreading in a box, and a shuffled deck — fail one or another of those requirements, and defend your replacement against them.

The misconception map. I predict these: entropy as disorder; the early universe being hot as the explanation for its being low entropy; the arrow being grounded in the expansion of the universe or in a time-asymmetric fundamental interaction; entropy as something observer-relative or epistemic; and the thought that since decrease is merely improbable, the arrow must be an artifact of our perspective. For each, tell me whether it should be pre-empted before it can form or allowed to surface and answered at the moment it surfaces, and locate it precisely in the sequence. Then name the one misconception I have not listed that you would bet on with this particular audience, and say where you would deal with it.

The point where I expect the session to break. If entropy increase is a matter of typicality rather than dynamics, this group will conclude either that the arrow is merely probabilistic and might locally reverse, or that typicality by itself yields no arrow at all. Show me how to stage this so that it is the payoff of the session rather than its collapse; and tell me whether the asymmetry between prediction and retrodiction — that a low-entropy posit at one temporal end does not by itself license probabilistic reasoning toward the other — belongs in this session at all, and if so where.

What to bracket, and where to say so. The origin of the low-entropy initial condition, cosmology, the connection to memory and to causation, and anything quantum: which of these must I explicitly refuse, and at what point in the hour and a half, so that the refusal reads as a stated boundary of the session rather than as evasion?

The check. One question to end on, plus the reason it discriminates between someone who has the structure and someone who has only acquired the vocabulary.

Please do not write the session itself, and do not suggest readings; I have the background. It is the sequencing, the carrying example, and the timing of each correction that I do not have.
