I am giving a forty-five-minute paper next month at a two-day workshop on democratic innovation, with half an hour of questions afterward, and I want to arrive with a prep sheet rather than a head full of arguments. The paper defends a variant of governance by prediction markets that I call chartered jury futarchy, and I would like the objections I will actually get from this particular room.

The proposal has three parts. First, a charter stage: an elected assembly, on a fixed cycle and never while a market is open, enacts two things only — an operational welfare metric, and a closed menu of enumerated policy options that includes the status quo. Second, a panel stage: a few hundred traders are selected by sortition from the enfranchised population, stratified, serving a fixed term on a fixed stipend, barred from outside positions correlated with the menu, permitted to hear testimony and question experts, and then required to trade conditional contracts rather than to vote. Third, a settlement stage: the option with the highest priced conditional contract is adopted, and the panel is paid at the end of its term in proportion to realized movement in the metric. Nothing outside the panel trades. My thesis is that the standard division of labor in these proposals — citizens decide what is aimed at, traders decide what causes it — is right in outline but leaves the market without any democratic pedigree and the demos without any purchase on consequences, and that closing the roster, salaried rather than capitalized, is what repairs both at once: the charter carries authorship of ends, the panel carries the causal judgment, and because the panel is a sample of the governed rather than a set of investors, the price can be read as a judgment of a demos rather than a signal from capital.

The room is about forty people. Roughly a dozen are deliberative democrats and democratic theorists, mostly hostile to markets as such and mostly convinced that legitimacy requires citizens giving and answering one another's reasons; they are the ones I have to win. Two finance economists work on prediction markets and market microstructure, and one of them has run internal markets inside a firm. One political scientist studies citizens' assemblies and sortition, and is my only natural ally — though I suspect she will think I have borrowed the badge of sortition and discarded what makes it legitimate. One philosopher of science works on social indicators and national wellbeing measurement, and will care about nothing but the metric. And there is a graduate student at the back who works on longtermist risk and will ask about tail outcomes. Nobody in the room is a convert to prediction-market governance.

I already have replies to two objections and do not need them rehearsed unless you think my reply fails: that a price is not a reason (my reply: since the charter fixes ends, the price is purely instrumental and need not function as a reason anyone can accept), and that if the panel deliberates it may as well vote (my reply: the scoring rule separates confidence from preference and penalizes cheap unanimity, which a vote cannot do).

Beyond those, give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do if I handled them badly, each attributed to the person or bloc who will raise it and phrased in that person's vocabulary rather than mine, each with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes rather than defend. Tell me, without hedging, which single objection sinks the paper if I lose it, and which concession would salvage it. And give me the question I am hoping nobody asks.
