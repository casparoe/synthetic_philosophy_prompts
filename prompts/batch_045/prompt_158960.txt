I'm on the three-person pay comittee of a ~50 person worker co-op (a small software consultancy). We've paid everyone the same hourly rate for twelve years and we're finally moving to a banded system, and I'd like us to do it with something better than vibes. Whatever we come up with we'll re-run every year, it has to be runnable in two afternoons by people with no philosophy training, and the output has to be one page that the whole membership can argue with.

Can you write us an actual procedure built on the veil of ignorance and the difference principle? I mean: what we hide from ourselves while we deliberate (we can't literally un-know who does which job — is anonymizing the proposal enough, or does the veil have to also cover our own tastes and how much risk we personally tolerate?), the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure just says no, and where it has to stop and hand the call to a membership vote because the question is one of values rather than facts.

There are three things I know will bite and I want you to settle them, not lay them out as options. Is the thing behind the veil pay only, or the whole bundle — predictability, schedule control, whose work gets recognized and whose is invisible? Is "the worst off" whoever is in the lowest band this year, or whoever ends up worst over a whole working life with us — we have two members with heavy caregiving loads and one with a chronic illness who will never take the high-band work? And the big one: the difference principle is supposed to permit inequalities that are needed to make the worst off better off, incentive effects included, but I don't want that to quietly turn into "anyone who can credibly threaten to leave gets more." What test separates a genuine incentive from bargaining power?

Then run it on three live proposals: (a) lifting the top of the band for infrastructure work because our senior infra person has an outside offer at roughly double, (b) a one-off retention payment to one person, (c) extra pay for a coordination / line-management role nobody wants but that has to be done by someone. At least one of them should come out as something we don't want to hear — I'd rather the procedure can say no to us than be decorative. Please commit: give me the version you'd actually defend and say why, and be explicit about where the procedure will misfire or just paper over a fight we should be having out loud.
