So I fell into a rabbit hole about the international life-evaluation rankings — the ones where you ask a national sample to rate their current life from 0 to 10 against what they themselves picture as the best possible life for them, average it per country, and publish a league table. Enough governments now treat their score in that table as an actual policy target — dedicated offices, budget lines, progress reports sitting next to unemployment and GDP — that it counts as a real institutional practice and not just a fun chart. And I keep feeling like there's something philosophically weird in it that I can't quite localize.

Two things bug me. One: the thing being targeted is a self-anchored judgement, not a read on anybody's mood. People supply their own top of the scale, so a population whose expectations got smaller scores higher at unchanged lived experience. Two: the goal gets stated as topping the ranking, but rank is positional and zero-sum while the underlying score isn't — every country's mean can rise together, only one of them can be first.

So, what does this practice presuppose? What view of wellbeing does it only make sense under — some judgement-based or life-satisfaction view rather than hedonism or desire-satisfaction — and which of those rivals does adopting the target quietly rule out? Does targeting the mean commit them to interpersonal comparability plus something about distribution, or is averaging just an unexamined default that several very different theories would equally license? I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed, and I'd like it split into the parts the practice strictly presupposes versus the parts it merely sits well with, because I suspect a lot of what looks like a commitment is actually underdetermined and a hedonist, a desire theorist, and an objective-list theorist could all sign off on the same office.

Concretely, suppose the wellbeing office has three proposals on the table and we need a principled way to separate improving from gaming: (1) public messaging aimed at shifting what people picture as the best possible life for them; (2) policies aimed at changing who people compare themselves to day to day; (3) concentrating resources on the easiest-to-help people, or more generally shrinking the denominator any other way, since as I understand it the survey samples residents and people who emigrate stop counting. Does the theory the practice actually presupposes have the resources to call any of these cheating? Which ones does it call cheating that I'd feel fine about, and vice versa?

Then the bit I'm most confused about: should a country want to top the table at all? If it swapped the implicit theory for an experienced-affect one — sampling what people feel hour to hour and summing positive minus negative — what concretely changes in the practice, and what would it have to stop doing? And is the wanting-to-be-first part even about wellbeing, or is it a national-prestige thing wearing a wellbeing costume? No citations, I just want the argument. I dont think "it's all Goodhart" is an answer, that's the thing I already believe and it doesn't tell me what to to do with proposal 1.
