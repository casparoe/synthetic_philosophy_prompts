I'm doing a 40 minute brown bag for my team next week — about a dozen backend / distributed systems folks, me included. None of us have any philosophy past a couple of half-remembered blog posts, and honestly half of them already think this whole area is word games. The topic I got voluntold for is the distinction between a mental state being accessible to the rest of the system (available for reasoning, reporting, deciding, controlling action) versus there being something it is like for the subject, and where global workspace theory is supposed to sit relative to that distinction.

I don't need the distinction explained to me. I need a plan for explaining it to them. Concretely:

1. The order the pieces have to come in, and why — what depends on what. My instinct is to start with the accessible-to-the-system notion, because that's the one I can cash out in architecture terms they already have (broadcast, consumers, capacity limits, what downstream modules get to see), and then bring in the "something it's like" bit as whatever that account leaves out. But I'm worried that ordering makes the second half sound like a made-up residue that I'm inventing because my diagram had a gap in it. Argue for or against that ordering.

2. One example that carries the whole thing. It should come out of our world — services, buses, caches, logs, queues, dashboards — and it has to survive being pushed in both directions: I need to be able to use it to make vivid a case where everything is broadcast and widely consumed and yet there's plausibly nothing it's like, and also a case where there is something it's like but the state isn't available to anything (that second direction is the one I can't figure out how to build). Tell me what the example must have and what it must lack. Two things I've already ruled out: nothing with a screen or a dashboard that a person reads, because that instantly smuggles in a little guy watching the display; and nothing like "a private field" or "a feelings enum", because that makes the phenomenal part sound like an implementation detail you could just add in a later sprint. Give me the one to actually use, plus why the obvious alternatives fail, so I know what I'm avoiding.

3. The misconceptions, and the exact point in the sequence where each one should get headed off. My guesses: "global workspace is just a message bus, so we've had conscious services since Kafka"; "if it can talk about its own states it's conscious — we already ship that endpoint"; "so the phenomenal part is just... extra?"; "if there's no test for it the distinction is empty"; and one guy will definately derail into whether the LLM we ship is conscious. Add the ones I'm missing, especially the ones that only appear after they've half-grasped it and started generalising.

4. A check for the last five minutes that tells me they got the distinction rather than just having learned two labels. A concrete system spec they have to sort into the two categories, and where the answer is supposed to be genuinely unsettled, would be ideal.

5. What I should openly refuse to cover at this level, with a one-line way of saying "not going there" that doesn't sound like a dodge. I'm guessing rival theories of consciousness, whether the distinction is metaphysically real, and AI welfare — but tell me what each omission costs me.

6. One thing I made up and want placed correctly: a system with two broadcast buses. Bus A behaves exactly like the workspace story says — limited capacity, competition between modules, feeds memory and report and control. Bus B broadcasts to all the same modules, but nothing it carries is ever written to memory, never reported, and never changes anything downstream — a silent broadcast. I was going to spring this near the end and ask "is the stuff on bus B conscious?", but I can't tell whether it sharpens the distinction or just muddies it. Say whether to use it, where, and what to expect them to answer.

Please don't give me a reading list or citations — I won't read them and it'll just make me grumpy.
