hey — need a second opinion on something I keep going in circles on. It's for some notes I'm writing up for a reading group, nothing formal.

Set up first so were on the same page. Epiphenomenalism: conscious states get produced by physical goings-on in the brain, but they dont themselves cause anything. The usual objection is that this saws off the branch its sitting on. My saying "that hurt" or "I'm consciously seeing red right now" is itself a physical event, caused by physical stuff, never by the hurt or the redness — so the very thing I'm reporting on never gets to make a difference to whether I report it. And then it looks like I dont really know that I'm conscious; I just emit the sentence, and I'd emit it, or something like it, in a world where there was no consciousness at all. My friend calls this the self-stultification objection.

She thinks its a bad argument, and she has this one analogy she comes back to every single time. Conscious states, she says, are like a vestigial status flag in a toy CPU. Say the overflow flag, in an instruction set where no instruction ever reads it: the ALU sets it, deterministically, whenever an addition overflows. Its a real, determinate state of the machine, it carries information, and it does absolutely nothing downstream — nothing consumes it. Now suppose the same instruction also appends a line to a debug log, something like "overflow occurred". That log line is triggered by the ALU too, not by the flag. And yet the log is a perfectly reliable guide to whether the flag is set. So: reliability about X doesn't require being caused by X. Being a sibling effect of X's cause is enough. Apply it: the brain state is the ALU, my conscious state is the flag, my judgment that I'm conscious is the log line, and there's your dissolution. The judgment can be knowledge even though the quale it's about contributes nothing to producing it.

I feel like its doing something sneaky and I cant put my finger on where.

Can you audit it? Specifically: (1) a correspondence table — what's the quale, what's the judgment, what's the brain state, what plays the role of the flag being read, and what plays the role of the engineer who knows the architecture; (2) where the mapping breaks, and — the part I actually want — which of those breaks matter for the conclusion she's after and which are just decoration; (3) whether her conclusion survives, or whether the analogy quietly assumes the thing in dispute. I have a half-formed suspicion that the engineer is carrying a lot of weight: she can check the flag and the log against each other from outside, and the only way to get a reading off a flag that nothing reads is to solder a wire onto it, which wrecks the setup. But I genuinely cant tell if that's a real point or a cheap trick. And (4) if it does collapse, whether there's a better analogy for what the epiphenomenalist actually needs here, or whether the thing being demanded is something no analogy could deliver.

One more thing while you're at it: is there any reading of "the judgment is reliable" on which a lawlike correlation via a common cause is enough for knowledge, even when the target is, by hypothesis, unreachable by every other route? That feels like the crux and I have no idea what to say about it.
