ok so something about how we talk about collapse has been bugging me and I cant quite say what.

Museums, textbooks, documentaries — theyre all fine saying 'the Maya collapsed' somewhere around 900 CE, or 'the Bronze Age collapse', or that Rome fell in 476, or that the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991. One date, one event, a before and an after. But nobody says Russia collapsed in 1917 when the tsar fell, or that France collapsed in 1958 when it swapped republics, or that Britain collapsed when it lost the empire. Egypt got conquered by Persians, then Greeks, then Romans, then Arabs, and no one says Egypt collapsed four times. And then the very same people who say the Maya collapsed will insist, correctly, that the Maya didn't vanish — millions of Maya people are alive right now. The same institutions that put an 'abandoned' label on a site also negotiate with living descendant communities about who the human remains and objects from that site really belong to, which seems to assume the group is still around.

So what does this way of talking presuppose? I mean the practice of picking out some changes as collapses, dating them, and treating them as the kind of thing that needs an explanation — usually an explanation in terms of something having gone wrong: overreach, ecocide, bad management, hubris — while big changes of other kinds get filed as just history. What is supposed to have ended here? A state? A level of social complexity and scale? A population? A language community? An economy? A way of life? Does the word only fit when the change is fast as well as large, so that the same end state reached over 300 years counts as transformation but reached over 30 counts as collapse? And is 'collapse' doing descriptive work at all, or mostly evaluative work, given that we say 'fall of the Berlin Wall' when we're glad about the ending and 'collapse of civilisation' when we aren't?

Test case: a society empties its cities over two or three generations, gives up writing and long-distance trade, keeps its language and religion, and ends up healthier with longer lifespans. Collapse or change? And if you say collapse, tell me what exactly it is that ended.

I guess what I want is the implicit theory: what the practice strictly commits us to, where several quite different theories would license the same way of talking, and what we'd have to rewrite if we dropped the idea that a society is the kind of thing that can come to an end at a datable moment.
