Two friends of mine — one works in philosophical logic, the other in philosophy of language — ended up in an argument after a department colloquium on Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, in which the Tortoise accepts the premises of a simple argument but refuses to accept the conclusion unless each new hypothetical stating that the premises entail it is itself written down as another premise. They went several rounds, neither would concede, and they asked me to referee. I've reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can. I have no settled view of my own here, so please don't read my reconstruction as favouring either side.

(1) NORAH: The puzzle rests on a category mistake, and once you see it the regress evaporates. A premise is a truth-evaluable content that you can accept; a rule of inference is a licence to move, and it is no more truth-evaluable than a chess move is. You cannot discharge a rule by adding it as a line in the notebook, because the new line is just another thing to which the rule would have to be applied — which is exactly why the list grows without end. So the regress is a reductio of the Tortoise's demand, not a discovery about deduction. Carroll's dialogue shows that you cannot treat a rule as a premise. It shows nothing about whether deduction can be justified, because on the only reading where the dialogue gets going, nothing was ever at stake.

(2) ELLIS: The category point is right, but as an answer it's a relabelling. There are two things the Tortoise might be after. It might want a premise that states the rule — call that the joke reading. Or it might want a reason to accept the rule — call that the normative reading. Your distinction handles the joke reading and is silent on the normative one. Say rules are licences; the Tortoise can then ask what makes a licence a good one, and it is asking that, not asking for another line. And if the answer is that nothing makes it good, that we are simply disposed to infer that way, then you have conceded that deduction rests on a brute habit — which is precisely what the Tortoise was needling Achilles about.

(3) NORAH: Grant the normative reading, then. Here is why I think it isn't a demand for anything. Suppose we give the Tortoise not one extra hypothetical but the entire infinite family of them: C0 saying that if A and A implies Z then Z; C1 saying that if A and A implies Z and C0 then Z; and so on without end. And suppose, to be generous, that the Tortoise is not finite in the relevant way — it standingly accepts every member of the family at once, all of them together, so there's no issue about completing an infinite chain of acts. It still cannot reach Z. Every member of the family is a conditional, and a conditional is useless without detachment. So the obstruction isn't the number of premises or the finitude of the agent; it is entirely whether the Tortoise accepts the rule. That shows the normative demand cannot be a demand for more content, and since the only thing anyone could hand over is content, the demand is either empty or asks for something of the wrong kind.

(4) ELLIS: I accept that argument, and I think it does kill the more-premises reading — but it also shows something you don't want. If what's at issue is acceptance of a rule, then acceptance of a rule is the sort of thing that can be in better or worse shape. A student who accepts the rule from all Fs are G and a is F, infer a is G is in good shape; a student who accepts the rule from everyone loves someone, infer there is someone whom everyone loves has taken on a bad licence, and we say what's wrong with it: it doesn't preserve truth. So there is a standard for licences, and the Tortoise is asking after the standard, not for another entry in the book. And the standard can't be that the rules fix the meanings of the connectives, since stipulating rules is cheap. I can introduce a connective — call it ⊗ — by two stipulations: from A infer A ⊗ B, and from A ⊗ B infer B. Anyone who has mastered those two rules has mastered the meaning of ⊗ on the inferentialist story, and can now get from any premise to any conclusion. Rules that are merely stipulated confer no warrant; some further condition on the rules is doing the work, and naming that condition is answering the Tortoise.

(5) NORAH: Agreed that licences answer to a standard — truth-preservation — but now watch where this goes. To show that modus ponens preserves truth you have to reason, and the reasoning uses modus ponens. So any vindication we can offer is circular. But there are two very different circularities. To justify a rule by assuming it as a premise is premise-circularity, and that is worthless, and it is what Carroll's dialogue is a picture of. To use a rule in the course of arguing for a general claim about that same rule is only rule-circularity, and that is not worthless: a reasoner can be entirely competent with the conditional and still not know whether every application of the rule preserves truth, so a single step of reasoning with the rule can establish something she did not already have. Notice too that the Tortoise has already accepted that A implies Z; it plainly grasps the conditional, so it isn't in a position to press a scepticism about meaning while helping itself to that premise.

(6) ELLIS: The premise/rule-circularity distinction is where I get off, and I think your own argument in (3) undermines the standard you appeal to in (5). Rule-circularity could only be informative if you could run the vindication while suspending the rule, and you can't: suspend modus ponens and there is no arguing left. Worse, your (3) shows that no propositional content can do the work of the rule, because every content needs detaching. But that truth-preservation modus ponens preserves truth is itself a propositional content — a fact about the rule, not the rule. So it is exactly the sort of thing that, by your own argument, cannot help the Tortoise detach anything. My conclusion is therefore not that deduction is unjustified. It's that no justification could take the shape the Tortoise demands, namely a stock of accepted truths from which acceptance of Z follows by more of the same. If anything meets the challenge, it will not be an argument at all.
