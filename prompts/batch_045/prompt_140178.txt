I write the public-facing text for a museum of archaeology — wall panels, object labels, the audio guide — and I do it with no training in the field; my degree is in English, and everything I know about these periods comes from our own catalogue and from reading around the subject on my own. Our current exhibition covers several past societies that went through sharp breaks, and I keep hitting the same problem: I don't know when I am allowed to use the word 'collapse'.

The word does a great deal of work in a label. It tells visitors that something went wrong, that people suffered, that the people themselves were in some sense to blame, and that the story ended there. 'Transformation' tells them the opposite on all four counts, and it has started to feel like the polite word that quietly removes the suffering. Our curators sign off on the final text, and a liaison appointed by a descendant community reads anything concerning her ancestors, so the word has to survive two very different kinds of scrutiny. I need something I can run in about twenty minutes per label, using only what is on my desk, and simple enough that our education officer can apply it to school worksheets without me standing next to her.

What I want is a decision procedure rather than an essay: the questions to ask, in order, about a given case; what would count as a good enough answer to each for someone who cannot go to a library; the points at which the answer is a clear yes or no and I can stop; and — this matters most — the points at which the question is really one of values, where the procedure should stop and hand the decision to a named person rather than pretend to settle it. Please also say plainly what the procedure cannot tell me.

Three distinctions I have been playing with, none of which I can get to do the work I want:

1. Loss of capacity versus loss of scale: people no longer being able to do something at all — read, quarry and move stone, sail beyond the reef, feed the same number of mouths — as against people still able to do it but only in smaller units or over a smaller area, a city of thousands becoming villages of a hundred.

2. Whether what was lost could be rebuilt from inside: if a skill or a system disappears, could the people who remain recover it from what they still know, or would it have to come back from outside? My sense is that the second is a deeper break even when fewer people died, but I cannot say why I think so.

3. Whether the break was available to the people living through it as a loss: someone who remembers the temples being maintained undergoes something her grandchild does not, and a later archaeologist sees something different again. I wonder whether a collapse should be dated to when it was felt rather than to when the evidence shows it, and whether a change nobody at the time seems to have mourned can count as one.

Tell me which of these, if any, belong in the procedure, which should be dropped as unworkable for someone in my position, and which distinctions I have missed that it needs instead.

Then run it on three cases. (i) A small, isolated island whose people, as I understand it, stopped carving and moving their large stone figures and had cleared most of their trees; visitors arrive already knowing it as the story of a society that wrecked its own environment, and the draft label says collapse. I suspect the word is doing marketing work. (ii) A lowland city civilisation of the sort people know from documentaries: the great centres were given up over a century or two and the royal inscriptions stop, while the farmers, the language and the descendants plainly continue — our liaison has asked that we not call this a collapse, since it suggests her people vanished, and my instinct is that she is right about the people while I still need some word for what happened to the cities. (iii) A palace-based economy around a sea where, within a couple of generations, the big buildings are burnt, the administrative script stops being used altogether, and for some centuries nobody in the region writes anything down, until writing returns in a different form borrowed from elsewhere.

At least one of these should come out with an answer I would rather not have. Tell me which, and tell me how to write the label anyway.
