Me and two friends got into a huge arguement at a board game night last week and I've been going in circles on it ever since. It started from that old thought experiment that gets posted around online -- would you rather have one person tortured for fifty years, or have some astronomically huge number of people each get a tiny dust speck in their eye, the kind that's a bit annoying for half a second and then you totally forget about it. We'd all heard the standard moves before ("obviously the torture", "no, at some big enough number the specks win", "but aren't you just adding up a bunch of zeros") and we weren't getting anywhere, so we started messing with the setup to make it harder, and now I genuinely don't know what I think.

The version we ended up arguing about is this. Say the tiny harms aren't dust specks but something nobody can perceive at all: each one takes a microscopic bite out of your future capacity to enjoy things -- it lowers your hedonic ceiling by a millionth of a percent, or shaves a sliver off what your ears can pick up. There's no moment of discomfort, nobody ever notices, and there's no number of these after which the person is having a worse life in any way they could detect or ever care about. But they're real in the boring physical sense: the machinery is degraded, the bit of you that would have produced a little enjoyment now produces imperceptably less. And it's spread one-per-person across a gigantic population, no repeats, no comparisons between people. On the other side is one person tortured for fifty years. That's the whole menu: you pick which happens, no probabilities, no third option, no trickery.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people arguing about this, roughly as equals -- one saying you should prevent the torture no matter how huge the number gets, one saying there's some number at which all the tiny stuff wins? Please don't make either of them a pushover. The torture side should have to actually handle the point that if each micro-harm is a genuine (if imperceptible) reduction, then refusing to add them up looks like just letting yourself be dazzled by a vivid image, or like quietly assuming that only what's experientially noticeable can count as a harm -- and if imperceptibility doesn't matter, what do we say about all the sub-perceptual wear and tear everyone is accumulating right now from just living an ordinary day? The aggregation side should have to handle the flip side: that any view on which enough invisible nothings beat a torture seems committed to saying basically everything we do is a moral catastrophe, and it needs some principled place to stop. I'd especially like them to grapple with that middle-looking option where the tiny stuff does get worse the more of it there is, but can never, at any quantity, outweigh something as bad as torture -- if one of them lands there, have the other press hard on what could make that true rather than just a convenient place to draw the line. Have them talk like smart people who've argued with each other before, not like they're giving a presentation, and let them shift a little or at least pin down where the real disagreement actually is by the end. Start straight in on the first line of dialogue -- no intro, no stage directions, no commentary before or after.
