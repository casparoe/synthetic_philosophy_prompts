You are a professional philosopher and decision theorist working on the rationality of sequential choice. Think the problem through carefully before answering: begin by mapping the dialectical options and noting which premises carry the weight, and flag any substantive assumption you rely on.

A friend and I have just spent a long evening disagreeing about money pump arguments and diachronic rationality. Take the case where on Monday I would pay to secure an opera ticket for Saturday, and come Saturday I would pay to swap it for the cinema. Her view is that the money pump argument works against synchronic intransitivity — cycle me through three options and I end where I began, poorer — but shows nothing about changing one's mind over time. Her analogy is that a person across time is like a committee, or a relay team: each time-slice is a competent agent with a coherent view of what is best, each trade in the supposed pump is one that its members freely accept at the time, and what looks like a loss is simply the cost of living in time. If there is a defect anywhere, she says, it is myopia — failing to bind oneself to a plan and sticking to it — not the preferences themselves.

I pressed her with what I called a money fountain: assuming, as these arguments usually do, that being willing to pay a little to move one way means being willing to be paid a little to move back, anyone who can be pumped can also be taken round the same cycle in the other direction and end up with the same bundle and more money, so exploitability as such cannot be what makes a preference structure irrational, and the argument must be trading on something else. She replied that this supports her instead: in the diachronic case there is no determinate same bundle to come back to, because what counts as the same outcome is precisely what the two time-slices disagree about, so the fountain never gets going — and that, she said, is exactly the disanalogy she was pointing to.

Audit her analogy. Give the table of correspondences — what plays the committee or team, the trades, the exploiter, whoever bears the loss, the plan — say where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that do real work in this dispute from the decorative ones. Then tell me whether her conclusion survives, whether my fountain objection bites against the synchronic argument, the diachronic argument, or both, and what a better analogy for a person across time would be, if there is one.
