hey — I run a little reading group (not a seminar, just people who like arguing) and I've been chewing on the "what even is an emotion" question for months. I want someone to poke holes in my profile before I present it, because its been nagging at me that some of this might not fit together. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Emotions are perceptions of value, not judgments and not bare feelings. To be afraid is for something to show up as fearsome, the way the Müller-Lyer lines show up as unequal even when you know better. I'm not a judgmentalist (Stoics, and the modern descendents of that) and I'm not a Jamesian either.
2. An emotion's correctness is a matter of fittingness, not truth — there's nothing more to fear's being correct than the situation's actually being fearsome.
3. Recalcitrant emotion is the datum that sells me on all this: I judge the glass-floored skywalk is safe and my heart still hammers. That's a perceptual illusion — content that outruns belief — not a contradiction in my head, and it's exactly the pattern perception gives you and judgment doesn't.
4. The phenomenology has two separable parts: the presentational bit (the world looking fearsome) and the bodily bit (heart, gut, throat). The second is just interoception, a sensation that doesn't represent anything.
5. Emotions are the same kind of state across species and development. A rat freezing at cat-smell, or a pre-verbal infant at the visual cliff, is frightened in the full sense — not "proto"-frightened.
6. We can be blameworthy for emotions — the racist flash of fear in the elevator, the envy at a friend's luck — and not merely for what we do about them afterwards.
7. Verdict on a made-up case: suppose a drug (call it "esthesiol," I'm inventing it) knocks out the interoceptive channel but leaves belief and the presentational component untouched. On the skywalk with esthesiol on board I want to say: still afraid, the fear still just as fitting or unfitting, you just can't feel it.

Does this hang together? I have a vague feeling that (1) and (6) can't both be true given that I also think emotions aren't up to us directly, but definately don't trust that instinct and I don't know which of these I'd give up. Go through the set, rank the tensions by how bad they actually are, tell me which pairs only look bad, and for each real one say what would have to go and what it'd cost me.

Also: separate what's more or less settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this — I don't want to walk in presenting a contested view as the consensus — and flag where you're confident versus where you're just repeating a party line. Keep the whole thing under 500 words, short paragraphs, no bullet soup.
