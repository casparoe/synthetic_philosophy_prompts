Im trying to write down what I should even be demanding of a theory of rational belief under uncertainty before I read another round of people talking past each other. Keynes territory: the cases where you dont have a base rate and cant get one.

The purpose I care about, so adequacy is relative to it: the theory should be good enough to guide somebody facing a genuinely new risk -- an insurer pricing a liability that has no loss history at all, someone deciding whether to move their family to a country whose political settlement may not survive the decade, a government building infrastructure against climate scenarios the models dont agree on. Not good enough to be elegant, good enough to tell that person what to do and why they should trust it.

So: write me the desiderata. What a theory of this kind has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a verdict on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the tastes of one camp. Here is the messy list I have so far -- reorganize it, split things, merge things, throw things out, add the ones Ive missed:

- It has to mark some real difference between risk (you have numbers, or a defensible way to get them) and uncertainty (you dont), and the marker cant just be that the agent feels unsure. Motivating case: the urn with a known 50/50 composition versus the urn whose composition you know nothing about -- what exactly is different, and why does the difference survive the fact that both urns force you to act?
- It has to say what rationality requires when you genuinely cant put a number on it. Is a number mandatory, or may the output be an interval, or a set of admissible probabilities, or no number at all -- and if no number, then what do you do, and how is that not just panic?
- Seperate slots for (i) how confident you are and (ii) how much that confidence rests on the evidence you actually have. Case: a drug given to ten patients, six recover; the same drug given to a thousand, six hundred recover. Same ratio, very different amount of evidence. I want the theory to say whether slot (ii) is allowed to change what you should do, or whether it is merely a feeling of comfort that a rational agent should ignore.
- Something about where the state space comes from: being uncertain about the model versus being uncertain within a model. My worry is that this distinction is fake, because which model is right is itself just another proposition you can have a probability about. So tell me whether distinguishing structural uncertainty is a genuine desideratum or a prejudice of the anti-Bayesian camp.
- It has to handle the ambiguity cases where people prefer known odds to unknown ones. But I genuinely dont know whether reproducing that preference is a requirement a theory must meet or whether a good theory is allowed to explain it away as a bias -- give me your view, with the reasoning.
- It has to say how confidence combines across independent matters when there are no numbers to multiply, and whether independence is even something the agent can be entitled to assume in the cases I described above.
- It owes something to an agent who cannot enumerate the possibilities: uncertainty about what your options even are, outcomes nobody has thought of yet. Is that a separate desideratum or just a special case of the model-uncertainty one?
- It is not allowed to smuggle in what it is supposed to be earning: no quiet assumption that the future resembles the past, or that unseen cases are drawn from the same distribution as the seen ones. But it also is not allowed to conclude that nothing can be said. Whatever it does with induction has to be done honestly, and I want you to say whether those two constraints leave any room at all.

The part I actually want, though: which subset of these cannot be had together. My guess is that representing confidence without numbers, giving a compositional rule for combining uncertainties, and yielding determinate action guidance are jointly unsatisfiable, but argue it properly, or swap in the triple that is really in trouble. Also tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking candidates out -- the one that excludes the most otherwise-decent theories. If the answer is that the whole list is only satisfiable by collapsing uncertainty back into risk, say that and tell me which requirement to drop to avoid it.

Keep it short at the end: a quick scorecard, table is fine, for a strict subjectivist Bayesian with one precise prior, an imprecise-probability or set-of-priors view, a Keynes-style logical-relation account with a seperate notion of evidential weight, and a maximin or robust-decision-making approach. Two or three lines each, the criteria are what Im paying for.
