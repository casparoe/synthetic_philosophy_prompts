I am refereeing a book manuscript on the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I would like your help with its final chapter, which the author announces but has not yet written.

The manuscript's framing is the familiar one: on a fitting-attitude or buck-passing account, for an object to have a value property is for there to be reasons of the right kind to respond to it in the relevant way, and the difficulty is that there also appear to be reasons to take up an attitude which do nothing to make that attitude fit its object — the stock example being a demon who will torture you unless you admire him. The book's organising claim is that the right/wrong kind distinction is not drawn in the same way for every attitude. For attitudes such as belief, fear and amusement, only considerations of the relevant kind — truth, danger, funniness — are right-kind. For attitudes that are forms of address to a person, such as blame, resentment, gratitude, admiration and trust, the admissible considerations include what is owed, due or called for in the relation. The author is also explicit that for this second class it is what the attitude constitutes that matters, not what it causes.

The verdicts she has already committed herself to:

(a) A demon will torture you unless you admire him: wrong kind.
(b) A prisoner's reason to believe that he will soon be released is that the belief would make his sentence bearable and improve his prospects at a parole hearing, although the evidence points the other way: wrong kind.
(c) Fear of a large dog straining at its lead a few feet away: right kind.
(d) Gratitude to a colleague whose small favour was done purely from self-interest, on the ground that gratitude would cement a collaboration that will produce excellent work: wrong kind.
(e) Resentment of someone who has since acknowledged the wrong in specific detail, made costly repair, and asked that the resentment be withdrawn: right kind — and, she insists, right kind even granting that the original wrong is unchanged and that continuing to resent him would be costly only to you.
(f) A moral reason not to believe, on the strength of a sound statistical generalisation, that a colleague of yours is likely to fail, since applying it to her would fail in the respect she is owed: wrong kind.
(g) A reason to believe that your department intends to deal with you fairly, where the reason is that believing this is partly constitutive of your continuing to work there as a colleague rather than as someone quietly assembling a grievance: wrong kind.

The case the final chapter is to handle is this. You and a colleague have jointly undertaken to direct a shared laboratory. The evidence about her reliability is mixed and does not settle the matter. You are offered the following as a reason to trust her with the lab's shared data and its joint decisions: not that the evidence supports her trustworthiness, and not that trusting her would produce better outcomes, but that the undertaking you have both entered is partly constituted by mutual trust — that to withhold trust while acting as if you had it would misdescribe the relation you are actually in, and that maintaining a standing demand for evidence would be a refusal of the undertaking itself rather than merely an inefficient way of carrying it out.

How will she rule? I would like you to set out the two or three candidate rules that fit verdicts (a) through (g) equally well, say what each of them predicts about the laboratory case, give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and name the further case that would separate whichever rules agree here. If one of the rules fits the earlier verdicts only by straining one of them, say which, and say what that strain consists in. If you think her own precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, tell me which verdict I should press her to adopt in the chapter and what principle each choice would commit her to. Finally, she states without argument that the chapter will extend the account to what she calls proleptic reasons — reasons that make their object fit rather than merely respond to its fitness, as in the suggestion that trusting someone can be what makes her trustworthy — so tell me what each of your candidate rules commits her to there, and whether any of them handles that extension more naturally than the others.
