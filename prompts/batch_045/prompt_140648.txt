I've got a running argument with my mate Dave about whether history is basically chaos or whether the big stuff mostly happens anyway. He's all for-want-of-a-nail; I think big outcomes are usually overdetermined, there's nearly always another road. Neither of us has any philosophy or history background, we've just read around a bit online, so I want something we can actually test rather than more opinions.

Can you build me a case to spec? Invent a country (not a real one) and give me one tiny event nobody at the time would ever have written down — a clerk with a headache, a ferry that sailed late, a letter put in the wrong tray — plus one big structural outcome twenty or thirty years later: a border, a state religion, a language policy, who owns the land. Not just who won a battle.

Here's the bit I've been chewing on, which I call the shadow route. I want the nail to be a real cause of the chain that actually happened — not a bit of colour while the economy does all the work — but I also want the same big outcome to have turned up anyway within about a generation, by a completly different chain that nobody took because the first one fired first. So the nail is load-bearing and dispensable at the same time. I don't think that's impossible; I think it needs a narrow bottleneck somewhere, plus a slow background pressure already pushing towards the same place, with the nail landing in the bottleneck.

Constraints: keep it realistic, no weather miracles, and no genius or villain doing the work — no single person's temper decides anything. The shadow route should land on roughly the same shape of outcome, not a photocopy of it, and it should exist because of the background pressure rather than because you say so at the end. And please don't stipulate the verdicts: I want them falling out of the setup. Also, no books or historians to look up, I've got nothing but this conversation.

Five numbered items, each one or two sentences, nothing before item 1 and nothing after item 5:

1. The invented setting, the tiny event and the big outcome.
2. The chain, link by link, from nail to outcome.
3. What Dave's chaos reading says about the case and what my overdetermination reading says, and why each gets the verdict it does.
4. Which single feature is doing the work, what happens if you remove it in each direction, and the best rescue a chaos person could try.
5. Your own committed answer: did the nail matter, yes or no, with the reason — and the one link in the chain you'd least like to have to defend to Dave.
