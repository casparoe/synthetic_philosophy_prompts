My question concerns the combination problem for the micropsychist version of panpsychism, and in particular a constraint on that problem that I have not seen addressed in the introductory discussions. Let me lay out what I want.

First, briefly and cleanly: the taxonomy. Distinguish the subject-summing version (how many micro-subjects yield one macro-subject) from the palette, or quality-space, version (how meagre micro-qualities yield the rich phenomenal palette I enjoy), and set both against the question of grain (what, at the fundamental level, is the bearer of micro-experience, and does physics supply a non-arbitrary grain at all, given that the candidates are things like particles, field values, and quantum states). Second, the constraint I care about, which I will call partition-invariance. If a bridging law takes a collection of micro-subjects to a macro-subject, then consider the micro-subjects that currently realize my experience, and partition them into two arbitrary halves, or into a hundred arbitrary clusters, and then fuse the results. It seems the law either has to be indifferent to how I do the grouping, in which case the intermediate clusters are themselves subjects and my experience comes out the same either way, or it has to privilege one partition, in which case whether I am a single subject depends on a gerrymandered grouping of the underlying micro-subjects. And if every intermediate fusion is a subject, then for N micro-subjects there are vastly more candidate subjects than there are micro-subjects, all of them overlapping. Is partition-invariance a real constraint or a pseudoproblem, and has anyone earned the right to violate it?

Here I want your judgment about the physical analogies, used critically. The additivity of charge or mass is indifferent to how you group the carriers, which suggests the constraint is cheap to satisfy.
