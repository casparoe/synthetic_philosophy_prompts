I'm writing a novel and have backed myself into a corner about state apologies, and I'm hoping you can help me find the principle I keep fumbling toward. The set-up: a small fictional country (my editor hates the name, but let's call it Vessia) has an institution called the Reckoning — once a year a citizens' assembly picks one historical wrong, and the state apologises for it in a televised ceremony where the text is read aloud not by the head of state but by someone chosen by lot from the population, on the theory that a nation should apologise to itself rather than be apologised for by its politicians. My protagonist is the junior civil servant who drafts these things, and about halfway through the book she starts refusing some of the commissions, and I need to know whether her refusals track anything real or whether I've just handed her my politics.

Here are my gut verdicts, case by case. (a) The new democracy apologises for a massacre carried out by the junta it replaced fifty years ago; the perpetrators are dead, the survivors' grandchildren are alive. Obviously fine, maybe required. (b) The state apologises for a pogrom carried out ninety years ago by a private mob, where the police stood aside and no one was ever charged. Also fine, and more apt than I'd have guessed, even though the state didn't do it. (c) It apologises for a sterilisation programme that was legal at the time, popular, and administered by doctors who thought they were helping. Fine, and I don't think 'it was legal then' helps the state at all. (d) It apologises for a famine caused by a policy whose authors genuinely didn't foresee the deaths. Fine — intent seems irrelevant to me. (e) It apologises for a wrong committed four hundred years ago by a completely different polity on the same territory, with no institutional continuity and a mostly different population. This one feels hollow, a bit of a stunt. (f) It apologises for a detention system it is currently expanding. Worse than silence — a decoy. (g) It apologises in advance for the displacement a dam will cause, and then builds the dam anyway. I find this more offensive than (f), and I can't say why, since both are apology-plus-ongoing-harm; it's something about the apology functioning as a pre-paid licence. (h) It apologises with generous reparations but names no wrong and no one responsible. Diminished — that's a settlement, not an apology. (i) Descendants of the victims walk out and refuse to accept it. Still counts, but something's missing. (j) The pair I can't reconcile: I genuinely don't think an apology is better for having been politically costly to the leader who gave it (a cheap, popular apology seems just as good to me), and yet I'm attached to the sortition bit in my novel — it matters to me who speaks, and I recoil at the president reading it. And then (k), the one I go back and forth on: nobody remembers the wrong, there are no descendants, no group still identifies as the victims, and the surviving records name no one. Is there anything left to apologise for?

My half-formed rule, which I don't trust, is that a state apology is apt only when some linking relation holds between today's state and the past wrong, and when the apology does something no other act — compensation, prosecution, repeal — could do; when the second condition fails, the apology is redundant at best and a decoy at worst. But I can't cash out the linking relation, and (e) makes me think I've smuggled in something about national continuity I'd be embarrassed to defend out loud. What I'd like back: a principle, or two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that a character could say it without sounding like a windbag; the case that motivates each clause; verdicts on cases I haven't thought of (a wrong whose victims' own leaders organised it; a wrong done to a group that no longer wants to be counted as a group; an apology issued by a court or a truth commission instead of a government; one where neither the state that did it nor anyone who remembers it still exists); and an honest account of which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate and which you'd overturn. Please also separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested, and flag anywhere my gut is probably just my politics in a philosophical costume — I've got a bad habbit of writing characters who sound more authoritative than the material supports, and I'd rather know where I'm bluffing.
