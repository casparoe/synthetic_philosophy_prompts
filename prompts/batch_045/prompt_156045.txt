Ok so I want you to make the strongest case you can for the NIMBY side, but not the version everyone already knows how to knock down.

Constraints: don't lean on property values, and don't lean on the "they bought in expecting low density, so changing the rules is unfair to them" line — I've heard it and it doesnt move me. Don't lean on local democracy either, since the obvious reply is that everyone locked out never got a vote. I want the argument to still work if the incumbent residents' only real stake is that they've built a life in that particular place.

Here's the idea I keep circling: the right to the city might not be a right to *this* city. A would-be resident's interest is in somewhere affordable with decent opportunities, and plenty of places could satisfy it, while the people already there have an interest in the continuation of a specific shared life on specific blocks — friends, the school, the walk to the store — that can't be satisfied anywhere else. If the interests differ in that structural way, maybe headcount or total welfare is the wrong metric, and the locals' claim is stronger even tho way fewer people hold it.

Turn that into an actual argument for why current inhabitants, not developers or newcomers, get to decide what gets built. Then go at it honestly: is this just "I got mine" with nicer framing? does the non-fungibility point really track anything, or can outsiders have place-specific attachments too (the neighborhood you grew up in before you got priced out, diaspora, people returning home)? and what does the view commit you to about a neighborhood whose character was produced by exclusion or redlining in the first place?
