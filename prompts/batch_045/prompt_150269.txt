I am an evolutionary biologist — my own work is on the evolution of cooperation, on niche construction, and on how much of a behavioural phenotype you should attribute to a genome versus to a developmental environment — and I have recently been reading Mencius and the Xunzi in translation, with no Chinese and no secondary literature. The dispute between them about human nature reads to me like a dispute I ought to be able to settle, or at least to restate in terms I trust, and I cannot quite do it. I would like you to do the translation for me, and then tell me honestly what the translation cost.

So that we are not relying on your recollection of the texts, here is where I have got to. Both sides share a great deal: both think there is something given (xing) and something achieved by effort (wei); both think sagehood is in principle open to anyone; both think cultivation, teaching, and the right environment are indispensable and that most people fail. Their slogans differ — Mencius: human nature is good; Xunzi: human nature is bad — but I am increasingly unsure the slogans are where the disagreement lives. Number these as I have them, because I want a claim-by-claim mapping:

1 (Mencius). Everyone is born with four incipient responses — alarm and compassion at another's suffering; a sense of shame and aversion; deference and a readiness to yield; a sense of what fits and what does not — and these are the sprouts of the virtues, not the virtues themselves. They need tending, and tending them is the whole of moral education.

2 (Mencius). The reaction to seeing a child about to fall into a well is not aimed at anything: not at currying favour with the parents, not at a reputation in the village, not at the unpleasantness of the cry. It is immediate and is not a means to an end.

3 (Mencius). Failure is loss, starvation, or inattention, not absence. His parable of the mountain whose fine trees were cut down and grazed bare, and which nevertheless puts out shoots again when it is rested, and his parable of the barley that comes up evenly and diverges only because of soil, rain, and effort, both say the endowment is real, uniform, and damageable — and recoverable.

4 (Mencius). Against Gaozi, who held that nature is simply what is alive, that it is neither good nor bad, and that rightness is external: Mencius argues that human agreement runs deeper than we credit. Mouths agree about flavours, ears about sounds, eyes about beauty; so too, he insists, hearts must agree about what is fitting and right, and the sage is only the first to arrive where the rest of us were going. What is allotted and circumstantial he refuses to call nature.

5 (Xunzi). Nature is precisely what cannot be learned or achieved by working at it; artifice is what can. Left alone, the raw endowment produces disorder, because desires are many and the goods that satisfy them are few, so unregulated seeking becomes contention. The famous images are of crooked wood that must be steamed and pressed straight on a frame, and of blunt metal that must be ground on a whetstone.

6 (Xunzi). Goodness is accumulated artifice: ritual and rightness as the sages made them, which do not extinguish desire but feed it and give it measure, distributing what is scarce so that wanting and having can be reconciled. And since the capacity to recognise and to practise this is itself part of what everyone has, the man in the street could in principle become a sage like Yu — though whether he does is another matter.

My vocabulary, which I use daily and which you should not condescend to: adaptation, exaptation and by-product; species-typical design; selective regime and fitness consequence; canalisation versus plasticity; sensitive periods and developmental entrenchment; reaction norms and genotype–environment interaction; developmental systems theory, on which the "innate" is not a preformed trait but a property of how reliably a trait appears across a range of developmental conditions; gene–culture coevolution and cumulative cultural inheritance; niche construction, where the organism's activity alters the selective environment its descendants develop in; and multilevel selection, where what evolves within a group and what evolves between groups pull in opposite directions.

What I want, in about 1,100 words of prose: take claims 1–6 one at a time and restate each in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can, resisting the temptation to improve it. Then give me the residue report I actually care about — which claims survive translation cleanly, which survive only by adding something my vocabulary smuggles in that the text did not say, and which cannot be expressed in it at all; and for each thing that cannot be expressed, your judgement on whether that is a poverty in my framework, a piece of vagueness or wordplay in the text that was never a clear claim to begin with, or a genuine mismatch for which neither side is at fault. Do not flatter my framework by finding a counterpart where there is none, and do not flatter the texts by treating a slogan as though it were a hypothesis.

Two further questions, which I suspect are the same question. First: the word translated "good". There is nothing in my vocabulary of that shape. I can talk about what is adaptive, what is functional, what organisms pursue, what conduces to the flourishing of a kind — and Mencius's claims look to me, on a good day, like a perfectionist claim about the last of these rather than an empirical claim about species-typical moral responsiveness. Say which reading you think the material in 1–4 supports, and what it costs me if it is the perfectionist one. Second: if that is right, then the disagreement may not be about the endowment at all, but about where the line between the organism and its niche should be drawn — and my own framework, via niche construction and developmental systems theory, is in the business of saying that line is drawn arbitrarily and only for convenience. So: does my vocabulary adjudicate this dispute, or does it dissolve it? Is dissolving it a result or a failure to engage? And run the translation in reverse for me: take the claim that a biologist would actually defend — that human moral responsiveness is a suite of partly canalised, culturally elaborated dispositions whose content is overwhelmingly inherited from a constructed niche — render it into the xing/wei/xin vocabulary of these two, and tell me which of them would have owned it, and whether he would have been right to. There is also a later view I was told about, attributed to Yang Xiong, that nature is a mixture of good and bad and that cultivation determines which grows; if it maps onto my vocabulary more comfortably than either of theirs does, say so and say what that tells us about whether this was ever an empirical dispute.
