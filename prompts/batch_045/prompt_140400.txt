I chair the investment committee of a small regional drainage and flood-risk authority. We rank twenty to thirty capital proposals a year — levee raises, pump replacements, voluntary buyouts, lead service line replacement — under a legal mandate that benefits to residents who have not been born yet must be counted. Our current practice is crude: discount everything at a single 5 per cent "market" rate and ignore whatever falls after year fifty. Three of us, none an economist, have about an hour per proposal, and our recommendations go to an elected board that must defend them in public and could be challenged in court, so I need a written procedure we can rerun, not case-by-case intuition.

Please give me that procedure in four parts. First, a screening step that separates the four things people conflate inside a discount rate — the productivity of capital (a dollar not spent now could instead earn a return for residents), the expectation that future residents will be richer so an extra dollar matters less to them, genuine risk that the benefit never arrives at all (including the risk that the authority itself will not exist to deliver it), and pure time preference, meaning weighting a benefit less purely because it comes later — and that makes us state, for each proposal, which of those we are actually applying. Second, a consistency check on pure time preference specifically: if we discount a health or safety benefit because it arrives in 2060 rather than 2030, would we equally accept discounting that same benefit if it arrived in 2060 to a child born this year rather than to a sixty-year-old alive today? It seems to me that a positive pure rate commits us to saying yes, and I want the procedure to force us to confront that. Third, rules about what gets discounted at all: whether lives, health and irreplaceable assets should share the money rate or run on a separate schedule, and how to treat a benefit whose recipients' existence depends on our own decision, since if we build the levee people move into the protected area and if we don't they never live here. Fourth, explicit stopping rules: where the procedure returns a hard no, where it returns a number, and where it must stop and hand the choice to the board as a value judgment the board has to own in public — including how to handle truncation at some horizon year and our uncertainty about what the right rate will be in fifty years.

Then run it on three live proposals and write up what we would put in front of the board. One: replacing the remaining lead service lines in the oldest part of the district, high cost now against health benefits spread over roughly forty years to today's residents and to the families who will occupy those houses later. Two: a voluntary buyout of a flood-prone neighborhood, large cost now against avoided damage and emergency response over eighty years, in a part of the district whose population is projected to shrink. Three: deferring a scheduled spillway upgrade for two budget cycles to hold this year's rate increase down, which shifts a low-probability, high-consequence failure risk forty to sixty years out and which the board is strongly inclined to defer. I expect the procedure to tell us something we would rather not hear in at least one of these; state that plainly, and state plainly what the procedure cannot settle for us.
