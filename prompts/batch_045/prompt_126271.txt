I have a fifty-minute seminar next week with a first-year PPE cohort: eighteen students, no economics yet, but they did a short game-theory module last term and can read a two-by-two payoff matrix and spot a dominant strategy. The session covers the tragedy of the commons and public goods together, which is the pairing I always find hardest to teach, because the two topics get delivered as neighbours and the students leave having fused them into one thing. They will have read a two-page Hardin excerpt beforehand — the herdsman parable. I don't want the explanation; I want a plan for delivering it, and I want the plan to be built around the dependencies rather than around a list of headings.

Three things in particular.

First, the ordering, given as a dependency structure: which idea has to be in place before which other idea can land, and what specifically fails if I invert the order. The ordering has to do real work on a distinction I think the session lives or dies by, and which I've been calling the two-valves picture: a shared resource has an appropriation margin (a valve on the way out, where each user's take imposes a cost on everyone) and a provision or maintenance margin (a valve on the way in, where each user's contribution benefits everyone). The canonical tragedy opens only the first; the canonical free-rider problem opens only the second; structurally they are the same thing with the sign of the externality reversed. I want the plan to say where in the sequence the two valves get introduced, whether they can be introduced together or whether one has to come first, and — importantly — whether a remedy aimed at one valve can leave the other untouched or make it worse. Don't assert that as a general law; show me a case where it happens, so I can say something honest if a student asks.

Second, the example that carries the whole session. I want one case, and I want it specified precisely enough that I can defend the choice: it must have both valves on the same resource; a user group small enough that the class can name every member (six to ten), so that the reciprocity of the externality is visible rather than abstract; a threshold or other non-convexity somewhere in the system, so that 'sustainable at the current level' and 'safe' come apart and the marginal step that triggers collapse looks exactly like every marginal step before it; and — this is the feature I care most about — a resource that could in principle be divided into private parcels but where the physical unit and the property unit come apart, so that enclosure fails to internalise the externality. It must not contain a villain, a growing population, an outside buyer, a technological fix, or a regulator already on the scene; and it must not be a pasture, a fishery, the atmosphere, or an office fridge. Crucially, the equilibrium must not be total destruction: I don't want anyone leaving with 'the commons is always ruined' as the takeaway. Gradual degradation is enough.

Third, the misconception map, with each one pinned to a point in the sequence and a note on which ones I should deliberately let form and then break. My guesses: that 'commons' means unowned and that unowned means doomed, so the fix is ownership and ownership means private parcels; that the tragedy is about greed, when it can arise with every user taking exactly an equal share; that commons and public goods are two names for the same thing, or that 'public good' just means 'something good for the public'; that since it's a Prisoner's Dilemma the prediction is mutual defection and therefore the answer is external enforcement; the no-difference excuse — my one animal, my one contribution, changes nothing — which I want handled via the reciprocity point, since the excuse is available to every user simultaneously; and average-rate reasoning about the threshold, the 'it's been fine for years, so one more is fine' move. Tell me which of these I've got wrong, which I'm missing, and in what order they will actually arrive.

Two closing requests. Give me one question for the last ten minutes that shows me whether the two-valves distinction has been understood rather than memorised — including what a superficially correct but actually empty answer would sound like — and give me the five-minute opening and the single sentence I should leave on the board for the whole session. Finally, tell me what to leave out and say out loud that I'm leaving out: the details of Ostrom's design principles (I want the name and the fact that state-versus-market isn't exhaustive, not eight principles), mechanism design, anything Coasean, and Hardin's later, more polemical writing, along with the empirical question of what actually happened to historical commons — I want to mark that as out of scope without making the model look like an empirical claim about it. And give me one thing I should flatly refuse to answer at this level, with the sentence to refuse it in.
