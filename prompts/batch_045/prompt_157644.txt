Consider the following stylized but, I think, recognizable regime of professional accountability. I describe it fully so that nothing needs to be looked up; I am interested in its implicit theory, not in whether any particular jurisdiction matches the description exactly.

A hospital and the professional regulator that licenses its clinicians operate under roughly these rules:

1. Review is triggered by outcomes. A file is opened when an adverse-event report is filed. No routine audit of conduct exists. An anaesthetist who leaves the operating theatre for eight minutes to take a personal call, and whose patient suffers a hypoxic injury, is reported, reviewed, sanctioned, sued, and named in the press. A colleague who does exactly the same thing, with exactly the same patient risk profile, and whose patient is unharmed, is never reviewed, because nothing happened.

2. When the regulator does review, it disclaims outcome-sensitivity. Its written standard is that it judges the clinician's process and not the result, and it will sanction an impaired or reckless practitioner whose patient escaped harm on the same schedule as one whose patient did not. Its operative vocabulary is risk, judgment, and fitness to practise rather than damage caused.

3. The grading taxonomy is outcome-based. Incidents are sorted by actual severity, with an unreached near miss ranked as a lower-order category than an event in which harm occurred, and the near-miss count is itself a function of whether anyone happened to notice.

4. Outcome also migrates into the finding of fault itself. After a bad result, the same conduct is retrospectively re-described as careless in a way it would not have been described had nothing happened: reviewers conclude that the clinician should have anticipated a difficulty that, ex ante, there was no particular reason to anticipate. The outcome fixes not only the severity of the sanction but the prior attribution of fault.

5. The tort system operating alongside all of this requires actual damage for a claim to exist at all, while simultaneously holding a defendant liable for the full unforeseeable extent of the harm once some damage is established.

6. Finally, some things are punished purely on a process basis with no outcome condition at all — falsifying a record, practising while intoxicated — and these attract sanction regardless of result.

What I want from you is the implicit theory of responsibility this pattern presupposes.

Specifically: reconstruct the view of blameworthiness that would make the whole package coherent, and show where the package is instead a settlement between two theories that disagree. Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and what it is neutral on. In particular, I want you to separate three roles an outcome can play, because I suspect the practice conflates them: outcome as evidence of a prior fault, outcome as a constituent of fault, and outcome as a scalar on the severity of a sanction for a fault already established. Which of these can a control-based view accommodate without conceding that outcomes are partly constitutive of blameworthiness, and which can it not?

Then say whether the regime is coherent. Is the gap between the outcome-driven trigger and the process-driven standard a contradiction, or does it have a defensible rationale that a pure control principle could endorse? Is the retrospective re-description in point 4 the same phenomenon as the sanctioning differential in point 1, or a different and more damaging one?

Finally, take the two clean alternatives and tell me what each would have to change in practice: a consistently control-based regime, and a consistently outcome-based one. I expect the interesting problem to be that a control-based reform has to grade conduct by expected harm under the agent's own epistemic situation, and that this baseline is itself infected by circumstances the agent did not choose and could not have known — so that the reform relocates the luck rather than removing it. Tell me whether that objection has real force or whether the ex ante and the ex post versions of luck are relevantly different.

Argue the case on its own terms. I do not want a survey of the literature, a reading list, or citations; assume I have read the standard arguments and want the analysis of this practice.
