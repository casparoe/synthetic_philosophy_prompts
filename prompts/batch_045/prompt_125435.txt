I am in my final year of secondary school and I run a small philosophy-and-economics club: about fifteen members, aged sixteen to eighteen, most of whom know the prisoner's dilemma and nothing else formal. We have one eighty-minute session. I am not a teacher and I do not want to stand at the front, so I need an activity that runs itself once the rules are explained, and in which the point is something the group walks into rather than something I announce.

The idea is the logic of costly signalling: that a signal can carry information only if it is differentially expensive for different kinds of sender, so that someone who would profit from being taken for a better type cannot afford the fake. Three further things matter to me, and I would rather the session produced them than asserted them:

(a) that what does the work is the *gap* in what the signal costs a high type and a low type, not how much it burns in absolute terms;
(b) that the outcome is held up by what receivers expect, so that the same material setup supports both a world in which everyone signals and a world in which nobody does, and participants can end up stuck in the worse one;
(c) a case where the very best senders conspicuously refuse to send the signal at all, and where that refusal is itself informative — the top type declining the badge while the merely good type cannot afford to decline it.

Here is the shape I have in mind, which you should feel free to reorder or replace if the mechanics will not deliver the above.

Hidden types drawn privately from a deck (three types would let me do (c) in the final round; say whether two types for most rounds and three for the last is cleaner). Each round, senders choose whether or not to send a signal, and also may send any free written message they like; receivers see only the message and whether the signal was sent, and decide whether to accept or reject the sender. Payoffs in poker chips, with a trivial prize at the end for the highest totals: a rejected sender gets nothing, an accepted sender pays the receiver a large penalty if their type is low and yields a large gain if it is high, the signal costs a fixed number of chips whether or not the sender is accepted, and the free message costs nothing. Roles rotate so everyone plays both sides; matches should be anonymous in the relevant sense, which I suspect matters, and I would like you to tell me exactly what I must do to stop reputations forming across rounds, since a reputation would let people coordinate without paying for it and would quietly destroy the lesson.

The number I want to vary on the board between rounds is the cost of the signal. I want the group to find, by playing, that there is a band of costs inside which signalling separates the types and outside which it does not: too cheap and everyone signals; too expensive and nobody does. Please give me the payoff table with actual numbers, and show the arithmetic for the band itself — the two inequalities that fix its endpoints, and the net payoff to a low type who fakes inside the band, so I can check the separation is genuine and not an artefact of numbers I picked badly. Then give me one further round in which I refund a fixed number of chips to every sender who signals: a uniform subsidy that makes signalling cheaper for everyone by the same amount. If I have this right, the separating outcome should collapse even though nothing about the types has changed, and total welfare should fall, which is the cleanest way I can think of to make (a) felt rather than stated. Tell me whether that actually follows from your numbers, and what size of refund makes it bite.

For (c), I want a final round in which the receiver can already see something about the sender: enough to rule out the bottom type, but not enough to tell the middle from the top. My hope is that the middle type then signals, the top type can safely skip the signal because being seen as middle means being treated as middle, and the receiver learns from the silence. Please check whether that outcome really is an equilibrium under the payoffs you have specified, state the condition on the payoffs that makes it so, and if it cannot be produced in a single round with index cards and chips, say so plainly instead of staging something that will not work.

Deliverables, in order:

1. Full rules, materials, and the exact script I read out at the start, which must not contain the theoretical vocabulary.
2. The round-by-round sequence with the payoff table and the worked arithmetic for the cost band.
3. A debrief, no more than fifteen minutes, that maps specific moments in what happened onto (a), (b) and (c), and that names the handicap principle only after the group has produced the mechanics themselves.
4. An honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — players signalling out of boredom or generosity, receivers trusting the free message out of politeness, the type distribution leaking from the deck, someone trying to be clever with a meta-strategy, the group failing to notice the band because the rounds were too short — and what to do in the moment in each case.
5. Which part of the underlying idea the game cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply in words. I am guessing it is something like this: in the biological case the signal is not chosen at all, the cost is fixed by anatomy and development, and the correlation between signal and quality is maintained by selection over generations rather than by anyone reasoning about what receivers will believe; a card game can generate the logic but not that. Say what you think the real gap is, and how much it matters for what my club takes away.
