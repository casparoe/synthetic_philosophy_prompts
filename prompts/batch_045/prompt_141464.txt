I'm expecting my first child in the spring, and an old friend from my undergraduate philosophy seminar — a convinced antinatalist, and the kind of person who has never once lost an argument by being insufficiently literal — sent me a long, affectionate email explaining why he takes my news to be, on balance, bad news for a subject who does not exist. I don't want to re-litigate it with him; I want to give him something better than another argument at my baby shower, which he has said he will attend. So I'd like a piece of sustained satire, and I want the target to be the machinery of his case rather than the man.

Specifically: write a parody of the annual impact report of a philanthropic vehicle — call it something like the Counterfactual Welfare Trust — that issues tradable 'non-existence credits' to donors, who fund contraception, abstinence stipends, and other birth-prevention programs, and who then book the averted pain of the children who never arrive against their own moral ledgers. About 800 words, laid out in the sections such a document would actually have: a mandate and 'theory of change' up front, a methodology note, a portfolio table with per-dollar yield in something like quality-adjusted averted-suffering units, a beneficiary FAQ, an assurance statement from the auditors, and a closing testimonial from a satisfied subscriber. The voice should be the flat, fiduciary, faintly celebratory register of carbon-offset and development-economics reporting: additionality, baselines, leakage, assurance, portfolio concentration, net position. The reductio has to be carried entirely by that register staying straight while the logic runs where it runs. Please build the jokes out of the real structure of the asymmetry argument — pain present is bad, pleasure present is good, pain absent is good even when there is nobody there to enjoy the absence, and pleasure absent is not bad unless there is somebody for whom it is a deprivation — and out of three consequences of it that my friend's email more or less concedes: that the good in the third claim is impersonal, a good not good *for* anyone, so the Trust's credits have magnitude but no beneficiary and can never be claimed, refunded, or sued over; that because the fourth claim never registers cancelled happiness as a loss, the Trust's ledger is structurally incapable of showing a loss and its optimisation problem has no interior solution; and that since the yield of a credit scales with the expected lifetime suffering of the person who would otherwise have existed, the cheapest credits come from the worst-off populations, which the report should present with satisfaction as an efficiency rather than notice as a moral problem. I'd also like the auditors to worry, in their own vocabulary, about two technical problems that are genuinely internal to this setup: the non-identity problem, since no credit can be issued against an identifiable child, only against a counterfactual cohort; and counterfactual double-counting, since a conception is prevented by two people at once and both would like to claim it. There should be one place where the report considers the obvious alternative of just funding pain relief instead, and dismisses it on cost-effectiveness and consent grounds without ever raising its voice.

Two constraints. First, fairness: my friend will read this, and I'd rather he laughed than winced, so nothing in it should be a view he wouldn't actually hold or a premise he'd disown — no suffering-free-euthanasia-of-the-unborn, no cartoon misanthropy, no cheap shots at depression. Exaggerate the real feature, don't invent one. Second, legibility: my sister and my in-laws will also read it, and none of them have touched this literature, so the asymmetry has to be reconstructible from the document itself and the irony has to stay deadpan enough that a reader who has never heard of any of this can still tell what is being sent up, without the author ever winking at them. End it somewhere the reader genuinely recoils — the Trust noting, in the same pleased tone, that its mandate contains no natural ceiling and that at full subscription it will have eliminated its own client base.
