I hold the position below and am deciding whether to build a paper on it. I have given its three components names of my own so that we can talk about them exactly. Please tell me what would count against it.

The position — call it Calibrated Transparency, or CT:

(C1) Neutralisation (the screening-off thesis). Higher-order evidence — evidence about what your evidence supports, or about how reliable you are at responding to it — never functions as a further first-order reason bearing on p. It bears on p only by removing, wholly or in part, the rational force of the first-order evidence you were relying on. So there is a residual evidence set E*, determined by what the higher-order evidence neutralises, and your posterior must be Cr(p | E*); in the limit of complete neutralisation, Cr(p | E and H) = Cr(p | H). The rival picture, which I reject, treats H as adding a consideration that itself supports not-p, so that your credence can fall below what neutralisation alone would dictate.

(C2) Calibration fixes the residual. How much neutralisation H achieves is fixed by considerations of calibration: H tells you how reliable agents like you, in situations like this, are, and E* is whatever evidence an agent of the reliability H attributes to you would be entitled to rely on. In the canonical case of disagreement with an acknowledged peer, this is what delivers something close to equal weight.

(C3) Transparency, or no-akrasia. Rational requirements are fixed-point requirements. A fully rational agent's actual credence in p is the credence her total evidence — higher-order evidence included — permits, and she therefore never occupies a state in which she believes p while taking that very belief to be poorly supported. Epistemic akrasia is never rationally permissible, and no genuine norm of rationality can require it. Whatever higher-order evidence does, it must do by shifting what you are permitted to believe, never by issuing a requirement you could satisfy only by fragmenting.
