Hey — I need a case built to order, for a grad seminar I'm running on the limits of expected value maximization. I want the reverse of the usual dialectic. Everyone knows how to make the fanatic look silly — the mugger who threatens astronomical torture unless you hand over your wallet, the St. Petersburg setup, that whole family of cases. What nobody can ever produce on demand is the mirror image: a decision problem where the fanatic (I mean it in the narrow sense: any finite cost is worth paying for a small enough chance of a large enough gain) gets the verdict a sane, reflective person would endorse, and it's the anti-fanatic who ends up saying something embarassing.

Here's what I need it to satisfy.

1. One paragraph, realistic institutional setting — a hospital trust board, a pensions regulator, a national biosecurity office. No gods, no matrix lords, no perfect predictors, no infinite utilities, and no unbounded sequences of bets.

2. The probability has to be genuinely tiny — below, say, one in ten billion — and it has to be a known, physically grounded chance, not anybody's testimony. The whole point is to to block the standard escape route where the anti-fanatic says your credence in the story is itself too shaky to act on. I want nothing left for that move to bite on.

3. The value can't be exotic either: no single person getting 10^50 units of happiness, no counting levels of reality. Lots of ordinary people, each getting an ordinary-sized benefit. Which I realize forces the astronomical number to come from headcount rather than intensity, and I want you to tell me whether that's cheating (see 5).

4. Structure it so that risk aversion in the ordinary sense can't do the work: the decision-maker is choosing on behalf of other people, and the certain cost is small enough that any plausible concave utility over the beneficiaries' welfare leaves the ordering the same. Stipulate away offsetting side-effects too — none of the "but what about cluelessness about the long-run consequences" reply.

5. After the scenario, do the arithmetic. Give me the actual expected value calculation, and show where a truncating anti-fanatic's threshold gets crossed. Then run two anti-fanatics separately: the one who just zeroes out probabilities below some cut-off, and the one who keeps the probability but compresses or caps total value (diminishing marginal value of population size, bounded axiology, whatever you like). Tell me what each picks and why.

6. Then the honest bit, which is what I actually care about. Which feature of the case is doing the work? Is this really a win for fanaticism about tiny probabilities, or just a win for the independent claim that very large populations (or the far future) carry astronomical value — a claim the fanatic and the value-capper were already going to disagree about before any tiny probability turned up? Name the single feature such that deleting it collapses my case straight back into the mugger. And give me the anti-fanatic's best reply rather than a strawman: is it that my intuition here is just my prior about existential risk wearing a decision-theoretic disguise?

Short coda if you can manage it: what changes if the same choice is offered a million times over, independently? If the truncating theorist's verdict flips in the sequence, is that an argument against truncation or just a different case with a different probability?

And if you think the specs can't all be satisfied together, say so and tell me which pair is in tension — that's a perfectly good answer too.
