I do software engineering, not philosophy, but I share a flat with someone writing a dissertation in population ethics, and we got into an argument I can't stop picking at. She says my verdicts are a mess; I think they're fine and it's the theories that are crude. So I've started keeping a spreadsheet of cases with my gut verdicts on them, and I want to know whether any principle actually fits them, or whether some of them have to go. Please take it as given that welfare is interpersonally comparable and cardinally measurable in the units below, with 0 as the neutral point where a life is neither worth having nor worth avoiding; I don't want to litigate measurability, and I don't want a bibliography or a reading list, since I've already read the survey material. I want the principle.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts. (1) Ten people, each at +80: my baseline. (2) The same ten at +80 plus an eleventh at +60: better, though only a bit; and if the eleventh is at +20 rather than +60, still better, but I feel it much less. (3) Ten at +80 versus ten million at +0.5, where each of the ten million lives is a long drift of mild contentment with nothing much in it: the ten are better, and not close. This is the pair I can't square with (2), and it also makes no difference to me if the ten million live in a causally isolated colony in another galaxy and never interact with the ten, or if they're spread one per planet across a billion years. (4) A choice between a policy that produces ten particular people at +50 and one that produces twelve entirely different people at +48, where nobody who exists under the first would exist under the second: the second isn't worse, and I'd probably take it. (5) Ten at +80 versus five at +95 and five at +65, same total and same headcount: I prefer the equal one; and against five at +99 and five at +70, which has the higher total, I still lean to the equal one, but weakly. (6) Ten at +80 plus one person whose whole life is -50, mostly agony: obviously worse than the ten alone, and I'd rather have the ten alone even if they were at +79, at +60, probably at +40; I genuinely don't know where I'd stop. (7) Ten at +80, except that one of the ten has, inside an otherwise +80 life, a two-week episode of agony at -50 that is later fully compensated: I do not count this as worse than the unmodified case at all. (8) An empty world versus a world with one person at +1: I have no reaction whatever, and I'm not sure there's a fact of the matter; but an empty world versus one person at +90 is clearly better, and an empty world versus a billion people at +0.5 is clearly better too. My own stab at a principle is a critical-level total view: sum up (welfare minus some positive threshold) across lives, with a threshold somewhere around 10 or 20, and then add a lexical veto or at least a heavy extra weight for lives below some negative level. But it has an obvious fault: if I apply it to whole lives, then (7) comes out fine and (6)'s veto works, whereas if I apply it to temporal segments or episodes instead, (7) turns into something very like (6), which is the wrong answer. I don't know how to settle that choice of unit non-arbitrarily, and I suspect it's the real crux.

Two cases I haven't put in the spreadsheet because I go back and forth on them, and I want to see what your principle says rather than what mine does. First, a risky one: Policy X gives a 90 percent chance of ten people at +80 and a 10 percent chance of nobody at all; Policy Y gives, for certain, twelve people at +45. Second, an exchange-rate one: a billion people at +80 plus one person at -50, versus the same billion at +80 with no such person; and then the same comparison again with a trillion instead of a billion. If your principle gives a finite number of happy lives that outweigh one life of agony, I want to know roughly what it is and what sets it; if it refuses to give one, I want to know what that refusal costs elsewhere.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no concluding remark: (1) your principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you haven't seen; (2) the clause that separates (2) from (3), and what sets the line between them; (3) your verdicts on my two unlisted cases; (4) which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and which one you'd overturn; (5) the nearest rival principle, and the single case that would decide between the two.
