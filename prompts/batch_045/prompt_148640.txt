In the mid-1840s, and most concentratedly in *A Literary Review* (the book published in Danish in March 1846 and usually issued in English as *Two Ages*), Kierkegaard set out a diagnosis of his own moment that runs roughly as follows. The age is sensible rather than passionate: it deliberates endlessly and decides rarely, so that public life fills up with reflection, comparison, and commentary instead of action for which someone can answer. Its characteristic social formation is not a community, a party, or a mob but something he calls "the public" — a monstrous onlooker that is everywhere and nowhere, an abstraction that no one belongs to, that no one can be addressed or held to account, and that nonetheless exerts a real pressure on everyone. The mechanism by which the public does its work he calls leveling: not the raising up of the low but the reduction of everything to a single measurable standard, carried out by the envy of the anonymous rather than by the hand of a tyrant. In the Hongs' translation he writes that "the trend today is in the direction of mathematical equality, so that in all classes about so and so many uniformly make one individual." Against all of this he sets the single individual, who cannot be reached as a member of a crowd and becomes himself only in inwardness, decision, and passion — and whose relation to the truth is not improved by anything that happens to him in public.

Suppose that a Danish parliamentary commission on media reform, convened in the present, were to ask Kierkegaard to serve as an expert witness and to file a written memorandum on three proposals it is considering. Assume he has been given a working account of how contemporary platforms function: ranked feeds, re-sharing without endorsement, engagement counts, pseudonymous or pseudonymous-in-effect accounts, and the fact that almost anyone can publish to almost anyone.

Proposal A, the parish principle. Every large platform must offer, and must enroll new users in by default, a bounded local assembly: membership capped at 150, admission tied to a verified residential address, real legal names required, strictly chronological ordering with no algorithmic ranking and no recommendation, and no re-circulation of anything posted inside the assembly to anyone outside it. Users could opt up into the open global feed, but only by an explicit and reversible act.

Proposal B, the abolition of number. Public engagement metrics — follower counts, like counts, re-share counts, view counts — would be prohibited outright. In their place, a poster would receive, exactly one week after publishing, a private message stating how many distinct people actually opened the post; the number would never be displayed to anyone else, never compared with anyone else's number, and never retained after being read.

Proposal C, the sealed witness. Before any post that names, depicts, or quotes a specific identifiable person can be published, the author must write a short confidential statement of what he or she stands to lose if the post turns out to be false, careless, or cruel. The statement is sealed, is shown to nobody, and is returned to its author — together with the post itself — at a randomly chosen point between one month and one year later. Non-compliance carries no penalty other than the inability to publish that particular post.

The commission also asks the experts to address a thesis one of its members has introduced under the name *second-order leveling*. Her claim is that leveling has changed character. It no longer works primarily by equalizing rank, authority, or distinction; it works by making every stance available to every person as a posture, so that earnestness and irony, conviction and performance, testimony and content become publicly indistinguishable. On this account the damage is not that the individual is dragged down to the average but that he can no longer tell, either from the outside or from within himself, which mode he is occupying — and that this uncertainty is itself the thing the medium produces and sells.

Your task is to write the memorandum Kierkegaard would submit. In your own prose rather than a pastiche of his style, and without inventing or citing any further texts, cover the following:

Rank the three proposals from most to least acceptable to him, and say plainly which one he would regard as actively dangerous rather than merely inadequate. Be careful with Proposal B: he may well think that abolishing public numbers removes the very mechanism by which leveling operates, but he may equally think that handing each person a private weekly tally of how many souls attended to him is quantification of inwardness, and therefore worse than the public number. Take a position on which of these he would find more compelling, and say why.

Say whether he would accept the thesis of second-order leveling as the consummation of his own account, or whether he would reject it as a misunderstanding — for instance on the grounds that the distinction between earnestness and performance is established inwardly and is therefore not available for the medium to dissolve, or on the contrary that the public's whole power lay precisely in making such distinctions unlivable.

Say whether he would agree to serve at all. He wrote under pseudonyms, he published polemics in the daily press, he was himself hounded by a Copenhagen satirical paper in the affair that immediately preceded *A Literary Review*, and in his last years he campaigned in polemical pamphlets against an established church he accused of letting people be Christian without being Christian. Given that record, would he file a memorandum under his own name, publish it pseudonymously, resign in order to attack the commission from outside, or refuse the invitation outright — and how, if at all, would that choice bear on the substance of what he recommends?

Finally, and in no more than 150 words, add a closing paragraph stating the strongest objection you take him to have against his own memorandum — the point at which his own principles turn against the advice he has just given.
