I teach a seminar on social ontology, and I want to get clear about whether generational categories are ever anything more than convenient slices of a birth-year continuum. Here are my verdicts; I would like the principle behind them, if there is one.

Not real kinds: Millennials as a birth-year band (roughly 1981–1996); Boomers (roughly 1946–1964) as a psychological or values kind; Xennials, the sliver of late-1970s birth years; digital natives, meaning people who had a smartphone before thirteen.

Real kinds: people who first looked for full-time work during the deep recession of 2008–2010; women who came of age where oral contraception was legally unavailable to unmarried women until the early 1970s; people born in the 1950s in a country that switched abruptly from a command economy to a market economy; the so-called generation of 1914.

The pairings embarrass me. The recession cohort and the Millennial band contain mostly the same people, and I say yes to one and no to the other; the market-transition cohort and American Gen X are the same birth years with opposite verdicts. And of my four yeses, I resist calling the recession cohort and the pill cohort generations, while I am happy to call 1914 and the transition cohort generations — apparently only because their members came to describe themselves that way. Relatedly: people who were seventy in March 2020 I count as a risk class, not a generation, though I cannot say what the difference is supposed to be.

So: is generation one word for two things, a causally structured cohort plus a self-interpretive one, or is there a single principle that sorts my list? My tentative formulation is that a cohort is a real kind just in case the shared timing of exposure to some event does explanatory work that neither age effects nor period effects could have done — which seems to run straight into the age-period-cohort identification problem and threatens to make my question undecidable in principle. Repair that formulation, replace it, or tell me which verdicts it forces me to drop; and tell me what the resulting principle predicts for the case my students keep raising, people who were fourteen in March 2020.
