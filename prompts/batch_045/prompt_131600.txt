I am trying to settle an argument, not to learn what Ayn Rand wrote, so please skip citations, reading suggestions, and summaries of her corpus: I want the argument space mapped, and I want it mapped without anyone looking anything up.

The argument was about effective altruism. My interlocutor reached for the title of Rand's 1964 collection, "The Virtue of Selfishness," and used it, in one breath, to two conclusions: first, that giving a large share of one's income to strangers is not merely optional but degrading, a self-sacrifice that a person of integrity would refuse; second, that a genuinely rational person would never make an anonymous gift, since an anonymous gift cannot come back to benefit the giver. He also leaned on the point that Rand glosses "selfishness" in her introduction as nothing more than concern with one's own interests, and on her definition of value as that which one acts to gain and/or keep.

I am convinced the one-liner is sliding, and I want the slide made explicit. Please give me a menu of the distinct precise theses the slogan could be asserting, and grade each one. My own attempts, which I expect you to correct, split, and add to, are these:

One: a purely verbal thesis. Selfishness is redefined so that any action flowing from what the agent values counts as selfish, in which case the slogan says almost nothing and cannot reach either conclusion.
Two: an objective-standard thesis. "Interests" are not whatever the agent happens to want but what serves her life as the kind of being she is, so the slogan has real content, but only if the account of a person's objective interests does independent work.
Three: a jurisdictional thesis. What is wrong with altruism is not the cost to the agent or the benefit to others but the location of the normative trigger: the agent acts on another's need rather than on her own judgment of value. This may have no behavioral content at all, so that a kidney donation and a refusal to donate could each be virtuous or vicious depending on their source.
Four: an anti-fixed-pie or harmony thesis: value is created rather than transferred, so benefit to another at cost to self is never the net gain it appears to be, and altruistic codes systematically destroy value.
Five: an epistemic thesiss: others' needs are not transparent to me, whereas my own interests are, so an altruistic code is a systematically error-prone decision rule.
Six: an empirical psychological thesis about what altruistic motivation does to character, to dependents, and to the giver's own capacity for sustained action.
Seven: a narrow scope thesis: no positive duty to serve others, gifts permitted as luxuries, i.e. a denial of obligation rather than a denial of permissibility or admirability.

For every reading, tell me: how to state it precisely; whether it is a definitional maneuver, a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, a substantive but contested one, or false; what would count as refuting it; whether Rand or a defender of the slogan is actually committed to it or whether it is an extrapolation; and, most importantly, whether it licenses the first conclusion, the second, both, or neither. Note where the readings differ along the axes that I think are doing the hidden work: the extension of "selfishness," the kind of normative status being claimed (permissible, praiseworthy, obligatory, admirable), and the direction of the contrast (altruism is not obligatory, versus never permissible, versus never admirable).

Two things I cannot work out and particularly want addressed. First, over what interval are interests summed? Rand's own framework must permit forgoing present goods for long-range ones, so the self whose interests matter is a life and not a time slice; say what that does to the bite against altruism. Second, if the content of the virtue is just concern with one's own interests, and if the standard is indexed to a living organism's survival, what principle internal to the view stops the boundary of the self at the skin, so that the interests of those I love cannot be counted as mine? If no such principle can be given, say what happens to my interlocutor's two conclusions.

Finally: tell me which reading he must have meant, and if no reading both survives scrutiny and supports what he said, say so plainly and tell me which suppressed premise is actually carrying the weight.
