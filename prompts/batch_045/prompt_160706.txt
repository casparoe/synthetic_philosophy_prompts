I run the philosophy club at my high school. We meet for one 55-minute period on Thursday afternoons, usually 13 to 16 students aged 16 to 18, with very uneven mathematical confidence: a few do competition maths, most have met averages and perhaps a little probability, and none has studied decision theory. We have no budget, a whiteboard but no projector, and I would rather not put real money in front of them; points convertible into something trivial at the last meeting of term are fine.

I want to build a session around the two-envelope paradox, but as something they *do* rather than something I describe. Please design it as a runnable game, with these things I want them to walk out having felt rather than been told:

First, that the argument for switching is genuinely seductive and not obviously stupid. I want them to generate it themselves, in their own words, before anyone names it or writes a formula.

Second, that the claim at the centre of it — that whatever amount I hold, the other envelope is equally likely to hold half or double — cannot possibly be true of every amount I could see. I want them to collide with the ceiling of the distribution rather than hear about it.

Third, that the argument is completely indifferent to scale and to cost: if it works for four units it works for four billion, and it works just as well if every switch costs you a chip. I want that indifference to strike them as a symptom rather than as a virtue.

Fourth, and most important: that opening the envelope is not epistemically inert. A rule that depends on what you see can really earn more than always keeping, even though always switching cannot beat always keeping inside a closed pair. That combination is the thing I most want them to sit with, and I am not sure how to stage it so that both halves land.

Concretely, I need:

Materials and preparation: what to print, how many envelopes or cards, how to generate the pairs so I can do it in five minutes without arithmetic errors.

Three rounds, roughly 12, 20 and 10 minutes: a blind round (choose an envelope, offered a switch, repeated, never look); a peek round (look at your own amount first, then decide, with the pair amounts drawn from a distribution whose ceiling is kept secret and which they are invited to infer from the values they have seen); and a friction round where every switch costs a fixed number of chips.

A closed-system element: I would like one student to play the house, keeping a ledger, so that any gain to the room has to come out of somebody's column. If you think that will misfire, say so and propose something better.

A prediction sheet they fill in before playing and return to after, with the questions you think are worth asking.

A short note on what the facilitator is allowed to say. I want almost nothing: no mention of expected value, priors, or the name of the paradox until the last round is finished.

A debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the specific claims above — which round is supposed to make which point, what I should write on the board and when, and how to reveal the hidden ceiling so that it arrives as a discovery rather than as an announcement.

An honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: the student who refuses to engage with points, the student who announces the answer in round one, the arithmetic that someone will get wrong, and how to handle each without breaking the staging.

Finally, be frank about which part of the problem this activity cannot reproduce and which part I will simply have to supply myself in the debrief. I suspect it is the point about no probability distribution over all amounts being able to make the equal-odds claim come out true, but tell me if you think it is something else, and tell me the least lecturing way to say it.
