I sit on the grants committee of a small health funder, and for the past two years we have scored candidate causes on the familiar three-factor scheme — importance, tractability, neglectedness — normally multiplied together into a single number. I have become uneasy about the neglectedness factor, which seems to me to be doing two different jobs at once, and rather than argue about it in the abstract I would like to run the framework against a case it was never designed to evaluate: the WHO campaign against smallpox, from the World Health Assembly's call for eradication in the late 1950s and the programme launched in 1959, through the Intensified Smallpox Eradication Programme begun in 1967, to the certification of eradication around 1980. What I want is a disciplined mapping rather than a narrative. Who, for the purposes of the framework, were the relevant actors — WHO and its member states, the endemic countries, the United States and the Soviet Union, the field surveillance workers — and what were the actual options on the table: universal mass vaccination, or the later surveillance-and-containment strategy, or settling for control rather than eradication in the hardest regions, or simply letting the earlier programme coast? What were the payoffs, and what was genuinely uncertain at the point of the 1967 decision — whether there was an animal reservoir, whether mild or subclinical cases would defeat surveillance, whether the last endemic areas could be reached at all?

Then score it, explicitly, on the three factors and say what the multiplication yields. Importance and neglectedness look to me like the easy ones: a disease killing on the order of two million people a year, a benefit that is permanent rather than recurring, and a burden concentrated almost entirely where the donors no longer were, since smallpox had already been eliminated in the countries with money. Tractability is where I expect the interesting work: no non-human reservoir, cases that announce themselves visibly, a heat-stable freeze-dried vaccine, a cheap two-pronged needle that made mass vaccination fast and economical — and yet a ferociously expensive last mile.

The part I actually care about is where the framework misdescribes the case. My own suspicion, which I should say plainly I have made up and have no idea whether anyone has argued, so please do not treat it as received wisdom, is that the framework is a heuristic for marginal expected good per pound and therefore presupposes a fairly smooth, roughly additive return to money, whereas eradication is a threshold good: nearly all the value sits at the very end, precisely where the cost per case averted explodes. Read literally, the framework should have said to spend the marginal pound elsewhere and let the final endemic pockets smoulder. I would like you to take that objection seriously and then test it. Related things worth handling: tractability here looks endogenous rather than given, since the strategy and some of the tools were invented or improved during the campaign, partly because the eradication goal existed; neglectedness depends on a counterfactual about what other funders would have done with the money; and the campaign created a permanent downstream liability in maintained stockpiles and vaccination readiness. There are also drivers the framework has no slot for at all — a single-minded champion, superpower prestige, and the surveillance infrastructure left behind afterwards.

Finish by running the case back against the theory. Does smallpox vindicate the three-factor framework, as the standard retelling would have it — a cause scoring highly on all three, funded, and won — or does it embarrass it, by showing that a framework with no vocabulary for threshold goods, endogenous tractability or permanent liabilities would in fact have recommended stopping before the end? Which reading is more defensible, and what would you have to add to the framework to repair the problem, something like a lumpiness or option-value term, and is that repair principled or merely ad hoc? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, distinguish the standard critiques of the framework from my own speculations, and mark where you are extrapolating beyond what the historical record clearly supports: I would rather have one confident verdict with its weak points named than a uniformly hedged survey. Treat all figures as order-of-magnitude only and do not build anything on precise numbers, and please do not give me citations, a bibliography or suggestions for further reading — I have no library access here and can only use what you tell me.
