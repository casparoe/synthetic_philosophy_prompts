I'm a software engineer by trade and have never studied philosophy formally, but over the last few years I've ended up in a workplace-adjacent volunteering and mutual-aid circle where people with quite different causes make explicit deals with each other: I take on a cost that matters to you, you take on a cost that matters to me, and we both walk away with more of what we each partly wanted. A friend once referred to these as moral-trade deals; I have no idea whether that is a real term of art. What I do know is that I have confident reactions to particular instances, that my reactions don't seem to line up with each other, and that I can't state the rule I'm applying. I'd like help finding it.

Here are my verdicts.

1. Priya cares mainly about animal welfare, Marcus mainly about local homelessness. Priya gives up a Saturday to work at a shelter, which she finds tedious but not awful. Marcus gives up meat for a month, which he genuinely dislikes. Each would rather have this arrangement than no arrangement. My verdict: good, obviously, and worth encouraging. This is my paradigm case and any principle I accept has to keep it.

2. Sam cares about malaria, Nora about AI safety. Each was going to donate two hundred dollars to their own cause anyway; instead they agree that each will donate two hundred to the other's. My verdict: empty. On my accounting both causes end up with the same money and both people give the same amount; nothing has happened except whose name is on which receipt. Not wrong, just pointless. And a corollary that I find myself believing without being able to justify: if one of them puts in noticeably more than the other, my reaction flips from pointless to someone is being taken advantage of, though there is no market price for a cause and so, as far as I can see, no standard by which to call the exchange rate unfair.

3. Alex offers to stop driving to work if Nora volunteers eight hours at a food bank. Alex has in fact already decided to stop driving, because a bike lane just opened on his route. My verdict: I dislike this. Alex hands over something that costs him nothing and extracts something real. But I get stuck here: suppose Nora knows about the bike lane, doesn't mind, and prefers the arrangement to no arrangement. I still feel something is off, and I can't tell whether that is a genuine objection or only my distaste for a deal with no friction in it.

4. Same shape, deliberately manufactured: someone takes up a practice they have no interest in, purely in order to have something to offer to give up. My verdict: clearly bad, and I treat that as settled. But I want to know where the line between 3 and 4 actually runs. Is the difference only whether the cost was created for the purpose of the deal, or does 3 already contain the seed of 4?

5. Dana and I each agreed to run a lunchtime session for the other's cause. We scheduled them in the same week; the audience split; each event did roughly half of what it would have done on its own. My verdict: this turned out to be a materially worse deal than either of us signed, even though nobody was misled and nobody did anything wrong. It makes me think the value of a concession is not a fixed quantity you can price once, but a function of what else is happening around it.

6. Two colleagues each agree to drop a workplace campaign the other dislikes, one on accessibility and one on pay transparency, in exchange for the other dropping theirs. Both are personally better off: less conflict, fewer enemies, more time. The people the campaigns were meant to help lose out. My verdict: wrong, and clearly worse than case 1, even though structurally both parties bear a cost and both gain.

7. My own case, two years ago. I agreed to stop publicly advocating for something I believed in — not to stop doing it, only to stop making the case for it — in exchange for a concession I valued more from someone on the other side. The terms were fair by any measure I can construct, and I would accept them again on the numbers, and I now think it was a mistake. Two things happened that I did not price: I found myself caring less about the thing afterwards, and people who cared about it began treating me as unreliable, while people who had been undecided seemed to infer from my willingness to sell that it cannot have mattered very much. My verdict: bad. And I cannot reconcile this with case 1, where the concessions were also visible to others and also public.

The best principle I can manage on my own has four clauses: each side must bear a real cost; the cost must be real against a baseline of no deal at all, not merely against a baseline where the deal exists; the values of the two concessions must be roughly independent, so that the pair is worth the sum of its parts; and nobody outside the deal may be made worse off. That handles 3, 4, 5 and 6. It does not explain why 2 strikes me as empty rather than merely permissible, and it fully permits 7, which I now reject.

So: state the principle precisely, or the two or three candidate principles if there is no single one, with the clause-by-clause note of which of my cases forces which clause; tell me which of my verdicts you think has to go and why; tell me whether 7 is really just a case of the fourth clause with an unusual kind of externality — my willingness to trade being itself a piece of information other people use, including me — or something that needs its own clause. I'm also unsure whether the vocabulary I half-remember from carbon offsets, where a credit is supposed to be additional, permanent and not double-counted, transfers cleanly to deals between people, or whether it distorts the question by making everything look like accounting. And there is a further worry I can't shake: if the people most willing to name a price are the least committed, then the trades that actually get made are the ones that most understate how much the traded things matter, which might make the practice self-undermining in a way that no participant sees — I'd like to know whether that argument works, or whether it proves too much.

Finally, give me two or three cases of your own construction where the principle you settle on returns a verdict I would not have anticipated, so I can test it rather than admire it. Please argue it out rather than point me at books or papers: I have no way to chase references, and I'm after the carving, not a reading list.
