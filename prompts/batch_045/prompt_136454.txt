Hey, I'm trying to make sense of this Hume passage in the section on the origin of justice and property, and how it lines up with David Lewis's take on conventions. Its a bit confusing because Hume says the rules get set up by artifice rather than by people explicitly agreeing to them, and he reckons folks couldn't just reason their way to the whole system in a state of nature. Here's the bit I'm stuck on:

"WE now proceed to examine two questions, viz. concerning the manner, in which the rules of justice are establish'd by the artifice of men; and concerning the reasons, which determine us to attribute to the observance or neglect of these rules a moral beauty and deformity. These questions will appear afterwards to be distinct. We shall begin with the former."

As far as I remember, Lewis says a convention is an arbitrary regularity in behavior that solves a coordination problem and is kept going by mutual expectations, where everyone prefers to go along with it on the condition that others do too. To make it concrete, imagine a group of seal trappers on a seasonal floating ice market who end up with a norm that whoever first sings a particular shanty while rowing out gets first claim to the seal blubber, even though splitting everything equally would have worked just as well. Nobody planned this rule, it seems arbitrary compared to equal splitting, and it sticks because each trapper expects the others to respect the singing claim. How would Lewis explain this norm getting started if, as Hume insists, nobody could have foreseen the whole setup in advance? And is there anything in Hume's gradual, habit-driven artifice that Lewis's definition of a convention (with its focus on a clear alternative regularity and common knowledge) doesn't really capture? I keep going in circles on whether a norm that emerges by tiny steps can really be called arbitrary in Lewis's sense. Can you help?
