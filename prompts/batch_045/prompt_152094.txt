I am a professor of philosophy, and I recently acted as internal examiner for a doctoral thesis in our logic group, jointly supervised with computer science. The external examiner, who is sympathetic to the intuitionistic tradition, has recommended rejection, and the substance of his objection is compressed into a line he repeats twice: in mathematics, truth is provability. He puts it to two uses. First, he holds that the student's central lemma has not been established. The only argument available for it is probabilistic in the following sense: there is a finite transcript, and a deterministic checker which, given that transcript and a random string, accepts every instance of the lemma and accepts any false instance with probability at most one third; running the checker on independent strings drives the error down as far as one likes but never to zero. A two-thirds chance of a certificate is not a proof, he writes, and truth does not come in degrees. Second, he infers that the lemma, as stated, is therefore not true — indeed that it has no determinate truth value until a proof is produced — and he proposes that the student either supply a proper construction or restate the result in the weaker form obtained by a double-negation translation. The student's complaint is that the second proposal changes the subject.

I would like to answer him properly rather than trade intuitions, and I suspect the sentence he is leaning on is being made to do more work than it can bear. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that truth is provability might be expressing, from the near-trivial to the radical, marking each as trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. I would like the menu to separate at least: the soundness platitude that whatever is provable is true; the identification of truth with derivability in some fixed formal system, together with the question of which system and of what the incompleteness theorems do to that identification; the semantic thesis that the meanings of mathematical sentences are given by proof or assertion conditions rather than by verification-transcendent truth conditions; the claim that every truth is provable in principle by an ideally resourceful agent, with some account of what in principle would have to mean; the stronger actualist claim that a statement acquires a truth value only when someone actually constructs a proof; the reading on which the slogan is really a claim about logic rather than about truth — that what it amounts to is the embedding of intuitionistic propositional logic into the modal logic S4 with the box read as provability; and a resource-bounded reading on which only feasible constructions count. For each reading, say what it commits one to about an open conjecture, and then tell me which readings, if any, would actually deliver the examiner's two conclusions: that the probabilistic argument fails to establish the lemma, and that the lemma is not true, or lacks a truth value, until a proof is in hand.

Two further things would help me. First, does the distinction between proving a statement and proving a statement about the reliability of a procedure that outputs candidates for it do any work here, or is that a red herring? Second, is any of these readings hospitable to a probabilistic or interactive proof at all — that is, is there a coherent constructivist position on which a transcript together with a proved error bound is a construction of the lemma, or of something near enough to it? Close with your view on which reading the examiner most likely meant and whether that reading survives its own standard.
