I work in continuing airworthiness for a mid-sized airline and I also sit on our internal safety review board. There is a pattern in our own procedures that I think reproduces a familiar philosophical dispute, and I would like it reconstructed properly rather than named. I will describe the practice as our organisation actually runs it; I am not asking whether real regulators word things this way.

After certain deferred-defect repairs, the engineer must record in the technical log a single number between 0 and 100, their confidence that the defect has been fully rectified, and then decide whether to release the aircraft. The manual defines that number as the same quantity whatever flight follows: whether the defect has been rectified is a matter of fact, and the standard for the logbook judgement is stated not to move with the flight. But the release thresholds attached to the number are explicitly stakes-sensitive. A figure that permits a non-revenue ferry flight to a maintenance base with only crew aboard is below the threshold for a cargo run, and well below the one for a night overwater passenger flight with a full cabin. The manual also runs two predicates rather than one: an aircraft either is or is not airworthy, and separately it either is or is not fit for the intended flight. The first is stipulated to be flight-invariant; the second is not. Finally, when something goes wrong, the review board is instructed to ask what a reasonable engineer with the evidence available at the time would have concluded, deliberately setting aside how things actually turned out.

A philosopher friend says this is the neighbourhood of pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism, and that the textbook examples are low-stakes and high-stakes pairs in which the same evidence counts as knowledge in one and not the other. What I want is the reconstruction.

So: what epistemology does this procedure presuppose? Does any part of it strictly require stakes-sensitivity in the epistemic notions themselves, or can a fully stake-invariant epistemology — fixed evidence, fixed standards for knowledge and justified belief — plus ordinary decision theory, with fixed credences and action thresholds set by the relative costs of a false release and a false grounding, account for the whole practice? I want to separate what the procedure strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with.

Does the two-predicate machinery settle anything, or does it just relocate the dispute? My worry is that by splitting airworthiness from fitness for the intended flight, the manual builds stakes-sensitivity into a second predicate and then congratulates itself for holding the first one fixed. But maybe that split is exactly what a sensible theory would recommend. Which is it?

Is there also a distinction to draw here between encroachment on knowledge and encroachment only on the norms of assertion and action? The logbook number and the airworthiness predicate are supposed to be reported the same way whatever flight follows, while the release decision is where stakes bite. Does that pattern favour a view on which practical factors shape what we may do or say while leaving what we know alone?

Say where the practice is underdetermined, where two or three of these theories would each license exactly the procedures we have. Then take the reform question in both directions. If we took pragmatic encroachment seriously, what in the procedure would have to change, and what could stay exactly as it is? If instead we took the invariantist-plus-decision-theory reading to be the correct description of what we are already doing, what would have to change: would the two predicates collapse into one plus an explicit cost matrix, would the clause saying the standard does not move become the whole story, and would the review board's question need rewriting?
