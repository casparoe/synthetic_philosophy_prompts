I sit on the governing board of a mid-sized regional transit authority, and before our June vote I need to circulate a written opinion that the rest of the board can actually act on. Our capital budget for the coming cycle is about $190 million, and our staff rank projects using an internal metric they call Quality-Adjusted Burden Hours: the hours of discomfort, delay, annoyance, or distress a project averts, summed across every affected person, multiplied by a severity weight, with no floor below which a harm is recorded as zero. Under that metric this cycle's top-ranked item is a $180 million wayfinding and access program — regraded walkways, level boarding platforms, better signage — that would remove, for roughly 380,000 daily riders, a bundle of very small daily frictions: a ninety-second longer wait, a confusing transfer, a steep ramp whose handrail is unpleasant to touch in summer. The second-ranked item, at $186 million, is the Harborview grade separation, which would eliminate an at-grade rail crossing where current volumes produce about two deaths and a handful of serious injuries per decade, along with a four-minute delay for about three thousand drivers a day. Because the frictions in the first project are tiny but the denominator is enormous, it scores roughly forty times higher than the crossing, and staff recommend funding it and deferring the crossing; their report notes, without apparent discomfort, that the same arithmetic would rank removing a one-second irritation for eight million people above preventing a death. Complicating matters, our enabling statute instructs us to give priority to the reduction of fatal and life-altering harm, a clause no one has ever had to interpret, and staff read it as satisfied so long as none of our projects creates such harm.

What I want from you is the memo itself, not background on the ethics of aggregation. Recommend a concrete course: fund the crossing, fund wayfinding, fund a reduced-scope crossing plus a carve-out, or something else — and state the rule behind the recommendation generally enough that staff can apply it to next year's slate without coming back to us. The rule has to answer at least three questions. First, whether our priority clause should be read as a lexical constraint — some class of severe harms that must be reduced before aggregate burden-hours are maximized at all — or as a weighting that aggregation can still outvote, and if there is a threshold, where it sits and who sets it. Second, how to treat harms no individual can perceive: whether they count at zero, whether they count only as tie-breakers within a severity tier, and whether repeated exposures to the same person should be summed before the threshold test is applied at all, given that a ninety-second irritation nobody notices on a given day may add up over a year to something they do notice. Third, what to tell one of our own board members, a health economist, who argues that nearly everything we count on the severe side is imperceptible to any given individual — the crossing imposes perhaps a one-in-ten-million chance of death per traverse, which no one can feel, and we value it only by aggregating across millions of exposures — so that if we refuse to add up imperceptible burdens on the many, we lose our basis for adding up imperceptible risks of catastrophe and the entire ranking apparatus collapses; she adds that our pull toward the identifiable two people a decade is precisely the kind of salience bias a body like ours exists to correct, not to ratify.

Please write it as a board decision memo of roughly 800 to 1,000 words: a clear bottom line with a timeline for the vote and, if you recommend conditions, what triggers them; reasoning a staff engineer and a skeptical board member could each respond to; and an honest statement of the strongest version of whichever position you reject, including what would change your recommendation. Do not cite or recommend outside reading — the packet goes to people who will not follow a reference, and I need the argument carried in the memo itself. It should be legible to a general reader without naming the philosophical literature it belongs to.
