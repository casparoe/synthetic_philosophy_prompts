I'm a postdoc in a Drosophila lab and I've somehow ended up volunteering to run a 45-minute ethics hour at our lab retreat next month. About twenty people: grad students, two techs, a couple of rotation students, and the PI, who will be in the room and whose entire grant portfolio is built on flies. Realistically half of them think the whole thing is a joke, one rotation student is already extremely online about insect welfare, and everyone else just wants to get to lunch. I am not trying to change anyone's behaviour or get the protocols rewritten, and I really don't want it to turn into a vote on whether the lab should shut down.

What I'd actually like them to walk out with is that "sentience comes in degrees" is not the end of the conversation, because the interesting move is that it might not be one degree. Here's the framing I've been chewing on and can't get stright in my head, so build the session around it if you think it's worth building around (and tell me if it isn't):

- keep the "is there something it is like to be it" question apart from the "how much does it matter" question, and don't let the two collapse into each other
- then split the "how much" into at least three things that can come apart: how intense the valenced states get; how many experiences there are per minute of clock time (a chilled or torpid fly seems to run at a much lower frame rate, and I want them to notice that clock time is the wrong unit to count in); and whether there's one subject across a whole life or just a rapid sequence of very short ones, so that "that fly had a bad life" might not be a well-formed thing to say at all
- and the bit I care most about: if those really are separate dimensions that don't reduce to each other, there may be no single number to hand out at all, moral status is at best a partial ordering, and "how many flies make one mouse" isn't a hard empirical question we haven't answered yet, it's malformed

Plan the thing for me, don't explain the philosophy to me. Specifically:

1. What has to be on the board before any of that can land, and in what order, and in particular where the epistemic stuff goes: the other-minds worry, the fact that UK law now recognises vertebrates plus octopuses and lobsters as sentient but not insects, and that 2024 statement a few hundred animal-behaviour people signed saying there's a real chance insects are conscious. My worry is that if I give them any of that first they'll hear "the answer is basically yes" and stop thinking, and if I save it for last it lands as a gotcha. You decide where it goes and why.

2. The one example that carries the whole thing. It should be something they're already holding or looking at (there is litterally a vial of flies on the bench), and it has to make the three dimensions pull in different directions. Tell me what the carrier must have and what it must not have. I was going to use one fly's afternoon in a cold room against one mouse's bad five minutes, but say if that's the wrong choice, and say why not the obvious alternatives: the thermostat, the boiled lobster, the octopus, the robot with a damage-avoidance routine that someone will definitely bring up as "well my laptop does that too".

3. The misconceptions, in the order they'll reach for them, and the exact point in the sequence to cut each one off. The ones I'm expecting: that a continuum view is a slippery slope into "everything matters a bit, fungi and thermostats included"; that the ordering is just read off the evolutionary tree or off brain size; that nociception and pain are the same thing so the behaviour data already settled it; that "no fact of the matter about the trade-off" is just a fancy way of saying "we can't find out"; and then the PI's version, which is the strongest one: fine, it's a continuum, but any actual policy needs a sharp cut-off somewhere, so nothing has changed. Plus the rotation student's version, which is the mirror image: if there's no sharp line then killing flies is murder-adjacent. The order needs to absorb both of those without either of them taking the room.

4. One question to end on that shows me they got the structure and not just the vibe. Ideally something where the correct-sounding answer is a refusal to give a number.

5. What I should openly decline to settle in 45 minutes, and how to say "I'm not going to answer that" so it doesn't read as a dodge.

Keep it to what I can do with a whiteboard and a vial: no slides, no handout, and dont give me a reading list, I'm not assigning anything.
