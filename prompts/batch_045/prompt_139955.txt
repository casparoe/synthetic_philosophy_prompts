For a third-year seminar on the philosophy of perception I'm making a one-page handout: a decision tree that starts from the bare Molyneux question — a person blind from birth whose sight is restored, shown a cube and a globe they already know by touch, asked which is which before they touch either — and sorts a student down to a named position on how the content of sight relates to the content of touch.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of schools: five to seven branch questions, each phrased as a verdict on a concrete case or claim that a student can answer without knowing which position it leads to, and each labeled by the distinction doing the splitting. The splits I want doing the work are: whether the question is settled empirically or by reflection on what a spatial representation could be; whether the two senses share an amodal spatial format or each has proprietary content; whether success means forced-choice matching, phenomenal similarity between how the thing looks and how it feels, or classification under a concept; whether a subject could pick out the right object demonstratively while having no representational content in common across the senses; and whether the shape is one the subject can name.

I also need three nonstandard variants handled inside the tree rather than appended to it — tell me which node each one discriminates and what each leaf predicts of it: (1) the delayed test, run after several weeks of ordinary visual experience but with no visual-tactile pairing; (2) the unnameable solid, an irregular polyhedron the subject has felt often but has no word or use for; (3) the picture variant, in which the subject must match the felt object to a photograph or line drawing of it.

Eight to ten leaves, each with a name for the position (or unnamed), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and, wherever you attach a historical name, a short flag on how confident you are anyone actually held it. Mark the answer-combinations the tree allows but nobody occupies, saying for each whether that is because it is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate.

Close with a note on where the instrument is weakest: which branch point is most contestable, where a leaf placement rests on a contested reading, and what result — empirical or argumentative — would make you redraw the tree. Please write in American English.
