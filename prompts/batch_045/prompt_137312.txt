I'm retired now — thirty-odd years as a developmental biologist, mostly on sea urchin fertilization — and I've been poking at metaphysics in the evenings, which may have been a mistake. I've got stuck on the origin business and I'd like someone to tell me where the wheels come off.

The claim, as I've met it, is the Kripke-flavoured one: a person could not have originated from any sperm and egg other than the actual ones. My parents were Catholic and I'm the middle of three boys, so over the relevant years there were a great many ejaculates, each carrying on the order of a hundred million sperm, and meiosis reshuffles the deck every time, so which one arrived first was pure chance. Suppose my parents had conceived a month later, as they easily could of: a different sperm wins, and there's no me, there's some other possible person instead. Origin essentialists say this cheerfully. But then I lose the thread. If it was a genuinely open, chancy matter which sperm got there right up to the moment it got there, then it was possible in every sense of "possible" I know how to use that a different sperm fertilized that egg — and the view says that couldn't have been me. So either the view is straightforwardly false, or "could not have" is being used in some special metaphysical sense that has come loose from how chancy processes in the world actually go, in which case I don't see what work it does or why I should care. My suspicion is the necessity is really conditional — if I exist at all, I came from these gametes — but if that's all it amounts to, it can't block the sort of "I could just as well have been somebody else" thought the view seems designed to block.

Second worry, from the old lab: gametes aren't clean packages of DNA. In us the sperm also delivers a centriole, some RNAs, whatever epigenetic marks it's carrying, while the egg supplies essentially all the cytoplasm and the mitochondria. So what is the essentiality supposed to attach to — those two particular cells, the DNA sequence, or the zygote that results? Two cases from the very same gametes: split the zygote at the two-cell stage and you get twins; inject sperm S's nucleus into an enucleated egg from a different woman (they do things along these lines in mice, as far as I remember). It looks to me like the view gives no principled verdict about wether the resulting person would be me, which makes me think the claim was never sharp enough to be true or false in the first place.

Where's the mistake? Keep it under 350 words, no citations or reading lists — I don't need references, I need the argument, and if I'm running two different things together please say plainly which two.
