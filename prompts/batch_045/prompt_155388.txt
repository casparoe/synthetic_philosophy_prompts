I'm an autodidact with no academic background in philosophy — I read and think in the evenings, roughly two hours a week, entirely for my own purposes. I want to spend the next year of that time on a single cluster of questions: whether my own death would be bad for me, and if so in what way, how much, and what follows from the answer. I have no library access and no interest in a reading list; please give me arguments and reasoning, not citations, bibliographies, or pointers to papers.

Please act as a professional philosopher who specializes in the value and metaphysics of death, and who is used to advising people on what is worth working on given a small budget of time. Take the question seriously and think carefully: before you produce an answer, do a short private brainstorm — list the candidate open questions in this area as you see them, note which ones depend on which, and only then commit to an ordering. Don't give me a survey of the field; give me a defended agenda.

Here is roughly where I've gotten on my own. First, there is the ancient symmetry line: the stretch of nonexistence before I was born does not seem to be bad for me, so why should the stretch after I die be any different? Second, there is the rival thought that death is bad not because of anything I undergo but because of what it takes away — the goods I would otherwise have had — plus the puzzle that if it takes them away, there has to be a time at which this is bad for me and a subject it is bad for, and both are hard to locate. Third, there is the question of what comparison class the deprivation is measured against: not "nothing", presumably, but the life I would in fact have gone on to lead.

What I can't work out is which of the live questions deserves my hours, and in what order. So: give me the three or four questions you'd put on my agenda, ranked, and defend the ranking explicitly — say what criteria you're using (how many other questions hang on the answer, whether someone working alone with a few hundred hours and no secondary literature can make real progress on it, how much the answer would change anything outside philosophy), and be honest about the trade-offs between those criteria. Tell me also which questions you'd drop as unproductive for me, and what kind of consideration would move something up or down the list — what would I have to notice for the ordering to flip?

Three ideas of my own have been nagging at me, and I suspect some of them are dead ends; I'd like you to steelman each briefly and then tell me whether it's worth my time, and why.

One: the replacement case. Suppose the decades I lose are not lost from the world — a stranger, unknown to me and unconnected to anything I care about, goes on to live a life containing exactly the goods mine would have contained, in the same quantities and of the same kinds. My instinct is that this makes no difference whatsoever to how bad my death is for me. But I'm not sure an account that measures what I'm deprived of can say that. Is working this out actually upstream of the main question, or is it a distraction?

Two: the interrupted-project reply to the symmetry argument. Maybe the real asymmetry isn't in the time but in the fact that in one direction there was something of mine already underway — plans, relationships, a life in mid-course — that got cut off. Is that doing genuine work, or is it just restating the intuition it was supposed to explain? And does the same move, run backwards, threaten to make the prenatal stretch bad for someone after all?

Three: the accounting question. If the badness of death is a matter of goods foregone, then it should scale with how good the foregone future was and shrink as the remaining future gets thin. That seems to imply death at eighty-five is a trivial harm, and that a death which prevents a mostly bad future benefits you — and I can't tell whether those are welcome results, reductios, or signs I've misunderstood the currency the accounting is done in.

Finally, the part I actually care about day to day: which of the alleged practical implications of this debate carry real weight for how a person lives, and which are overrated or don't follow at all? I'm thinking of things like whether fearing one's own death is rational, how much grief is appropriate, whether the answer should change how much money and effort go into health and life extension, and whether late-life goods should be discounted. Rank those by how much they'd really be changed if someone took a view seriously, and say plainly which ones are insulated from the philosophical question.
