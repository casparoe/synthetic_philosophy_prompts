I'm the lead editor of a 900-page clinical procedures manual for a hospital network — roughly seven hundred discrete recommendations (dosing tables, contraindications, escalation thresholds), each one traced back to a source document and initialled by one of six reviewers, me among them. I'd defend any single one of them, unprompted, if you stopped me in the corridor and asked about it. What I can't get straight is the thing I have to do next week, which is sign a one-page attestation for the compliance office. As drafted it says, roughly, that I attest that all clinical content in the manual is accurate and reflects current evidence as of the date of publication. It's a formality in the sense that the manual does not ship without it; it isn't a formality in the sense that my name goes on it and I intend to mean it.

The argument I can't shake came from one of our reviewers, a statistician, over lunch. If each of seven hundred recommendations has even a one-in-a-hundred chance of having gone wrong somewhere between the source document and the printed page, she said, then the chance the manual is entirely error-free is under one in a thousand — so either signing is arrogance, or the attestation doesn't mean what a plain reader would take it to mean. She thinks the honest course is to keep the customary front-matter disclaimer (every effort has been made to ensure accuracy; the authors and publisher accept no responsibility for errors or omissions) and refuse the attestation, or have it rewritten. My wife thinks I'm overthinking a signature. I have three half-formed reactions and don't trust any of them. First, her arithmetic treats the errors as scattered and independent, and they aren't: one retracted trial, or one mis-entered unit conversion in a shared template, takes a whole family of entries down together. That seems to make the aggregate number worse in one way and meaningless in another, and I can't work out which. Second, the disclaimer and the attestation can't both be honest, can they? Yet every textbook on my shelf carries that disclaimer, which suggests it is doing some work other than reporting what I believe. Third, someone proposed I dodge the whole thing by attesting to the process rather than the product — that each recommendation was checked against a source document by a qualified reviewer under a stated procedure. That sounds like the grown-up answer, except the same difficulty reappears one level down, since the claims about the procedure are themselves multiple, checkable, and individually less than certain.

I don't want references or a reading list; I couldn't chase them down this week, and I gather this is a known puzzle only from something a friend muttered at me. What I need is a recommendation I can act on: what should I actually sign, in what words, and what should I say to the statistician when she asks me, straight out, whether I believe every recommendation in the book is true. If the right answer is that I can rationally stand behind each one and still not stand behind all of them together, and that this isn't hypocrisy, I want that explained well enough to say out loud in front of the compliance committee without sounding like I'm splitting hairs to protect my signature. If the right answer is that I'm confused about something more basic — about what accurate means in a sentence like that one, or about what kind of object an attestation is, or about what the disclaimer is really for — then tell me that instead, and tell me which of my three reactions is the one that's pointing at the real problem.
