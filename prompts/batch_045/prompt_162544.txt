Four years ago I walked away from a five-year doctoral program in its fourteenth month, and I have regretted it almost continuously since — not mainly because of how things turned out, but because I abandoned a plan for reasons I had predicted in advance and, at the time of predicting them, did not endorse. I have just signed a three-year training contract with no exit clause and a comparable curve, and before I start I want to settle whether I am rationally permitted to treat the plan as binding on me later.

The case I care about has three stipulations that strip out the usual machinery. First, the preference change is pure: no new information, no change of belief, no discovery — I know now roughly what I will want in month fourteen, and I will know then that it passes. Second, there is no bookie: nobody is offering me trades, nothing will be taken from me, and there is no external party who could exploit any inconsistency in me. Third, I have no commitment devices — no deposit, no side contract, no one I can ask to stop me. So if anything rationally constrains my month-fourteen choice, it has to be the bare fact of my own foreseen sequence of preferences: not a pump and not a bond.

There is also a wrinkle I have never seen handled, and I suspect it is where the real action is: in my case the pump, such as it is, runs backwards. If I accommodate my month-fourteen self, nobody exploits him; he simply ends up worse off by his own lights, and at month thirteen he would have paid to be prevented from quitting at fourteen. Call this retroactive endorsement. My credence that anticipated retroactive endorsement gives me a genuine reason now — a requirement of rationality, not merely a prudential tip — is about 0.7. My partner's is about 0.2; she thinks my month-fourteen self is just a person with different preferences, and that overriding him is paternalism toward someone who happens to share my name.

Give me your number on that proposition, decomposed: how much rides on whether the money-pump argument has any normative force in the absence of an actual pump (or whether it was only ever a dramatization of an inconsistency), how much on whether there is such a thing as diachronic rationality at all rather than synchronic rationality plus prudence about future welfare, how much on whether pure preference change is coherent and whether foreseeing it undercuts it, and how much on whether retroactive endorsement is a distinct consideration or the same aggregation problem in different clothes. A probability and one line of justification for each term, then the combination; then say which term you are least confident in, what would move it most, and which of the two of us is most likely wrong about which sub-question. Close with your final credence in the form \answer{0.XX}.
