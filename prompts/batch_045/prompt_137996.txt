I keep going in circles on this and it's starting to annoy me. Set up: an admiral is deciding tonight whether to order a sea battle tomorrow. In the naval archive there's a Ledger — every evening at dusk a clerk in a sealed room, with no contact with the fleet and no way of knowing anything, writes one line: "battle tomorrow" or "no battle tomorrow". Over the last 200 days its been right every time. Nobody has any idea how. The clerk's line doesn't cause anything, nobody reads it untill the morning after, and the admiral has never seen it.

(1) Whatever the clerk wrote last night is now part of the past, and the past is fixed — nothing the admiral can do now makes it the case that a different line was written.
(2) If X is fixed and X can't be true without Y, then Y is fixed too: I can't make Y false without making X false, and by hypothesis I can't make X false.
(3) The line "battle tomorrow" can't be true without there being a battle tomorrow.
(4) So whether there is a battle tomorrow is already fixed. It isn't up to the admiral.
(5) Deliberation about whether to give the order is worth anything only if whether the order gets given is up to her.
(6) So her deliberation is worth nothing, and she might as well flip a coin or just do nothing.

Two things bug me. First, nothing in (2) to (4) depends on there being a clerk at all. Any true record anywhere would do the job, so the argument looks like it's really just "there is already a truth about tomorrow, and truths don't come and go" — the Ledger is only decoration. Second, it proves more than I can swallow. Run it again with the line "the admiral will weigh her reasons for ten minutes and then announce that deliberation is pointless" and (6) tells us that isn't up to her either, so the conclusion can't be acted on. Run it on a claim about what she is doing right now and it says the same about now. And if the fixity in (1) is really just the timelessness of truth, I don't see why the argument can't run backwards: whether she deliberated yesterday isn't up to her now either, and nobody thinks that makes yesterday's deliberation worthless.

I'd most like to keep (5) and the bit in (1) about the past being fixed. I really don't want to say there was no truth of the matter last night about tomorrow, since the line is already written down and has been right 200 times. So which step do I reject, what exactly is the slide, and what does rejecting it cost me? Does anything change if the clerk's line is somehow caused by tomorrow's battle rather than the other way round?
