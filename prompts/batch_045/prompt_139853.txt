hey — you're a professional philosopher who works on moral responsibility, especially the Strawson / reactive attitudes stuff, and I need you to think carefully and do some brainstorming before you answer. I'm writing my position up for a symposium on revisionism and I want someone to audit it before I commit it to print rather than explain the literature to me.

Roughly where I've landed:

(1) I'm Strawsonian about the metaphysics: the practice of holding each other responsible is constituted by the reactive attitudes, and there's no standpoint outside the practice from which determinism could show it to be unjustified. So determinism isn't a threat, and all that stuff about whether the agent could have done otherwise is barking up the wrong tree.

(2) Blame is apt or inapt depending on what the action expresses about the agent's will. It's a matter of fit with the meaning of what they did, not of what blaming accomplishes — bad consequences don't make a fitting blame unfitting.

(3) But I also think most of us blame way too much and that we should deliberately train ourselves out of it, drop to the objective stance more often in daily life, sub something forward-looking in for resentment. The revisionists are basically right that the practice does real damage.

(4) In particular: where someone's bad will is itself the product of an unjust background — the sexism they were marinated in, the options their upbringing closed off — blame is a mistake, because they didn't get to pick the will they're expressing.

(5) At the same time I think it's obviosly fine, maybe even called for, to resent the drunk guy at a dinner party who says something vile. I feel no pressure to go objective there, and I don't think consequentialist worries get any grip on that case.

So: which of these actually conflict, and how badly? Rank them by severity, say for each what would have to give and what that costs me, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I have a vague sense there's a load-bearing distinction I'm not making between the attitude itself and what you do with it, but I can't tell if that rescues (3) and (4) or just relabels the problem. Two specific things I want you to call: someone told me (1) versus (3) is a fake tension because Strawson lets the practice reform itself from inside — if that's a real dissolution and not a dodge, say so; and I want to know whether (4) as I've stated it quietly swallows (5), or whether there's a way to keep both.
