hey, I run a little reading group and I need a dialogue for tomorrow — Williams-style internalist about reasons vs. an externalist, but I don't want another run through the gin/petrol confusion case or the guy who's cruel to his wife, we've done those to death. Use this case instead, it's one I made up and I want to see how it gets handled:

Owen is about to let a colleague, Mara, take the blame for a screw-up he knows he caused. Nothing dramatic, no careers ending, but she'll eat it and he knows it. Three things about Owen's psychology, all stable and all known to him:

1. He has a standing policy: any consideration that reaches him in the shape of a moralising "you ought to" — from anyone in a position of authority, or from anyone who is visibly trying to improve him — gets zero deliberative weight. He doesn't just resist it, he stops counting it as a consideration at all. It's not that he weighs it and rejects it; the route is closed.

2. He genuinely and strongly wants to become the kind of person who notices and responds to what other people need. Call this his reform desire. But he pairs it with a no-manufactured-virtue clause: he won't act on any motive he can tell he talked himself into. For him a motive only counts as *his* if it wasn't installed on purpose. So he wants to be moved by Mara's situation, but any motive that arrives via deliberate self-reform is disqualified by that very fact.

3. He has no first-order inclination whatsoever toward coming clean. No guilt, no fear of being found out (he's pretty sure he won't be), no loyalty to Mara in particular.

So the shape of the problem, and this is what I want them to actually fight about rather than skate over:

- The externalist wants to say Owen has a reason to confess. The internalist wants to say that on their view there's no reason, because there's no sound deliberative route from Owen's actual motivational set to confessing, and the reform desire can't supply one.

- Non-composability: Owen seems to have a reason to *become the sort of person who would have a reason to confess*, and that person would have a reason to confess, but the two don't add up to a reason for Owen to confess now. Is that a reductio of internalism — reasons that hover around an agent without ever reaching him — or is it just the truth about a person who has carefully arranged not to be reachable? Whoever's view it favours, the other one has to say something real about it.

- The self-defeating bit: by clause 1, the externalist's own utterance "Owen, you have a reason to come clean" is a consideration arriving in exactly the discredited shape. So if the externalist's argument *works* as a speech act on Owen, it disables the very route it needed. And most moral reasons in ordinary life get announced in roughly that shape. Is externalism then self-undermining in practice, or only in a philosopher's mouth? And can the externalist distinguish believing there's a reason from being told there's one?

- Also have them settle, or at least visibly fail to settle, whether Owen is blameworthy, and whether anything hangs on that: is the point of an external reason to explain action, to make blame apt, to guide deliberation, or something else? The externalist shouldn't get to just assert the reason is there; she has to say what in the world makes it true and what work it's supposed to do. The internalist isn't allowed "any rational agent would see it."

Format and ground rules:

- Set it somewhere with a little specific detail — a bad conference hotel bar, a shared taxi, whatever — and keep it around 800 words, dialogue only, no stage directions beyond the odd beat.
- Both of them are good at this. Each has to land at least one hit the other genuinely answers instead of talking past, and each has to make at least one concession that costs them something, not a fake one. No agreeing at the end, no "so we were both partly right."
- At least once each, have them state what their view commits them to in plain non-technical words, the way you'd explain it to a smart friend who's read none of this. Minimise the jargon — "subjective motivational set" can appear maybe once, and if it does it should be immediately cashed out.
- Give them names, make them sound like different people, and let one of them be a bit funnier than the other.

Last thing: finish with a single line saying who you think had the better of it, in a fixed form — \answer{internalist}, \answer{externalist}, or \answer{neither} — followed by one sentence on what decided it for you. If you honestly can't pick, use \answer{neither} and say what's missing.
