I serve, somewhat improbably, as the resident philosopher on the editorial board of a multivolume series on non-human causation in world history, and it falls to me to draft the memo before our next acquisitions meeting. What we actually vote on is not whether germs, weather, or volcanoes cause things — everyone on the board grants that — but a narrower, expository question: may an author write a chapter in which a non-human cause appears as something that *does* things, the subject of verbs like approach, invade, refuse, retreat, decide, or must it be written up as a mechanism, a variable, or a stage? Years ago I gave the board a four-way vocabulary that it now uses unreflectingly: actors (things with a clock and a dynamics of their own), conditions (things that hold still long enough to be background), metronomes (cycles and tempos, which set a beat without acting), and echoes (non-human causes that on inspection turn out to be human actions in costume). Our rulings so far, in the order we made them: the plague bacillus in 1347–51, yes — an actor, with a minute recording that no one alive at the time knew what it was, so that invisibility is no objection; the rinderpest panzootic of the 1890s in eastern and southern Africa, yes — an actor, even though it arrived in the Horn inside cattle imported to feed an Italian army, and our minute noted that its spread was helped along by drought; the El Niño–linked droughts of those same African years, no — a metronome, a phase of a cycle rather than an agent; malaria and the Anopheles mosquito of the Roman Campagna, yes — an actor, but the minute insists it is "an actor and not an explanation," and the author had to keep landholding, drainage and pilgrim traffic on the page as co-authors; the Little Ice Age taken as a whole, no — a metronome again; the 1815 eruption of Tambora, yes for the eruption as a single event, subject to the same co-authoring requirement we imposed on the mosquito, so that grain prices and poor relief had to be named alongside the plume; the (historically contested) salinization of Sumerian fields, no — a condition, or possibly an echo; and, most contentiously, a proposal that made the tsetse fly the protagonist of a chapter on precolonial state formation in the savanna, no — on the explicit ground that granting the fly agential grammar would have let it carry the whole weight of a continental pattern and made that pattern look inevitable. I should say that I have never been able to tell whether our two 1890s rulings — the virus yes, the drought no — were an application of a principle or simply fatigue, and that matters a great deal for what comes next.

The proposal in front of us is a chapter on the collapse of the northern cod fishery off Newfoundland, ending in the 1992 moratorium. As its author frames it, this is a wild population with a bounded identity and dynamics of its own — recruitment failure, truncated age structure, apparent fisheries-induced evolution toward earlier maturation — whose decline ran across decades, inseparable in the telling from diesel trawlers, sonar, factory ships, subsidies and a federal department's optimistic quotas; and it ends in something as punctual as any siege, a closure in 1992 that ended a fishery half a millennium old and emptied outport communities of a good part of their population. I would like you to do three things. First, reconstruct the two or three rules that fit all eight of our precedents about equally well, and say plainly what each predicts here: my own sense is that some rule about self-generated dynamics and bounded identity pulls toward yes, some rule about determinism and explanatory weight pulls toward no, some rule about entanglement with human technique pulls toward no, and some rule about punctual legibility splits the case in two, but I cannot rank them. Second, tell me what we should decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which precedent is the closest analog, and — if, as I suspect, our precedents genuinely underdetermine it — what principle we would be committing the series to with each choice, since whatever we say about the cod will be cited at us for the remaining volumes. Third, name the single further hypothetical proposal that would best separate the surviving rules. Please also mark how confident you are in each part of what you say, and single out the one claim you are least sure of; I would rather present the board with a memo that flags its weak joint than a tidy one that hides it.
