I got into a shouting-ish argument at a dinner party a few weeks ago with a friend who's finishing a PhD in medieval history — I'm a statistician by trade and read popular history on the train, so I was out of my depth and have been chewing on it ever since. The fight started over whether the Scientific Revolution is a real thing or something historians cooked up in the twentieth century; I said it's real, she said that's exactly the sort of thing people always say about whichever stretch of time they happen to care about. Since then I've been trying to work out what I actually believe, and I've half-invented four tests that I keep applying, and they don't agree with each other. (1) The export test: a period is only a real period if the question of whether some other region was *in* it has an answer — so 'was Japan in the Renaissance?' should come out yes or no, and if it just doesn't apply, that's a movement, not an age. (2) The clustering test: the boundary has to show up in roughly the same place across unrelated subject matters — painting, farming, warfare, banking, religion — and not only in the one you happen to study. (3) The delete test: remove the label from the historian's toolkit and see whether any explanation gets worse; if nothing does, it was never more than a filing cabinet. (4) The acquisition test: a period is real when some capacity gets acquired that can't subsequently be lost, so that afterwards people can do something they couldn't do before. My worry is that 1 and 2 seem to be telling me something different from 3 and 4, and I can't tell wich pair is load-bearing.

Here are my gut verdicts, which I strongly suspect are inconsistent. Real: the Middle Ages — even though it's named for being a gap between two other things, which by my own lights ought to disqualify it — plus Classical antiquity, the Industrial Revolution, the Scientific Revolution, and Late Antiquity, which I feel oddly strongly about given that the label was basically invented in the twentieth century, and that fact should probably teach me something. Not real: the Dark Ages (that's just name-calling), the Axial Age, the Age of Revolutions, postmodernity, and the Baroque, which is a style rather than a stretch of time. Real but only as a convenience — I'd defend it as useful and deny it as discovered: the long nineteenth century and the Renaissance. Genuinely back and forth, can't settle: the Enlightenment, the Great Divergence, and the Digital Revolution, which I'm living through and so maybe shouldn't trust myself about at all.

So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or close to all of them? I'd like it stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself, with the case that motivates each clause, and I want to know which of my four tests is doing the real work, whether any of them is redundant, and whether 1 and 2 genuinely come apart from 3 and 4 or I've just been applying them sloppily. Be honest about which of my verdicts has to go — my guess is the Middle Ages is the most exposed and Late Antiquity is the one that breaks whatever rule I was unconsciously leaning on, but I want your read rather than my own. Then push whatever principle you land on past my list: what does it say about the Anthropocene, the Neolithic Revolution, the Bronze Age collapse, the Cold War, and the Information Age, and is there one of those where the principle returns an answer I'd hate and might therefore have to swallow? And finally: does the principle predict anything about which of our own labels historians two hundred years from now will still be using, and does it allow a period to be real for one subject matter and not for another, or does that concession quietly collapse into saying that none of them are real?
