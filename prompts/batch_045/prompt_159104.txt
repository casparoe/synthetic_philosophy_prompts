I run a small monthly philosophy-of-science reading group at our local library — I'm a retired GP, not an academic, and the group is mostly other retired people and a few students — and I'm trying to write up a short piece for our newsletter about an argumentative pattern I keep running into when we read popular evolutionary psychology. It started at a dinner party with a dispute about whether music is an adaptation: one guest gave a bonding-or-mating story, another said it's a byproduct, invoking the well-known cheesecake comparison, and someone else complained that the byproduct claim was just as unfalsifiable as the story it was meant to replace. What I've since noticed is how cheap the byproduct move is. When it is done properly the speaker names the trait that was actually selected for, says what the developmental or architectural connection is between that trait and the one being explained, and often says what finding would overturn the account. But very often it is done with none of that: the word spandrel, or just byproduct, or not an adaptation, is produced as a gesture, no selected trait is ever named, no constraint is described, and the move is treated as requiring no evidence of its own, while any adaptive story is held to an impossibly high standard. The failure of the adaptationist to prove her case is then quietly counted as a point in favour of the spandrel claim, and the matter is closed. I have watched exactly the same shape of argument in discussions of religion, of the human chin, of homosexuality in animals, of why we find some landscapes beautiful.

I don't think this is just the thing Gould and Lewontin were complaining about in their 1979 paper, and that is part of what I want to get clear. Their target was over-specification: the invention of a selection narrative for everything. What I am describing is under-specification, a refusal of any narrative at all, and it seems to me to be the mirror image of that vice while being a different move. It also isn't quite the same as protecting a theory with a face-saving auxiliary assumption, though it is close: what it protects is a background suspicion that adaptationist explanation is overreaching, and it protects it from ever being tested. And it seems to me to have something in common with arguments that treat our current ignorance as if it were positive evidence, which is why I half-expected there to be an established phrase on the model of a gap argument. On the other hand, I don't want to condemn the move outright: nobody thinks the male nipple needs an adaptive story, the mechanism is obvious, and I gather that treating drift or constraint as a kind of default or null hypothesis is perfectly respectable in some contexts. So what I need is the edges, not a verdict.

There is a second wrinkle that may or may not be the same phenomenon wearing different clothes. A trait gets labelled a spandrel, and then someone shows that it does something useful now — it has been co-opted, it is maintained by selection for something, it serves a function in the organism's current life — and the discussion immediately concludes that it was an adaptation after all, as though having a current function and having been shaped by selection for that function were the same property. Something about that slide feels like the converse error, and I can't tell whether it belongs to the pattern I described or is a separate mistake with a name of its own. So: is there an established concept for the first thing — the bare, unspecified byproduct gesture and its evidentiary asymmetry — and if there are several near neighbours, which fits best and how exactly does my case differ from each of them? If there is nothing established, please coin a term, define it tightly enough to separate the honest version from the conversation-stopping version, say what evidence would discriminate the two, and give me two or three boundary cases that fall just inside and just outside it. Please don't just hand me a reading list or tell me which authors to look up; I want the distinction carved, not a name to attach to it.
