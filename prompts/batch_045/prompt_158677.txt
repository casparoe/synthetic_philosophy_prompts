You are a professional philosopher who works on social ontology and the philosophy of science. Please think carefully and at length before answering: spend your first pass brainstorming several candidate principles, including some you go on to reject and say why, and only then settle on the single best one and defend it. I want a committed answer — a definite principle, a definite recommendation about which of my verdicts to give up, and reasons for both — not a survey of the options with the choice left to me. Where your principle rests on a stipulation rather than an argument, say so, and say what empirical or conceptual finding would lead you to abandon it.

I am trying to work out what I actually think about social construction, and I trust my verdicts on cases more than I trust my theory, so what I want is a principle that earns them, or else a clear account of which ones I have to surrender.

Two axes, so we are clear. For each kind I record (i) whether I think it is socially constructed, and (ii) whether I think it is real — where real means: there are truths about who falls under it that are not up to any individual, and membership can do genuine causal-explanatory work. I have invented two of the cases so that we do not get sidetracked by the actual history.

1. The US dollar. Constructed, obviously. Also paradigmatically real: it was true last Tuesday that I had forty dollars in my wallet, and the barista handed over the coffee because that was true.

2. Being Black in the United States. Constructed, and I do not think there is a biological essence it tracks. Still real: she is Black is true regardless of what anyone believes about her, and Blackness explains things — differential treatment by landlords and employers, differential exposure, differential health outcomes.

3. Being a witch, in early modern Europe. Constructed, and empty: nobody was one. I know there were feedback effects here too — the accused confessed, neighbours reinterpreted everything they did, some of them came to act the part — but I do not want to say that this made the kind real, the way I do want to say it in case 6. I cannot yet say what the difference is supposed to be.

4. Sex, defined by gamete type. Not constructed. Except that I notice the choice to define sex that way is ours, and other definitions are available, and that makes me less comfortable than I would like.

5. Gender. Constructed and real. But nobody instituted it: there is no declaration, no rulebook, no date at which it was brought into being, no one who could have done otherwise. That makes me unsure how much it really has in common with the dollar.

6. The Sillans (invented). In 1891 a colonial administration needed a tax unit and drew a boundary around three villages that spoke mutually unintelligible languages and shared no name, no institutions and no ancestry; everyone in the district had to tick Sillan on the census. By the 1970s there were Sillan political parties, a Sillan literary language with a press and a school curriculum, and noticeably more marriage within the district than across its border. Verdict: constructed at the outset, certainly. I also want to say it is real now. What I cannot say is whether it became real, whether it was always going to be real given what followed, or whether I am just being sentimental about it.

7. NRD, or nocturnal regulatory deficit (invented). A sleep clinic introduced the label in 1974 for a cluster of questionnaire responses and sleep-lab traces; decades later a large genetic study found that almost everyone carrying the label shares a specific polymorphism, which also predicts response to a particular drug. My verdict, which I suspect is incoherent: the label was invented, the kind was discovered, and this is not a socially constructed kind at all — even though, had the genetic study come back empty, I would happily have called it one. Whether a kind is constructed should not depend on how the science later turned out, so one of my two verdicts here has to go.

8. Unemployment. Constructed, real and causally potent; but nobody designed it, nobody decreed it, and the measurement conventions about who counts as looking for work feel arbitrary in a way that the constitutive rules of money do not.

9. Biological species. I want to say: discovered, not constructed. Yet there are several mutually incompatible species concepts, each adopted partly because it serves our explanatory interests, and which one you adopt changes how many species there are. I feel this ought to bother me more than it does.

10. Whiteness, where I go back and forth. If race is a social construction with real causal power, then whiteness should be the most real racial kind there is: it has done enormous causal work in lending, hiring and housing. And yet I keep thinking that whiteness is thinner than Blackness, largely defined by what it excludes, and therefore somehow less real. I do not think I can have both, and I do not know which one to drop.

My own tentative principle, for you to test rather than to flatter: a kind is socially constructed just in case what falls under it depends constitutively on some community's representations, practices or institutions, rather than solely on how things are anyway; and a constructed kind is real just in case those practices make membership in it robust and causally efficacious, so that people's being of that kind explains what happens to them. I think this gets cases 1, 2 and 8, that it silently conflates the category with the kind in case 7, that it is silent on 3 versus 6, and that it may make 9 come out constructed. Repair it if you can, replace it if you cannot.

Three further things. First, tell me whether construction really is one thing or whether I should be distinguishing the construction of a category from the construction of the kind it purports to pick out, and whether case 7 forces that distinction. Second, give your principle's verdicts on three cases I have not listed: marriage, nationhood, and the diagnostic categories of a DSM-style psychiatric manual such as major depressive disorder. Third, state plainly which of my verdicts above your principle cannot recover and which one you would advise me to overturn, in the order in which you would overturn them.
