Our housing co-op has to decide what to do with an unexpected legacy — fix the roof, buy a van, or put solar panels on the block — and before we'd even agreed on a procedure someone on the mailing list waded in with a long post invoking the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem to argue that every voting system is riggable, so we might as well let the chair just decide. I've pasted the relevant bit below, lightly tidied. My instinct is that the stuff about approval voting at the end is dodgy, but the rest sounds roughly right to me and I honestly can't tell where the theorem stops and his editorialising starts.

His summary went like this:

'Gibbard and Satterthwaite independently proved in the 1970s that any voting system with three or more candidates is either dictatorial or manipulable. "Strategy-proof" — the term of art — means that no group of voters can ever get a better result by coordinating and mis-stating what they want, and "manipulable" just means not strategy-proof. "Dictatorial" means there is a single voter whose favourite always ends up winning. The upshot is that this isn't a flaw of any particular system: first past the post, STV, Borda count, whatever — they are all manipulable, in exactly the same way and to exactly the same degree, so there's no mathematical reason to prefer one to another. There is really only one escape route, which is approval voting, where you tick everyone you find acceptable instead of ranking them: because the ballots aren't rankings, approval voting isn't covered by the theorem at all, and it is genuinely strategy-proof. It's basically Arrow's impossibility theorem restated for single-winner elections.'

Two things from our side that I'd also like checked. First, one member says we can dodge the whole result by adding a random tie-breaker: we all rank the three options, run a Borda count, and if two options tie we flip a coin — so the procedure isn't deterministic and, he says, the theorem doesn't apply. Second, our constitution says the van can only be bought if there's money left over after the roof is fixed, which in practice has always meant the van never comes up as a live possibility. Does that matter, or is it still a three-option choice as far as the theorem is concerned?

So: is this a fair summary? Which bits would someone who actually works on social choice accept, which would they wince at, and which are just wrong — and do the practical conclusions he draws (let the chair decide, switch to approval voting, bolt on a coin toss) survive once the summary is corrected?
