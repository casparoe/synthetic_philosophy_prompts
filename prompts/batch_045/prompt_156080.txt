ok so I'm in a value theory seminar this term and for the last short paper we're supposed to write up where we've landed on hard choices so the rest of the group can take it apart. I've tried to write down my actual position rather than something polished, and I'd like to know if it hangs together before I hand it in. Here it is, roughly in the order I'd defend it:

1. Some hard choices are genuinely incommensurable. There is no fact of the matter whether the music career is better than, worse than, or equally as good as the law career, and this isn't just ignorance or vagueness on my part — even if I knew everything about both futures I think the question would still have no answer.

2. Separate but related: for these pairs there's no common measure, no exchange rate between the values involved, and no "better all things considered" fact waiting to be found. That's what incommensurable means to me.

3. I don't buy the voluntarist line we read — the idea that in a hard choice you create value by throwing your will behind one option, so that choosing is what makes it the case that this option is better for you. That sounds like magic. Value is there or it isn't; I'm not conjuring it into existence by deciding.

4. But I also don't want to say the choice is therefore arbitrary. Some resolutions seem better than others: quitting music for a safe office job purely because your parents nagged you into it is a worse way to resolve the choice than quitting because you've come to care about something else more. So the resolution can be good or bad even though, given (1) and (2), nothing makes either option better.

5. Methodologically: the main reason I believe (1) and (2) is the phenomenology. Deliberation doesn't settle these choices however long you go on, the reasons seem to run out rather than balance out, and afterwards there's this residue of regret that doesn't feel like a mistake. I know that isn't a proof, but I lean on it pretty hard.

6. In practice I still make all-things-considered comparisons across different values all the time and I trust them. I'm confident that a life with slightly less integrity but a lot more security is better overall in some cases, and that funding malaria nets beats buying myself a nice guitar. And when I'm stuck on a hard choice, what actually unsticks me is asking which option makes for a more coherent life story, or fits better with the kind of person I'm trying to become.

7. Two case verdicts. (a) If two options are incommensurable, I think it's fine to pay some small cost to switch from one to the other and then back again later — I don't think that's irrational, and I don't think the small-improvement argument (surely if you nudge one option slightly upward it now beats the other?) touches (1). (b) I think regret after resolving a hard choice can be fitting, and I also think it can be fitting to choose the same way again if you had it to do over. I want both of those.

My seminar leader says I can't hold (2) and also keep talking as if the options are comparable — that if there's no fact of the matter about which is better then there's nothing there to compare in the first place. I feel like that's wrong but I can't say exactly why, and I suspect there's something else in the list that's more badly broken than that.

So: does this hang together? I want an audit, not a summary and not a lecture. Go through the commitments pairwise, tell me which ones really pull against each other and how badly, and which ones only look like they conflict until you're careful about a distinction. For each real tension, tell me what the cheapest repair is and what it would cost me elsewhere in the set. Rank them by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. And if you had to get me to drop exactly one commitment to clean up the most, which is it — I don't have a favourite to protect here, so please just pick one instead of telling me there's some compromise version of everything.
