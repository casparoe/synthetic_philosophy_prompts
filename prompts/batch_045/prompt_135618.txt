I am setting the take-home final for a fourth-year seminar on metaethics, and I need one question on free-floating rationales — normative reasons that are not reasons for anyone. Candidates have forty-eight hours, a limit of 1,500 words, and the question carries 30 marks.

The difficulty I want the question to isolate is this. Every candidate in the cohort can already recite the two headline positions: that reasons are irreducibly normative facts that obtain independently of any agent's motives and perspectives, and that a reason is necessarily a reason *for* someone, so that the fundamental normative facts are always agent-indexed. What I want to test is whether they can see that the second position appears to purchase its result by relocating the irreducibly normative to some other kind of item, and whether they can then say whether that relocation is a real gain or merely a change of subject.

The argument I have in mind, which I have been calling the remainder argument, runs roughly thus. Any relationalist theory must state some general principle specifying which considerations are reasons for which agents to do what; and it must also say how the reasons of distinct agents bear on judgments that are not about any single agent, such as which of two outcomes is better, or whose opportunity matters more. Neither the general principle nor the rule of combination is itself a reason for anyone. So the relationalist seems to have abolished free-floating *reasons* only by admitting free-floating *normative principles*.

I want the question to force candidates to run that argument on a concrete case instead of gesturing at it, and I want the case to be built so that the standard escape routes cost something. The case I have been working with has two agents who cannot affect each other's situations at all: Ada, who can prevent one severe injury to a stranger, and Bo, who can prevent five mild injuries to five different strangers on an island that Ada can do nothing about. There seems to be a fact about whose opportunity matters more, yet it is not a reason for Ada, not a reason for Bo, and not a reason for anyone else, since no one else is positioned to act on it.

Please produce three things.

1. The question exactly as it should appear on the paper: one self-contained essay question that states the case and the remainder argument within itself, with the word limit and the marks. It should be impossible to answer well by summarising the debates over reasons fundamentalism, constructivism, or buck-passing accounts of value, and it should compel the candidate either to vindicate the remainder argument or to show precisely where it fails. Do not attribute any view to a particular philosopher, and do not require knowledge of any reading beyond the question itself. The question should close by instructing candidates to say explicitly what they take the underlying motivation for relationalism to be, since whether the remainder argument bites depends on what the view was supposed to achieve.

2. A model answer of roughly 800 words, in the voice of a very strong candidate who nonetheless commits to one substantive position rather than surveying options and hedging.

3. A marking scheme totalling 30 marks, organised around five must-hit points worth six marks each. Each point should name a specific move a top answer has to perform — for instance, distinguishing two readings of "free-floating," or showing why the comparative fact in the case cannot be reconstructed as a reason for a hypothetical observer or for the agents jointly — rather than naming a topic to be discussed. For each point, say what a merely competent answer does instead of the required move, and add a single line on the way candidates typically get it wrong. Close with a short note on what separates a first-class answer from an upper-second.
