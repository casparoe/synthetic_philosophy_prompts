The course has covered the naive regularity theory, Lewis-style best-system accounts, Armstrong-style necessitation, and dispositional essentialism.

QUESTION (one of three; 30 minutes)

Two distinctions matter for the Humean/anti-Humean dispute:

(i) Supervenience: do the laws of a world supervene on a purely categorical "Humean mosaic" of local matters of particular fact, or on a base that also contains irreducibly modal properties, such as causal powers or dispositional essences?

(ii) Governance: do laws merely describe or summarize what happens, or do they also govern, generate, or produce the events that fall under them?

(a) Show that these two distinctions are independent of one another, and give an example of a view that is anti-Humean in sense (i) while remaining non-governing in sense (ii).

(b) Call the following "powers best-system theory" (PBST): the laws of a world are the axioms and theorems of the system of generalizations that achieves the best combination of simplicity and strength, where strength is measured over a base that includes the dispositional powers of particulars.

Assess PBST against both distinctions. In particular: does the presence of powers in the base do any work in *selecting* which generalizations are laws, or only in settling what the candidate generalizations are true of? And does the theory thereby answer the two standard difficulties for Humean accounts — the problem of distinguishing genuine laws from merely accidental generalizations, and the objection that Humean laws cannot account for the counterfactual robustness and explanatory role of laws?

STUDENT'S ANSWER (verbatim)

Humeanism is the claim that there are no necessary connections between matters of fact. Two things need to be separated. First, supervenience: laws must supervene on the mosaic, the totality of local, particular, categorical matters of fact. Second, governance: laws do not make events happen; they only describe the pattern those events happen to exhibit.

The two distinctions are independent, because a view can reject the mosaic while still holding that laws govern nothing. Dispositional essentialism is the example: if salt's power to dissolve in water is part of what it is to be salt, then laws supervene on a base containing irreducibly modal properties, so the view is anti-Humean in the first sense. But laws still do not govern on such a view — the powers do the producing, and a law is just the generalization that reports what those powers produce.

PBST is anti-Humean in the supervenience sense, since the base over which strength is measured contains powers rather than a categorical mosaic. It is also anti-Humean in the governance sense: because the powers are in the base, the laws now have something to do — they describe what the powers bring about, and so they govern the events that follow.

The main advantage of PBST is that it solves the problem of accidental generalizations, which sinks both the naive regularity theory and Humean versions of the best-system account. The problem is that a true universal generalization such as "every coin in my pocket is silver" is not a law. PBST solves it because powers guarantee their manifestations: where a power is in the base, the corresponding regularity holds of necessity, whereas the coins in my pocket simply happen to be silver. Accidental generalizations are exactly the ones with no power behind them, and a system that trades simplicity against strength will leave them out.

The same point disposes of the objection about counterfactual robustness and explanation. Because the laws are backed by powers, they support the relevant counterfactuals: it is the power of salt to dissolve in water that makes it true that this cube would dissolve if it were immersed, while "every coin in my pocket is silver" supports no counterfactual at all. That is also why laws, unlike accidental generalizations, can appear in scientific explanations.
