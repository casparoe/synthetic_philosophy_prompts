A friend and I watched Arrival last night and argued late about whether the film is essentially Einstein's own position dramatised. His claim: the package Einstein actually held — cosmic religion, meaning reverence for the lawful order of nature with no personal God, no providence and no afterlife, together with a determinism about the will that he nonetheless combined with going on choosing, praising and blaming like everyone else — is exactly what Louise is living, and her decision to have a child whose death she has already been shown is that package in the first person. I pushed back but couldn't make the pushback precise, so I want a reading rather than a verdict on who won the argument.

Two places I suspect it goes wrong. First, I think the analogy may smuggle in the thing Einstein rejected: his awe attaches to structure, whereas the film's central motif is that the future is given to Louise, the language is a gift, the whole hangs together as a story with an arc. Call that latter attitude narrative piety, awe that depends on the world being addressed to you. Is that distinction stable, or does cosmic religious feeling collapse into narrative piety the moment someone actually lives inside it rather than asserting it in an essay? And if it is stable, which side of the line does the film end up on? Second, the free-will thread: does Louise's situation really illustrate determinism, or does it illustrate something the determinist picture has no vocabulary for — knowledge of an outcome that is itself a causal ingredient in producing it, as in the scene where she uses a private remark she has not yet been told in order to avert the war? I want to know where the Einsteinian reading does genuine work and where it is only atmosphere.

So: take the film seriously and take the philosophy seriously. Don't reduce Arrival to an illustration, and don't stretch Einstein to make it fit; say which scenes carry the reading and which resist it, and whether in the end the film adds anything to these two debates that the arguments alone don't — the felt difference between submitting to a foreseen loss and consenting to it, say — or whether that appearance of depth is manufactured by the last act. And tell me where your reading is most likely to be wrong and what would change your mind: which assumption about what cosmic religion really commits a person to, or about what the film is doing in its closing movement, would flip your answer.
