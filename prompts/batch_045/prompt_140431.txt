I'm revising a paper draft in which I defend a version of omissive eliminativism: roughly, that absences, failures, and non-occurrences are never themselves causes, and that every apparently causal claim about an omission is either elliptical for a claim about some positive event or state that did occur, or is really a normative, explanatory, or epistemic judgment wearing causal vocabulary. My worry is no longer whether the positive argument works; it's whether the view has any exposure left at all. Every time I press on it, it seems to absorb the pressure by redescribing the case, and I can't tell whether that is the ordinary stability of a research programme or a sign that I have qualified the thesis into something unfalsifiable. So I want a map of what it risks, not a survey of the literature and not a reading list: I want arguments, sorted, with the sorting defended.

Here is my current inventory of candidate defeaters, which I'd like you to rank from fatal to merely awkward and then go beyond. (1) The interventionist parity argument: in the formal frameworks used to model prediction and control, whether the gardener waters can be set, randomized, and intervened on just as cleanly as how much she waters, and a variable taking the value zero behaves like any other value; if causation is whatever answers to intervention and supports explanation, omissions qualify. (2) The default-relativity parity argument: I claim that omissive causation is disqualified because whether the plants died because of the not-watering depends on what counts as the normal or expected course of events, but structural-equation and contrastive treatments suggest all causal claims are sensitive to a contrast class or a default, so my disqualifying feature is not special to omissions. (3) The relevance-preserving paraphrase problem, which is the one that actually keeps me up: when I paraphrase there was no heat input as the fuel valve was shut, the positive state I substitute seems to matter only because it excludes heat input, so the absence appears to be doing the causal work in the explanation after all, and my paraphrase looks like it borrows its relevance from the very thing it was supposed to eliminate. (4) Double prevention: much of biology and medicine appears to work by something's not happening, as when cell death results from a repair mechanism's failing to engage, and eliminating omissions here may cost more than it saves. (5) The cost-to-practice argument: if I am right, an enormous amount of ordinary, historical, and scientific explanation is systematically in error, and a thesis that implies that much error had better be nearly forced. (6) The empirical evidence that ordinary attributions of causation to omissions track whether the agent was supposed to act rather than bare counterfactual dependence, which I currently treat as supporting my view but which I concede might cut the other way if the norm-sensitivity turns out to be present for actions too. (7) The suspicion that the whole dispute is verbal, so that nothing is really at stake.

For each of these I want three things: whether a committed eliminativist should concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it as question-begging, which further case or result it stands or falls with, and what would have to be true for it to become decisive rather than merely annoying. I'd also like you to add defeaters I haven't listed, especially any that tie the thesis to a commitment elsewhere that I might not notice I have. Two further questions I care about particularly. First, does anything outside philosophy actually turn on whether omissions are causes: negligence liability, the way inquiries into disasters apportion responsibility between what was done and what was not done, epidemiological claims about harms that followed from a programme's not being implemented? If nothing practical turns on it, is that itself fatal, or is it compatible with the thesis being true and merely terminological? Second, and relatedly: if you conclude that the view is well insulated, tell me honestly whether that is a defect for a thesis of this kind or simply what metaphysical theses about causation look like when they are in good shape. I am most likely to be moved by something that shows my paraphrase strategy cannot preserve explanatory relevance, and least likely to be moved by straightforward appeals to what people are inclined to say; please weight accordingly, and say if you think those weightings are themselves a mistake.
