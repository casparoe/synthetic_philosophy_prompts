So I got dragged to a city council budget hearing last month by my roommate (she's in a tenant union) and I've been chewing on it ever since. There was an ammendment to move something like 8% of the police budget into an unarmed mobile crisis unit, and the word "defund" got used by basically everyone in the room, apparently meaning different things. One councilmember voted no and said defund is just abolition by the back door, so a yes vote is a vote to end policing whether you'll admit it or not. A guy from the union afterwards said the opposite — that the ammendment was plain old reform and that abolitionists backing it are kidding themselves. Then some op-ed I read said a few cities already tried defunding and crime went up, so the idea is dead. Same slogan doing three different jobs, and I'm sure at least two of them are relying on a reading that won't hold, but I can't say which.

Can you lay out as a list the distinct precise claims "defund the police" could be making, and for each one say whether it's near-trivial, substantive and defensible, or false/incoherent? I don't just mean "cut the budget by X%" versus something bigger. The distinctions I keep tripping over: is it about the *level* of funding or the *direction* of the marginal dollar; is the money going to another city agency, to community orgs, or simply disappearing (nobody seems to defend that last one but it might be what "defund" literally says); is it about which *functions* police still handle — traffic, mental health calls, schools, encampments, noise complaints — as opposed to how much money they have for the functions they keep; is it armed vs unarmed response; is the endpoint a permanently smaller department or zero police; is it a one-time cut, a ratchet that keeps going, or a tactic meant to force a bigger restructuring; does it include legal stuff like the union contract, disciplinary rules and who does the investigating, or is it purely a budget question; and does it include dissolving the department and re-creating it under a new name and contract, which as far as I know does happen in real life, and if so whether that counts as abolition or as the opposite of abolition.

Then tell me which reading each of my three people needs for their claim to work, and whether they get it. Does the "abolition by the back door" charge depend on the reading where it's a ratchet, or does it work even on the narrowest readings? Does the op-ed actually refute anything, or does it assume a reading defenders don't hold?

Here's the sorting device I've been using, and I want you to use it or tell me it's broken: (a) the authorization set — which agents the state permits to use force, and in what circumstances; (b) the deployment set — which agents actually get dispatched to which situations; (c) the capacity set — money, headcount, gear, and legal protection. My thought was that reform shrinks (b), defund shrinks (c), and abolition empties (a), and that the thing people always repeat about the state holding a monopoly on legitimate physical force is a claim about (a) only — so shrinking (b) and (c) leaves the monopoly completely untouched, and emptying (a) is the only move that dissolves it. But there's a wrinkle: a monopoly you can't enforce maybe isn't a monopoly, so maybe (a) needs some amount of (c) to be real at all. And a second wrinkle I can't place: an unarmed crisis team that can still detain someone or start an involuntary hold is doing coercion without violence, so which set does it go in, and does it sit inside the monopoly or outside it?

I want the conceptual map here, not crime stats and not a reading list — nothing I'd need a library for. And if one of my readings is a strawman nobody actually holds, say so.
