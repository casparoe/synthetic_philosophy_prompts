I teach a 75-minute session for about 18 second-years on a "Signals, Games and Meaning" module — roughly half philosophy, half biology, most of them have seen a prisoner's dilemma but nothing formal. I want a game that makes them *feel* the handicap principle rather than hear about it, and I'd rather not say the phrase (or mention peacocks — everyone's seen that one a hundred times) until the debrief.

The three things I want them to walk out with, in order:

1. That a signal being expensive does nothing by itself. It has to be expensive *differently* for the two types of sender.
2. That the honest outcome isn't a property of the signal at all — it's a loop. What the signal is worth depends on what receivers do with it, and what receivers should do depends on who's sending it.
3. That even a properly differential cost stops working if the sender can avoid actually paying it.

For (3) I had an idea I want you to build round three around, but I'm not sure it tests what I think it tests. Two variants, you pick or replace:

- A pawnshop: before sending, any player can take out a loan to cover the signal's cost, repayable only out of the payoff they get if the receiver accepts. If they're rejected the debt is forgiven.
- A resale window: after sending a costly signal you get a receipt worth something, and receipts can be sold to other players at a discount, so the person displaying the signal needn't be the person who paid for it.

If neither of those actually isolates the point, say so and swap in something better. Something should visibly break in round three and it should feel like a scandal when it does.

Constraints: no real money (I've got poker chips and chocolate, and I can offer a tiny participation credit), no tech beyond a projector for a tally sheet, materials limited to cards, envelopes and chips. Senders know their own type, receivers never do, and I'll have receivers sit facing away with signals passed in envelopes. I don't want a lecture up front — they should get the payoffs, play, and reconstruct what happened afterwards.

What I'd like from you: the full runnable thing (setup, role assignment, how many rounds and how matching works, the actual payoff numbers worked out so a second-year can do the arithmetic in their head, what I write on the board), then a debrief that maps specific moments onto (1), (2) and (3). Plus the honest parts: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it in the room, and which feature of the real idea the game simply can't reproduce so I'll have to say it myself.
