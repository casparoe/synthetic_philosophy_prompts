I am drafting the framing chapter of a dissertation on the epistemology of markets, and before I build anything on it I would like an audit of the position I have arrived at. I have tried to order the commitments myself and I cannot tell which one is load-bearing in the wrong way. The set is this.

1. The no-trade results associated with Milgrom and Stokey are the right place to start. Under a common prior, and when the way traders come by their information is itself common knowledge, differences in information alone do not generate mutually advantageous trade. So the null hypothesis for heavy speculative volume is never that somebody knows something; it is risk-sharing, liquidity demand, non-common priors, or error, roughly in that order of preference.

2. I am resistant to behavioural explanations. High volume, persistent disagreement, and what looks like overconfidence do not strike me as evidence of irrationality, and where a rational-choice explanation is available I prefer it. A large part of what attracts me to (1) is that it seems to underwrite this: it lets me account for trade without invoking error.

3. I think the disagreements that actually move markets are not disagreements about probabilities within a shared space of possibilities. They are disagreements about which partition of the space is the right one: about the model, not the prior. On this reading the common prior is best understood as a coherence constraint on the analyst's own model rather than a claim about anyone's psychology, and much of what goes by the name of disagreement in markets is not representable in the framework the standard theorems use at all.

4. A consequence I also want to keep: if (3) is right, then a population of traders who genuinely shared a model would be epistemically worse off, not better. Diversity of models is partly what makes prices informative, so the common prior is not merely unrealistic; it describes a state of affairs I should hope not to see.

5. Hayekian aggregation. Prices pool dispersed, local, largely inarticulable knowledge in a way no planner or expert panel could replicate, and this is the main epistemic argument for deferring to markets. I also accept the Grossman and Stiglitz point that perfectly informative prices would remove the incentive to produce information, and hence that efficiency has to be partial and that noise trading has to exist. I do not think that undercuts (5); I take it only to bound it.

6. A verdict in the spirit of the Aumann agreement results. Two forecasters with access to the same evidence, who know each other's published numbers and each other's calibration records, and who still disagree: at least one of them is making an error or misrepresenting what she believes. I do not think there is room for reasonable disagreement in that configuration.

7. A verdict on inside information. I support prohibiting trade on material non-public information, and I want to justify that epistemically rather than by appeal to fairness: trading on undisclosed private information, I want to say, degrades the market's capacity to aggregate information.

What I would like is an audit rather than a survey. Which of these are genuinely in tension with which others, ordered by severity rather than listed as equally serious; for each real tension, which commitment has to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set; and which of the apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, with a note on whether the dissolution is complete or leaves a residue I should record. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If I am relying on some term in two different senses in different places, say where, and if two of these are consistent only because of a distinction I have not drawn, draw it for me and say whether it is a distinction I can live with.
