I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the two-envelope paradox, and I have to teach it in eleven days, so I would rather not improvise the session.

Here is roughly where we got stuck. She holds that the whole thing dissolves into a schoolboy error about variables: once you notice that the "x" in "the other envelope contains either half or twice what yours does" is not a constant but a placeholder standing for different amounts in the two branches, the five-quarters calculation collapses and there is nothing left to explain. I said that diagnosis is too fast, because the argument can be rewritten with no suspicious algebra at all and still seems to go through, and that the real hinge is the step where you treat the two cases as equally likely once you have looked inside your own envelope. She replied that symmetry settles everything — before you open an envelope and after too, since nothing you can see is evidence about which envelope you were handed. I said that last claim is straightforwardly false for at least some of the ways the money could have been put in. We never closed the gap, and we were also talking past each other about whether "always switch" can ever be genuinely correct. She thinks that question is empty because the expected value is infinite anyway; I think that is one special case rather than the explanation.

So: one eighty-minute session, about thirty first-year students in an introductory rational-choice course, mostly philosophy and economics majors. They can multiply out a simple expected value and that is the extent of their probability background — they will not follow a conditional-probability calculation, so whatever the session does with the idea of "equally likely, given what you see" has to be done with counts, or with a table, rather than with notation. None of them has met the puzzle. I am not asking you to explain the paradox to me; I am asking for a plan for explaining it to them.

Please give me the following.

1. The order in which the pieces have to arrive, and what each step depends on. In particular, where the closed-envelope version, where you switch without looking, belongs relative to the open-envelope version, and why. I currently think the closed version should come first, but I cannot say precisely what it buys you.

2. One carrying example that the whole session hangs on. I have been playing with a stipulation of my own and would like the plan built around it: a machine that places two-to-the-k dollars in the smaller envelope, where k is fixed by a procedure I can write on the board and then vary, and a display on your envelope that shows the amount only to within a range — say the leading digit is legible and the rest is smudged. Tell me what such an example must have and must lack in order to do the job, how to set k for the first pass, and what to vary on it and at what moment. If a different example would carry it better, say so and say what my machine is missing.

3. The misconceptions to expect, in the order they will form, with the exact point in the sequence at which I should head off each one. The two my friend arrived at — that the variable point is the whole story, and that symmetry settles the open case too — need to be handled as live errors rather than as strawmen, since a good part of the room will reach them unaided. Add whatever else you would expect from this audience, including any that the design of my machine invites.

4. The one thing I should openly decline to explain at this level, and say out loud that I am declining.

5. A single end-of-session question that would show me whether they had actually gotten it, as opposed to having learned to repeat "it depends on the prior." Ideally a version of the machine with a parameter they set themselves and a number they have to produce.

Please write the plan in American English.
