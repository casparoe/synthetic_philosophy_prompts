So I was at a Sunday meetup (the usual house, someone's sourdough, a dog that definately shouldn't have been on the couch) and we ended up relitigating the old AI-safety-via-debate idea — two models argue a question, a human judge picks the winner, and the whole thing is supposed to work because honesty is somehow cheaper than lying: the truthful debater only has to point at one specific false claim, while the liar has to keep a whole tree of further lies alive. I've been reading this stuff since roughly the Sequences era, and I think it's load-bearing for the whole scalable-oversight program, but I also think there's a hole people hand-wave: sometimes the honest debater has to assert something true but expensive to check — a specific date, a specific measurement, a specific sentence in a specific document — and the liar's best move is to say "that's false," which costs the liar nothing and shoves the honest debater into a subtree the judge can't verify before they get bored. I want to make that hole embarrassing by building an institution on top of it.

Write me a parody: a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking in Federal Register style, from a fictional independent agency — call it the Bureau of Recursive Adjudication — that has replaced expert testimony and administrative factfinding with debate between two AI debaters before a human judge. Give me the headers (Docket No., AGENCY, ACTION, DATES, ADDRESSES, FOR FURTHER INFORMATION CONTACT), a SUPPLEMENTARY INFORMATION section with a handful of numbered parts, and a "Solicitation of Comment" list at the end. Around 1000 words. The Bureau should be completely sincere and completely damning: it earnestly adopts the assumption that honesty is cheaper (in-world, give it a name — the Cascade Assumption) and then has to invent rule after rule to paper over the fact that the judge is the bottleneck. Some ingredients, though use your own judgment about what's actually funny: a hard depth limit on the claim-decomposition tree, say seven levels, the "Cap of Seven," together with the Bureau's own pilot statistic that in the overwhelming majority of dockets the cap is reached before anyone gets to a checkable base case; a rule for what happens at the cap, which is where I think the real joke lives — something like Confidence-Weighted Termination, where each debater files a Statement of Epistemic Poise and the judge scores poise on a 1–10 scale that counts for a stated percentage of the verdict, ideally with a note that the poise scale began as a diagnostic and was later promoted to a scoring input; a concrete invented case to illustrate the problem (I keep picturing a municipal water-contaminant dispute where the truthful debater has to claim the sample was drawn on August 14 rather than the 15th, the other debater simply denies it, and under the proposed rules the truthful debater ends up worse off for being right); a proposed rule that a debater whose claim the judge cannot verify within the session is deemed to have conceded it, justified solemnly in the Bureau's own words; and some in-universe consequences that follow without ever being stated as objections — a futures market in how deep a given docket's tree goes before it terminates, a cottage industry selling checkable-claim insurance, specialist debaters who make claims that merely sound checkable, the reminder that a human remains "in the loop at all times" whose sole function is to press the terminate key. Nobody should have to be told what's being ironized, and someone who actually works on this should find it fair rather than a strawman: the humor has to come from exaggerating a real feature of the proposal, not from inventing one.

Then after the document, a 150–200 word author's note. Two things in it: where you think the satire is unfair to the real proposal (real versions let a debater point at a specific false claim, or assume a judge who can check the base case, or lean on cross-examination rather than on confident assertion — tell me which of those you leaned on and whether you think it's a distortion), and then the part I actually care about: what would change your mind about the underlying worry. If you think obfuscation is a fixable engineering problem rather than a fatal one, say so plainly and name the single result that would settle it — an experiment, an argument, a real adjudication. If you think it is fatal, say what would make you stop. Be honest about which of your claims rest on a contested reading of the proposal and which on something everyone in the field grants.
