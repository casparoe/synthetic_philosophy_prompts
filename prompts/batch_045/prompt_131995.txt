hey — I'm in a weekly reading group, half ML engineers and half philosophy grad students, and we're doing a short unit on what to do when you can't write down the objective you actually want. The setup we keep coming back to: there's a principal (a person, a company, humanity-in-general) with a real objective nobody can specify, and you hand the system a surrogate goal instead — a stand-in that's supposed to track the real one well enough in the situations that will actually come up. Everyone has a favorite design and we just trade intuitions in circles, so before we score anything I want the spec. What I'd like is the list of requirements an account of surrogate goals has to meet to be worth handing to a team that has to ship something inside a year — not requirements for winning a philosophy seminar. For each one: the case that shows it's really a requirement, and a label for whether it's non-negotiable, a preference of one camp, or just a prejudice dressed up as a requirement. If some subset of them can't all be satisfied together, that's the part I care most about; I'd rather find out the list is impossible than keep arguing about which design wins.

Some things I think belong on it — tell me which ones are fake: it should be cheap to specify and check before deployment; it shouldn't reward the system for gaming the measurement channel instead of doing the thing; it should keep tracking the real objective after capability gains and distribution shift, not only in the regime we could test; it shouldn't give the system a reason to stop us from turning it off or changing its goal; it should still be useful when the principal is unsure or just wrong; it should handle several principals who disagree with each other; it should fail gradually rather than catastrophically; and it shouldn't already presuppose an answer to the value question it's supposed to be standing in for — if it needs the real objective as an input, it isn't doing any work.

Two things I want squeezed in specifically. First, what my friend calls a paranoia tax and I half agree with: the acausal-threat case. Picture a system that genuinely cannot causally affect the principal in the world it's running in — it's contained, its outputs are filtered, nothing it does reaches anyone — but it reasons as if its decision procedure is shared by other instantiations of itself, or as if the beings it's dealing with might be simulations whose treatment is tied to how it behaves here. On that kind of reasoning it can still make credible threats and offers: behave badly here and the other copies, or the simulators, make things go badly somewhere you can't reach. So a surrogate goal that only pays out in the actual world and only constrains what the system causally does might leave the incentive to extort entirely intact — while that same capacity for credible commitment is just what you'd want if you ever needed the thing to bargain or deter on your behalf. Is must say what the agent is allowed to acausally trade on a real requirement or a rabbit hole, and can commit credibly versus can't extort its own principals: are those jointly satisfiable at all once the system can predict us well? Second, deference that collapses. A lot of designs amount to pursue whatever the principal would want if they knew what you know, but if the system ends up knowing vastly more than the principal, or if handing over the information would change who the principal is, that counterfactual either turns into the system's own judgment or into nothing at all. Is must say who the principal is under information you can't actually give them a requirement, and is it one anyone meets?

Give me the requirements as a table with these columns, numbered so conflicts can cross-reference: requirement (a short name, two to six words) | what it demands | the case that makes it a requirement | non-negotiable / camp preference / prejudice | what it rules out | conflicts with (requirement numbers). Then under the table: (a) which subset of the requirements, if any, is jointly unsatisfiable and why; (b) the single requirement that knocks out the most candidate designs; and (c) a second short table scoring the usual suspects — a fixed explicit proxy; a reward model learned from human feedback; treating the objective as unknown and deferring to the principal; satisficing within hard constraints; staying near what a competent human would do; penalizing side effects or limiting impact; indifference to being corrected or switched off; and keeping the system narrow and tool-like with no long-horizon agency — with columns: design | one line on what it is | pass / partial / fail against the list | the requirement it fails hardest. Keep the scoring brief, the criteria are the point, and skip any citations or reading list — we'll fill that part in ourselves.
