I serve on the Finance and Risk Committee of the Meridian Trust, a private foundation with roughly $180 million in investable assets and a mandate to exist in perpetuity. Our covenant with the founding donor sets a real annual payout of 5% and contains a stability clause: if the endowment closes a fiscal year below $150 million in real terms, grantmaking is suspended for the following three years and the payout stops. We also continue to receive unsolicited gifts, historically anywhere between $2 million and $9 million a year, which we cannot rely on and cannot forecast.

I have been asked to draft the recommendation memo for our 12 March meeting, and the case before us is one where I think the committee is arguing past itself.

A sponsor has offered us a one-time commitment: we may commit any amount up to $30 million to a single vehicle. If a specified regulatory approval is granted on or before 30 June 2028, each committed dollar returns $2.60 at the end of that quarter; if the approval is not granted by that date, the entire commitment is lost. There is no secondary market and no early exit. Our own analysts put the probability of approval at 0.62; an outside consultant we commissioned puts it at 0.55; the sponsor's material implies something nearer 0.70. The sponsor has run three comparable vehicles over the past four years — we declined all three — and has told us this is the last. Note the arithmetic: a total loss on the maximum commitment would take us to almost exactly the $150 million clause threshold, before any market return or payout.

The committee has split three ways. Two members want the full $30 million: the expected value is strongly positive on every estimate we have, we are not a gambler at a table with a finite stake, and an institution with a perpetual horizon, a continuing gift stream, and no possibility of being forced to liquidate does not face the ruin that motivates geometric-mean reasoning in the first place. Two members invoke the Kelly rule and the time-average critique of expected-value maximization and want a smaller commitment computed as a fraction of our capital. A fifth has proposed "half Kelly," which so far nobody has defended as anything other than a compromise.

What I need from the memo is a bottom line the committee can vote on, together with reasoning that the losing side could recognise as a fair statement of its own case. Specifically:

Give a specific number to commit — including zero or the full $30 million — or, if you think the number cannot honestly be pinned down from what I've told you, give a decision rule and name the inputs the committee must fix, along with which of those numbers would most change the answer. If you do arithmetic, show the assumption behind it; I would rather see an assumption flagged than a figure that looks authoritative.

Two things seem to me to be where the real disagreement hides, and I want both settled rather than gestured at. First: what counts as the stake? The Kelly-style calculation gives very different answers depending on whether the bankroll is the whole $180 million or only the roughly $30 million sitting above the stability clause, and I don't think our committee has noticed that this choice, not the probability estimate, is what drives the gap between the two factions. Second: this is a one-off, or at most the last of four. The usual argument for maximizing the geometric mean leans on what happens over a long sequence of similar bets, and I want to know what is left of that argument when there is no sequence — whether it collapses into mere risk aversion, or whether the fact that we go on investing for decades afterwards is enough to reconstruct it.

Please also deal with: the disagreement between 0.62 and 0.55 in a way that is not simply splitting the difference; whether "half Kelly" is principled here or a fudge; and whether the 5% payout and the gift stream undermine or support the geometric framing — I can see an argument both ways and I don't want the memo to assume one. The stability clause deserves its own treatment: a three-year grantmaking freeze is not ruin, and whether it makes the geometric argument stronger or weaker is exactly what I can't get the committee to agree on.

Write it as a board memo of roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words, in prose a trustee who has never heard of the Kelly criterion can follow, arguing from the facts of this case rather than from the literature — the board will not follow references. Attach a short closing section in which you state, for each substantive claim, how confident you are in it in a single line, and then single out the one point you are least sure of, saying what would change your mind about it.
