so i'm one of three unpaid admins on a tiny modded Minecraft server, running since 2013, about forty regulars, a few of whom have been building the same town for basically a decade. A couple weeks ago another admin quietly flattened a player's build to make room for a new spawn area — didn't ask, didn't announce it, restored it from backup three days later when the rest of us found out, and the player in question never noticed a thing because they only log on a couple times a year now. The discord still exploded about it for a week. People were furious, and what I noticed is that basically every argument in that thread was secretly an argument about whether the thing that got destroyed was *real*. Now I have to (a) rule on whether an apology is actually owed and (b) write 2000 words for my applied ethics essay, due in three weeks, and I want the essay to be better than the thread.

What I need from you is a thought experiment. One paragraph for the scenario — you can take as long as you like on the analysis after. Set it in something that already exists: our kind of server, an old MMO, a forum-based nation-game, whatever. But no VR rigs, no neural interfaces, no crypto, no full-dive anything, and no real-money trading anywhere in the story.

Here's the spec. I want a single case where three accounts come apart in a specified direction:

- the "it's only a game" skeptic: nothing of genuine value exists in a virtual world, so nothing of genuine value can be destroyed there, and no apology or repair is owed.
- what I've been calling the *derivative* view — virtual objects aren't really real, but virtual harms can be real, derivatively, by causing real experiences in real people (upset, frustration, lost hours of future enjoyment). So the size of the harm tracks the size of the real psychological effect.
- what I've been calling the *constitutive* view — some objects, and some harms to them, are real because they're constituted by an ongoing rule-governed practice that has its own internal standards and internal goods. On this view a real wrong can be done even if nobody ever finds out and nobody's feelings get hurt.

The direction I want: the skeptic and the derivative view both have to say *nothing, or nothing much, happened*, while the constitutive view has to say *something real and non-trivial happened, something that warrants repair* — and I want the case to make the constitutive verdict feel like the right one without you just announcing that it's the right one.

Constraints, because these are exactly the ways I cheat when I try to write this myself:

1. Zero offline consequences. No money, no reputational damage, no physical or psychological harm outside the game, no dead players, and the person whose thing gets destroyed is an adult and not otherwise vulnerable. If your case only works because somebody ends up crying at their keyboard, it doesn't work.
2. The destroyed thing's value has to be entirely internal to the practice — hundreds of hours, irreplacable, no external audience, no monetization, not a memorial for anything outside the game.
3. The destroyer's motives have to be neutral-to-decent. Not a griefer, no enjoyment in it, and they need a reason an outsider would take seriously (server performance, room for new players, cleaning up abandoned land). I want the wrong, if there is one, to come from the destruction and not from the malice. Make up your own mind whether the destroyer is an admin or just another player, and tell me why you picked that.
4. The destruction is unwitnessed and would never have been discovered except that the destroyer mentions it afterward. The owner never notices. Nothing whatsoever changes in their experience.
5. There's a perfect backup, but restoring is a deliberate act that also costs a small amount of unrelated progress by other people. So whether to restore is a real choice, not a formality.
