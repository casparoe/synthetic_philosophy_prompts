In a seminar on nuclear strategy last week, a speaker argued as follows: brinkmanship is just the game of chicken. In chicken the driver who visibly throws his steering wheel out the window wins, because the opponent then faces certain death and has to swerve. The nuclear counterpart of that move is to take the human decision out of retaliation, by means of an automated second-strike system with nobody in the loop and no possibility of backing down, so a state that wants its deterrent to be credible should build one.

I want an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on the policy. Give the correspondence table (what plays the driver, the steering wheel, the crash, the swerve, the onlookers), then the disanalogies, and for each say whether it actually damages his conclusion or is merely cosmetic. Three that I cannot sort out myself:

1. In chicken the throw-away is a single move, made in plain view and known to be irreversible. A machine is a physical system of unknown reliability, whose existence and trigger conditions the other side may learn only partially, and whose builders may retain some override. Is partial observability fatal to the analogy or just noise?
2. In chicken the crash occurs only if the other driver fails to swerve. A system that retaliates on warning also fires on a false alarm, and the game contains no analogue of a false alarm.
3. Chicken is one-shot, symmetric, two-player, with common knowledge of the payoffs and a binary choice. The real case involves secure second-strike forces, a ladder of rungs rather than swerve-or-not, deep uncertainty about the other side's capabilities and resolve, and repeated crises in which reputation is at stake.

Does the steering-wheel move have any nuclear counterpart, or is looking for one the mistake? Does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? And if chicken cannot carry it, is there a better analogy, or does credibility have to be secured some other way?
