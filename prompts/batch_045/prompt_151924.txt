I'm designing one session of our department's upper-level course on democratic innovation — I'm a PhD student in political theory and I have the "institutional design" block to myself: 17 students, mostly third-years who have already had the survey in normative theory, one 90-minute meeting, a room with movable chairs, and no budget beyond printing. My allotted topic is selection rules: sortition, term limits, and the standing complaint about career politicians.

I don't want to run it as a comparison of three reform packages, because that is what the reading does and they will arrive having read it. What I want them to feel is something I have started calling the migration thesis: the claim that any rule which lowers the return to holding office does not lower the assembly's need for institutional knowledge, it only relocates the people who hold that knowledge to actors nobody selected — permanent staff, lobbyists, the civil service, or the members of the cohort before. Relatedly, I want them to notice that "career politician" bundles at least two different complaints that the standard reform menu answers separately: a complaint about who gets filtered in (the selection runs on the desire to hold office, so it selects for a certain kind of person regardless of what they then do) and a complaint about whom the officeholder must answer to in order to keep the job.

The design I keep sketching and cannot get to work: the same small legislative game played two or three times over a fictional mid-sized regional assembly's budget — a fixed pool, maybe five named line items, coalition-building required — with the selection rule swapped between rounds while everything else is held constant. A few students are drawn at the start and stay for the whole session as a permanent Secretariat: they alone have the full rulebook, they convert any member's idea into votable text, and they set the order of business. They score on how many provisions they drafted survive to a final vote, so their interest is in being needed rather than in any particular outcome. Members get private district cards and score on how close the outcome is to their district's priorities. Round one is ordinary re-electability; round two is a hard two-term limit; round three is selection by lot for a single non-renewable term. Members can claw power back from the Secretariat by spending floor time, which is scarce and which competes with actually passing things. I am also toying with a fourth, hybrid rule — call it cohort pairing: each member chosen by lot is shadowed by a randomly drawn veteran of the immediately preceding cohort, who may speak, brief, and draft but not vote, and whose own prospects after office rise with the pair's record — as a way of testing whether memory can be institutionalized without re-electability. Tell me whether that fourth round belongs in 90 minutes or whether it buries the debrief.

What I need from you: a runnable activity on these lines or a better one — role sheets and a one-page rulebook I can print, a payoff scheme in points rather than money, a minute-by-minute timeline for one facilitator with no props beyond index cards, a deck of cards, and a timer. The debrief should map specific moments the students will actually have had onto the migration thesis and onto the two complaints I want separated, and it should let them name the diagnosis before I do. Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I recover without breaking character, and which part of the idea the game cannot reach at all and I will simply have to assert afterwards — I assume real sortition's descriptive representativeness and anything depending on days of facilitated deliberation are in that category, but tell me what else.
