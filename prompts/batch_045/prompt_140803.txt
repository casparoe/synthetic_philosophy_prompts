I write fiction rather than scholarship, but next month I'm giving the evening lecture at a university's African American literature series, and I want to be ready for the questions.

The thesis I'll defend: that Du Bois's propaganda criterion in "Criteria of Negro Art" — his insistence that all art is propaganda, and that a Black artist's work is to be measured by whether it serves his people's freedom — is best read as a claim about a work's stance toward its public, not about its message or its intended effects. Call a work claim-bearing when it addresses a public it expects to answer. I add a second condition of my own, which Du Bois does not state: the work must leave that public room to refuse. Call that answerability. On this reading art-for-art's-sake is not neutral ground but claim-bearing without answerability — the posture of a writer whose readers already share her premises — and so it, too, is propaganda in Du Bois's sense rather than an escape from it. I'll argue this beats the crude reading, on which propaganda is a message the work carries and which makes Du Bois's own novels merely failed art; and it beats the diplomatic reading, on which the propaganda passages are only rhetoric of the occasion. I close by showing the distinction at work in my own novel, where the propaganda is carried not by anything a character says but by a narration that withholds sympathy from a woman until the reader's own demand for sympathy is what stands indicted.

The room will be about eighty people: mostly MFA students and creative writing faculty, who hold as a working faith that the writer's job is not to persuade and who have heard "propaganda" as an insult all their professional lives; two scholars of African American literature who work on the Harlem Renaissance, one avowedly on Alain Locke's side of the old quarrel with Du Bois; and an art historian who has written on propaganda and will want to know exactly how my two conditions differ from definitions already in circulation.

I want a prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. Give me the objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way each of these groups would phrase them — the writers' version of an objection will not sound like the scholars'. For each: a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and a note on where that reply is softest. Tell me what to concede before anyone raises it, which single reply has to land for the evening to be worth having, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks.

Two instructions on how to write it. Commit: rank the objections, say plainly which does the most damage, and tell me where I am simply wrong — do not hand me a menu of stances I might take. And lead with one sentence giving me the bottom line, the objection that will hurt most and what my answer to it turns on, before the sheet itself; no preamble and no restating what I've told you.
