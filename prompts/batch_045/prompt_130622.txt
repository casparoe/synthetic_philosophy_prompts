I sit on the Arts, Culture and Leisure Committee of our town council — seven members, of whom I am the one who has been asked to draft the written opinion we will circulate before we vote. I would be grateful for a draft I can work from.

The proposal. A former resident who has done well in business has offered to pay for the conversion of a disused mill building into a 280-seat theatre, and to underwrite its running costs for five years through a trust she alone will control. Marshfield, where I live, has some 11,000 people and no theatre at all; the nearest is forty minutes away by car, and most residents never go. Her offer is conditional on two things: that the council commit to a fixed annual grant of £25,000 for ten years, and that the building be run as a receiving house for a professional company from the county town, which would staff it and choose its programme. There is no other bid for the building, and no prospect of a referendum.

The objection. A long written objection has come in from a retired local teacher, Mr Peter Ansell, who argues explicitly from Rousseau's polemic against introducing a theatre into his native Geneva. His case, as I read it, has four parts. (1) A play amuses without improving anyone; the claim that watching deepens our sympathy for others mistakes a feeling had in a seat for a disposition to act, and such feelings are spent in the having of them, leaving the spectator quietly pleased with himself and no more inclined to help anyone than before. (2) A standing theatre manufactures the taste it then satisfies — for polish, staging, costume, professional finish — and with it a trade in late eating and drinking around it; that is a new want created, not an existing one met. (3) It converts people who do things into people who watch things. Our shared civic life consists of the choir, the amateur dramatic society, the summer carnival and the bonfire; an audience is a roomful of people sitting side by side having private experiences. (4) Because a professional company lives by selling tickets, and because a town our size cannot fill 280 seats from its own households week after week, the seats must be filled with visitors, and the programme will be shaped by what visitors will pay for. Cheapening the tickets with a council grant does not remove that dependence; it merely transfers it from the box office to us. He also notes that the company's commitment ends after five years while ours runs for ten.

The complications. The amateur society, the Marshfield Players, has written in support, saying that a proper stage and professional standards will improve them rather than finish them — which bears directly on Mr Ansell's third point. And the committee is split over his proposed rule of thumb, which he wants written into our cultural strategy: that public money should support performance only where the performers and the audience are largely the same people.

What I need. A clear recommendation — accept, decline, or accept with conditions — together with a short statement of reasons, and with any conditions, limits and review points set out concretely enough to be voted on. Two things matter to me. First, the opinion must take Mr Ansell at his best rather than treating him as a killjoy: if it recommends accepting, it should say plainly what he is right about and what should be done about it. Second, it should be honest about the largest difficulty for whichever way it goes — either that a participation rule applied without fear or favour would also threaten things we already fund (our carnival is mostly watched rather than joined in), or that any such rule amounts to telling residents that their own judgements about their own leisure are not to be trusted.

Please write it as a formal opinion of roughly 800 words, in British English.
