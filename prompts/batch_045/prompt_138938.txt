I run a tiny podcast from my spare room — me, a mate with a philosophy degree, and whoever says yes — and next week my guest is someone who defends what she calls lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: her claim is that suffering past a certain intensity isn't just worse than any amount of mild pleasure, it's in a whole different league, so no pile of small enjoyments or mild aches can ever outweigh it no matter how big the pile gets. I've been turning this over on my own for months with no academic background at all (my mate got me into it), and I want questions that go past her just restating the view.

Here's what I want you to draft: exactly five questions, numbered, one or two sentences each, no intro and no outro. Each one should come with a follow-up aimed at whatever answer she's most likely to give — she's sharp and she'll probably say the boundary is vague, or that the threshold is about the nature of the experience and not a number, or that the practical advice comes out the same either way, so build the follow-ups around those. Cover these five things:

1. The right next to the line: if the cliff sits at roughly 8 out of 10, then a 7.9 and an 8.0 are barely distinguishable to the person having them, yet one is tradeable and the other isn't. Press her on whether a difference that small can really do that much work.

2. Adding up from below: can a million stubbed toes, each well under the line, ever collectively outweigh sixty seconds above it? Then think about a trillion people with a mild headache forever — and ask whether she's quietly added a second, higher threshold for groups while denying the first one works that way.

3. Why one direction only: if intense pain lexically dominates any heap of mild pleasures, why isn't there a matching cliff where some amazing joy dominates any heap of mild pains, and if she does allow one, is her view still negative utilitarianism in any sense that would surprise anyone?

4. My own idea, which I haven't seen anyone put this way: it sounds like she's saying the exchange rate between pleasure and pain shoots off to infinity at the threshold — a vertical asymptote — rather than just getting brutally steep. Ask why reality would have a discontinuity there instead of a very steep smooth curve, and if she says it makes no practical difference, point out that a smooth curve always leaves some finite amount of mild stuff that wins, so she does owe us a number where she'd switch.

5. Not knowing where the line is: we're never sure exactly where the threshold falls or whether a given pain is above it, and once you attach probabilities to that, the expected badness of a pain looks smooth again and the cliff seems to flatten into a steep slope. Ask how a lexical threshold survives being uncertain about its own location, and if she reaches for something other than expected value, push on what the actual decision rule is and how it ranks two options that might both be over the line.
