**Question 3 (20 marks). Answer both parts.**

**Part A (8 marks)**

Ada and Ben are colleagues walking out of a department meeting. Ben asks, "Did the meeting go long?" Ada replies, "The chair brought sandwiches." Both of them know, and each knows that the other knows, and each knows that the other knows that they know, that in this department the chair brings sandwiches only to meetings timetabled to run past two hours, and that a meeting running past two hours is, in their shared usage, a meeting that went long. Ben takes Ada to have told him that the meeting went long.

(i) Set out the Gricean derivation of what Ben takes Ada to have conveyed. In doing so: identify which maxim Ada is failing to fulfil; identify, in Grice's terms, the way in which she is failing to fulfil it, as opposed to the other ways of failing to fulfil a maxim that he distinguishes; and explain why that way of failing, and not one of the others, is the one that can give rise to a conversational implicature. [4]

(ii) Is what Ben recovers part of what Ada said, a conventional implicature, or a conversational implicature? Apply two of the standard Gricean diagnostics — cancellability, detachability, calculability, behaviour under embedding — and say what each one shows in this case. [2]

(iii) Ben afterwards learns that the meeting was adjourned at 1:50, and that the chair had merely brought in leftovers from a catered lunch. Ada was not mistaken and was not lying. Did Ada implicate something false? Explain the difference between saying something false and implicating something false, and say whether the Gricean apparatus can mark that difference here. [2]

**Part B (12 marks)**

Kalen is an imaginary language. Every declarative sentence in Kalen must carry exactly one of two suffixes: *-vi*, which encodes that the speaker directly witnessed the event she reports, and *-nu*, which encodes that she did not witness it but was told or inferred it. There is no neutral or unmarked form, and no grammatical way of reporting an event without committing to one or the other.

Meera says, in Kalen, "Door open-vi": the door is open, and she witnessed it. In fact she did not witness it — she was in another room and inferred it from the draught under the door. Everyone in the conversation knows she was in the other room.

(a) Is the witness-status content — that Meera witnessed the door's being open — part of what is said, a conventional implicature, or a conversational implicature? Run the diagnostics and say which of them settle the question and which of them leave it open. [5]

(b) Detachability looks as though it gives an easy answer here: the grammar leaves no alternative form, so there is nothing else to substitute and the witness content cannot be detached. Does that settle that the content is conventionally rather than conversationally implicated? Say exactly what non-detachability can and cannot establish, and whether the test is even well defined in a case where the grammar permits no alternative formulation. [4]

(c) Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the witness content is a conventional implicature. Meera's audience knows she witnessed nothing. Is her utterance false in some way? Has she said something false, implicated something false, both, or neither? What does the answer depend on — in particular, does the maxim of Quality govern only what is said, or a speaker's whole contribution including conventionally implicated content? Argue for a position rather than reporting one. [3]

**Part A**

Ada does not say that the meeting went long. What she says is only that the chair brought sandwiches; the rest is recovered by inference. The derivation runs like this. Ben asks a yes/no question. Ada's reply is, on its face, irrelevant to it, so she violates the maxim of Relation — she says something that does not answer the question she was asked. Since the violation is one Ben can detect, he works out what she must mean rather than concluding that she has stopped cooperating: he assumes she is still observing the Cooperative Principle overall; he knows, and knows that she knows he knows, that sandwiches appear only at meetings timetabled past two hours; he knows she could easily have said "Yes" or "No" or "It ran to three" and chose not to; and there is no reason to think she was trying to deceive him. The only assumption under which her remark makes sense as a contribution to the exchange is that she intends him to conclude that the meeting went long. So she means that it went long, and Ben's recovery of that is correct.

What Ben recovers is a conversational implicature, and specifically a particularized one: it depends entirely on the local background knowledge about the chair's sandwich habit, and in a department with no such habit the same sentence would carry no such content. It is not part of what is said, because what is said is fixed by the conventional meanings of the words plus reference assignment and disambiguation, and none of that includes anything about the meeting's length. It is not a conventional implicature either, since it is not carried by any word or construction in the sentence the way the content of a connective or particle is. Two diagnostics confirm this. First, cancellability: Ada can add, without contradiction, "…although the meeting still finished on time — she just had leftovers," whereas a conventional implicature cannot be cancelled without producing something anomalous. Second, embedding: in "If the chair brought sandwiches we are in trouble," the suggestion that a meeting went long does not survive into the conditional, which is the behaviour of conversational rather than conventional content.

On the third point: Ada did implicate something false. She did not say anything false, since the chair did bring sandwiches, and that is all she said. So there is a real distinction to be drawn here, and the Gricean apparatus draws it: truth and falsity attach in the first instance to what is said, and implicature is a separate layer of speaker meaning that is not subject to the same evaluation. This is the mirror image of the irony case, where a speaker says something false in order to implicate something true; here she says something true in order to implicate something false. Because she has not said anything false, she has not lied, though she has been misleading. The apparatus marks the difference by locating the falsity at the level of implicature rather than at the level of what is said.

**Part B**

The witness content is not a conversational implicature. A conversational implicature is generated by the assumption that the speaker is observing the maxims in the specific circumstances of the utterance, and it would disappear if the circumstances changed while the sentence stayed the same. The witness content does not behave like that: it is present in every context in which the sentence is used, whatever the participants happen to know or want, because it is encoded in the suffix. So it is conventional — attached to the meaning of a grammatical morpheme.

That leaves the question whether it is part of what is said or a conventional implicature, and here the diagnostics do real work. Detachability is decisive. A conversational implicature is detachable in principle: one can find another way of conveying the same primary content that does not carry the implicature, or, conversely, the implicature can be stripped off by reformulating. In Kalen there is no reformulation available at all: the grammar forces a choice between *-vi* and *-nu*, so there is no expression with the same primary content that lacks the witness content. Non-detachability is the mark of content that is genuinely part of the speaker's commitment, so the witness content is part of what Meera said.

The consequence is straightforward. What Meera said includes that she witnessed the door being open. She did not witness it, and her audience knows she did not. So she said something false, and the maxim she violates is Quality: she has made a contribution she knows not to be true. It is no defence that the door was in fact open, since the falsity lies in the witness component rather than in the door's being open. Nor does it help that her audience was not taken in; the maxim governs the contribution, not its effect.

One caveat on the framework itself. Calculability is Grice's weakest requirement. On his account the hearer must be able to reconstruct, step by step and consciously, the argument from the maxims to the implicature; but everything we know about real-time comprehension suggests that hearers do no such thing, and that children and second-language speakers manage implicatures without any such reasoning. A theory of speaker meaning that makes conscious inference a condition on understanding over-intellectualises ordinary conversation, and the Kalen case does not avoid the objection.
