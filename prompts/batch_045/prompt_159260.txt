On my moral philosophy course we did a week on promising last term and I came away thinking everyone was argueing past each other, so before I read any more I want the spec. Write me the desiderata for a theory of promissory obligation: what any account has to get right to be worth having, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. The purpose I care about is this — I want a theory that can tell me what I owe once I have promised, and that explains why breaking a promise is a wrong of a distinctive kind rather than just a case of lying, or letting someone down, or being unreliable.

Cases I want handled, plus whatever ones you think belong on the list: a deathbed promise to look after someone's sister who will never hear about it and would not want it anyway; a promise made in a language the promisee does not speak; a promise to do what you already owed anyway; breaking a promise nobody could ever find out about; and the one that bugs me most — the promisee can release you from it, but you cannot release yourself, and neither can anyone else. Why them, and where does that power come from?

Then tell me which requirements are the real thing and which are just what one camp happens to want — I suspect the demand that promises bind even when keeping them helps no one is doing that kind of work. And say which single requirement does the most work in knocking out the usual candidates.
