I'm writing the third chapter of my dissertation, on the norms governing an agent's credence in propositions about whose objective chance she has partial information. Before I commit myself to a formulation, I want the specification: what a principle linking credence to objective chance would have to deliver in order to count as adequate, with the case behind each requirement. Not a survey of the literature and not a bibliography — I know the literature, and I can locate it. Name a view only by a description I would recognize.

Let me fix the purpose, since adequacy is relative to it. The principle is to serve as a norm on the credences of an embedded agent: someone who does not know the whole past, who is one agent among several, whose information arrives in stages, and whose own acts can bear on what the chances are. It is not to double as an analysis of what chance is, nor as a semantics for chance ascriptions; I want to stay neutral between Humean and non-Humean accounts. Say if that neutrality is itself one of the things I cannot have.

Here is the material I want the list built around, though you should add what I have missed:

The measure-zero case: a genuinely chancy setup with a continuous outcome space, where every exact outcome has chance zero and yet some exact outcome will occur. A principle that simply identifies the admissible conditional credence with the chance will force the agent to divide zero credence across an exhaustive partition, which cannot be right. Say precisely what the principle must do here — whether it must be restricted, reinterpreted, or supplemented by a regularity or congruence condition, and whether regularity is a requirement or a taste.

Action-dependent chances: cases where the chances at a time depend on what the agent believes or does at that time, so that a chance-credence norm becomes a fixed-point condition rather than a one-way constraint. I have in mind maintenance-of-machinery and market-panic examples, where deferring to a low chance of failure is part of what keeps the chance low. There may be no solution, one, or several. What should a principle say in each case, and is the demand that it always yield a unique verdict a genuine desideratum or a prejudice inherited from static Bayesian models?

Relativized and self-locating chance: an agent who knows the chances obtaining in each of several set-ups but not which set-up she is in; or an agent who knows the chances conditional on a locus she cannot locate herself within. Is an unrelativized chance of the outcome required before the principle applies at all, and if not, what is the object of deference?

Chance gaps: setups which, at the time of deliberation, have not yet acquired a determinate chance of the outcome — only a chance of later acquiring one. Does the principle owe a verdict, and is principled silence a defect in the norm or a correct description of its scope?

Aggregate without single-case chance: the agent knows the proportion of outcomes in a finite reference class but has no reason to think any single member of the class has a chance attaching to it. Is this a case the principle must handle, or a case it should refuse?

Undermining: chance facts which, if admitted, are evidence that the chances are otherwise than they state. I want to know whether tolerance of undermining is a genuine desideratum or an artefact of one metaphysical camp, and what happens to the list if it is dropped.

The admissibility clause: any formulation needs a way of marking off the evidence the agent may retain while deferring. I want a requirement that this notion not be circular — not defined, in effect, as "the evidence such that deference is correct" — together with your view on whether a non-circular specification is achievable at all, and what it would cost.

Two further items I suspect are camp preferences rather than requirements, and want you to adjudicate: that a chance-knowing bookie should not be able to exploit the agent across time; and that a chance-credence norm ought to fall out as a special case of a general schema of deference to better-informed sources, including experts, frequencies, and the agent's own later credences. For each, say whether it belongs on the list, and why.

Finally, the parts I care about most: flag any subset of the desiderata you take to be jointly unsatisfiable — if the list is itself a result, I want that stated baldly — and tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive formulations. Then, briefly, score three contenders against the list: the original unconditional formulation, the later reformulation that restricts the embedded proposition to the outcome's holding at the time of the chance, and an "chance as expert" treatment that derives deference from a general principle about deferring to better-informed probability functions. A table is fine, and I would rather have three rows than ten. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; roughly 1,200 words, prose with a table at the end.
