I am putting together a diagnostic for the graduate seminar I teach on markets and commodification, and I want it to sort students by what they say about cases rather than by which label they will put their hand up for. Please build me a decision tree: eight or nine branch questions, five to seven leaves, answerable cold in fifteen minutes.

Every node must be a concrete case or a single claim, phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing which position it leads to. Nothing phrased as "is the good inalienable?" or "is commodification corrosive?".

The distinctions I need the tree genuinely to separate are: (i) what the objection is supposed to attach to — the kind of good, the bare fact of transfer, the money form as opposed to a non-cash return, the motive the exchange expresses or cultivates, or the spillover onto non-participants; (ii) which direction the repugnance runs in, seller-side, buyer-side or both — the kidney case should appear twice, once from each side, since students who find buying disgraceful often admire giving, while those who find selling disgraceful rarely object to receiving; (iii) whether consent cures the objection when it is uncoerced but given under desperation; (iv) whether the right response is prohibition, channelling into non-price allocation (lottery, priority-for-giving, altruistic register) or permission under restrictions; (v) whether the objection binds the individual agent or the law, since students standardly hold these apart and then discover they cannot.

Cases that must appear as nodes: a kidney sale between strangers where the vendor is poor but unpressured; disaster-time price rises set by an automated pricing system, where the higher price is what moves the good to those who need it most and no human intends exploitation; vote selling where receipts are verifiable; and a scheme in which no money changes hands but donors gain priority on the transplant list.

Before naming any leaf, set out the questions and the number of answer-combinations they generate, then locate the actual positions in that space; name any combination nobody occupies and say whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any two leaves your questions cannot in principle separate. Then trace two contrasting answer-patterns through to different leaves, so that I can check the key actually discriminates. British English throughout, and no footnotes or reading list — I know the literature.
