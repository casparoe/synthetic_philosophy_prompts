I'm a backend engineer who got moved onto the model-behavior team at my company about a year ago, mostly to build the labeling pipeline, and I keep feeling like the rules we've ended up with are quietly assuming a bunch of stuff about values that nobody ever wrote down. I have no philosophy background beyond a couple of podcasts, so I can't even tell which parts are load-bearing.

Here are the rules, concretely. For "subjective" prompts we get seven contractors in seven countries to rate two model replies on a 1-7 scale and pick a winner, then we strip out everything about who those contractors were so the model learns one generic voice. Rule 1: we throw the 1-7 number away and train on the pairwise winner, so six people who mildly prefer reply A beat four people who strongly prefer reply B. Rule 2: if how people split correlates with an axis on our "protected" list — nationality, religion, gender, or party preference in the annotator's home country — we drop the item entirely rather than training on the majority; if it correlates with anything else (age, income, whether they have kids, how risk-averse they are, whether they say they're a utilitarian when you ask) we keep it and train the majority label. Rule 3: a handful of things never go to a vote at all — we just write them into the spec and tell annotators to enforce them no matter what they or anyone else thinks. And once the model is trained, nobody can contest or amend any of it; the only power a user has is switching to a competitor or typing their own system prompt, and we do sometimes defend ourselves by saying exactly that.

What I want is for someone to tell me what all this presupposes — not "what should we do" in general, but what has to be true about values, or about when a small group gets to set rules for a much larger one, for these rules to be anything other than vibes. Rule 2 looks like it's saying that disagreements along some axes are the reasonable kind nobody should adjudicate, while disagreements along other axes are just noise around a single right answer, and I can't figure out what makes nationality or religion the identity-ish ones while having kids or being risk-averse isn't — is there a principled version of that list, or is it just whatever looked politically safe? Rule 1 looks like a commitment that you can't compare how much different people care, except we literally collect the 1-7 number and then binarize it, so is that a real commitment or just laziness? And stripping the annotator identity out plus rule 3 plus the "switch if you don't like it" line seem to add up to something like: one generic standpoint that isn't indexed to any constituency, a hard floor set unilaterally by us, everything above the floor settled by counting heads, and legitimacy coming from people choosing to keep using the thing. Is that a coherent package or are those bits fighting each other?

So: reconstruct the implicit theory, and be clear about which parts the rules strictly require versus which are just what happens to fit. Then tell me what would actually have to change in the pipeline if we swapped in each of the rivals my teammates keep pushing: (a) stop aggregating and train the model to figure out each user's own values and serve those, with no default; (b) keep one model but train it to give the answer that's actually correct on contested questions even when most people disagree; (c) make it properly democratic somehow, with a real way for the rules to be amended after training; (d) weight people by how much the decision actually affects them instead of counting heads. For each one, what does it presuppose that our current setup doesn't, and what breaks that isn't broken now? And flag any pair of them that our current rules can't really distinguish — i.e., something we might already be doing without realizing it.
