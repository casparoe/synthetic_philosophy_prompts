I am a PhD student in philosophy writing on personal identity, and I am leading one 75-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar on persons and identity: 20 students, one room with moveable chairs, a whiteboard and a projector, me as sole facilitator though I can press one volunteer into service as a helper. The students have just finished Parfit's reductionism and reacted to it in a way I find more interesting than the view itself: they can state correctly that identity is not what matters, and they feel exactly as they did before they read it. Nothing moved. They have no background in Buddhism, and several are openly allergic to anything that smells like mindfulness culture, so the session cannot open with mats, candles, closing of eyes, or the word meditation. It has to earn its strangeness in the first ten minutes by doing something rather than by announcing a thesis.

The target I want them to run into is something I will call the intermittence thesis: for any human subject, (i) the sense that there is a single owner who has these experiences and initiates these actions is an occurrent, causally conditioned event rather than a standing structural feature of the stream; (ii) that sense has onsets and offsets the subject does not choose; (iii) when it lapses, experience does not go dark — there is still seeing, hearing, intending, and acting — but nothing in the stream is thereby left unowned or orphaned, because ownership was never a property of the stream in the first place; and (iv) nothing in the intrinsic character of an experience settles whether it is accompanied by that sense. The contrast I want them to feel is with what I will call the auditor model: the self as a standing inner subject who witnesses, owns, and commands what goes on in the stream — the thing most of them will defend within about ninety seconds of being asked whether they have one.

A second target matters more to me than the first, and it is the one I expect to be hard to design for. The students will grant (i) through (iv) intellectually inside twenty minutes, and the auditor model will be fully back online before they leave the room. I want the session to end with that relapse felt as the interesting datum rather than as a failure of teaching — because it is the point at which the specifically Buddhist claim becomes visible: what has to cease is not a belief whose falsity one comes to see but a recurring activity of treating experience as mine and oneself as its subject, and the tradition's interest in anatta is in the cessation of that activity, not in the correctness of a metaphysical thesis. A classroom cannot produce the cessation. It can, I think, produce the relapse, and stage it so that the relapse is the thing people notice.

Please design the session. What I need:

A full runnable plan with materials, rules, and a timeline in five-minute blocks. One to three linked exercises, not more. No lecture at the front end, and the words anatta, the five aggregates, and the tradition's pairing of I-making with mine-making should not be uttered until the last fifteen minutes; the naming comes after the experience, not before it.

Each exercise should be engineered so that what happens in the room is the argument, not an illustration of one I have already given. I have been toying with two devices and would rather you improve on or replace them than execute them as stated: a command test, in which students try to exercise exactly the control the auditor model attributes to them and discover that they cannot command the failure any more than they commanded its arrival; and a five-line ledger on which they write what they take themselves to be across the categories of body, feeling, perception, disposition, and awareness, then are walked through what each line can and cannot be made to do. There also has to be one moment in which the class catches itself in the act of narrating a reason for something it did not in fact have that reason for — I want them to discover the confabulation from their own first-person reports rather than to be told that people confabulate — but the mechanism has to be something I can run with index cards and no sleight of hand, it must not leave anyone feeling ridiculed, and any misdirection in it has to be fully unwound in the debrief. Small real stakes, please: a public prediction each student commits to and the aggregate result, or something better.

Two predictable misreadings have to be blocked by design rather than by my saying so: that the view is nihilism, i.e. that nothing exists or nothing matters; and that it is just Parfit again, or just cognitive science with a foreign vocabulary. Say how the structure of the exercises makes each of these come out wrong, and give me what I should actually say to the student who asks, in a flat voice, whether on this view there is no one to be blamed.

A debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto specific clauses of the intermittence thesis, in the order I should take them, including how to handle the student who reports zero mind-wandering and thereby becomes a local celebrity, the student who opts out of the introspective parts, and the skeptic who refuses to play.

An honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce. I already assume it cannot produce the uprooting of the appropriative stance, only its temporary lapse, and that nothing in it shows the lapse to be revelatory rather than merely quiet; tell me what else it leaves out and what I will therefore have to assert on the tradition's behalf rather than demonstrate.

Finally, give me a way of telling whether it worked: an exit ticket with a small number of forced options, the distribution across those options that would count as success, and the distribution that would tell me the session has backfired into nihilism or into reductionism-with-feelings. And a twenty-minute compressed version I can fall back on if the room simply refuses to participate.
