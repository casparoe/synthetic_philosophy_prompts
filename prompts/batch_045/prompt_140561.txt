I was re-reading some old thread here about the Born rule problem and it got me to dig up this pop-physics piece I bookmarked years ago (some blog or maybe a magazine article, I genuinely can't find it again) that lays out the Everettian take on probability. Roughly, from memory:

Every outcome with nonzero amplitude happens in some branch, so there's no collapse and no fundamental randomness — the universal wavefunction just evolves deterministically, and the appearance of randomness is an illusion created by decoherence splitting the world into branches that can't see each other. The hard part is probability: if every outcome happens, what can it even mean to say this one has probability one third? The now-standard Everettian answer is that you have self-locating uncertainty. Before a measurement you don't know which branch you're going to end up on, so you assign a credence to each possibility, and the rational credence turns out to be the squared amplitude — which is just the Born rule. The squared amplitude is really a kind of measure of existence: branches with larger amplitude are, in some sense, more real or thicker or take up more of the multiverse, and that's why you should expect to find yourself in one in proportion to its amplitude. The Deutsch–Wallace decision-theoretic argument makes this rigorous: they show that any agent whose preferences satisfy a handful of obviously-rational-sounding constraints has to bet exactly as if the Born rule were true, which settles it — the probabilities aren't bolted on to the theory as an extra postulate, they're derived from the dynamics plus rationality alone, so the Born rule is now a theorem of many-worlds rather than an assumption. Collapse models and Bohmian mechanics, by contrast, have to put the Born rule in by hand, so Everett wins on parsimony. One upshot people balk at: because the measure of a branch can get arbitrarily tiny without ever reaching zero, there's always some version of you that sees a million heads in a row — it's just extremely low-measure, so you shouldn't plan around it.
