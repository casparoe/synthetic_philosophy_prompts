I'm trying to work out what I actually think about infinite hotels, and I would rather start from my verdicts on cases than from a theory. Grant me, for the sake of the exercise, that a hotel with rooms numbered 1, 2, 3, … and one guest in each is possible; what I cannot settle is which further things can be coherently said about it, and why some of the stories branch off into nonsense while others do not.

My verdicts:

(a) One new guest arrives; each guest moves from room n to room n+1 and the newcomer takes room 1. Fine, and every guest afterwards occupies a perfectly determinate room.

(b) A coach with countably many new guests; everyone moves to room 2n and the newcomers take the odds. Fine.

(c) Countably many such coaches arriving at once. Fine too, though it took me much longer to accept.

(d) A single coach with continuum-many new guests. Not fine, and not merely impractical: I do not think there is any arrangement that does it, so whatever licenses (a)–(c) gives out somewhere.

(e) Everyone in an odd-numbered room checks out. I have two verdicts here and suspect they cannot both stand: there are exactly as many guests as there were before, and the hotel is now half empty.

(f) At times 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, …, guest 1, then guest 2, then guest 3, and so on, checks out. At t = 1 the hotel is empty. Determinate, no complaint.

(g) A lamp in the lobby, on at t = 0, switched at exactly those same times. There is no answer to whether it is on at t = 1, and I mean that as a defect in the story rather than as my ignorance.

(h) At those same times, every guest simultaneously moves to twice their current room number, repeating at every step. I am happy to say the hotel is empty at t = 1, everyone having gone off the end — and equally happy to say that there is no room guest 1 is in, though whether that means "no determinate room" or "no room at all" I cannot tell.

(i) At those same times, the guests in rooms 1 and 2 swap, as do those in 3 and 4, and so on, at every step. No answer to who is in room 1 at t = 1; defective, as in (g).

(j) A motel next door whose rooms are numbered by the rationals in their usual order, so that there is no first room and no next room. One new guest arrives. I think accommodation is possible, but I am uneasy: the only scheme I can see empties an entire interval of rooms rather than one, and the instruction "everyone move to the next room along the corridor" cannot even be stated there.

My tentative principle is that a story of this kind has a determinate upshot just in case everything the question turns on is eventually constant — constant guest by guest for "where is this guest?", constant room by room for "is this room occupied?" That gets (a), (f), (g), (h) and (i), but it says nothing about (d), (e) or (j). Is it one principle or two? Can it be stated so that it also explains where the line in (d) falls, and what my two verdicts in (e) really amount to? Give me the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, with the clause that each case motivates, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts a clean principle would force me to drop. Then say what it predicts about a case I have not listed: a hotel whose rooms are numbered by all the integers, negative ones included, where at each of those same times every guest moves one room in the negative direction.
