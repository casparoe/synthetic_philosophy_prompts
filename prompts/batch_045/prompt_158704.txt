I am the philosopher member of our hospital's Critical Care Allocation Committee, which must reauthorize the nursing-hour allocation protocol for our long-stay ventilator step-down unit at its meeting a week from Thursday. The unit has a fixed census of about thirty residents, most of whom will remain for many months. The protocol now in force is aggregative: it maximizes total quality-adjusted life-months across the resident population, treating that population as a single patient. Each week it shifts one-to-one nursing hours from whichever resident has the lowest marginal return to whichever has the highest, and in some weeks the resident on the losing end dies within days — identifiable by name, in advance, with no uncertainty about who it will be. The rival protocol is person-affecting: it never withdraws from a resident unless that same resident is net benefited by that very decision.

What has split the committee is this finding from our modelers. Because clinical status fluctuates, the same thirty people are repeatedly on both sides of these transfers, and over any six-month window every resident has more expected life-months under the aggregative protocol than under the person-affecting one. So the standard ex ante reply to the separateness-of-persons objection does not need to hide behind a veil of ignorance: nobody is uncertain who will be sacrificed at any decision point, and yet each persisting person comes out ahead. Half of us think this answers the objection; half think it only reveals that the objection was never about uncertainty, and that gains spread across a life cannot answer for a loss imposed at a decision.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate. Use To/From/Re headings and give me the bottom line first: reauthorize, suspend, or reauthorize with conditions — and if conditions, which ones, with a fixed review date rather than open-ended monitoring. Most of it should be the reasoning. Say whether, on your view, the separateness objection constrains each distribution or the shape of a whole life, and why; put the losing side's argument in the strongest form its defenders would actually press, not a restatement of the objection; and tell me what residents should be told at admission. Argue from the structure of the case, not from the literature — no citations. Under 600 words.
