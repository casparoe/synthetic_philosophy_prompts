I'm on the little rotating committee at work that decides who gets publicly celebrated — all-hands shoutouts, the internal blog post, the engineer-of-the-quarter thing — and I've realised I can't predict our own chair, which is becoming a problem because we're rewriting the recogntion guidelines for next year and I'm the one stuck drafting them. Context that probably matters: we're about half Austin and half Oslo and Stockholm, and the two halves have opposite priors about her. The Americans think she's running some imported Jantelagen / tall-poppy thing — don't think you're anyone special, nobody is more than anybody else, that sort of attitude — and the Scandinavians think the Americans are embarrassing and need to calm down. I don't think she's doing either of those, quite. Her track record so far: she loudly celebrated a junior dev who caught a nasty prod bug, on the explicit grounds that other people should copy what she did; she refused to celebrate and actively deflected congratulations when someone got promoted to staff engineer, saying a title is not an achievement; she celebrated privately only a guy who won the internal hackathon with a demo that got a lot of attention, her reason being that the core idea came out of a discarded prototype somebody else had written; she cut down pretty visibly a senior engineer who started speaking at conferences and building a personal brand, stopped inviting him to internal talks, made jokes about his fan club; she celebrated, against her wishes and over her objections, someone who had quietly mentored three juniors for a year and never mentioned it; she said nothing at all about a designer whose side project went viral and got acquired, her comment to me being right place right time; and when one salesperson closed by far the biggest deal in company history she celebrated the team but not the individual.

The way I've started thinking about it, mostly because I have no philosophy background and needed something I could actually reason with, is that there are two seperate questions tangled together here: whether the person is creditworthy at all, and whether credit is a thing we should be handing out in public in the first place. She might have an answer to only one of them, or she might be answering both with the same test. The candidate rules I can see fitting her verdicts: celebrate only when the cause was effort and agency rather than luck or circumstance; celebrate only when publicising it will make other people act that way; celebrate the reluctant and deflate the self-promoting; celebrate only what can be shared out and refuse to celebrate anything that creates a visible rank; possibly others I haven't thought of.

The case actually in front of us: a backend dev in the Oslo office spent roughly three years of his own time on an open-source library, which unexpectedly became the thing half our candidates bring up in interviews; he now has a big public following, earns more from paid workshops on the side than from his salary, and has put in a request to be made Principal Engineer with a profile on the company site. He isn't boastful about it but he's definately not hiding it either, and the request came from him rather than from us. So: what should we do here if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which of our precedents is the closest analog, which of those rules best fits her whole track record rather than just the cases I happen to remember, and where do the plausible rules come apart on this specific one? And if the precedents genuinely don't settle it, I'd rather find out now, along with what principle I'd be silently writing into the document with each choice — because whatever we publish, the Austin half will read it as a statement about whether people here are allowed to be proud of things, and the Oslo half will read it as a statement about the opposite.
