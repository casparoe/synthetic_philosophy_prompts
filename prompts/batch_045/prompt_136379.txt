I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and I run my department's graduate interview series, where a speaker gives a short paper and then I interview them for about forty minutes. My next guest is a moral philosopher who has built a research programme around what she calls the *symmetry response* to consequentialist cluelessness. I'll call her "R" below, because the position I describe is my reconstruction from her paper and correspondence rather than anything I should be quoting.

Roughly, her view has four moving parts. First, she accepts the core cluelessness claim: for almost any ordinary act, our evidence does not favour the hypothesis that the act's total net effect on value, over an open-ended future, is positive over the hypothesis that it is negative. Second, she denies that this defeats the reasons supplied by the act's near-term effects: because the long-run considerations are, as she puts it, evidentially symmetric across the options, they cancel, and the near-term effects decide. Her signature line, which she uses to shut down a lot of objections at once, is: "cluelessness is a fact about our forecasts, not a fact about our reasons." Third, she insists the upshot is not quietism but a reallocation: since acts come out roughly tied at the level of their long-run effects, the expected value of *information* about those effects is where the practical action is, so cluelessness supports funding forecasting research, institution-building and moral-hedging strategies rather than agonising over individual choices. Fourth, she treats all of this as a reason to read consequentialism as a criterion for evaluating decision procedures and institutions rather than individual acts. She is formidably good at presenting this package as modest, and at making objectors sound as though they have confused an epistemological limitation with a normative one.

I want ten to twelve questions with follow-ups, ordered as I should actually ask them, that do not let her restate the view. At least one question each on the following, with the follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give:

1. Whether "symmetry" is something cluelessness gives you for free or a substantive second-order empirical claim she is not entitled to: cluelessness in her sense is insensitivity of credence to evidence, but symmetry requires that her evidence support positive and negative long-run effects *equally*, which looks like a claim about the shape of her uncertainty rather than the absence of one — and if she has imprecise probabilities rather than a symmetric sharp one, standard ambiguity-averse rules give verdicts she has not considered.
2. The magnitude problem: by her own lights the long-run effects are many orders of magnitude larger than the near-term ones, so "they cancel" cannot just mean "their expected values are both around zero" unless she defends risk-neutrality about moral value; and if she does defend it, whether her view quietly entails that the far future is decision-irrelevant, which is a substantive anti-longtermist conclusion she has never owned.
3. The continuity or arbitrariness worry: chaotic amplification means her forecast reliability does not decay at some principled boundary, so whatever horizon she implicitly uses has no non-arbitrary setting; and the rate at which reliability decays is itself something she would have to forecast, so her view generates a hierarchy of higher-order cluelessness about which she owes us a stopping story.
4. The conflict between parts two and three: if long-run considerations genuinely cancel, it is unclear why information about them has expected value, and "wait and learn" is itself an act with long-run effects, in a case where the window for acting may close.
5. The level-shifting move: if cluelessness is severe enough to make act-level consequentialist evaluation idle, why is rule- or institution-level evaluation not idle for exactly the same reason, given that the evaluation of a rule aggregates over the same open-ended future?
6. The collapse worry: her recommendation in ordinary cases appears extensionally identical to what a moderately thoughtful non-consequentialist would say, so I want to press on what consequentialism is still *doing* in her theory — whether the criterion has become idle, and what would be lost if she dropped it.

Please anchor at least two of the questions in the case I plan to put to her: a graduate student choosing between staying in academic philosophy, where the long-run influence runs through students and ideas and is unforecastable beyond a decade or so, and taking a finance job to fund insecticide-treated bednet distributions, where the near-term effects are well measured and the long-run effects on population size, economic development and eventually on far-future generations are precisely where cluelessness bites.

Two further constraints. Give me a one-sentence setup for each question so listeners who have not read the paper can follow it, and avoid anything generic about how she came to the topic. And I would rather you commit than survey: produce one sequence, in the order you would ask it, with your reasons for the ordering, tell me which single question you expect will actually make her move and why, and explicitly flag any of my six angles that you think is weak or likely to be absorbed by her machinery — I would rather cut a weak line of questioning than pad the hour. Where a follow-up depends on a guess about how she will answer, state the guess so I can adjust in real time. Finally, end with one question about what evidence could count against her view, framed so that "we would just need better forecasting" is not available as an answer.
