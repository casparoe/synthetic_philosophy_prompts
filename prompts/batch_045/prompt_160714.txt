A standard form of hospital surge-capacity protocol for ventilator shortages pairs two rules. At allocation, when clinical prognosis does not separate two patients, priority goes to the patient who is worse off in lifetime terms: a benefit of a given size counts for more the lower the recipient's lifetime well-being, so that a small benefit to a badly-off patient can outweigh a larger benefit to a well-off one. But once a patient is on a ventilator, they are not withdrawn so that a newly arrived patient who is worse off and has a better prognosis can have it; incumbency settles it, and the incumbent's lifetime well-being is no longer consulted. The protocol often adds, in the same breath, that it is not comparing the value of lives.

What does that combination presuppose? The second rule looks like exactly what a consistently prioritarian protocol should reject: if benefits to the worse off count for more, then transferring a resource from a better-off incumbent to a worse-off newcomer can raise the weighted total even when it lowers total well-being, which is prioritarianism's residual version of the leveling-down worry rather than something it escapes. Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the no-withdrawal rule: is it a deontic constraint on harming, a doing/allowing distinction, reliance or institutional trust, rule-prioritarianism, or something about whose resource this is? Distinguish what the protocol strictly presupposes from what merely fits it, and say where it is underdetermined, so that utilitarian, sufficientarian, or egalitarian rationales would license the same pair of rules. Then tell me what a protocol written to be consistently prioritarian would have to do about incumbents, and whether the result reads as a reductio of the weighting or of the no-withdrawal rule.
