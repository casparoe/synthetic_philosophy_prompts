My friend Priya and I spent like three hours last night arguing about whether thought experiments in philosophy ever actually show anything, or whether they're just rhetorical gadgets (she kept calling them intuition pumps, which I think is Dennett's phrase) that make a conclusion feel obvious without giving you any reason for it. She says they're the closest thing philosophy has to data; I said they're persuasion with a lab coat on. We ended up going case by case, and I came away with a pile of verdicts that I now realize don't obviously add up, so I'm trying to work out what I actually think.

Here's where I landed. Gettier's little story about the job candidate and the coins in somebody's pocket (I'm probably mangling the details, you know the shape) — legit. It's tiny, there's basically no imagery, and it turns on exactly one move: justified true belief that still isn't knowledge. Thomson's violinist — legit too, even though the setup is completely fantastical, because the fantasy is a stand-in for something real and the weirdness isn't what's pushing me. Parfit's teletransporter — legit, and I trust it more because my answer barely changes whether the copy is made before or after the original is destroyed, or whether there are two copies. Nozick's experience machine — I think it's rigged. If I say it flatly ("you reliably get whatever you most want and you never find out") my resistance mostly evaporates, and what's left doing the work is the picture of a body floating in a tank. Same with the utility monster: the monster part is doing all the persuading. But the repugnant conclusion I want to keep, even though it's also a pure aggregation intuition and also kind of gross, because I can put it in completely flat language and still feel the pull. The Chinese room — I want to throw out, on the grounds that whether the guy in there "understands" Chinese is precisely what's being argued about, and the image of a man shuffling flashcards is one side's conclusion dressed up as a neutral setup. Mary the color scientist — I also want to throw out, but for a different reason: I think the question "does she learn something new" slides between learning a fact and gaining an ability, and you get a different answer depending on which one you hear. And here's the pair I keep going back to: I'm keeping the teletransporter, which is science fiction, while junking the experience machine, which is also science fiction, and my only story about the difference is that the teleporter's fiction stands in for a real question about what matters in survival while the machine's fiction is a picture of the very thing being argued about — which feels like something I invented on the spot to save the verdicts I already liked.

The rule I half-articulated to Priya over the second round of drinks was: a thought experiment earns its conclusion only if (1) the fantastic element is a substitution for an ordinary situation rather than a picture of the disputed thing itself, (2) the verdict survives being restated in flat language with all the imagery stripped out, and (3) the verdict doesn't flip when you re-describe the scenario from the other side, or swap the loaded details for neutral ones. She pointed out that clause (2) probably sinks my verdict on Mary: stated drily — someone who knows every physical fact about color vision sees red for the first time, is there something she comes to know? — I still want to say yes, which means either the case is fine and my verdict is wrong, or clause (2) isn't enough and I need a further clause about setups whose question has more than one reading. And she said clause (1) fails my own teletransporter verdict, since the scanning machine there is arguably a picture of survival rather than a stand-in for it. I've started calling the irrelevant imagery "sticky details," and my worry is that everything I distrust just happens to be a case where I dislike the conclusion.

So: can you give me a principle that actually captures the pattern, or tell me straight which of my verdicts I have to give up? I'd like it stated precisely enough that I can apply it, with the case that motivates each clause, and I want you to be honest about where my set is simply inconsistent rather than papering over it with a disjunctive clause for every case. Then tell me what the principle says about the three I couldn't settle: Chalmers's zombie case (conceivable that there's someone physically identical to me with nothing it's like to be them) — flat restatement doesn't dissolve it for me, but the setup feels like a picture of the thing in dispute in the same way the Chinese room does, so my rule is pulling two ways; a near-future version of the teleporter where it's just a commute and the scan is imperfect, where I notice I'd probably use it, which makes me think my survival intuition is tracking something other than identity; and a trolley case rewritten as a product-safety decision at a car company, where the numbers are real (a recall costs 40 lives, skipping it costs 12) but the version I was shown includes a named six-year-old. I'm running a undergrad reading group next semester and I need a defensible rule for which of these things I put on the syllabus, so ideally the principle is something I can say in a sentence or two and then actually use.
