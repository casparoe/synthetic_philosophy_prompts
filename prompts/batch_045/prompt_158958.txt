For a 12-person upper-level seminar on consciousness (one 90-minute session), design a staged activity — roles, rules, materials, and a timeline — that makes students run into the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if experiences cause nothing, they do not cause the judgment that one is having them, so epiphenomenalism appears to remove the very thing that would make first-person judgments about experience justified or reliably true.

Three things matter to me. First, no framing lecture and no naming of the thesis up front: students should assemble the paradox out of what they are made to do — ideally they produce a confident first-person report, then get to inspect the causal history that produced it and see that the experience itself never entered it. Second, the activity must not be defused by the standard reply that the report still tracks the experience because both are effects of a single physical cause; I want reliability-without-causal-connection to surface as a felt problem rather than something I assert, via a report that comes out true for reasons that have nothing to do with what it is about. Third, they should leave able to separate, in their own words, three claims the epiphenomenalist has to prise apart: that the judgment is caused by the experience, that it is justified by it, and that it is made true by it.

Give me a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto those three claims, an honest account of what will predictably go wrong when I run it and how to handle it, and a note on which part of the paradox the activity cannot stage and I will have to say aloud.
