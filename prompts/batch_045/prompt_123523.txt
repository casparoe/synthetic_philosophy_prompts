I chair the Admissions Criteria Review Committee at a large public university's undergraduate honors college, and we have to report a recommendation to the dean before the vote on 21 March. Our governing board's policy requires that holistic review include a personal character and integrity dimension, but it says nothing about how that dimension is to be measured, and we cannot simply drop it. Our current instrument is two teacher recommendation letters that invite global trait adjectives — honest, kind, persevering — and an external reviewer last year called those letters unreliable and possibly worse than random. Our psychology department has proposed replacing them with something they call the Contextualized Behavior Inventory: instead of global traits, we would solicit reports from at least six independent observers who have seen the applicant in nominated different settings (classroom, residence hall, laboratory or studio, athletics, paid or service work, family) and would credit a candidate with a trait only where those reports converge. They piloted a version of this on 380 of our current first-years and report three findings I find hard to shake: agreement across settings on any global trait was close to zero, while rankings formed by averaging many observations within a single kind of setting were highly stable across the year; the best predictor of a later conduct violation was the frequency of a quite specific behavior in a quite specific kind of setting, not any overall character score; and roughly one student in nine showed a strikingly context-particular profile — punctilious in the lab, say, and careless with other people's property in the residence hall.

I teach one seminar that spends several weeks on virtue ethics, and the split on our committee maps onto a split I recognize there. Two members want to keep the letters, on the grounds that teachers who know a student over years are picking up something real that no inventory captures. Two want to adopt the inventory as proposed. I am in the third camp but not confidently: it seems to me that a virtue, on the Aristotelian picture I teach, was never supposed to be a matter of cross-situationally frequent behavior in the first place, but rather of a settled and reliably exercised way of perceiving, judging, desiring and acting — which would mean the inventory measures the wrong thing entirely rather than measuring the right thing badly. A colleague adds a nastier worry: the specific behaviors that turned out to be predictive are drawn disproportionately from settings where some of our applicants are watched closely and others are not.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the committee, about 900 words, since it goes to a board with no patience for footnotes and no one on it can look anything up. It should recommend one course of action — retain the letters, adopt the inventory as designed, or adopt a revised version with stated conditions and a validation timetable — and give reasons a psychologist and a philosopher could each actually answer. It should treat the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly rather than as a straw man, and it should close with the short paragraph we would send to an applicant rejected on character grounds, telling them, in plain terms, what we took them to lack.
