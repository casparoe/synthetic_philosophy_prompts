I direct the policy team at Emberhold, a platform that hosts several hundred thousand user-built virtual worlds. Our moderators handle roughly forty escalated complaints a week in which one user says they were genuinely harmed by what another user did to something inside a world: a structure destroyed, an avatar maneuvered into simulated sexual contact, a scarce item duplicated until it was worthless, a memorial defaced, a guild's treasury drained. Right now each of these is decided ad hoc, and our enforcement notices read inconsistently as a result — sometimes we write as though an object was damaged, sometimes as though nothing was damaged at all and only a person's feelings were affected, and we have no way to say which framing a given case called for.

I need a written decision procedure my moderators can actually run. Please build it, and please write your answer in British English.

What the procedure has to work with. Our worlds have three persistence classes, which we can read off the logs: "spawned" objects, which regenerate identically at the start of every session; "anchored" objects, which exist in exactly one copy, are bound to a single account, and which we can restore from a nightly snapshot; and "heirloom" objects, which are bound to one account, exist in one copy, and are flagged non-restorable — not even we can bring them back. We keep a provenance ledger recording build time, authorship, and transfer history for anchored and heirloom items. We sell an in-world currency, shards, at a fixed rate and redeem it on the same schedule; there is also a player-to-player market in items that we tolerate but do not run or guarantee. Some worlds use a "tethering" mechanic in which damage dealt to one avatar is mechanically propagated to another avatar or to a construct that player maintains. Worlds can be tagged "open PvP," "permadeath," or "no-loss" by their creators, and entering a tagged world requires a click-through that states the tag.

Constraints the procedure has to respect. It will be run by moderators with no philosophy or legal training, in under half an hour, using only evidence in our logs and ledgers. It may not depend on identifying anyone off-platform, on any clinical or psychological assessment of how upset a user was, or on any case-by-case judgment about how much an item "meant" to someone beyond what the provenance ledger shows. Its outputs should be one of: enforce as a property-type violation; enforce as a conduct violation without any claim about objects; no enforcement, with the reason recorded; or escalate. Escalations go to a five-person review panel that meets weekly. Every outcome has to be summarizable in one sentence for a public transparency report, so the reasoning cannot be allowed to sound incoherent once compressed.

What I want you to deliver. First, an ordered set of named tests, each with a statement of what counts as sufficient evidence for it from our records, including at least one gate where the procedure stops outright with no enforcement and at least one where it must hand the case to the panel on the grounds that the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Include an explicit "we cannot tell" outcome for cases our data cannot distinguish. Second, a worked application to three cases: an heirloom construct destroyed by a trap in a world tagged permadeath, where the builder had clicked through the warning and the ledger shows nine hundred hours of building; a case of non-consensual avatar contact in an untagged social world in which nothing was destroyed, lost, or devalued; and a duplication exploit that put ten thousand copies of a scarce cosmetic into circulation, where the loss to any individual holder is trivial and the aggregate loss is large. At least one of the three must come out in a way my team will not like, and I want you to say plainly that it does and why. Third, a one-page version for moderator use, plus a short log of the substantive assumptions baked into each step.

Two further things matter to me as much as the cases. My team is split between those who think virtual objects are perfectly real objects that can be damaged, those who think nothing was really harmed and only the person was, and — separately — those who think any loss here is fully explained by invested labor and scarcity versus those who think the object's role inside the community's ongoing practice is what does the real work. I do not need you to settle any of that, but at every step where the outcome would flip if one of those questions were resolved the other way, I want that flagged in the margin. And I want a closing section on what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, which kinds of case it will systematically misdescribe, and what it would take — in evidence or in policy — to fix it.
