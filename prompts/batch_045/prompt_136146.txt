My partner had a brief psychotic episode last year, and the psychiatrist explained it to us as the brain's predictions getting more weight than the evidence coming in. Since then I've fallen into reading about predictive processing, and the line everyone repeats is that perception is controlled hallucination: what you and I do all the time is the same kind of thing, just reined in by the senses. A guy in my lab pushes it much further — he says there's no principled line between ordinary perceiving and delusion, only a continuum of how badly the generative model ignores the evidence, and that it follows we don't really perceive a mind-independent world.

I'm not convinced, but I can't say why. Two things bug me. Hallucination seems to be defined as perception without a proper object, so if perception *is* hallucination, the contrast the comparison rests on vanishes — it looks circular. And my partner couldn't be talked out of it: turning on the light, touching the thing, nothing made her revise. That makes me think the real difference isn't how much weight the prior gets but whether there's any evidence left that could still move you. Also, if agents minimize prediction error partly by acting on the world, then a hallucination and a veridical perception look equally good at error minimization, so error minimization can't be what separates them.

Can you audit the analogy properly: a mapping table of what plays the role of the drug, the sensory signal, the reality check, the patient's insight; where the mapping breaks; what it quietly smuggles in about veridical perception being available as a baseline; and whether either of his big conclusions survives or whether the analogy was carrying all of it. If there's a better analogy for what perception is on this picture, give me that too.
