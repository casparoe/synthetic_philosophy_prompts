I'm a senior in a philosophy-and-fiction elective, and our final assignment is a short story (around 1500 words) that has to dramatize a philosophical idea rather than explain it. I want to write about epistemic injustice, but not the case everyone writes about.

The setup I have in mind: a near-future public high school where any student reporting something serious — bullying, harassment, an assault at a party — submits the account through a district system that clarifies it before the disciplinary committee sees it. The system fills in context, irons out grammar and dialect, and replaces vague or borrowed phrasing with the recognized vocabulary for what happened: unwanted sexual contact, credible threat, pattern of intimidation. Its purpose is exactly to stop reports from being brushed aside because the speaker lacked the words, and mostly it succeeds. My protagonist's report gets upgraded into a clear, fluent, legible narrative that wins her the outcome she wanted — but the fluent version also says one thing she never quite said and would not have chosen to say: it names someone she was trying to protect, or it reframes an ongoing campaign of humiliation as a single incident she is now classifying as an assault. She is believed, and she loses authorship of her own account; when she tries to correct the record, the correction is itself clarified back into the official version.

The reason this interests me is that the standard framing — the wrong of being denied the credibility you deserve, and the wrong of lacking the shared words to make sense of your own experience — doesn't obviously cover it. Here the speaker is given both, at the cost of no longer being the one who says what happened. Being made intelligible on someone else's terms looks like a real harm even though it produces the good result the standard view wants.

Could you write this? I'd like third-person limited from her side, the committee members written as decent people with decent reasons rather than as villains, no character explaining the philosophy out loud, and an ending that doesn't settle into a clean moral. After the story, please add about 150 words on what you think the case shows that the usual two-part framing misses, and one way someone could argue it isn't a distinct kind of injustice at all.
