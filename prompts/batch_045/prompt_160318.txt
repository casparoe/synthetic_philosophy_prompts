I'm a senior at a public high school in Ohio, and I compete in Public Forum debate. Our current resolution asks whether American companies operating abroad should be held to American anti-bribery rules rather than to local practice. In practice rounds my partner keeps closing our case with "when in Rome, do as the Romans do," and it seems to end the argument every time. The example he uses is a truck full of parts sitting at a border crossing for three days until someone hands a clerk a small amount of cash, after which the paperwork is stamped and the truck moves. Everyone at that crossing treats the payment as normal. So, the slogan is supposed to establish, the payment isn't corrupt — or at least it isn't corrupt *there* — and American rules are just one culture's taboo that we are trying to export.

It feels like the proverb is doing several different jobs at once, and I want to know which one my partner actually needs. Could you lay out, as a list I can bring to practice, the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, from the trivial to the radical? For each one I'd like a verdict on three things: whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive but arguable, or whether it is straightforwardly false; and, most importantly, whether it would actually get my partner his conclusion.

A few complications I'd want the list to handle, because I think they are where the argument really lives:

First, I'm not sure which "Rome" is supposed to be doing the work. There is the local statute, there is what officials actually do, there is what ordinary people there say they approve of, and there is the group that ends up paying for the favor. In a lot of these cases the people harmed by a diverted contract or a skipped inspection are not the people whose conventions set the etiquette. Is the proverb a claim about the conventions of the insiders or of everyone affected?

Second, my teacher suggested that a practice can be locally *expected* without being locally *endorsed* — that plenty of people make these payments and still think the system that requires them is rotten. If that's right, it seems like one reading of the proverb (the descriptive one) is satisfied while another (the moral one) does nothing at all. I'd like that gap made explicit, and also the related question of what happens if the payment turns out to be illegal under that country's own law too.

Third, and this is the part I care about most: my partner slides between calling the payment a "gift," a "fee," and a "bribe," and I don't think those are the same shape of thing. As I understand it, a gift opens an ongoing relationship with an unstated return; a bribe is closer to a price that settles the matter on the spot; and a clientelist exchange is neither, because its point is a durable bond of protection and loyalty rather than any single trade. If the standard Western complaint is that the transfer buys a favor that an impersonal rule was supposed to allocate, then what is the matching complaint *inside* the clientelist picture — something like the betrayal of an obligation of care, perhaps — and what would the proverb have to mean for the second complaint to cancel the first instead of just being a different complaint?

Finally, is there a reading on which the proverb isn't a moral claim at all, but a claim about who is in a position to interpret what the payment means — locals understand the gesture, so my classification should defer to theirs? If so, does that kind of epistemic deference ever turn into moral deference, or is my partner smuggling the second in under cover of the first?

I'm not looking for sources or a reading list; I just want the menu of readings and the verdicts, ideally short enough to read aloud at practice.
