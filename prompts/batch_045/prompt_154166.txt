I am part of a small group drafting an internal options paper on migration for a public agency, and I have noticed that one analogy is doing nearly all of the argumentative work in our meetings, usually without anyone saying so out loud. The analogy is that the polity is a private club: the members built the clubhouse, pay dues into a common fund, have agreed to a set of house rules, and may therefore decide whom to admit and on what terms — including admitting people as probationary members who pay in at the full rate but cannot draw on the fund. When the discussion turns fiscal, the club quietly becomes a mutual insurance pool, and the point about adverse selection is added: a pool that must accept every applicant regardless of expected claims will not survive.

Three conclusions are being rested on this. First, the general conclusion that a political community with a generous welfare state has a right to exclude would-be entrants, derived from something like freedom of association. Second, the institutional conclusion currently on the table in the jurisdiction I am writing about, call it Meridia: a Five-Year Rule under which newcomers contribute at full rates for five years before becoming eligible for the means-tested minimum income benefit or for unemployment insurance, while accruing pension credits from their first day of work and using the tax-funded health service immediately. Third, the broader incompatibility claim, that openness and a redistributive welfare state cannot both be had, so that anyone who wants the second must give up the first.

The reason I am suspicious is a list of features of Meridia that the club picture seems unable to represent, and I would like to know which of them actually damage the conclusions and which are decorative.

One is what I have been calling the arrival-age transfer. Meridia finances schooling out of general taxation and recoups the cost through a lifetime of subsequent earnings taxes; it also pays the largest benefits late in life. A person admitted at twenty-five arrives after somebody else has paid for their schooling, and a person who leaves at sixty before drawing a pension has paid the most and drawn the least. The club has no line item for who financed a member's training before they applied, and no way to represent the fact that the value of an entrant to the fund is a function of their age on arrival and of how long they stay.

A second is that Meridia itself depresses early contributions. Its occupational licensing regime and language requirements mean that a newcomer's earnings, and therefore their dues, are low for years for reasons the receiving polity's own rules create. In the club, applicants compete on equal footing once admitted; here the clubhouse is simultaneously setting the handicap and then citing the result.

A third is the status of people living in Meridia without legal authorisation, several hundred thousand of them. They pay value-added tax and have payroll contributions deducted, they are fully subject to the criminal law, and they cannot draw on the fund. On the club picture they are simply not members, but they are ruled, which is not a relation clubs have with non-applicants.

A fourth is that no current member built the clubhouse. Clubs are founded by their members; polities are inherited, along with their debts and the terms on which past entrances happened. A fifth is that Meridia recruits nurses and engineers from polities whose own contributory pools depend on exactly the contributors who leave, and a club of clubs would presumably treat poaching as something to be compensated or forbidden; I cannot tell whether the analogy generates any conclusion here or falls silent. A sixth is that children born in Meridia to non-citizen parents are citizens at birth and thus full members with no probationary period, which the club has no analogue for. And a seventh, on the integration side: Meridia's labour-market data show a persistent employment and wage gap for the children of immigrants relative to comparable natives, a gap that does not close after controlling for education, language proficiency and region. The club picture can only render this as a failure to learn the house rules, but if the gap survives those controls the house-rules framing seems to put the causal weight in the wrong place, since credentialing, hiring networks and school placement are things the receiving institutions do.

What I would like from you is an audit rather than a verdict on the merits.

Please set out the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the clubhouse, the dues, the common fund, the membership committee, the house rules, the bouncer, expulsion, the probationary member and the waiting list — and note any element of Meridia for which the club has no slot at all.

Then, separately for each of the three conclusions, tell me which disanalogies are load-bearing and which are noise: which ones would actually change what the club picture can support, and which only make the picture feel less flattering. In particular I want to know what the club imports into the polity that the polity lacks — voluntary membership, separability of membership from presence on a territory, the ability to refuse admission without coercing the refused, a fixed membership, a real exit option, an ownership share in the fund — and, conversely, what the polity has that no club does, such as the power to tax and to arrest people it does not count as members.

Then tell me whether each of the three conclusions survives once those features are on the table. I would also appreciate a strict separation of the moral question, whether Meridia may exclude, from the fiscal question, whether openness would bankrupt the fund; our group slides between them constantly, and I suspect the analogy is what makes the slide look harmless.

Finally, if the club is the wrong picture, say what a better one would be. Candidates our group has floated are a firm with a funded defined-benefit pension plan that hires from outside, a municipality inside a federation with free internal movement and a tax base it cannot fully control, a condominium association with compulsory membership and no exit, and a professional body with the power to licence. Say which of these preserves more of the structure the argument needs, and whether it still yields any of the three conclusions or quietly withdraws its support. If none of them is adequate, say so, and tell me which single case would best discriminate between the pictures we have — my own guess is that the returning native who spent twenty years abroad and comes back, or the unauthorised resident who has paid in for a decade, or the citizen child of a probationary member, would each force a different one of the analogies to show its hand.
