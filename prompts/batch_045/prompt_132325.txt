I am giving a forty-minute paper at a technical AI safety lab's weekly seminar next month, with a twenty-minute question period. The room will be about thirty people: the lab's own alignment team (who have shipped a production assistant and think about values in terms of reward models, preference elicitation, and distributional shift), two visiting political philosophers (one contractualist in the Scanlonian idiom of reasonable rejectability, one act-utilitarian who is friendly to interpersonal comparisons of welfare), one social choice theorist who works on Arrow-style and Harsanyi-style aggregation results, one value pluralist who thinks evaluative considerations are irreducibly many and partly incommensurable, and a handful of governance people whose real question is whether any of this could be defended to a regulator. Very few of them will have read the same things I have.

The thesis I am defending, titled "Constituency by Veto: Enfranchisement Without Aggregation," is this: for a general-purpose AI assistant serving a pluralist population, the question of whose values it should serve should not be posed as an aggregation problem over a fixed population, because for such a system the population is endogenous — it is fixed by which action is on the table. So I propose a two-stage construction, an enfranchisement rule followed by a positional rule.

Step one, enfranchisement: a person is enfranchised with respect to a candidate action if and only if that action foreseeably sets her prospects back, relative to her own counterfactual baseline trajectory, by more than a fixed threshold. Call this the non-regression baseline. Two features matter. Only setbacks enfranchise, never benefits, so the system never acquires a mandate to promote the good of people it merely affects favourably or is merely correlated with. And setbacks are measured against each person's own trajectory rather than against a common scale, so the rule makes no interpersonal comparisons of welfare and does not require the incommensurable to be commensurated.

Step two, aggregation: for each enfranchised person, construct an ideally situated counterpart — she knows the relevant non-moral facts, her own psychology, and the feasible menu, but she is not required to be fully informed about everything or to be impartial. Elicit her ranking of the feasible actions, and take the median ranking over the enfranchised set — the ranking that minimizes the number of enfranchised persons who prefer some alternative to it — with ties broken in favour of the ranking of whoever is worst off on the non-regression baseline.

Step three, the case for it: the justification is not fairness to values but a legitimacy claim. Setbacks are permitted only where they are authorized by the median of those who suffer them, so each setback can be defended to the person who suffers it by appeal to considerations she could not reasonably reject. And the construction buys three design virtues at once: no interpersonal utility comparisons, no summation over an unbounded and possibly infinite affected population, and no need for the system to take a stand on which evaluative standards are the correct ones. Liberal neutrality is achieved not by the system being neutral among values but by its never having to rank them.

I know the construction has two soft spots I will concede in the first five minutes: the constituency varies with the candidate action, so the pair of rules is really a fixed-point condition rather than a social choice function; and a system scored on outcomes has an incentive to prefer actions with friendly or smaller enfranchised sets, a kind of constituency shopping.
