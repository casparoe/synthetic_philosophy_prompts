i work on the eval team at a mid-sized AI lab and we put out a quarterly scorecard with one top-line capability number on the first page. Procurement people at customer companies actually read that number, so the fight about whether it means anything is not academic. One of our senior researchers keeps killing the aggregate in review meetings with the same move: the mind is a Swiss army knife. Evolution didn't build a general-purpose blade, it bolted on a pile of special-purpose tools as needed, so there is no such thing as general intelligence, and a single top-line score is a category mistake. He uses it to argue we should publish a radar chart of unrelated skills and drop the number. I think the knife is doing all the work in that argument and I want it audited before I write the report.

Give me the correspondence table first: blades, the handle, the spring, the factory, the jobs the knife is bought for, wear on the tools — mapped onto whatever the right relata are on our side (modules? subnetworks? training domains? the eval tasks themselves? compute? the training distribution?). Then where it breaks, and please seperate the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that are just decoration.

Three things from our own numbers I want it to answer to rather than hand-wave past. We ran about forty tasks: score accuracy on hard items and you basically get one dominant factor, but score easy items on time-to-first-token and the correlations mostly evaporate and you get four or five clusters instead. Fine-tune hard on one cluster and a neighboring cluster reliably gets worse, and the size of the hit tracks how strongly the two loaded on the same factor. And the clusters we get depend on which tasks we happened to write — throw in a batch of spatial stuff and "verbal" and "numeric" stop being the axes.

The bit I can't get straight is the handle. On a real knife the tools share a handle precisely because nothing is being optimized at runtime — they're riveted to one piece of metal and they don't compete for anything. But if what people call general intelligence just is the shared thing the blades fight over, or the thing that makes training on A bleed into B, then the analogy has smuggled the general factor back in and renamed it hardware. Is that fair? Does the knife picture even survive once you let the blades compete for a budget, or does it quietly become a different picture?

Then the practical question: which of his conclusions can the analogy carry and which can it not, and is there a better analogy for whatever is actually going on. Tell me how confident you are in each of the main mapping claims, flag the one you're least sure of, and say plainly if you think our forty-task result is too weak to hang anything on.
