i keep half-winning an argument with my reading group on Turing and education, and now I think it proves too much but I can't see where to cut. Here it is.

(1) What education is for is reach: the educated person produces correct answers to questions outside the set they were trained on, rather than merely storing answers.
(2) Turing's reason for preferring a child-machine to programming an adult mind: if you write the answers in directly, the machine has exactly the coverage you gave it and nothing more. Reach can't be installed, it has to be acquired.
(3) So the content of instruction — the propositions a student is taught — is not what education consists in. Instruction matters only as causal input to whatever process builds the dispositions that reach consists in.
(4) If content isn't what matters, then two curricula that reliably produce the same dispositions are equally good as education, whatever they happen to be made of.
(5) Now consider the false-colour curriculum: take a real syllabus and apply a systematic relabelling to it — swap every pair of proper names in the textbook, add a fixed amount to every quantity, keep every inferential relation exactly as it was. It's false top to bottom, but from the inside it's indistinguishable from the true one, and students trained on it generalize just as well and just as confidently; they simply land on the relabelled answers.
(6) So by (4) the false-colour curriculum is as good as the true one. That's mad — and not only because it's false: the students who got it are also harder to correct later, since their errors prop each other up.
(7) So something upstream is wrong.

I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than give either up — I really do think Turing's point carries into classrooms and that teaching to the test isn't teaching — but I don't want to say a syllabus of beautiful lies is fine, and I also don't want to smuggle truth back in as just one more disposition among others, which feels like cheating. Which step do you reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost me? And separately: is the false-colour curriculum even possible, or does (5) assume something about belief and inference that couldn't happen?
