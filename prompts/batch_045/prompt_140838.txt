I'm writing a novel (lit fic, not genre) and about a third of the way in my main character Nadia gets a diagnosis — I've been writing it as a depressive episode but I keep changing my mind — and there's a scene where the psychiatrist tells her that mental illnesses are illnesses like any other. I've had that line in my head since I started, I like how it sounds coming out of a calm person, but the more I sit with it the less I know what it's actually asserting.

The work it has to do in the book: later Nadia's brother tells her she's using the diagnosis as an identity, and I want her to throw the psychiatrist's line back at him — the idea being that the label described something that was already there, it didn't install it. But a friend who read the draft said the line is sneakier than that: with a broken leg the classification doesn't feed back into the leg, whereas a psychiatric category is the sort of thing the person classified can read about, organise around, join a forum about, and start to inhabit. She said there's a name for this, some philosopher — Hacking? — and the word looping. I didn't really follow and I dont want to have to read a whole book just to finish my chapter.

So: what are the different precise things that one sentence could be claiming? All of them, from the almost-empty to the wild. Is it a claim about what illness means, about blame and excuses, about what causes the thing, about whether psychiatry should be run like the rest of medicine, about whether these categories are real joints in nature that we discovered rather than drew, about whether the person is the same person before and after the label? Give me them as a list, one line each, with a verdict on each — trivially true, defensible but contested, or just false — and here's the bit I actually need: which of those readings would let Nadia use it against her brother, and which of them quietly hands him the argument.

And one more thing, because it happens in my draft and I can't tell if I'm being clever or incoherent. A few chapters after the diagnosis Nadia finds the actual entry for it in a diagnostic manual in a waiting room, reads the criteria, and starts renarrating her whole twenties in that vocabulary — winters she'd always filed under a bad year get refiled as episodes, symptoms, prodrome — and I want the reader unsure whether she's finally understanding herself or being rewritten by the page. If the looping business is right, is the psychiatrist's line not just shaky but self-undermining in a way it wouldn't be for diabetes? Or is there a thin version of "like any other" that survives even that, one she could still say honestly? I need to know what my psychiatrist character thinks she's saying, because whether she reads as smug or as subtle depends entirely on it.
