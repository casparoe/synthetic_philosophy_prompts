I'm a third-year maths-and-philosophy student, and on Thursday I'm taking one of the problem sessions for a first-year course called Chance, Symmetry and Evidence. The students can integrate a density and have met the uniform distribution on an interval, but they have no measure theory, no group theory, and no formal epistemology, and I have twenty-five minutes to use the paradox that goes back to Bertrand — the probability that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, with the three familiar answers 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 each falling out of a parameterization that sounds equally random — to get them to see what goes wrong with the principle of indifference, or principle of insufficient reason, when it is applied to a continuous quantity. I don't need the paradox explained to me: I need a plan for explaining it, and the part I keep getting wrong is the sequencing.

Three things in particular. First, the order and the dependencies: which idea has to be in place before the paradox can even look like a problem rather than a trick — my instinct is that the three chord answers cannot come first, because students who see 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 before they have felt the pull of the original question just conclude that the question was vague and move on, and that instead something has to be built first about what an indifference argument is supposed to be doing when it distributes credence over a continuum — but I'm not confident about what that something is, or about whether the distinction between the parameterization doing the work (pick the midpoint, pick the radius, pick the endpoint) and the measure that results should be made explicit before or after. Second, the carrying example, and whether the one I've been toying with actually carries: a point chosen at random in a circular dartboard of radius one, and the probability that it lands in the concentric circle of radius one half, which is 1/2 if you choose a radius uniformly and then an angle uniformly, and 1/4 if you choose uniformly by area, and which I like because the two procedures can be drawn on the board as pictures of each other and because the difference in the densities is visible rather than merely algebraic. My supervisor says my dartboard is a cheat: here there is a privileged answer, since the area-uniform distribution is the one that is invariant under the Euclidean motions that preserve the board and the uniform-radius distribution is not, so the case can only show that students picked a silly parameterization, not that a well-meant indifference argument can fail in a way nobody can repair. Is she right, and if she is, what does a carrying example have to contain that mine lacks — must it have no privileged measure at all, or must it merely have one that is not obvious to a first-year — and is the dartboard worth keeping as a five-minute warm-up before the chord anyway, or will it install the wrong moral? Relatedly, I've been dividing the failures into two kinds for my own notes: cases like mine where the rival parameterizations disagree because one of them quietly introduces a length scale or a preferred region that the setup does not contain, and cases like the chord where the rivals disagree even though each respects every symmetry the setup obviously has. Is that distinction load-bearing or cosmetic, and if it is load-bearing, where in the sequence does it belong?

Third, the misconceptions, in the order they will actually arrive and the point at which each should be defused rather than merely corrected at the end. The ones I expect are: that uniform just means every outcome equally likely, so the paradox is only about infinity; that there must be a true answer among the three and we simply don't know which; that the moral is that probability is subjective, or that the setup was under-described and any answer is therefore as good as another; that one can average the three answers and split the difference; and, from the sharper students, that maximum entropy repairs indifference, or that the whole difficulty is that a uniform distribution on a length is not a uniform distribution on the area or on the ratio, which is true and yet somehow not the end of the matter. For each I'd like to know whether it should be headed off preemptively in the setup, absorbed when it surfaces, or deliberately left standing until the last two minutes, and what I should say in one sentence if it surfaces early and I don't want to derail the arc. Finally, I want a check that discriminates: a question I can put to them cold at the end, ideally set in a new case rather than a circle, that a student who has only memorized the phrase it depends on how you choose at random cannot answer, plus your view on what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing — I'm thinking anything involving the uniqueness of invariant measures on a group, any derivation of a maximum-entropy distribution in the continuous case, and the historical question of what Bertrand himself took himself to be doing, but tell me if I'm being too cautious or not cautious enough. For what it's worth, I got into an argument in the maths society afterwards in which one friend insisted that the chord question has a correct empirical answer because you could always go and throw straws at a circle drawn on the floor, and another insisted that this just relocates the choice into the throwing; if either of them is pointing at something my session should make room for, I'd rather find out now than on Thursday.
