I'm in the second year of a PhD in modern European history, and I've been asked to give a short paper to my department's historiography reading group on counterfactual reasoning about the origins of the Third Reich. Before I write it, I'd like someone to audit the position I've ended up at, because I have the feeling it is one distinction short of being stable and I can't locate the gap. Here is the set, roughly in the order I argued myself into it. (1) Historical explanation answers contrastive questions of the form why this rather than that, so the question whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler isn't well posed until you say what the alternative was supposed to be. (2) Once the contrast is fixed at a coarse grain — some authoritarian, expansionist, violently antisemitic regime in Germany in the 1930s — I think the answer is that something of that kind arrives anyway: the Depression, the collapse of the centrist parties, the settlement after the First World War, and the existing habits of the civil service, the courts and the officer corps did the work. (3) At a finer grain I think Hitler is genuinely difference-making: for the form and the timing of the genocide — extermination camps rather than the deportation-and-resettlement schemes that were the live alternatives earlier on — the individual matters, and the structural story does not settle it. (4) I want causation here to be plain difference-making: a factor mattered if, had it not occurred and had something drawn from the pool of candidates the situation actually made available occurred instead, the outcome would have been different. I call these supply-side counterfactuals, to set them apart from ones that simply delete the person from the record. (5) I reject great-man explanation as a general theory of history on the ground that it has no generalising or predictive power — nobody can say in advance which individuals will turn out to be world-historical, so it sorts things out only after the fact and explains nothing. I don't think my structural story in (2) is vulnerable to the same objection, since it cites conditions of a kind that recurs across cases. (6) But I also want to say that we can identify world-historical individuals after the fact, and that whether such a person was decisive is an objective matter, not just a question of which story is more gripping. (7) I hold a robustness principle: where a structure makes an outcome robust, in the sense that many distinct available role-fillers would each have produced it, the causal credit owed to any one of them shrinks; where the structure has a single point of failure, credit grows. (8) And I hold that a counterfactual whose antecedent does its own work — remove Hitler and install a competent moderate chancellor in his place — is no test of Hitler's causal role at all, because it tests a compound rather than a factor. Finally, a case verdict I'm attached to: Hindenburg's appointment of Hitler as chancellor in early 1933 was not by itself the difference between a Nazi regime and no Nazi regime, because had he held out the process would have reached something very similar by another route within a couple of years.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey of the literature, which I've read: go through this set and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like it, because I'd rather you discriminate than flag all eight as problematic. Where there is a real conflict, say what would have to be given up, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair costs me elsewhere in the set — in particular, I want to know whether the split in (3) between a coarse grain that the structure determines and a fine grain that the individual determines is a principled distinction, or a way of assigning each level whichever answer I already wanted, and whether (7) and (8) leave any room at all for the supply-side counterfactual that (4) requires. Rank the tensions by severity instead of listing them as equally serious, and finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most.
