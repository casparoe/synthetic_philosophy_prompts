I'm giving a talk at our Centre for Mind and Cognition colloquium in three weeks — 25 minutes plus maybe 20 for questions, which is never enough — and I could really use a Q&A prep sheet rather than another round of feedback on the slides.

My thesis is that the imitation game is the wrong *shape* of behavioural criterion for mind, and I'm deliberately not going with the usual complaints: I'm not saying it's too easy, or that a giant look-up table could in principle pass it, or that it ignores consciousness. My objection is structural. The game asks whether a system can be *mistaken for* us, so the competence it rewards is successful deception — and there's a well-known version of this worry where a system passes partly by imitating human failings, being slow at arithmetic, hedging, and sounding plausible rather than being right. If that's the target, then a better actor is a better passer, and it's at least arguable that a better actor is a *worse* candidate for mind, not a better one. The criterion I want to put in its place is what I've been calling the disagreement test: a system counts as a minded interlocutor if it can (i) track what you meant rather than just what you typed, (ii) hold you — and be held — to your earlier commitments after the conversation has moved on, and (iii) argue against you for reasons it expects *you* to accept, and actually lose when it should, i.e. visibly update. Roughly: minds are the sort of thing you can genuinely disagree with, not the sort of thing you can be fooled by. I also want to claim it's harder to game in a specific way — simulated fallibility gets you nowhere, because hedging and plausible-sounding vagueness don't get you through (iii).

The room will roughly be: two people who think today's large language models already basically pass the ordinary Turing test and that the debate has been settled by engineering rather than philosophy; one embodied/enactivist sort who thinks any purely linguistic criterion is a non-starter and that no criterion drawn from conversation could ever tell us anything about minds; a comparative psychologist who works on corvids, who will point out that we've had behavioural criteria for animal minds for decades and they're all endlessly contested, so "here is a behavioural criterion" is where the trouble starts and not where it ends; and a psychometrician who will want to know how any of this gets scored, what the error bars look like, and what would count as convergence across items rather than one striking transcript. What I want is the eight or so objections I'll actually get from *these* people — in their vocabulary, not in the idiom of the Turing-test literature — ordered by how much damage they'd do, each with a two-minute-ish reply and, importantly, a note on where my reply is soft. Which of them should I concede up front, before anyone asks? Which is the one I have to win? And what's the question I'm definitely hoping nobody asks? I've been chewing on this since a seminar last spring where someone said my criterion might just be the imitation game with a longer time horizon and a friendlier name, and I still don't have a good answer to that one, so its probably going to come up again.
