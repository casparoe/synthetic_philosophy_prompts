I am a novelist, and I need your judgment about something that is a craft problem before it is a philosophical one.

The novel I am finishing is set in a settlement of roughly nine hundred people — a enclosed valley with no realistic exit for most of them, everyone's labour entangled with everyone else's, the same forty families looking at each other across the same tables for generations — in the decades after a catastrophe of omission: the valley withheld its seed grain from a neighbouring hamlet through a bad winter, and the hamlet's children did not all survive. In the second act the elders do something slightly improbable: they convene to choose, deliberately, which moral emotion the settlement is to be run on thereafter. Some argue for institutions that cultivate guilt, by which I mean a distress that attaches to what a person has done, names a specific harm and a specific person harmed, and is quieted by repair rather than by concealment. Others argue for institutions that cultivate shame, by which I mean a distress that attaches to what a person is and to how she appears before others, and is quieted by withdrawal, by hiding, or by the driving out of somebody else. The scene is, I think, the best thing in the book. My difficulty is that I cannot tell who should win, because I cannot tell which of the six institutions on their shortlist would actually do what its advocates promise. I do not want a defence of guilt or of shame in the abstract, and I do not want the familiar anthropological habit of sorting whole cultures into guilt cultures and shame cultures; I want your assessment of these six particular devices, in this particular valley.

1. The unpassivizable verb. In the valley's dialect, verbs of harming have no passive and no impersonal form: one cannot say that the grain was not sent, only that I did not send it, or that you did not send it.

2. The naming hour. One evening a week, any resident may stand before the assembly and name, in the first person, a specific harm she did that week; the assembly's only permitted response is silence — no thanks, no absolution, no comment of any kind.

3. The second-person audit. Whoever has caused a harm must recount it to the person harmed, in that person's presence, in the second person, naming the concrete loss; one witness attends, whose only office is to strike out euphemism.

4. The stranger's ear. Once a season a boat brings an outsider who knows nothing of the valley, hears whatever anyone chooses to tell her, keeps no record, and departs; nothing said there has any consequence in the settlement at all.

5. The blackened lintel. A household answerable for a public harm paints its lintel black for a fixed term, and while the paint is up no one in the valley is permitted to name the reason aloud.

6. The counted absence. For a period set by the injured party, a place is laid at every common meal for the person who was harmed, and it is served by the one who harmed her.

Before you take the six individually, give me one number from 0 to 100 for how much of a community's moral-emotional character you take to be available for deliberate institutional design at all, as against being fixed by whether people can leave, whether they can hide, and whether their survival depends on the same neighbours year after year. Then, for each of the six, three more numbers from 0 to 100: first, how reliably it would cultivate guilt rather than shame in the senses I have given; second, how well it would survive two generations of being gamed, performed, or quietly hollowed out into theatre; third, how legibly it plays in a scene — my narrator is eleven years old at the time of the choosing, and she understands what adults do but not what they intend, so a device that works only when explained is worth less to me than one she can read off a doorway or a table. Then rank the six in the order in which you would have the elders adopt them, tell me which single one you would strike from the shortlist as not worth the ink, and tell me which one, adopted alone, would change the valley's character most — and which would most plausibly read to my narrator as mere cruelty. Where a number is close to a coin flip, say so, and say what in the valley's circumstances would settle it.

One last thing about form: please use no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, and no table. I want continuous prose, with the numbers carried inside sentences, the way you would write me a letter.
