I have no formal training in philosophy — I work in logistics and have been reading about Newcomb's problem on my own for a couple of years, mostly through long online essays and forum threads. What strikes me is not that the problem is hard but that the argument around it seems to be frozen. Everyone knows the set-up: a highly reliable predictor has already placed a large sum in one box conditional on having predicted that you will take only that box, and a small sum in a second box that you may take as well. Everyone knows the two answers and everyone knows the reasons for them. One side says the boxes are already sealed, nothing I do now can change their contents, and whichever way they were filled I come out with more if I take both — so take both. The other side says that in every actual run of this case the people who took one box walked away with the large sum, my evidence strongly indicates that I am in the good case exactly if I take one box, and a rule that reliably leaves you poorer than an available alternative is not much of a rule — so take one. Over fifty years of this and, as far as I can tell, nobody has moved, and both sides have stopped expecting anyone to.

I want a diagnosis of the stalemate, not a verdict on the case. My own hypothesis is that the two answers are answers to two different questions that happen to share a name: one about which act, performed now by the person already standing in the room, brings about the better outcome given the boxes; another about which general policy or disposition it would be best to have, in a world where predictors reward one-boxing dispositions and punish two-boxing ones. If that is right, the fighting is happening because each side keeps hearing its own question in the other side's answer. But it might instead be a verbal dispute about what it means to say I can affect something, or a real clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for, or a hidden question of some other kind doing all the work.

To test this I invented two variants, which I have not seen discussed anywhere (I may simply not have read widely enough).

The first is about where the predictor's reliability comes from. In the Gene variant the predictor runs a blood test for a gene that also happens to make people impulsive about money; the prediction and my later choice have a common cause and neither causes the other. In the Simulator variant the predictor ran a perfect simulation of my deliberation last night and filled the boxes according to what it did. The stated accuracy is the same in both. My question is whether the right thing to do changes between them. If it does, then the original dispute has an unstated question about causal structure buried inside it, and its frozenness may be an artifact of arguing about a set-up that never specifies one. If it does not, then the causal story is doing no work at all and the real disagreement lies elsewhere.

The second variant adds a choice before the choice. Call it the Pamphlet variant. Before entering the room I may read a two-page argument — say, the argument from the sealed boxes for taking both. I am told that the predictor's accuracy is around ninety-nine percent overall but close to chance for subjects who read any decision-theoretic argument in the hour before choosing; reading makes me unpredictable. So there are now two questions: whether to read, and what to do afterwards. My sense is that whichever theory tells me to read is telling me to make myself worse off in precisely the choice the theory is about, and that this self-undermining feature — a theory whose adoption defeats its own point — is what the disagreement is actually about. Is a theory of rational choice supposed to be something you can use, or something that tells you which outcomes your acts bring about whether or not using it helps you?

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really — verbal, substantive, two questions under one heading, a hidden question about causal structure, or incompatible starting intuitions? What does each side's reaction to my two variants reveal about what it is actually committed to, and are either of the variants genuinely diagnostic or do they smuggle in what I was trying to test? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal result, a distinction like the act-versus-policy one I gestured at, an experiment with actual people, or the discovery that the question was ill-posed? I would rather have reasons than a survey of the named positions and their histories — the surveys are what left me stuck.
