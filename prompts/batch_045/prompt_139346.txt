i'm TAing my first solo section this term, 50 minutes, first-years, and next week i have to do causation by omission. They've just come off two weeks of Humean regularity stuff and Mackie-style INUS conditions, and they haven't done counterfactuals or possible worlds properly, so I'd rather not lean on "in the nearest world where..." if I can avoid it. I tried this last year with the plant that died because nobody watered it and it went badly: within ninety seconds someone said "so the Queen of England killed your plant," everyone agreed that was absurd, half of them concluded omissions can't be causes and the other half concluded that everything is a cause, and I had no move ready for either. So: don't explain causation by omission to me — I want a plan for how to teach it.

What I'd like most is the order. What has to be on the board before "can a not-doing be a cause?" even feels like a real question rather than a verbal trick, given where these students are (they're happy talking about INUS conditions and drawing little sufficient-condition diagrams, and they currently think causation is a relation between events that happen)? Where does the "fine, but when and where did the not-watering happen?" worry fit — before the metaphysical "are absences things?" bit, after it, or should I skip that bit entirely at this level? And does the counterfactual way of putting it have to be smuggled in early anyway even though they don't have the machinery for it? For the carrying example I want one case, not three, that does the whole job: tell me what structural features it has to have and what it has to lack (my guess is exactly one person with a standing reason to act, nothing positive happening at all between the last chance to act and the outcome, and a counterfactual that is realistic rather than silly), why the plant fails — I think because it's too easy to reconstrue as a positive act, or because the real cause is obviously the sun and the dryness — and why the lurid medical cases will derail us into blame before we've even got the puzzle.

Then the misconceptions, including the ones I've named myself, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off: what I've been calling positive reconstrual — they turn the omission back into an action, "he chose to sit there," "she was doing something else instead" — which I think is the dangerous one because it makes the topic dissolve instead of getting answered; profligacy panic, the Queen of England line, which ate my whole class last year; the slide from cause to blame, which I suspect is what's really driving the "of course not" intuition; and the shrug where they decide it's just counterfactual dependence and move on. I've also been toying with a distinction I made up, between bare omissions (nobody had any reason whatsoever to expect you to act) and loaded omissions (there's a role, a practice, a standing expectation that you would): tell me whether bringing that in early gives me a clean way to handle profligacy without ending up having quietly substituted norms for causation, or whether it just teaches them that what causes what depends on what we expect of people — and if it's the latter, whether that's a bug I should hide for a week or a feature I should own out loud. Finally, what should I openly refuse to settle in fifty minutes and say out loud that I'm refusing (I'm guessing the legal act/omission doctrine, any structural-equation or difference-making formalism, and the free-will-adjacent material, but you tell me), and one question for the last five minutes that would actually show me they've grasped the shape of the problem rather than just picked a verdict.
