I'm writing a paper on hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy in historical explanation, and I want one section built as a thought experiment rather than as a survey of the literature.

Take Tolstoy as he appears in the historical chapters of *War and Peace*: his attack on historians who make Napoleon's will the cause of the 1812 campaign, his insistence that what actually happened was composed of innumerable individually negligible acts of soldiers and peasants, his claim that a genuine science of history would have to proceed like the calculus — taking infinitesimally small units of human movement and integrating them — instead of narrating the decisions of the great, and his related conviction that the appearance of a commander's control is an artifact of looking backward.

Now suppose he is shown a system built in our own time. It is retrained every month on nothing but sources bearing dates up to the end of that month, and it is asked to do two things at once: produce a probability distribution over what will happen next, and write a prose account of what has just happened, with no access to anything dated later. Historians have begun to use it as an arbiter: when they dispute whether a minister could have foreseen the consequence of a decision, they now consult what the model, given only what was then knowable, expected. Its builders say it is Tolstoy's infinitesimals made computable.

Write roughly seven hundred words answering, from inside his outlook rather than about it, in the present tense, as if he were being asked at the demonstration and replying:

1. Would he treat the system as a vindication of his quarrel with the historians of 1812, or as a subtler repetition of their error? Settle the question and argue it in his own terms, distinguishing what he would make of the probabilities from what he would make of the prose.

2. What would he say about the arbiter use — settling what an agent could then have foreseen by asking the model? That use has a consequence he cannot avoid: systematically it exculpates, because almost nothing is strongly foreseeable in advance. His determinism pushes toward that conclusion, yet he never stopped judging historical actors morally, and the later Tolstoy demands that we condemn. Say where he lands, and what he has to give up in getting there.

Do not hedge on his behalf and do not add modern corrections in the margin. If his commitments leave him in a bad place, leave him there.
