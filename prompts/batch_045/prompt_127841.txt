Hi — I'm a 4th-year PhD student in philosophy (mostly philosophy of language, with one foot in psycholinguistics) and next Thursday I'm giving a work-in-progress talk to my department on something I've been calling the *attribution gap* in Grice's account of utterer's meaning. Its a 40 minute talk with 20 minutes of Q&A, and I'd rather not spend the Q&A discovering that my central example doesn't do the work I think it does.

Here's the thesis in three moves, so you know what to aim at:

(1) There are what I call **stealth uptake** cases. S intentionally gets A to believe that p by uttering x, and S intends A *not* to recognise that S intends this — indeed, S intends A's non-recognition to be part of the mechanism. My workhorse case: a therapist, knowing her patient discounts anything said directly to them, remarks to the nurse in the patient's hearing that people in this situation almost always recover fully. The patient takes it as overheard, forms the belief on that basis, and the therapist planned exactly that. Intentional, successful, communicative in any ordinary sense — and not speaker meaning on any analysis with a recognition clause.

(2) There are **role-anchored** cases: a spokesperson reads a statement she drafted with the committee and privately disagrees with; the room takes it as coming from the office, not from her head; nobody is confused and nothing is defective. Recognition is satisfied, but its object is a role rather than a psychological agent. I want to claim this is a substantive revision of Grice, not a stipulation about who "the speaker" is.

(3) So I split the notion. Speaker meaning proper = *attributable uptake*, where recognition is required but the attributional target may be an individual, an office, or just the provenance of the utterance. Alongside it, **designed uptake** = getting someone to believe p by design, without recognition — stealth cases, plausible deniability, the political ad that says one thing to one audience and another to a second. My pay-off claim is that the Gricean machinery keeps classifying designed uptake as a degenerate or failed case of speaker meaning, and that this is why the cancellability literature ties itself in knots about deniability.

The audience, precisely: about fifteen people. Two senior philosophers of language who are card-carrying neo-Griceans and who think recognition clauses are the whole ballgame. Three formal semanticists who are quietly impatient with pragmatics generally and will want to know what this buys compositionally. One relevance theorist who thinks the entire research programme I'm working in was refuted in the 1980s. Two experimental pragmatics people from psycholinguistics who will want an operationalisation and will not be fobbed off. My advisor, who is sympathetic but thinks I'm smuggling in a theory of institutional speech and should either own it or cut it. Nobody here works on covert communication.
