I convene a final-year seminar in practical ethics, and for the session on how one ought to choose between causes — whether to devote a career, or a grant budget, to this rather than that — I want the students to build the specification before I show them a single existing framework. So please write me the desiderata. Fix the purpose first, and say what you are fixing it as: I have in mind a small charitable foundation with a fixed annual budget, a ten-year spending horizon, no capacity to run experiments or to wait for feedback before committing money, and a board of trustees who disagree with one another about population ethics, about whether future benefits may be discounted, and about how much weight to give to risks of civilisational collapse. Write the list of requirements any framework for prioritising causes would have to meet to count as adequate for that decision-maker: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results from decision theory or economics it must not contradict — with the concrete case or consideration behind each requirement, since I want the criteria motivated by examples rather than by slogans.

Here are the candidates I have been circling in my own notes, which may or may not all be genuine desiderata; tell me which are and which are merely the prejudices of one camp. (i) That the framework score grants or actions at the margin rather than causes in the aggregate, because a problem can be enormous and badly neglected in total whilst the thousandth pound into it buys almost nothing, given limits on how fast an organisation or a field can absorb money. (ii) That it be invariant to how finely the cause space is carved: lumping sub-causes together or splitting one cause into several must not reorder the ranking, and a framework whose output improves when you redraw a cause's boundaries is being gamed rather than consulted. (iii) That its factors be genuinely independent, so that combining them — by multiplication or otherwise — does not count the same consideration twice; I strongly suspect that "neglectedness" and "tractability" both smuggle in something about diminishing marginal returns. (iv) That it credit counterfactual impact honestly: funging, crowding out, and the difference between bringing an outcome about and merely occupying a role someone else would have filled. (v) That it assign value to buying information — pilots, research, capacity-building — and not only to intervening directly, which seems to require handling option value and the value of resolving the board's own disagreements. (vi) That it remain usable under moral uncertainty and cluelessness, neither demanding spurious precision about long-run effects nor collapsing into a refusal to rank anything at all. (vii) That it cope with interaction between causes, complementarities as well as substitutability, so that two middling causes pursued together can beat the top-ranked cause pursued alone. (viii) That it be legible and adoptable by trustees who are not expected-value maximisers, and robust to their attempts to steer it towards causes they antecedently favour. Mark each as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and add any requirement I have missed that you think belongs on the list.

The part I most want, though, is the diagnosis of the list itself. Flag any subset of the desiderata you think cannot be jointly satisfied — in particular, say whether carving-invariance, sensitivity to interaction between causes, and marginal-unit scoring can be had together at all in a framework that outputs a single ranking, and whether the demand for a complete ordering of causes is even compatible with taking moral uncertainty seriously rather than merely averaging over it. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate frameworks, and why. Finish with a short scorecard, a few lines each and no more: the familiar three-factor multiplicative framework that scores causes by how important, how tractable and how neglected they are; straightforward expected cost-effectiveness estimation with explicit probabilities; and a portfolio or "hits-based" approach that spreads bets across many causes. I shall use the scorecard only to provoke discussion, so keep the weight on the criteria.
