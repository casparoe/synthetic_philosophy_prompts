I have been reading about the ethics of memory lately — Margalit's *Ethics of Memory*, and Rieff's *Triumph of the Therapeutic* — and I keep running into a thing that neither of them seems to describe, though both circle it. I would like to know whether it has a name, and if it does not, to have it named and carved precisely enough that I can tell where it stops.

Three instances, in ascending order of how much they bother me.

In the village my grandmother came from there is an annual commemoration for the Jews deported from it in 1942. The names are read aloud. For roughly fifteen years the reading has not been read by anyone present: it is a tape made in 1987 by two survivors, played over speakers. Attendance is stable, people stand, nobody objects, and afterwards there is a strong and apparently sincere sense in the village that the matter has been taken care of for another year. No one in the square can pronounce the names. My impression — and this is what I want examined — is that the tape is the *reason* no one needs to be able to.

At a company I worked for, a colleague died. Within a week there was a bench with a plaque, a recurring calendar invitation bearing her name, and a channel that posts a photograph on the anniversary. Four years later nobody in the office had worked with her. The remembering was continuous, punctual, and institutional, and it seemed to me to have entirely replaced whatever it was installed to preserve: people who had known her had stopped mentioning her, and behaved as if mentioning her would now be redundant.

On a social network I use, the profile of a man who died in 2019 still receives cheerful automated birthday greetings from the platform, and still receives them from a few dozen people who click the button the platform puts in front of them on that day. The greeting is addressed to no one in particular, and is triggered by a date rather than by a memory. Somebody is, in some sense, being remembered every year by a mechanism and by a crowd that has forgotten him.

Here is the feature I am trying to isolate. Nothing is hidden or suppressed: the record is more complete and more reliably retrieved than any human remembering could manage. And that is exactly the problem. The availability of the record is what licenses everyone to stop. The persistence of the memory produces, in the people around it, the experience of an obligation having been met and the feeling of release — while the remembering itself is no longer anybody's act and no longer the object of anybody's care. Care appears to have migrated into something incapable of it, and the result is not amnesia; it is something that from the outside looks like discharged duty and from the inside feels like being let off.

I can see three nearby things it is *not*, and I would like the answer to say whether I have got these right and what else is nearby.

It is not forgetting by suppression or cover-up, since nothing is concealed and the material is maximally accessible. It is not ordinary cognitive outsourcing either: a diary or a shopping list is something I consult, and consulting it is part of my own remembering, whereas here the record is not consulted by anyone in particular and its operation seems to *pre-empt* rather than support the act. And it is not, as far as I can tell, the ordinary and unobjectionable way a family's or a people's memory is spread across many heads, so that no single person holds all of it — because in that case there is still, somewhere, somebody who cares about each part and would feel its loss; here there need not be.

Three further questions, which a bare label would not settle for me.

Does it matter whether the arrangement originates in a genuine act of care whose reach is later extended (the 1987 tape) or in a system that produces commemoration with no originating act at all (the algorithmic birthday)? I suspect these are not one phenomenon at two temperatures but two different things, and I want to know which of them deserves the name.

Where does this sit with respect to the duty to remember? Is the duty here being discharged by a substitute, or quietly extinguished while continuing to produce the moral phenomenology of being discharged — and if the latter, is that worse, from the point of view of someone who thinks the duty is real, than straightforward forgetting would be? Symmetrically: Rieff described a culture that wants to be released from what it inherits. Is this what that release looks like when it has been *engineered* rather than argued for — forgetting achieved not by convincing anyone that the past should stop binding, but by building something that binds in their place?

And is there a version of this that is a virtue rather than a failure? A society that builds an archive of an old grievance precisely so that its members can stop reenacting it looks, on the description I have given, structurally identical to the village with the tape. If those are different, I want to know which feature separates them: the presence of a decision, the preservation of the capacity to remember even where it is not exercised, the identity of who is being released, or something else.

Please do not answer with a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I have a library and what I lack is the distinction. Argue for the nearest established concepts if there are any, say exactly how the phenomenon I have described differs from each and which fits best; if none fits, say so plainly and coin a term, define it in a sentence, and give me at least two boundary cases — one just inside and one just outside — so I can test the definition against further instances I meet.

End your reply with two lines and nothing after them: on the first, a one-sentence definition of the term you propose; on the second, the term itself in the form \answer{...}, whether you have found it or made it up.
