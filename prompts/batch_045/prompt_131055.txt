I'm a backend infrastructure engineer with no formal philosophy training — my only real route into these questions has been years of arguments about whether production should be mutable, blast-radius limits, and how much a platform team should be able to lock down what product teams can deploy later — and I got comprehensively nerd-sniped at a dinner party last week by a friend who works in policy-tech and is very enthusiastic about what he calls "constitution as code." His position, as I understood it: a constitution is essentially a Ulysses contract, a people tying itself to the mast, so the whole interesting design problem is better rope — supermajority requirements, provisions that cannot be amended at all, independent central banks, debt brakes, statutes that sunset-proof themselves, guardian institutions with wax in their ears. I pushed back, but badly. My instinct was that the self-binding analogy is doing an enormous amount of unexamined work, that each cleverer piece of rope seems to regenerate the original problem one level up, and that the people designing the rope always seem to be the ones who don't have to be tied up by it — but I couldn't make any of that land, and I'd rather settle the argument by making fun of it than by losing it again.

So: write me a satirical piece that does the job I failed to do. Form and voice: the launch blog post for a fictional infrastructure company announcing its flagship product — a self-binding toolkit for states, something like "MAST: the Managed Autonomy & Self-Binding Toolkit," pitched as precommitment-as-a-service for republics. It should read like a real, competent, slightly breathless infra/product launch: headline, byline, a short "the problem" section written in the language of incident postmortems, a pricing tier table, an FAQ, and at least one sidebar case study. Aim for roughly 800–1,000 words. The target isn't my friend personally; it's the practice of treating constitutional precommitment as an engineering problem with an engineering solution, and the style of discourse that talks about it.

The jokes have to come from getting the philosophy right rather than from inventing silliness, so please work the following material in as product features, limitations, and fine print, without ever stating any objection in so many words: (1) the product needs to tell which future preferences are the bound party's considered will and which are siren-song, and its heuristic turns out to be circular — any change of mind is by definition the sirens, which makes the binding unfalsifiable and unappealable; (2) whoever can refuse the bound party's later, genuinely changed request is the real sovereign, so the flagship tier ships an independent Guardian Council operating under a "Do Not Hear Petitions" service level, and the piece should quietly let the reader notice that the guardians, not the people, now hold the power; (3) in thirty years most of the people being bound are not the people who tied the knot, which the company handles with a Continuity Rider that stipulates that the polity is the same agent across total population turnover; (4) unforeseen circumstances are handled by a Changed-Circumstances Oracle that is itself bound and may only consult evidence available at signing; (5) each tier's guardians need binding of their own, solved in the enterprise tier by recursive entrenchment sold as a feature and terminating only in a limit the reader can see is absurd. The reductio should be legible without being announced: a fully self-bound polity has no decisions left to make, and the mast has quietly become a coffin. The case-study sidebar should make it plain that adoption is selective — everyone buys rope on the way out of power. Stay deadpan throughout, no winking, no footnotes, no last-paragraph turn into a sincere civics lesson; it should be funny to someone who knows the Ulysses-contract and constitutional-precommitment literature and still readable to someone who doesn't.
