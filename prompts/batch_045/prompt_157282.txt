At our end-of-term department drinks last week two of my colleagues got into a long argument about saving the many versus saving the one, and I refereed it badly — I teach the seminar where the old Taurek paper comes up, so I thought I knew where the pressure points were, but they found ones I hadn't thought through and I've been turning them over since. The case they started from was the plain one: through no fault of yours, six strangers are in peril; you can either rescue Ada or rescue the other five; everyone is otherwise on a par, all strangers, no special duties, no side effects.

Owen opened with the obvious: save the five, because five deaths is the worse outcome, and someone who can prevent more of that kind of thing and doesn't is behaving badly. Ruth said: worse for whom? Not for Ada, since her death is as bad for her as each of the others' death is for each of them, and not for any of the five, each of whom loses exactly what he would have lost in the other outcome. So the extra badness doesn't land on anyone, and "five deaths is worse" hasn't earned its keep. Owen said outcomes can be better or worse without there being a single person on whom the difference lands; there's just more of what we have reason to prevent. Ruth said that is precisely the move she rejects — adding losses that belong to different subjects into one quantity nobody experiences — and that if you can't do that, then no one's claim outweighs anyone else's, so the thing to do is give each of the six an equal chance of rescue. Owen said that just lets the numbers in the back door, since the five now collectively get a five-in-six shot, and in expectation your procedure saves about 4.3 lives rather than 5, so you're choosing a lottery that foreseeably leaves more people dead than the option you refused, purely to avoid arithmetic. Ruth said chances are how you respect several equal and uncombinable claims at once; that they happen to be more numerous on one side isn't why they get weight. Owen then tried a variant: options are "save Ada" or "save Ada and Bea". Ruth happily saves both, and points out she does it for free, without counting anyone. Owen called that the tell: she saves the many for sure when the groups overlap and starts flipping coins the moment they don't, and the only difference is whether Ada was going to be rescued anyway — if numbers really are irrelevant, why should that flip her? Ruth said it's conflict, not cardinality, that forces a lottery: in the nested case no claims are in conflict, in the disjoint case they are.

Then Owen's last move, which is the one I can't shake. Change the stakes: the five would each lose a leg, Ada would die. Grant that you can't sum across people; still, you do compare, since Ada's loss is worse for her than a leg is for each of the others. If you can compare sizes but not add them, the single largest individual loss wins outright, so you save Ada — and you'd save her against a million leg-losses too. But if you refuse to compare sizes across people at all, your equal-chances rule loses its motivation, and you'd hand a dying man a one-in-a-million shot against a million people with headaches. Ruth said maybe she takes the second horn and the headaches really do get their share, and maybe the whole "how bad is it for them" framing is what needs reworking, and then we got kicked out of the pub. I've written up my own thoughts on it but I don't trust them, and I'd rather have an outside verdict.
