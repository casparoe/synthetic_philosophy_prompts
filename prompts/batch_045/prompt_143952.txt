So I've been using one of the big LLMs pretty much daily for a year and a half — working through papers I wouldn't otherwise get through, drafting code review comments, cleaning up prose — and lately I've noticed something I can't quite put my finger on. It's not that I've gotten worse at the things I use it for, at least not in any way I can demonstrate. What's changed is narrower: I'm worse at noticing when it's *subtly* wrong. Badly wrong output I still catch immediately, but the plausible, slightly-off thing now sails right past me in a way it didn't before. A friend of mine who learned the same material the slow way first and only then started delegating says she has the exact opposite experience — the tool extended what she could do and her grip on it didn't loosen at all. We were arguing about this the other night and got nowhere, because "do you still understand this?" turns out to be a garbage question: you can understand something and not be able to reconstruct it on a whiteboard, and you can reconstruct it and not understand it. I'm also going to have to help write a policy about tool use for our first-year lab course, so I'd like to have something better than vibes to say.

What I want is a spec, not another take. Give me the requirements a theory of understanding would have to meet to be fit for one particular job: grading a *practice* — some specific way of delegating a cognitive task to a tool, by some specific person at some stage of learning — as degrading, preserving, or extending that person's understanding. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are just aesthetic preferences of one camp. Here are the cases I already know I want handled, though I assume you'll think of ones I'm missing: the person who can reliably check and repair an answer they could never have produced themselves; the competence that exists only inside the loop with the tool and evaporates when you take it away (navigating with the map app vs. without it); the case where offloading the low-level stuff is what *creates* a new, higher-level understanding rather than replacing one; the learned-then-delegated vs. never-learned asymmetry between me and my friend; my near-miss case, where whatever degraded isn't a stock of true beliefs or a production ability but the capacity to notice that something is off; the mechanic who can fix it but can't explain why and the theorist who can explain but can't fix it; and the case where nobody on a team individually understands the system but the team as a whole does. I also want the distinctions a theory isn't allowed to collapse — understanding vs. mere reliable deference, ability vs. access to the ability under pressure, the person's understanding vs. the joint person-plus-tool performance, and which of the two we should even be grading — plus a requirement about measurability, since you obviously can't test for erosion by removing the tool (the counterfactual is confounded, and testing changes the thing you're testing), and self-report is worthless here.

Two more things I especially want. First, flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied — my hunch is that "understanding has to show up in what the person can do unaided" and "don't count every act of scaffolding as degradation" are quietly at war, and if that's right then the list itself is the interesting result and I want to see the tension made explicit rather than papered over. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidates out, and why. You can skip citations and I don't need a literature review, but if it's quick you can score the obvious families at the very end — ability/manipulation accounts, explanation- or knowledge-based accounts, virtue-theoretic ones — a few lines each; the criteria are the part I actually care about.
