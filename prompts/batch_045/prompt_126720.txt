I'm part of a small postgraduate reading group that has been working its way through the literature on anthropic reasoning, and last week we spent an entire session on the doomsday argument. I came away convinced by the bare inference but entirely unsure what to do with the conviction, and I'd be grateful for your help in finding where I've gone wrong.

Here is how I've been putting it to myself. Take the usual back-of-an-envelope figure — somewhere around a hundred thousand million human beings have lived before us — and treat my own birth rank as a random draw from the set of all humans who will ever live. On that assumption, the hypothesis that we are somewhere near the middle of the human story looks overwhelmingly more likely, given my birth rank alone, than the hypothesis that we are among the first tiny fraction of an enormous future population. So the argument really does seem to force the conclusion that the total number of humans who will ever exist is not much larger than the number who have already existed. The trouble begins with what follows. The rest of the group immediately began talking as though we had been handed a dated prediction of extinction within a few centuries, and argued about which risk — asteroids, engineered pathogens, nuclear war — the argument must be pointing at. But it can't be pointing at any risk at all, can it? It reaches its verdict purely from my position in the sequence, which is a fixed fact about me that nothing I do can alter. Given that, it seems to me that however seriously one takes it, it cannot change what we ought to do; in particular it cannot give us any further reason to spend money on mitigating existential risk, since the catastrophe it warns of would be exactly as likely however much we spent. The only way I can see to make it decision-relevant is what I've started calling the quietist reading: what the argument really supports is not a bang but a whimper — a very long future inhabited by a very small human population — and if that is right, then policies that increase the number of people in the far future become precisely what the argument tells us to avoid. But surely it would be preposterous for a piece of reasoning about my birth rank to tell us to have fewer children, so I assume the quietist reading must be defective too.

My friend takes the difficulty in the opposite direction and says it is a reason to reject the argument outright: we should, she says, adopt the self-indication assumption rather than the self-sampling one, on the grounds that a world containing more observers is intrinsically more likely to contain you, and once that adjustment is made the doomsday argument's effect is cancelled entirely and we can go back to estimating extinction risk in the ordinary way, from evidence about asteroids and the like. That strikes me as suspiciously tidy. So my questions are these. Is it correct that the argument's verdict is decision-irrelevant because it is evidence about my birth rank rather than about anything within our causal reach, and if not, by what mechanism is it supposed to bear on action? Is the quietist reading merely confused, or does it capture something the rest of the group is missing? And is my friend right that the self-indication assumption removes the argument's effect altogether, or does it only blunt it, and does it carry costs of its own? Please write your answer in British English.
