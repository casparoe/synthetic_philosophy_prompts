This is going to sound like nothing, but it's become weirdly important to me: I moved into a forty-odd person housing co-op last autumn, got put on the rota committee more or less by accident, and we've spent the last three months arguing about one thing. The house runs entirely on unwritten rules — the washing-up rota, quiet after eleven, whoever's last downstairs locks the back door, the communal milk — and it mostly works. Then three new members moved in, kept breaking things, and one of them said, completely reasonably, that nobody had ever actually told her any of it. So now there's a vote in a fortnight on whether to write everything into a handbook, and half of us think that writing it down is precisely what will kill the thing that works.

I did some philosophy years ago and the argument in the house has turned into two positions that keep missing each other. One is the Lewisian / convention-first side: the unwritten patterns are solutions to recurring coordination problems, everyone conforms, everyone expects everyone else to, everyone would rather conform given the others do, and there's some alternative pattern that would have served just as well — that's the rough shape of it as I remember, so don't hold me to the exact formulation. The other is the norms-first / normativist side: a real social norm needs normative expectations (people think you ought to comply, and think others think so) plus some readiness to sanction, and coordination equilibria can't give you that; conventions are at best a subclass of norms, not the explanation of them.

What I want is the document an adversarial collaboration between those two would produce, and I want it to be about my house, not about the literature in general. Please don't give me a reading list or citations — I've got no library access out here.

- A numbered agreed-claims section: things both sides can actually sign, and non-vacuous ones, the kind someone could deny. Not "people sometimes follow rules."
- A disagreements table, one row per disagreement, with each row phrased twice — once in the formulation the convention-first person would accept for their own view, once in the formulation the norms-first person would accept for theirs — and a marker on each row for whether you think it's a real disagreement or a fight about words. If any of it is verbal, say which part and why.
- A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, an argument or case both sides agree bears on it, and each side's prediction about it written down in advance.

Four cases I want them to have to handle, because these are the ones the house actually argued about:

1. The laminated sheet. We now have a written rota on the kitchen wall. Nobody remembers agreeing to it, nobody has ever read it properly, and everyone complies. Is that a convention, a norm, both, or neither — and is it the same object as the unwritten thing it replaced, or a different one?
2. The phantom convention. Everyone believes quiet hours were voted in in 2009. I checked the minutes and they never were; the pattern has just been stable for years. Call this a 'phantom convention': a regularity sustained by a widely believed but false story about its origin. Does the false origin story matter to what the thing is?
3. The milk. Whoever buys the communal milk is slightly out of pocket and slightly put out. Everyone agrees someone has to do it and that it'd be unfair if it were always the same person. This isn't clean coordination — there's a small sucker payoff in it. Can the convention-first account handle it, or does it need fairness, or turn-taking, or something irreducibly normative?
4. Pluralistic ignorance. Suppose everyone privately resents the back-door rule and locks up only because they believe everyone else is keen on it, and they're all wrong about that; on top of which a newcomer has no expectations about any of it at all. Does the pattern still count as whatever it was, and does it still count as something she has a reason to go along with?

Then a short section where each side says what would count as evidence against their own position — the thing that would actually make them give it up — and whether there's anything at all both agree would settle the dispute. If it's a survey of what people in the house actually expect, say what it would have to show for each side.

Finally, half a page max: if the collaboration had to make a recommendation to us about the handbook vote, what's the most both could sign off on together? I genuinely don't know which way to vote and the meeting is in two weeks. British English, please, and sorry if this is a lot — the house has been at it for months and I'm definately losing perspective on it.
