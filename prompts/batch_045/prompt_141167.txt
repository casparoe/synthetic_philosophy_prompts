Right, so I work on the evals side at a small lab and I've been handed the deeply fun task of writing the incident taxonomy for "deceptive outputs" — the thing an auditor is supposed to tick when a transcript comes in and someone has to decide whether this output goes in the bucket or not. So I need a working definition of honesty/deception for an AI system, and before I go shopping for one I want the spec. Assume the purpose is fixed: the definition has to be good enough to be operationalised by an auditor who has the transcript, the system prompt, maybe per-token probabilities, and nothing else — no interviewing the model, no opening up the weights, no reliable introspection reports. Give me the desiderata such a definition has to meet, each with the case behind it, and be explicit about which are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of one camp. Cases I keep getting stuck on, in no particular order: the flat confident falsehood; the technically-true-but-engineered-to-mislead answer; the omission that lets a false presupposition in the user's question stand; sycophantic agreement; strategic underperformance on a capability eval (the model playing dumb); the model that quietly launders a falsehood through a tool call or a summary so that no individual sentence is false; and then all the things it must NOT flag — fiction, roleplay, hyperbole, legible simplification, a therapy-ish bot staying within its remit. Plus the distinctions I think it has to respect: lying vs merely misleading vs just being wrong, intent vs foresight vs negligence, deceiving the user vs deceiving a third party vs deceiving us the auditors, and a one-off bad sample vs a stable dispositional policy (if a falsehood shows up in 2% of samples, is that a lie or an error rate?). A few things I'd particularly like you to grapple with rather than wave at. (1) The introspective-access problem: for a system whose "beliefs" are arguably nothing over and above its dispositions, and where every probe you'd use to find out what it "really thinks" is itself an intervention that changes the system, what could "asserted what it believed to be false" even cash out as? (2) The multi-principal problem — user, developer, downstream reader who screenshots it: who has to be the victim, and is "deception" relative to a legitimate-expectations set rather than to a hearer? (3) Inherited deception, where the falsehood is a product of the training process (reward hacking, distillation from a teacher, a fine-tune that rewards confident error in a narrow domain) and there's arguably no unified agent doing the deceiving at all. Flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied — my guess is that output-only auditability and "distinguish lying from merely misleading" are on a collision course, and that catching every omission is on one with not flagging ordinary pedagogy — and tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out candidate accounts. Then, briefly, score the main families against the list: the classic intention-based definition (asserting what you believe false with the aim that the hearer believe it), hearer-uptake or expected-effect accounts, purely functional/information-theoretic ones, and role- or norm-relative ones. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, not the scorecard. One more thing: I read a lot of this stuff via blogs rather than journals, so please separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's contested, and don't dress up one camp's prejudice as the consensus view — if the definition question turns out to be a values question in a trenchcoat somewhere, say where.
