I'm putting together a two-page prospectus for a one-year dissertation fellowship, and before I send it to my committee I want the framing sharpened, because right now I have a thesis and not yet a question. The topic is Machiavelli's treatment of fortuna and virtù. My half-formed idea is that the standard reading — virtù as a flexible, amoral competence that lets an actor seize whatever occasion fortune throws up — captures only the individual deployment of the vocabulary, and misses a second one. In the Discourses, republics appear to enjoy a durability that principalities lack, and I want to explore the claim that this is a matter of what might be called institutionalized virtù: a republic can harbor within itself a plurality of incompatible humors and modes of proceeding, and therefore stay in phase with the times as the times change, whereas a principality is hostage to whether one man's native way of acting happens to match his moment. On that reading Machiavelli's republicanism is partly an insurance policy against fortune rather than a freestanding doctrine about popular legitimacy, and the notorious remark that fortune governs about half of our actions gets a much less mystical sense than it usually receives: fortune would not be chance intruding on agency from outside, but the structural fact that any fixed style of action will eventually come out of phase with the world it was fitted to. My advisor's objection, which I think is the serious one, is that this collapses into the banal claim that institutions outlast individuals; I need the prospectus to distinguish the Machiavellian version from that generic one, or to admit that it cannot be distinguished.

What I want the proposal to do is this. First, state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, and split it into its parts: whether fortuna is a single notion across the two books or two notions sharing a name; whether virtù can intelligibly be predicated of a collective agent at all, or is irreducibly first-personal in a way that makes "institutionalized virtù" a category mistake; and whether the republican argument is best read as reducing a polity's exposure to fortune or as placing a different and better kind of bet. Second, say why the available positions leave the question open — the reading of virtù as mere effectiveness, the reading of fortuna as luck or as the residue of what agents cannot control, and the "occasion" reading on which fortune supplies only the matter while the agent supplies the form — and where each of them would predict the Discourses material should go. Third, lay out what I would actually do over twelve months and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives (a purely conceptual analysis of the terms; a textual-comparison reading that treats the two books as expressing a settled doctrine; or the intellectual-history approach that traces the fortune vocabulary back to its classical and humanist sources). Include what the possible conclusions are and which considerations — textual, but also internal-coherence and argumentative — would decide between them, and name in advance the findings that would count as the project failing: for instance, if the river-and-dams passage in chapter 25 and the claim that fortune favors the impetuous turn out to be unifiable only on a reading of fortune as an external force, or if the Discourses passages about institutional flexibility are about something too thin to connect to virtù in the first place.

Please keep it to proposal length and structure — a precise question, motivation, method with phases over the year, expected deliverables, and the failure conditions — plus, at the end, two or three sentences I can use directly in the "why this matters" paragraph, pitched for a committee that is not composed of Machiavelli specialists. Be honest about scope: if the three sub-questions are too much for a single year, say which one should be cut and what would be lost.
