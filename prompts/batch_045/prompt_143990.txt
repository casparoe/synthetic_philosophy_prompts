I am a professor of philosophy and, this year, the chair of my university's committee on conflicts of interest and research integrity. We adjudicate some fifty cases a year — most of them trivial, a few capable of costing us a campus licence, a researcher's safety, or a colleague's career — and we have been asked to replace our current guidance, which is little more than a gift register with a monetary threshold, with an actual protocol. The protocol has to serve our home campus and two campuses abroad, where the surrounding political economy is clientelist: permits, access to research sites, and official goodwill run through patrons and brokers; a gift is what acknowledges a relationship rather than what buys a favour; and refusing one can be a grave affront rather than a punctiliousness. Some colleagues hold that our home rules simply travel and that anything else is relativism dressed up as cultural sensitivity. Others hold that an impersonal-office ethic applied in such a setting makes our research impossible and punishes local staff for the way their own society works. Both cannot be right, and "when in Rome" is not a procedure.

What I need is a decision procedure the committee can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the answer is simply no, and the points at which it must stop and hand the decision to a person because what remains is a question of values rather than of fact. It has to work in about twenty minutes, over email, by academics with no legal training, producing one recorded paragraph of reasoning that our general counsel's office can sign off on asynchronously within five days; it has to be usable by a colleague who has never read a line of political philosophy; and it has to say what to do when the relevant fact cannot be had, since we usually cannot know why someone gave something.

The crude tests we have tried do not work, and I would like you to say why before replacing them. Publicity — would you mind this being known? — misfires where gift-giving is public and expected. A value threshold misfires in both directions: a cheap gift can be the visible token of a clientelist relationship, while an expensive one, a funded conference or a studentship, can be entirely at arm's length. My suspicion is that what does the work is neither size nor secrecy but whether the transfer redirects a practice away from the end that makes the office worth having — admissions is for admitting those qualified to study, peer review for assessing the quality of work, procurement for getting the building built. If that is right, the procedure should turn on something like whether the giver is acquiring a claim on a good the officeholder has no right to dispose of, and whether the transfer would survive that officeholder's leaving office tomorrow; if it is not right, say so and build it on something better. It should also return more than yes and no: what is prohibited, what is permitted, what is permitted only on the record, what our rules permit but should not — and, the category I care about most, what is not a corruption question at all but a question of justice or of whether our institution should be operating there as it does, and is therefore nothing this committee has authority to decide.

Apply it to three live cases. First: an official who must renew the licence of our campus abroad mentions that his son has applied to the Master's programme whose admissions I chair, and asks me to look at the file. Second: six months after graduating with a First, a former student's family sends me a handwoven rug worth roughly two weeks of local wages, against a rule forbidding gifts above a nominal value. Third: a publisher whose introductory textbook we are deciding whether to require, at the students' expense, offers to fund a three-day curriculum retreat for my department at a coastal hotel. I expect the procedure to give me an answer in the third case that I will not enjoy; give it anyway. Finally, tell me honestly where a procedure of this kind will misfire: which innocent arrangements it will flag, which real corruptions it will miss because they present as hospitality or gratitude, and which questions it is not even able to ask.
