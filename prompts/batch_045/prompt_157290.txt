I'm retired now — used to be an experimental physical chemist — and I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group, which is how I ended up reading a chapter in a pop-sci book (one of those big-name trade paperbacks, I won't name it) about why time has a direction. The chapter ends with a summary that I've typed out below. I'd like to know how much of it I can trust, because I want to bring it up at the next meeting and I'd rather not repeat something silly.

"Time has a direction because the universe began in a state of extraordinarily low entropy and has been relaxing ever since. Entropy — disorder, roughly — is the only thing in basic physics that treats the two time directions differently: the underlying laws are time-symmetric, so the arrow can't come from the laws and has to come from the boundary conditions. And cosmology hands us the boundary condition for free, since the early universe was smooth and uniform, which is just what low entropy means. Once you've got that, everything else is bookkeeping: entropy increases toward the future, memories and records pile up toward the past, causes come before effects. Boltzmann's critics pointed out that a system in a low-entropy state ought to have entropy rising in both directions away from that state, and Boltzmann's answer was that this is right and we simply live on one slope of a vast fluctuation — which is why we see an arrow at all. One consequence people find spooky but that follows straight from this: if the expansion ever turns around, entropy will run backwards and the arrow will flip, so the inhabitants of a recollapsing universe would remember what we call the future. And since entropy is doing all the work, the arrow is local too: wherever entropy is going down — inside a growing crystal, inside a living cell — effects precede their causes and the fixed, knowable end is the one we'd call the past."

My own guess is that "disorder" is the sloppy bit and that the bit about cells running backwards can't be right, but I can't say why, and the rest sounds plausible enough that I cant see where the seams are. Could you go through it more or less sentence by sentence — what a philosopher of phsyics would happily endorse, what they'd wince at as a simplification, and what they'd say is just wrong? And do those last two corollaries, the flip on recollapse and the local reversal, actually follow from the parts that are right, or are they extras bolted on?
