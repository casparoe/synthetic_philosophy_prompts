You are a senior political philosopher with a working knowledge of migration law and of the practical constraints under which resettlement agencies operate. Take the following seriously and think it through carefully before you write: I would rather have a slow, exact specification than a fluent survey.

I work in the policy unit of a state resettlement agency, and we are rewriting our internal guidance. Two questions keep getting run together in our drafts, and I want them prised apart before we go further: first, who counts — which displaced people are in the class that generates duties at all, and on what basis, given that the 1951 Convention definition restricts the triggering harm to a well-founded fear of persecution on the grounds of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion, while the 1967 Protocol stripped out its date and geographic limits and later regional instruments, such as the 1969 OAU Convention and the 1984 Cartagena Declaration, extended the trigger to events seriously disturbing public order. Second, who owes what — which states are under a duty to admit or otherwise provide for which people, and how much. My strong suspicion is that most of the arguments in our office are really arguments about the second question wearing the vocabulary of the first, and I want the specification written so that this move is visible.

So: give me the desiderata. Not a survey of views, and not a recommendation of one — a specification of what an adequate account of each question would have to deliver, given the purpose I am about to fix, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a judgement about which requirements are non-negotiable for that purpose and which are the preferences of a particular camp dressed up as constraints. The purpose is operational: the account has to be usable by a caseworker in an interview with an applicant, defensible by a minister to a sceptical legislature and to other states, and actionable under partial compliance, when most states are doing much less than their share. Adequacy here means adequacy for that, not adequacy as an ideal theory of global justice.

Before writing, spend two paragraphs brainstorming: list the candidate desiderata that occur to you, including the ones you are inclined to reject, and say which you are cutting because they are disguised conclusions rather than constraints — demands that already contain the answer they are supposed to test. Then write the specification itself.

Here are the cases I need any account to handle, and I have deliberately included some that are not in the standard repertoire. Someone fleeing a non-state actor, such as a cartel or a militia, where the state of origin is willing but genuinely unable to protect her — and the mirror case, where the state is able but unwilling. A household from a low-lying atoll whose land and water have slowly become unusable over decades through sea-level rise and salinisation, with no persecutor, no datable event, and no agent whose act caused the departure. A dissident denationalised by decree while abroad, who is now outside any country of nationality. A person who leaves before the threatened harm has materialised, where the only evidence of the threat is held by the threatening authority and the adjudicator must decide on the record available at the time of decision, not with hindsight. A flow with composite causation — an intervention, arms sales to the regime, sanctions, a neighbour's proxy forces, cumulative emissions — where no single contributor's causal share can be computed. A rich state that closes its land routes and dismantles its reception system, so that it can truthfully report no capacity to receive. A state that funds, equips and politically guarantees the camps of a poorer neighbouring state, or that offshores its processing there. A state that transfers arrivals onward to a third country where the original risk resumes by a different route. A post-conflict state that wants its skilled displaced citizens back and objects to their selective resettlement elsewhere — the case where admission is a benefit someone else wants, not a burden being shifted. A protracted situation, fifteen years in, where return has stopped being foreseeable. And the standing structural fact that the overwhelming majority of the world's displaced people are hosted by states adjacent to the conflict, most of them low-income.

For each case, say what it is doing: which distinction it is probing, and what an account must be able to say about it to count as having handled it rather than merely taken a position on it. I care especially about the distinctions people collapse: between having the status and having a duty-bearer; between owing admission to this individual, such that she can complain if refused, and owing a share of a global burden that any complying allocation discharges; between owing admission and owing to bring about that someone is admitted, which is what the outsourcing cases turn on; between the stringency of a duty and the identity of its bearer; and between a duty's existence and its temporal shape, including what happens to temporary protection when return ceases to be foreseeable.

On the constraint side, I want you to be explicit about what counts as a genuine desideratum here and what does not. Administrability is, for my purpose, non-negotiable: an account must be decidable under asymmetric evidence, must not require an adjudicator to resolve counterfactuals that no available evidence bears on, and must not require facts that nobody can obtain, such as precise causal shares in a composite flow. Robustness to strategic behaviour by states is also, I think, non-negotiable: the account must not reward a state for making itself inaccessible, for degrading its own capacity, or for acting first, and it must say what a duty becomes when others default rather than either releasing everyone or piling everything onto the compliers. Tell me if I am wrong to put those in the non-negotiable class, and tell me what you would demote: I have in mind candidate requirements such as that duties must track causal or historical responsibility for the displacement, that they must respect a state's claimed right to determine its own membership, that they must be impartial between claimants with no tie to the admitting state, that they must never require admission without consent, that they must be neutral with respect to the claimant's religion or ethnicity, and that they must not make admission conditional on anything about the claimant's usefulness. Some of those look to me like constraints on an adequate theory and some look like one camp's conclusion inserted into the list of tests; I want your ruling on each, with the reason.

Then the part I most want. Find the subsets of desiderata that I suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say so as a result rather than as a lament. My own candidate is this: require that every person with the status have some state who owes them admission, require that duty track causal responsibility for the displacement, and require that a state with no causal involvement bear no duty — and then consider the case in which the only causally responsible agent is the persecuting authority itself. Either the persecutor owes asylum, or an uninvolved state owes it, or the duty has no bearer. I take that to be unsatisfiable, and I want you to test it, to give me the other jointly unsatisfiable subsets you can find, and, where a subset is unsatisfiable, to tell me which member is least costly to give up for my purpose and why. I also want the single requirement that does the most exclusionary work — the one that knocks out the largest number of the accounts people actually defend, ideally stated so tightly that it reads as a theorem about what any account satisfying it must concede.

Only after that, and briefly — a paragraph or two, no more — run the scorecard over the main families of account you would expect to be offered: capacity-based, causal or historical-responsibility-based, proximity or first-asylum, associative or special-ties, fair-share or global-distribution, consent or self-determination based, and market or tradable-quota based. I want just enough to see which families survive the list and where each fails, because I will do the detailed scoring with my colleagues.

Two constraints on your answer. I have no library access today and I do not want citations, authorities, or reading suggestions: argue from the cases and from what the requirements are for, and if some legal or empirical detail matters, flag it as something to check rather than building on it. And I want the whole thing in continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, no numbered lists. Roughly fourteen hundred words, or a little more if the unsatisfiability results need the room.
