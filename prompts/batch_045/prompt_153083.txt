I'm in year four of a philosophy-of-physics PhD and I'm putting together the reading-group session on the hole argument — the modern Earman–Stachel–Norton version, not Einstein's 1913 one — and I keep failing to explain to my friends why anyone ever thought this was a problem about determinism rather than just a long-winded way of saying that diffeomorphic models represent the same world. My housemate does numerical relativity and says it's obvious the drag-along is gauge and the whole thing dissolves into Leibniz equivalence, and I genuinely can't tell whether she's right or whether she's smuggling in the conclusion. So instead of writing another handout nobody will read, I want a short story I can hand out at the start of the session — around 1300 to 1800 words, in American English, and it has to be an actual story, with characters and stakes, not two people in an office explaining differential geometry at each other.

Here's the setting I've been daydreaming about, and please build on it or change whatever you like. Take a near-future world where the metaphysics has been made administrative: there's a bureau (call it whatever you want) that keeps the ledger of spacetime points, each of which is a parcel with a permanent identifying number and a deed, because in this world everybody was taught that points are individuals with their own identity and the bureau's job is to say which point is which. The routine operation at the center of the story is the drag: a smooth re-identification of every event inside some bounded region while everything outside the hole stays exactly as it was. Engineers use it to reconcile two survey atlases of the same universe, and for as long as anyone remembers the official line has been that a drag is a no-op, the deeds are bookkeeping, two atlases related by a drag are one world and not two. Then the inciting incident: some new instrument starts registering a faint residual signal at hole boundaries — as if the manifold had a slight stiffness, as if the dynamics weren't quite invariant under the drag after all and the operation left a trace. Your protagonist, I think, should be someone who performs drags for a living and has always been told they do nothing.

What I want the fiction to actually accomplish is this. First, before the anomaly, make both horns of the argument land as genuinely uncomfortable: if points really do have their own identity, then the laws plus the complete state of the universe outside the hole cannot tell you which distribution of the fields inside the hole is the actual one — the same past is compatible with indefinitely many futures — and separately, no instrument anyone could build could ever tell you which one you're in. I want a reader to feel why that was ever treated as a problem and not as a trick question. Then, after the anomaly, show what breaks: determinism comes back, points turn out to have observable identities, differences that used to be merely haecceitistic become measurable, and the usual Leibniz-equivalence move loses whatever was motivating it. That should feel like a relief and also like a loss. Then give me the turn — and give it to a character who is as smart as everyone else, not a strawman — where someone argues that the stiffness isn't a broken symmetry at all but a piece of structure the models left out, a background field, and that once you put it back in the models the diffeomorphisms are symmetries again and the whole argument reappears one level up. The upshot I'm after is that spotting surplus structure takes a physical ground and not a mathematical one, and that the hole argument is a template rather than a verdict. Please don't close with a moral; end on whatever residue is left over, even if it's just your protagonist filing something they can't classify.

A few constraints, sorry to be annoying. Keep the physics honest: the drag is a smooth bijection, both the dragged and the undragged model satisfy the same field equations, and that's precisely why the laws can't adjudicate between them, and the failure is a failure of determinism for the substantivalist rather than for the relationist. Keep Leibniz equivalence, sophisticated substantivalism (permutation symmetry without primitive identity), and relationism as three genuinely different things, even if the characters only gesture at them. No info-dump monologues, and no real names or footnoted references or anything that looks like a citation, since I'm handing this out to people who won't have read the literature — it should be readable for a sharp undergrad who knows no GR and still not make anyone who knows the debate wince. I can't keep the responses strait in my head, so if there's a wierd place where the story wants to go somewhere I haven't thought of, go there rather than forcing my outline.
