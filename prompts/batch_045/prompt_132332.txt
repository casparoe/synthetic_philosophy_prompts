I'm writing a novel and I've painted myself into a corner, so please humour me.

Near the end there's a courtroom scene. A young barrister is being sued by her old teacher over a fee, under a contract that says she only pays once she's won her first case — and the case in front of the court *is* that case. (I stole the setup from an old Greek story about Protagoras and his pupil, which I afterwards found out is a genuinely ancient puzzle.) So whichever way the judge goes, the contract looks like it's both satisfied and not satisfied. My expert witness — an elderly logician, meant to be the smartest person in the room — testifies that the contract really is both binding and not binding, that this is a true contradiction, and that there's a respectable philosophical position, dialetheism, that lets him say so.

I tried to check it and found an essay online (a substack thing; the guy seemed to know his stuff) which put it more or less like this:

"Dialetheism is the view that at least some contradictions are true — that for some A, both A and not-A hold. It's motivated mainly by the liar paradox: there's no way to give 'this sentence is false' a truth value without running into a contradiction, so the dialetheist bites the bullet and says it's both true and false, and the paradox dissolves. The logic that goes with the view is called paraconsistent logic, which is basically classical logic with the law of non-contradiction taken out: in classical logic a single contradiction lets you derive absolutely anything, the so-called principle of explosion, so the paraconsistent logician gets rid of it and a contradiction becomes a local mess instead of a global catastrophe. Graham Priest is the main defender, and people use 'dialetheism' and 'paraconsistent logic' more or less interchangeably. The consequences are more radical than that sounds, though. Once you admit one true contradiction you give up the right to call any belief irrational: you can always accept both a claim and its negation, and since nothing follows from a contradiction any more, logic has no way to adjudicate anything."

Then my own problem, because I've allready written him a different line in an earlier chapter: I'd always assumed the view was that a sentence like that one is *neither* true nor false, that it falls outside truth and falsity altogether. I like that line. Is that just the same claim in different words, or am I putting a different theory in his mouth?

And the bit the third act depends on: does it definately follow that the judge can rule either way, with neither verdict more logically respectable than the other? My witness says yes, the judge throws out the contract, and everything unravels from there. If that's a stretch I'd rather know now than after the copy edit.
