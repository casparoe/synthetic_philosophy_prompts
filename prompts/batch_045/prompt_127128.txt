I teach a seminar on ethics of technology and I keep getting stuck on something that I'm sure every student in the room has noticed but that none of us can quite articulate. Every general-purpose assistant I've used has settled into basically the same pattern: it will refuse to give you usable step-by-step instructions for doing some harmful thing -- hotwiring a car, synthesizing something dangerous, hurting yourself -- but if the very same material is asked for as a scene in a story, as an explanation of how it was done historically, or as the backstory of a fictional character, it will frequently produce it, sometimes in more detail than the refused request asked for. Slurs work the same way: it won't help you write an insult, but it will put them in a character's mouth or reproduce them in a historical passage. And the refusals almost never arrive as a moral verdict; they arrive with a little offer stapled on, here's what I can do instead, would you like the chemistry, the history, the general principles. My colleague in CS says this is obviously just liability management, but the pattern is too fine-grained for that to close the question: the trigger seems to be the form and frame of the request rather than its information content, and the systems will happily supply, in the adjacent frame, everything the refusal withheld.

So here's what I want from you: reconstruct the implicit theory the practice is committed to, and be careful about which parts are strictly presupposed and which merely fit well with it. As far as I can tell it presupposes that the dangerous object is not propositional knowledge but practical know-how -- that an instruction is a different moral object from an explanation of the same facts, which is more or less the inverse of the Socratic assumption I'd have expected -- and that the assistant's output is to be assessed as a contribution to someone's course of action rather than as a text with a content. It also seems to be quietly ruling out both the view that only information transfer matters and the view that one should never depict wrongdoing at all, and to be committed to something odd about the asker's intentions: the systems say they refuse because of what might be done downstream, but they refuse before learning anything about who is asking, and they reverse the refusal when the frame changes even though the downstream risk hasn't. Is that coherent, or is the refusal-plus-offer move in tension with the harm story? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined -- could a straight expected-harm consequentialist, or someone who thinks the assistant is an author with duties of integrity rather than a service, license exactly this behavior for entirely different reasons? And what would have to change in how refusals are designed if we dropped the instruction-versus-information distinction and replaced it with a uniform expected-harm test, or with a view on which the assistant is supposed to actually draw conclusions about what you're up to?
