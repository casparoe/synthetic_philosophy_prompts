I am trying to work out what I actually think about announcements of surprise, and I want the principle behind my verdicts rather than a survey of what has been written about the puzzle. Below are eleven cases and how each one strikes me. I fix some terminology first, and at the end I offer the principle I keep falling back on, which I suspect is wrong.

Terminology. Call an event a surprise for an agent at lead time τ if, τ before the event occurs, the agent's credence that it would occur then was low. I want to leave open for now whether *low* means below a fixed threshold, or means not deducible from what the agent then knows, or something else again; part of what I am asking is which reading my verdicts commit me to. Call an announcement satisfiable if the announced event can both occur and be a surprise in the relevant sense.

1. A judge tells a prisoner on Sunday that he will be hanged at noon on one of the next five days, and that he will not know which day it is until the morning of the hanging itself. The prisoner reasons that he cannot be hanged at all, and is hanged on Wednesday, startled. My verdict: Wednesday's hanging is a genuine surprise and the prisoner's argument is bad; a Friday hanging would not be a surprise. This is the verdict I am least willing to surrender.

2. One-day window: on Sunday evening a teacher announces an exam for Monday, adding that the students will not be able to tell from the announcement which day it falls on. Verdict: unsatisfiable; there is no way to make that announcement true.

3. Two-day window: same announcement, Monday or Tuesday. Verdict: a Monday exam does not surprise me, since eliminating Tuesday seems to force Monday. I am uneasy about this, because whatever makes Monday un-surprising here looks like exactly what made Friday un-surprising in case 1, and there I was happy to drop Friday while keeping Wednesday.

4. Hundred-day window. Verdict: an exam on day 3 surprises me; an exam on day 99 surprises me less but still surprises me; only day 100 is not a surprise.

5. A window with no last admissible day: a teacher announces at the start of a term, stating no end date and no last possible day, that there will be one surprise quiz during the term. Verdict: plainly satisfiable; this is just how ordinary pop quizzes work.

6. As in case 1, but the teacher narrows the window by adding that Friday is excluded. Verdict: a Wednesday exam still surprises me. Shrinking the window by a day seems to change nothing at all, which I find suspicious given that the argument against surprise proceeds by repeated shrinking.

7. As in case 1, except that the teacher has been truthful in about nine of her last ten announcements and the students know it. Verdict: surprise is now even more clearly possible, and the argument against it visibly breaks. But it bothers me that the possibility of surprise should depend on the announcer's track record, since in case 5 nothing seems to depend on anyone's reliability.

8. Sealed envelope: the teacher announces exactly one exam on one of the next twenty days and states that the day was fixed in advance by a uniform random draw whose result is sealed in an envelope in the staff room; the students know all of this. Verdict: an exam on day 2 surprises me, an exam on day 20 does not, and the days in between come out as a matter of degree. I do not like this. I do not want to say that the day-2 exam is more of a surprise than the day-12 exam, since surprise feels all-or-nothing to me; yet every way I have tried to make it all-or-nothing either rules out surprise everywhere or draws the line somewhere I cannot motivate. I notice I allowed degrees in case 4 while resisting them in case 8. If that is the inconsistency it looks like, say so.

9. As in case 1, except that the teacher also promises to email the students one hour before class on the morning of the exam. Verdict: still a surprise. So the lead time cannot be arbitrarily short, but I cannot say what sets it.

10. Degenerate case: the students read the announcement, work through the argument, conclude that no surprise exam is possible, and are therefore genuinely startled on Wednesday. Verdict: this is a surprise, but a cheap one, of a different kind from case 1. A principle that cannot separate the two is missing something.

11. As in case 1, except that everyone in the prison has long believed this judge always hangs prisoners on the first day of the window. Verdict: Wednesday's hanging still seems to me a surprise; the announcement should not become unsatisfiable because of what the prisoner happened to expect. This is the pair I cannot reconcile: in case 8 I am perfectly happy to let the stipulated uniform draw settle which days are surprising, while in case 11 I refuse to let a distribution that lives only in the agent's head settle the same question. If no principled line separates a distribution stipulated as part of the case from one supplied by the agent's prior, then one of these two verdicts has to go, and I want to be told which.

My tentative principle: an announced event's occurring on day d is a surprise just in case, on the evening before d, the agent's credence that it occurs on d — conditional on the announcement being true and on the event not having occurred earlier — is below one-half, that conditional credence being got by spreading credence uniformly over the remaining admissible days; and the announcement is satisfiable just in case some day in the window meets that condition. I know this mishandles the gradations in case 8, and I suspect it mishandles cases 6 and 11.

What I want from you: the principle, or the two or three best candidates if there is no unique one, stated precisely enough that another person would derive the same verdicts from it, with each clause traceable to one of my cases and with any clause flagged that does work none of my cases motivates. Then tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which you think I should abandon. Finally, give me its verdicts on three cases I have not listed: an announcement that attaches a chance to the event rather than asserting it, as in a nine-in-ten chance of one exam on one of the next five days and it will surprise you; a window that is finite but very long, say a year of daily meetings with a stated last day; and one case of your own choosing, preferably one that separates your principle from its nearest rival.

Please keep the entire answer under 600 words. Lead with the principle in at most two sentences, then a compact list of its verdicts on my eleven cases marking agreement or conflict, then one paragraph on the verdict I should give up, then the three new cases.
