I've been trying to write down where I've landed on dispositions, and reading it back as one list it feels less stable then it did when I wrote the commitments one at a time. I want an audit, not a primer — I know what finks and masks are, so don't spend paragraphs explaining them to me, and don't try to talk me into eliminativism about dispositions either, I'm not going there.

Where I stand:

1. The simple conditional analysis is dead. Finks and masks both kill it.
2. Some conditional analysis is still right, and I favour Lewis's fix: x is disposed to M in C iff x has some intrinsic basis B such that, were x in C and to hold on to B long enough, x would M. I like it because it ties dispositions to counterfactuals about behaviour instead of making me intuit the essences of properties.
3. Fink case: a live cable wired to a gadget that kills the current the instant you touch it — still disposed to conduct.
4. Mask case: a wineglass packed in a foam-lined crate, struck hard, foam eats the impact — still fragile.
5. Fake case: a dead cable wired to a gadget that runs current through it the instant you touch it — not disposed to conduct, even though touching it woudl shock you.
6. Dispositions do real explanatory work: the fragility is part of why the glass broke, not just a shadow cast by its molecular structure. I want to resist the line that only the basis does any causing.
7. I'm a pandispositionalist about the fundamental properties: every fundamental property is purely a matter of what its bearer would do, no categorical or quidditive residue.
8. Dispositions are intrinsic to their bearers — I don't buy extrinsic dispositions, the weight and visibility examples don't move me.
9. Humean about laws: laws are just the regularities that show up in the best system, no necessary connections in nature.
10. Masks aren't metaphysically deep: a mask is just another disposition interfering, the foam's disposition to absorb shock against the glass's fragility.

So: which pairs here actually pull against each other, and how badly? Which ones only look like tension and dissolve once you spell them out — I specifically want to know which of my alarms are false alarms. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Rank them by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and tell me which single revision cleans up the most. Don't guess at which commitment I'm most attached to, just run the audit on what I wrote.

End with a final line in the form \answer{N — short phrase}, where N is the number of the commitment you'd revise first and the phrase names what I'd be giving up, e.g. \answer{2 — conditional analysis}.
