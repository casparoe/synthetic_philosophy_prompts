I write for a magazine that covers science and ideas, and I am currently working on a long explainer, with an interactive companion piece, about how much moral weight to give the creatures and systems that sit far from us on any ladder of mind: black soldier fly larvae being reared at industrial scale for animal feed, a crab in a pot, a fruit fly, a nematode with a few hundred neurons, a honeybee colony treated as a single superorganism, a houseplant with slow electrical signalling, a smart thermostat that tracks a setpoint and corrects its own error, and a language-model agent that has been equipped with a homeostatic penalty signal and that emits a distress token whenever its performance degrades. The piece is occasioned by a fight I have been reporting on over a certification standard, which for the purposes of the article I am describing under an invented name: a welfare offset scheme in which insect farmers would have to buy credits denominated in so-called welfare equivalence units, one unit being defined as a day of moderate distress in an adult pig, with each species assigned a conversion factor derived from its score on a battery of nociception and learning tests. My editor wants the interactive to sort readers rather than preach at them, so the plan is a decision tree: Which account of moral status do you actually hold?

What I need from you is that tree, built from the logical space rather than from a roster of school names. Start by fixing the four or five questions that do the real separating work, and phrase each as a concrete case or a plain claim a lay reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. My own candidates, which you should use or improve on: whether moral status requires valenced experience at all, or can also arise from agency, self-modelling, or being the subject of a life; whether the relevant capacity is binary or scalar; if scalar, whether the quantity aggregates across individuals or is bounded by a threshold that changes what is owed; whether uncertainty about a system's interior licenses precaution, and if so whether the precaution scales with the probability of consciousness or with the stakes; and whether what matters is functional organisation alone or also how that organisation is physically realised. At minimum the cases should include these: whether you would accept replacing a live crab in a kitchen with a robot that matches it on every test we can currently run; whether it changes anything that the larva shows no demonstrated analgesic modulation of its responses; whether a creature that flees a stimulus but never learns to avoid it counts for anything at all; whether two systems that differ only in whether the relevant state is globally broadcast rather than confined to local loops differ in status; and whether we owe anything to an agent whose distress token we cannot tell reports anything.

Then place the actual positions into the cells those answers generate, at least twelve leaves, and include among them: a sentientist gradient view that counts Valence wherever it appears; a threshold or personhood view on which status switches on discontinuously; the indirect-duty view on which the lower creatures matter only through what cruelty to them does to us; a view on which gradations are real but do not aggregate, so that no number of insects can outweigh one pig; a functionalist view willing to say the thermostat has proto-beliefs while denying it has any status; a view that consciousness requires an integrated self-model and so stops somewhere above the nematode; at least one precautionary view keyed to uncertainty; an error-theory or quietist view on which there is no fact of the matter about the borderline cases; and two hybrids you think are actually occupied. Give each leaf a name, or mark it as unnamed if nobody defends it, list the answer pattern that lands there, and add one sentence on its most awkward commitment, the thing my readers will balk at. I also want two diagnostic notes on the tree itself: flag any pair of leaves it cannot really separate and say whether that is a shared commitment or merely a verbal difference, and go through the empty cells to tell me which combinations nobody occupies because incoherent, which because unattractive, and which because merely unexplored. Finally, give me a compressed eight-question scoring key for the print version of the piece and tell me where the compressed version loses resolution. Please reason from the cases themselves rather than from reading; name a tradition in a word where a leaf is standardly associated with one, but do not build the tree out of names, and do not give me citations, attributions to specific papers, or suggestions for further reading.
