I hold a deliberately narrow version of epiphenomenalism, which I will call character-inert epiphenomenalism. Everything about a mental state except its phenomenal character — its functional role, its representational content, its access relations, its effects on behaviour and on later cognition — is exactly as my physicalist friends describe it and does all the causal work they want; only the what-it's-like-ness is causally inert. I hold it because it looks like the cheapest way to keep the explanatory gap while conceding nothing that a functionalist account of the mind needs.

In conversation I keep reaching for T. H. Huxley's images from his 1874 presidential address to the British Association: the steam whistle that accompanies the working of the locomotive without influencing its machinery, and, from the same discussion, the bell of a clock, which plays no part in keeping the time. The images are meant to do two jobs. First, to make inertness intelligible: a byproduct can be real, lawfully correlated with the process that produces it, and still be idle with respect to that process. Second, to take the sting out of the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if my judgment that I am now in pain is caused not by the pain's qualitative character but by the same neural goings-on that realize the pain's functional role, then the judgment can be perfectly reliable and my claim to know my own experience survives, even though the character itself moves nothing.

A colleague has begun pushing back in a way I cannot shake. She says the images carry the first job by importing features the target lacks, and quietly sabotage the second. Three of her points, in my words: (i) both the whistle and the bell are audible, so neither models something globally inert — only something inert with respect to one designated mechanism; (ii) both are artefacts with a purpose, placed there so that someone can hear them, whereas nothing in my view gives phenomenal character a function or an audience; (iii) we know independently that the whistle does not drive the pistons, because we can inspect the mechanism and, if we like, disconnect the whistle and watch the train keep moving — and it is precisely this kind of intervention that character-inert epiphenomenalism denies us, since there is no way to mute a quale while holding fixed everything functional.

I would like an audit of the images rather than a tour of the dialectic.

1. Give the correspondence table for each image separately — locomotive and clockwork, steam and gears, whistle and bell, the motion of the machinery, the hearer of the sound — and say where the two images diverge from each other, since Huxley used both.

2. Sort the disanalogies into those that matter for job one and those that matter for job two. In particular, say what work the hearer is doing in each image, and who or what the hearer would have to be in the target case.

3. Take the intervention point seriously. If what distinguishes a genuine cause from a mere correlate is that intervening on the former changes the effect, what is left of the thesis that qualia are causally inert: a substantive empirical claim, a claim that is true almost trivially given how the view is stated, or a claim the view has insulated from any possible test?

4. I have two repairs in mind, and I want to know which of them, if either, lets me keep both jobs. Call the first the second gauge: put the bell in a soundproof housing and add a second pressure gauge that shares the bell's input and inscribes in the engineer's log that the bell is sounding. The entry tracks the bell reliably while the bell causes nothing. Does this model reliability without causation, and is the reliability thereby explained or merely asserted? Call the second the isomorph: the log entry is not caused by the sound but is a casting of it, sharing its waveform, so that the record bears the bell's form without being produced by it. That is meant to model the idea that an introspective judgment deploys a recognitional or templated concept whose tokening is type-identical with the experience it is about, so that reference and reliability come from constitution rather than causation. Can an artefact model carry that? And if it can, do I still hold the view I started with, or have I slipped into a physicalism that was never threatened by the paradox?

5. If both images fail, offer a better one and say what it imports. I have been toying with the waste heat of a processor: real, measurable, tightly correlated with the computation, and doing nothing the computation requires — except that real chips throttle on it, so its idleness is an engineering contingency rather than a metaphysical necessity. Better or worse, and why?

6. Finally, consider whether the failure here is local. Is there a reason to think that no analogy can do this work — that argument by analogy is the wrong instrument precisely because what is contested is the target's causal structure, which is what a mapping has to fix in advance in order to be a mapping at all?

Tell me honestly whether the conclusions the images were used to support survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the images were carrying them the whole time, and rank the difficulties from fatal to merely awkward.
