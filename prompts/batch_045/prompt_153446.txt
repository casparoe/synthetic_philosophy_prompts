I'm TAing an upper-level seminar on blame and moral responsibility this term and I have to write one of the questions for the take-home final (closed book, roughly 1000-word answers, about 45 minutes per question, 25 marks). My assigned topic is standing to blame and hypocrisy, and I really don't want a question that can be answered by writing something like "hypocrisy undermines your standing to blame because you haven't held yourself to the norm you're invoking" and then stopping. That's basically the motte of the whole debate and half the class will turn in exactly that.

Some context on why I care: I've been reading LessWrong-adjacent stuff since around 2014, and there was a huge flamewar in my local rationalist/EA-ish group chat a few weeks back about whether someone who did X can call out someone else for X — the usual tu quoque / whataboutism spiral. What struck me is that people were running together at least three different moves: denying that the thing was wrong at all, claiming the critic lacks standing to blame, and just adding a second complaint ("also, you're a hypocrite") that doesn't actually answer the original blame. I want the question to force students to pull those apart and then say what standing would have to be for the standing-claim to do real work rather than just being a conversational derailment. The bit I really want them to hit, and which I think is the actual crux, is whether the force of "you too" is genuinely normative — something about rights, or authority, or the blamer's unworthiness — or whether it's basically epistemic and heuristic: a cue that this person is an unreliable or self-serving judge of whether a wrong occurred, which we track with standing talk but which isn't really about anyone's normative powers.

The test case I'd like built into the question (steelman or modify it however is best) is this pair: A is the only person in a small workplace willing to call out the boss for something genuinely bad, and A did the very same thing two years ago, was sanctioned for it, owned it, and seems to have genuinely reformed. B never did it, but only because the opportunity never came up, and everyone knows B would have done it in a heartbeat. My intuition is that A has more standing than B, but I can't quite say why, and any account on which standing tracks clean hands or "has actually lived up to the norm" seems to get this case backwards. So: write me the question, a model answer of about 900-1000 words that argues for a definite position instead of surveying options, and a marking scheme out of 25 built around maybe five must-hit points with marks assigned to each, where the scheme clearly separates an A from a B — name the specific move that has to be present for the A, and the specific way candidates usually botch it for the B or below. Two constraints: the question has to be answerable closed-book without naming or citing any author or paper (we told them the exam is citation-free and half of them skipped the readings anyway), and the model answer should be honest about the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than just asserting the conclusion.
