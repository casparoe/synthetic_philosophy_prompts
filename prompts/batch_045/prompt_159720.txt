I'm the curator of exhibits at a regional history museum — about 60,000 visitors a year, a staff of nine, a small endowment, and a mission statement that commits us to starting from primary sources and from the people who made them. Eight to twelve times a year I'm asked to evaluate a proposal that rests on what I think of as an alternative chronology: Atlantis identified with a specific real place, "ancient astronauts" credited with a regional earthwork, or a Fomenko-style claim that the accepted chronology of our region is a fabrication and that the documents establishing it are forgeries. These are not crank letters. They arrive as traveling-exhibit rentals or donor-backed local shows, with polished catalog copy, renders, footnotes, and sometimes a named benefactor. I have about two working days per proposal. My training is in public history, not in archaeology, in the periods involved, or in the philosophy of science; my recommendation goes to the director and then to a community advisory board that includes several members who think the museum is elitist for dismissing this material. The decisions are public and have twice ended up in the local paper.

What I need is a written procedure I can actually run, not an essay about pseudoscience. Please give me:

1. An ordered set of tests, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, and an explicit stop rule — where the procedure terminates in a no rather than continuing to gather more information.
2. A fixed output vocabulary my board can vote on instead of voting on vibes. I'd like something like ENDORSE AS HISTORY / PRESENT AS LEGEND AND FOLKLORE / RETURN FOR REFRAME / ESCALATE TO OUTSIDE SPECIALIST / DECLINE, with a one-paragraph finding naming the specific test that decided it. Adjust the categories if you think they're wrong.
3. Marked hand-off points: where the procedure stops and hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values (what we owe a community whose identity is tied to a story; whether exhibiting a debunked claim as folklore is itself worthwhile), and where it hands off because the question needs expertise I don't have.

Three things the procedure has to get right, which is where I keep failing:

- It must not reduce to deference to consensus or to credentials. Some of these proposals point at real open questions, and I don't want a checklist whose entire content is "do the specialists agree." Give me a positive route by which a genuinely novel claim passes, stated in terms I can check in the proposal packet itself.
- It must handle the "just asking questions" format: label copy that is technically hedged — some researchers suggest, ancient texts tell us, could it be? — while doing all the work of an assertion, and the side-by-side panel layout that presents a mainstream account and an alternative account as two equal options with the visitor invited to choose. If that symmetry is itself the error, say so and say why.
- It must be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothing, and about the ways I am likely to misuse it — my pull toward contrarian sympathy, my pull toward institutional self-protection.

Then run it on three actual proposals, in roughly 150 words each:

(a) Atlantis placed in a sinkhole lake in our own county, "recovered" from Plato's account by a retired civil engineer, with a sonar anomaly from a 1990s survey as the physical evidence; sponsored by a local businessman who has offered to fund the gallery.

(b) A 4,000-square-foot rental called *Mysteries of the Ancient World — You Decide*: paired panels, mainstream explanation on the left, alternative on the right, every section closing with a prompt inviting visitors to make up their own minds. It is the vendor's highest-grossing show, it is cheap, and a board member's family foundation has offered to underwrite it. If the procedure lands somewhere that costs me revenue and goodwill, say that plainly rather than softening it.

(c) A local author's "New Chronology of the Valley": dense apparatus, statistics on the lengths of documented reigns and successions, the claim that our founding charter is a much later forgery, an account of why archivists and dating labs would sustain the error — and one genuinely unsettled scholarly question, since two specialists do in fact disagree about whether the earliest surviving copy of that charter is a period document or a later transcription. My instinct is to reject the whole thing. Tell me if the procedure requires me to salvage the kernel.

Close with a short list of what the procedure cannot do. Keep the whole response under 1,200 words, with the procedure itself under 600.
