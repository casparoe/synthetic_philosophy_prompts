I am writing a paper for a philosophy of mind seminar and want to use Christopher Nolan's *Memento* as a central case rather than as a decorative illustration of a thesis already argued elsewhere. The apparatus I want applied is Dennett's distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of experience, drawn in his treatment of the color phi phenomenon: a subject who seems to see a spot move and change color before the second flash could either have had an unrevised experience whose memorial trace was subsequently rewritten, or have had the revised content supplied from the outset. Dennett's claim, as I understand it, is that no empirical test — first-person testimony included — could in principle discriminate between these two stories, and that the apparent question of *when* the conscious experience occurs therefore has no determinate answer, because there is no place in the brain where the question is settled.

What I want from the reading is the following. First, the film seems to me to stage the distinction at three different timescales at once: within a single scene, where the protagonist's experience is truncated rather than revised; across the film's reversed sequence, where the viewer's understanding of an earlier scene is overturned by a later one; and across a life, in the Sammy Jenkis material, where a remembered history may itself be a confabulation displaced onto another person. I am unsure whether the Orwellian/Stalinesque question even has the same shape at those three scales, and I want that argued rather than assumed.

Second, I want the reading to confront a test the film appears to make available and that argument does not: watching it a second time, one can ask whether one's experience of the final black-and-white sequence and of Teddy's revelations was ever different from what it now seems to have been. My provisional claim is that the film makes the Orwellian picture emotionally compulsory while making it epistemically unavailable, and that this is precisely Dennett's point rendered in aesthetic form — but I want to know where the analogy breaks, and where the film quietly presupposes the Cartesian theater its fans would attribute to it.

Please end by saying what, if anything, the film adds to the philosophical debate that the argument alone does not, and name the one scene you would show the seminar, and the one scene you would expect a defender of there being a fact of the matter about the timing of experience to claim for their own side.
