I'm putting together a two-page handout for a graduate seminar on aggregation in normative ethics, and I want its centrepiece to be a decision tree that takes a student from the bare situation there are six people and I can either save five of them or save the one to a named position on whether the numbers count.

Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of positions: five or six branch nodes, each a single yes-or-no question a student could answer without knowing which view it leads to, each phrased as a concrete case rather than as a slogan, and none of them reducible to do you think we should save the greater number. The cases I want doing the work: five versus one with everything else equal; a matched variant of that case in which the five are known only statistically while the one is an identifiable child in front of you, so the tree has to say whether it is really the numbers pulling; five versus one where the five would each lose a finger and the one would lose a life; two versus one; a certain five versus a fifty-percent chance of ten; and a policy case in which you are writing the standing triage rule for a service that will face a hundred structurally identical emergencies and do not yet know who will end up in which group.

The distinctions the tree must be able to separate are: whether numbers generate reasons at all or the apparent pull of numbers is really identifiability, a special relationship, or the fact that saving the many maximises each person's ex ante chance of survival; whether counting is ex post on actual numbers saved or ex ante on expected numbers; and whether numbers can count at the level of a standing policy while not counting in the individual case. Leaves should include strict anti-aggregation on the equal-claim model, equal-chance and proportional-chance lottery views, straightforward additive aggregation, diminishing-weight or lexically-thresholded aggregation, a pairwise-contractualist view on which numbers enter only as a tie-breaker, and the view that the numbers never settle anything.

Each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then list the answer-combinations your nodes allow that no actual position occupies, saying of each whether that region is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot in fact separate, and name the further case that would separate them.
