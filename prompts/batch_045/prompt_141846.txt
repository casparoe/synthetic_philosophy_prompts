I'm giving a talk next week to the Cognitive Science Program's interdisciplinary consciousness seminar, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view. The working title is "Consciousness as a rationing device," and here is the thesis I'm committed to, so you can aim objections at it rather than at illusionism in general.

The claim is that consciousness is a user illusion, but not in the way that view is usually spelled out. On the standard version, the self-model has an epistemic job — it monitors the system's own states — and the illusion consists in its misrepresenting them as having qualitative properties they lack. I want to replace that with an economic story: the self-model's job is to ration a scarce central resource among subsystems whose demands are otherwise incomparable, and phenomenal character is the format in which those demands are made comparable at all — a single, coarse, scalar currency for how much of the bottleneck a state is currently claiming. Second, I want to say that what the model primarily misrepresents is not sensory quality but authorship: it presents states as the doings of one unified agent who could have attended to them or not. Call it the scarcity-and-authorship version.

Three consequences I will say out loud and will not retract under pressure: (i) consciousness should track the severity of the bottleneck, not overall computational richness, information integration, or behavioural sophistication; (ii) damaging the authorship component of the self-model should abolish the relevant states rather than merely mislabel them, which is what I take the passivity phenomena in psychiatry to show; (iii) an architecture with no bottleneck — unbounded, fully parallel, nothing competing for a shared resource — would have no phenomenal consciousness no matter what else it got up to.

The room will be: two vision neuroscientists who work on binocular rivalry and who now infer conscious content from eye movements and pupil dilation rather than button presses, precisely because they think report contaminates the neural measures; one higher-order-thought theorist who thinks my view is representationalism wearing a costume; two enactivist or phenomenologically inclined philosophers who think the whole family of views is incoherent and who will press on pre-reflective self-awareness and the lived body; one clinical researcher on passivity symptoms and agency; and one person from a digital-minds research group who will ask what any of this implies for whether artificial systems could matter morally. A graduate student who is an orthodox illusionist will also be there and will defend the standard version against mine.

Give me seven to nine objections, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by how likely they are, each phrased the way the relevant person in that room would actually phrase it — the rivalry people will talk about paradigms and confounds, the phenomenologist will talk about what can be coherently believed from the first-person standpoint, the clinician will talk about what patients say. For each: the strongest formulation, a reply I can give in about ninety seconds, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Then tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks anything; which single reply has to land for the talk to survive; and what the question is that I'm hoping nobody asks.

Skip the objections I get from every audience — that illusionism needs consciousness in order for there to be an illusion of it, and that it changes the subject rather than answering it. I have answers for those, well-rehearsed; they are not what this room will hit me with, and I don't want them taking up slots. Please also don't give me reading suggestions or pointers to papers: I know the literature I need, and I want the sheet to be usable as written.
