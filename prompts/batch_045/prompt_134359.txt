Write an essay (British English please) on how far the strange inversion really goes. Darwin seemed to show that you can get exquisite adaptive competence without any designer who understands what the design is for, and Turing that you can get intelligent-looking behaviour out of brute mechanical processes without any understanding in the machine. The controversy I want you to settle: does that same inversion apply to comprehension itself, or does understanding — and consciousness along with it — mark the point where the inversion breaks down?

Set it up with these two systems. Both play a hard strategy game and explain in plain language why their moves are good. Helix came out of a massive automated search over billions of variants, selected purely on results; nobody designed its internals, nobody can read them off, and no one can say why it plays the way it does. Ledger was hand-coded top-down by a team who understand every line and can derive each move from principles they can state out loud. Helix plays much better, and its explanations are better too — more accurate, more useful to learners. Ledger's programmers understand the game; nobody understands Helix.

Then the wrinkle. Helix also emits apparently apt first-person reports: it says it can see why a line fails because a piece is pinned, and it revises its own earlier judgements in a way that looks like genuine self-criticism. Does the inversion deflate those reports too?

Weigh both sides properly — the view that meaning, understanding and even consciousness are just further competences that the inversion can generate and explain without anyone or anything comprehending them first, and the view (think of Mary's room) that the inversion only ever explains behaviour and function and leaves out what its like to undergo something. Then commit: say whether comprehension is just one more competence or whether something resists, and defend your verdict against the strongest objection you can think of. Around 600 words.
