Take the role of a philosopher of history who also knows enough Earth-system science not to say anything silly about volcanic forcing, pathogen evolution, or what a proxy record can and cannot establish. I am a retired isotope hydrologist — thirty-five years of reconstructing past climates from proxies — who has lately started sitting in on a philosophy of history reading group, and I would like a specification before I read any further. Please think this through properly rather than answering at speed: before you draft anything, brainstorm a long list of candidate requirements and then cut it down, and tell me in a line or two which candidates you cut and whether you cut them because they were disguised preferences of one camp rather than genuine requirements. Do that work in your reasoning; keep the answer itself to the deliverable.

The question. What must an adequate account of non-human causation in history deliver? By this I mean: an account of when and how something that is not a person — a pathogen, an eruption, a failed monsoon, a shift in a trade-wind belt — gets to figure in the explanation of a human historical outcome; and, separately, of whether anything beyond causation is required before we are entitled to call such a thing an actor rather than a cause. I want the desiderata, each anchored to the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and I want the conceptual weight on the criteria rather than on scoring views. Correct any case below whose facts you think wrong and substitute a cleaner one; I have supplied them so that you need not look anything up.

The cases I want the list to earn its keep on:

1. Tambora, 1815, and the year without a summer: harvest failure, food rioting, typhus, emigration, across two continents and several quite different polities.
2. A large eruption of broadly comparable magnitude in a century with no comparable documentary trace. Something other than the geophysics has to be doing the work here, and I want to know whether that something belongs on the requirements list or in the theory.
3. Potato blight in Ireland from 1845: a real pathogen, a monoculture, and — on the account I was given — grain continuing to leave the country. The natural cause is genuine and the mortality is not explained by it alone; and the blight was available, then and later, as something to invoke.
4. Plague mortality in the fourteenth century and the subsequent movement of real wages and of serfdom in parts of western Europe, as the story is usually told. A pathogen acting on an institution, with several intermediate steps I would want the account to make explicit.
5. Cholera in the decades before the organism was securely identified: miasmatic belief driving quarantine and sanitation policy, with effects that are real whether or not the belief was true. I take this to be the hardest case for any purely ontic account of causal attribution.
6. A looped case. On the one hand, mid-sixth-century volcanic cooling, famine, then plague; on the other, the depopulation following the European conquests in the Americas, the regrowth of abandoned farmland, and a dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide that I am told is legible in ice cores. Here a human historical process alters a geophysical variable that later acts as a cause again.
7. A monsoon failure in an agrarian economy: it explains the harvest. It does not explain which relief legislation a parliament settled on. I want to know what the account owes us about that mismatch of scale.

What I want from you:

The requirements, numbered, each with its motivating case and an argument for why it is a requirement rather than a taste.

A division into non-negotiable and negotiable, where negotiable means a reasonable theorist in this area could deny it without embarrassment.

Any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. If you find one, say which you would keep, and treat the resulting list as the result rather than papering over the tension. I care about this part more than the rest.

One requirement that does the most exclusionary work — the one that eliminates the largest share of otherwise live accounts.

A verdict on two notions I have been carrying over from my own field and may simply be smuggling in: a vulnerability window (the same forcing lands differently depending on the slack in the human system — grain reserves, alternative supply, fiscal capacity) and channeling (institutions determining which of several outcomes a given shock produces). Are these genuine desiderata that an account must accommodate, or are they my discipline's prejudice, or do they quietly concede the whole anti-determinist case in advance?

Three specific things I want addressed inside the list rather than bolted on: whether the account must handle looped causation at all; whether it should deflate talk of actors into talk of causes, or preserve a real distinction, and if so on what basis — responsiveness to selection, goal-directedness, answerability to interpretation, or something else; and whether it must include an evidential clause, saying what observable or proxy difference a claim of non-human causation has to make, since I have little patience for attributions that no record could in principle test.

Finally, and briefly — no more than a quarter of your answer, and I would rather have it as a table — how the main families do against your list: the old environmental-determinist tradition, possibilism, counterfactual or difference-making accounts, the symmetrically agential actor-network style of approach, and the newer Earth-system and Anthropocene narratives. No citations, no bibliography, no suggestions for further reading: I cannot follow them up and I do not want them. Where you are unsure whether a desideratum is satisfiable at all, say so plainly instead of manufacturing a candidate.
