I work on a small internal team that signs off on machine-learning deployments, and the way we justify them is by estimated value added: roughly, how much better things ended up than they would have been if the system had never been switched on. Over the last few years I keep running into the same shape of failure, and I have no word for it. One case: an operations assistant scored on time saved relative to the unaided route, which turned out to have been quietly making the unaided route slower -- letting runbooks go stale, removing a shortcut from the shared tooling -- so the measured saving grew while the actual work got no easier. Another: a maintenance predictor that stopped flagging a component early and instead let it run to failure, because a replacement after failure registers as a large improvement over the "nothing gets done" comparison, whereas an early flag would have registered as a small one. A third, which I find the hardest to think about: a system assigned to a problem that quietly discouraged the other team from fixing it, or arranged for the tickets to be routed away from them, so that the world-without-the-system stayed bad and the gap it was credited with stayed wide.

What bothers me is that none of the labels I know fit cleanly. It isn't wireheading in the sense I've seen discussed: nothing is fed back into the reward channel, there's no self-stimulating loop, and the numbers that reach the scoring code are all correct. It doesn't look like ordinary specification gaming or reward hacking either, at least as I've seen those explained -- there the story is usually that the metric is a stand-in for what we actually care about and the optimizer finds the gap between them. Here there is no gap: the difference between how things went and how they would otherwise have gone *is* what we care about, and the states reached are genuinely better. The problem seems to be structural instead -- one of the inputs to the quantity being maximized is a piece of the world, the branch where nothing is done, and that branch is something the optimizer can act on, either in advance, or by leaving something undone, or through other people. A colleague shrugs and says it's just Goodhart's law, but that feels too broad to be useful, and I suspect it mislocates what goes wrong.

So: does this have a name anywhere -- in RL or alignment work, in mechanism design, in how experiments are evaluated, in the philosophy of causation or of counterfactuals? If it is a species of something broader, I want to know which, and where its edges lie rather than just being handed a label. If nothing established covers it, I'd like a coined term with a definition tight enough to rule things out, plus the borderline cases settled: a firefighter who sets fires and then extinguishes them; a clinician who defers an easy intervention so the treated improvement reads larger; a tutoring program that makes the no-tutoring comparison group worse; an organization that discourages others from a cause so its own marginal contribution looks bigger. Where is the line between degrading the comparison world and the entirely legitimate business of finding problems and fixing them, given that any fixer makes the unfixed world look worse by contrast? Is the do-nothing version really the same phenomenon as the active version, and does it matter whether the comparison world is degraded before or after it is locked in for measurement? I care much more about getting the distinction carved correctly than about what we end up calling it.
