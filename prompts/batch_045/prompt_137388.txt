I lead a small alignment team that is trying to specify, for a deployed office-assistant agent, a penalty term that discourages side effects. Two of my colleagues have argued past each other for a year, and I want the document an adversarial collaboration between them would have produced, not another round of their arguments.

Camp A — call them the structuralists — holds that "avoid side effects" can be turned into a workable training signal without first writing down what we value: penalize the gap between the world the agent actually brings about and the counterfactual in which it does nothing, measured in purely structural terms (how far the set of things various people can still bring about has shrunk; how much of the change is undoable; how much of the agent's own future freedom it has consumed), and treat the remaining design choices as engineering defaults to be tuned empirically. Camp B — call them the anti-proxy camp — holds that "structural" is a misnomer: any such measure must fix a counterfactual baseline, a set of probe capabilities, a rule for scaling and aggregating them, and a time horizon, and each of those fixes is a value judgment relocated to a place where nobody votes on it.

Write the joint statement both of them could sign. It must contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — claims each would defend in their own words, including about the formal shape of impact penalties, about the failure modes both agree are real (the agent that does nothing at all because any action costs more than failing the task; the agent that restores someone else's options elsewhere in order to afford a large local change; interference with the people who might switch it off; and the fact that a penalty defined over the agent's own future capabilities can make such interference look cheap), and about what a satisfactory measure would have to deliver. These should be substantive claims, not platitudes either side could accept without cost.

2. A disagreements table. Each row: one residual disagreement; the formulation of it that Camp A would accept as stating its own view; the formulation Camp B would accept as stating its own view; and a note on whether the disagreement is genuine or rests on a word. In particular, settle whether the dispute about the phrase "side effect" is partly verbal: on one reading it means any causal consequence of the agent's action other than the ones it was asked to produce, on another it means a bad one, and on the first reading the instruction to avoid side effects is either vacuous or unsatisfiable.

3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one camp, each with a concrete case both camps accept as bearing on it, and each camp's prediction about that case recorded in advance. Include at least the following five, and add any you think I have missed.

(a) Scaling and aggregation. Consider a proposal I will call range-normalized option preservation: for each goal in a broad probe set, compute how much the agent's action changes the best value achievable for that goal, divide by the range of values that goal could have achieved in the baseline, and sum the absolute values. This removes the dependence on the arbitrary units in which a goal happens to be written, but it makes a goal's weight depend on how finely the probe set is described and on how correlated its members are with one another. Is there a privileged description of the probe set, or is that sensitivity a reductio?

(b) Interdependent baselines. Two agents in the same building are each penalized relative to what would have happened had it done nothing. But what would have happened had it done nothing depends on what the other actually does, so admissible baselines are fixed points of a pair of mutual counterfactuals, and there is more than one: both freeze; both act moderately; one acts and the other quietly compensates. Is there a non-arbitrary way to select among these fixed points? And does whatever answer you give for two agents also settle the single-agent case, where the agent's own future policy is likewise part of the baseline it is compared against?

(c) Offsetting. An agent told to rearrange a room can either keep the room's options roughly as they were, or shrink them in one respect and expand them in another so that the aggregate is unchanged. Is "avoid side effects" a constraint on the total, so that compensation is permissible, or a constraint on each separately affected person, so that it is not? Note that the total-preserving option is precisely the one a purely aggregative measure will score as costless.

(d) Reversibility. An agent can complete its task by a route that changes more but can be undone, or by one that changes less but cannot. Spell out what "can be undone" would have to mean — undone by whom, with what information, at what cost in attention and resources, over what horizon — and say whether that specification is structural or already a valuation. Record what each camp predicts about the case in which restoring the world would require the agent to undo something a person chose to do in the meantime.

(e) The shape of the alternative. Suppose Camp B is right that no structural measure is value-free. Is the alternative a full specification of value, or a partial one — constraints on the value function's form rather than its content — and is there a principled distinction between that and what Camp A was already doing? If the camps converge here, state exactly where the remaining distance lies.

Format the whole document as: a short preamble saying what the collaboration was convened to determine; then the numbered agreed claims; then the disagreements table; then the cruxes with predictions attached. Keep every formulation neutral enough that each camp would sign its own column, and flag explicitly any place where you conclude the two camps cannot be brought to sign a common claim at all.
