I help run a volunteer transcription project at the county historical society: on Saturday mornings four or five of us type up nineteenth-century poor-relief minute books and militia enrollment lists so that they can be searched online. A disagreement broke out among us last spring that we never managed to settle, and it eventually led me to start a small podcast for the volunteers and the local history reading group. Next week's guest is a scholar of archives (I'll identify her only by her first name on air) whose forthcoming book defends a specific and fairly strong claim: that the silences in an archive are not absences left behind by accident but absences that were manufactured, and that large-scale digitization makes the problem worse rather than better, because it multiplies the reach of decisions made decades earlier about what was worth keeping while simultaneously giving users an impression of completeness — she has a name for this, the completeness illusion. She is also known for batting away methodological worries with two stock phrases: "that's a description problem, not a power problem," and "that's an access problem, not an appraisal problem." I would like eight to ten interview questions that make both phrases progressively harder to fall back on, ordered so the episode builds, with the deflection-frustrating ones toward the end. Please keep them genuinely technical rather than conversational — no "what first drew you to the archives?" — and imagine an interviewer who has read widely outside the academy but has no training in it.

Here are the places I want the episode to go, drawing on distinctions my friend and I worked out while arguing about our own minute books: (1) the parish-rate problem — our fullest records of poor people, enslaved people, and Native people are precisely the records produced by the agencies that administered them, so I want to know whether such records can ever present those people as agents rather than as objects of administration, and whether working from them is a continuation of the governance that produced them or a turning of that governance's tools around; (2) the custodial bind — a community that deposits its own papers with a university or state repository gains preservation but loses control of arrangement, description, and access, and I want to know whether there is any form of deposit, short of keeping the papers in the community, that does not reproduce this; (3) inclusion as re-description — newly acquired material from an under-recorded group has to be catalogued in the repository's existing controlled vocabulary and shelved beside the records of the agency responsible for excluding it, so I want to know whether the gain in visibility is partly cancelled by the loss of the material's own categories (this is where "description problem, not a power problem" will come out, and I want the follow-up that forces the distinction to do real work); (4) retrievability bias — once everything is full-text searchable, only the recorders' vocabulary is searchable, so questions about how people described themselves become systematically unanswerable; (5) whether "silence" is even the right metaphor, since an archive full of formulaic boilerplate repeated thousands of times is arguably noisy rather than silent, and I want to know what her view would predict differently if we reframed the phenomenon as distortion or amplification instead; (6) the negative-space problem — how one tells a manufactured silence from a banal one (nobody wrote it down because it was not the sort of thing anyone wrote down, or the papers simply burned), what test she would propose, and how much of her case survives if a large share of absences turn out to be banal; (7) the symmetry worry about counter-archives — if every archive is an instrument of whoever keeps it, then a community-run archive is an instrument too, and I want to know whether that is merely a change in who holds the power and whether the same critique applies to the sources her own book rests on; and (8) the budget question — with finite money, should a repository prioritize acquiring new collections from under-recorded groups or re-describing what it already holds, since her view seems to imply re-description first and I want to see whether she will own that implication.

For each question, please give me the reply she is most likely to make and one follow-up keyed specifically to that reply. Then close with a short paragraph of your own telling me three things: which two or three of these questions rest on the shakiest premises, and what exactly the shaky premise is; what kind of answer should change my mind — that is, what would she have to say that would make me conclude the "silences are manufactured" framing is the wrong way to think about what we are doing on Saturday mornings, and where the questioning itself is most likely to misfire; and whether any of the questions presuppose something about how archives actually work that a non-specialist like me has probably gotten wrong.
