I'm in eleventh grade, and for the past two semesters I've run a small after-school discussion group on artificial intelligence and society. There is one argument we keep having, and it has stopped going anywhere, so I'd like a diagnosis of it rather than another round.

Whenever the conversation turns to what transformative AI will actually be like, three analogies get produced, and then the discussion stops. One group reaches for nuclear weapons: a small number of labs, a decisive capability, catastrophic downside, and therefore the lesson is that verification regimes, export controls and something like deterrence thinking have to be built before the capability arrives rather than after. A second group says it is the printing press: a technology for producing and spreading information, cheap to copy, impossible to contain, empowering individuals while eroding the authority of existing institutions, and therefore the lesson is that control is a fantasy and that resilience, pluralism and media literacy are the only honest goals. A third says the Industrial Revolution: not an event but a long economic restructuring, with productivity gains that took decades to appear in the data and with labour displacement that was severe long before anyone measured it, and therefore the lesson is patience about timelines and attention to slower institutions like unions, schooling and the welfare state rather than to acute catastrophe.

What frustrates me is that nobody is wrong in a way anyone can point to. Each side cheerfully concedes that its analogy breaks down somewhere: the nuclear people grant there is no single test, the printing press people grant the economic effects will be far larger than those of cheap print, the Industrial Revolution people grant the timescale may be compressed from a century into a decade. And still nobody moves. I've come to suspect the analogies are doing work that nobody is auditing.

My own hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than humour, is that these are not three answers to one question. They strike me as three answers to three different questions — how fast the change arrives, how concentrated its development and control are, and which kind of bad outcome dominates — where each side's analogy quietly answers all three at once and thereby quietly delivers a policy recommendation too. If that is right, the debate is stalled in a specific and slightly embarrassing way: the real disagreements are empirical, but they are being conducted as disagreements about which historical episode is most apt, and an analogy is cheap to generate and almost impossible to refute.

So: is that the right diagnosis, or is something else going on? Is it instead a clash of bedrock intuitions about how much a single technology can change, a hidden factual dispute about timelines, a verbal dispute about what transformative is supposed to mean, or several different questions sharing one label? Please commit to one answer and give your reasons, drawing on what each side in my description actually claims, and please don't just survey the three analogies again. Then tell me, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction we should draw, a question we should replace this one with, or a commitment test — for instance requiring anyone who offers an analogy to name a measurable indicator and a date by which the analogy would be shown wrong — that would make these claims answerable. And please flag where your own confidence is lowest, and what you would actually bet on if forced to pick one of the three framings today.
