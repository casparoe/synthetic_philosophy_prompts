So I was at a panel last week — one of those atrocity prevention / "lessons of the twentieth century" things the international relations department puts on — and the same two moves kept happening without anyone noticing they were talking past each other. One speaker closed with "never again," and used it to argue that the word genocide is load-bearing: once an episode gets called that, the rest of us are supposedly on the hook in a way we aren't for crimes against humanity or just for mass killing generally. The other speaker more or less said the opposite: the label is lawyers' business, it takes a court years to hand it down, the bodies pile up the same whatever you call it, and what actually matters is stopping the killing. Nobody engaged with the other at all.

On the walk home my flatmate (philosophy, insufferable about it) said both of them were using "never again" as if it packed a whole argument, and that the real disagreement isn't about the word but about when our obligations attach. Her version: if the only thing that switches on a duty to intervene is a legal finding of genocide, and legal findings of genocide basically arrive only once the killing is over, because courts need the aftermath and the evidence of intent, then "never again" as a prevention slogan is self-defeating by construction — it guarantees we act too late. So she wants to junk the trigger model entirely and replace it with a graded one, where the strength of the duty scales with how bad the expected outcome is and how sure we are, and the legal label drops out of the picture.

That feels too fast to me but I can't say where it goes wrong. So: can you lay out the distinct precise things "never again" might be claiming? I want a menu, not one answer. Please cover at least: whether it's a prediction about the world or a pledge made by whoever says it; who "we" and "again" are meant to be (my understanding is that the term was coined to name a kind of destruction that could happen to any group, not to mark one particular group's catastrophe — if I've got that wrong, say so); whether the "never" is about recurrence or about standing by and watching; and whether the slogan has anything to say about my flatmate's timing problem — does the special intent requirement in the treaty definition, the bit that's supposed to separate genocide from ordinary mass murder, matter for what we owe people, or is it just an artefact of what can be proved in a courtroom?

For each reading tell me whether it's trivial, substantive but defensible, or actually false, and which ones would give either panelist the conclusion they were reaching for. Also please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this — I dont want to repeat a confident-sounding reading in a seminar next week that turns out to be one side of a live fight.
