I'm a PhD student in political economy writing a chapter on whether commitment technology can underwrite cooperation in one-shot encounters with no shadow of the future — two states, or two autonomous trading agents owned by rival firms, meeting once with no prospect of retaliation.

Please write a dialogue in British English between two equally competent theorists. The first holds that the program-equilibrium construction dissolves the one-shot prisoners' dilemma: each side submits to a trusted executor a program that is shown the other's source code and returns an action, so a program that cooperates just in case the other program is identical to it makes mutual cooperation a Nash equilibrium, and by the folk theorem for this setting every feasible, individually rational payoff pair is attainable in some program equilibrium. The second holds that this relocates the problem rather than solving it: unconditional defection remains an equilibrium, the cooperative ones rest on fragile syntactic coincidences or on commitment devices no real agent could credibly enter, and the construction quietly presupposes both a trusted executor and full mutual transparency.

Stage the exchange around a case of asymmetric stakes: one side's gain from defecting is many times the other's, so they disagree about which equilibrium to coordinate on, and the usual focal devices — identical source code, an even split of the cooperative surplus — are no longer neutral between them. Suppose additionally that the high-stakes side's program is proprietary: the other can execute it but not read it.

Have them answer each other's actual moves rather than trade set pieces. Can the disagreement be recast as bargaining over the surplus with mutual defection as the threat point, and does that vindicate or deflate the first theorist's claim? Is there a stake-proportional variant, or an escrow arrangement, that survives the proprietary-program problem? Let each concede at least one substantial point, and close with the disagreement narrowed rather than settled — including an honest verdict on whether the original dilemma has been dissolved or merely restated one level up.
