I teach a graduate seminar on comparative memory culture — German Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japan's postwar settlement, Turkish denial of the Armenian genocide, the American South and the Lost Cause — and I want to close the term with a literary case: Toni Morrison's Beloved.

Read the novel through that framework, but through the framework's blind spots rather than as an illustration of it. The hinge I have in mind is Morrison's notion of rememory: a memory that no longer needs a rememberer, that lodges in places and things and can be walked into, so that the past is re-inhabited rather than represented. Set that against the German ideal of working through, which presumes a subject able to bring the past to consciousness and to survive having done so.

Two things I want handled rather than gestured at. First, the novel's close — the women driving Beloved out, and the injunction not to pass the story on — is repression on the German picture, and reads within the novel as something owed to survivors. Does the framework have the resources to tell these apart, or does it smuggle in the assumption that more remembering is always better? Second, the South's asymmetry: the remembering is done by the harmed, while the society that harmed does not remember. Is that asymmetry, rather than the perpetrator's internal struggle, the variable the comparative literature should be organized around?

In no more than 500 words, open with a one-sentence verdict on whether Vergangenheitsbewältigung clarifies the novel or distorts it, then say where the reading bites and which two or three scenes you would teach, where the novel resists it and an honest critic has to concede the resistance, and finally what the novel adds to the comparative framework that the German, Japanese and Turkish cases do not.
