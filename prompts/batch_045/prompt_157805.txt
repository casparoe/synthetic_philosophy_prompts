You are a professional philosopher who works on philosophy of action and the nature of intention. I would like a careful, point-by-point adjudication of an argument I overheard last week, so please think it through before writing: begin by mapping the logical space — the distinct claims about intention that the dispute turns on (whether intending entails believing that one will act; whether a bond or other commitment device is a way of intending or a substitute for intending; whether intention tracks reasons, expectations, or adopted plans) — and only then work through the exchange.

At a department colloquium dinner, two of my colleagues argued about a variant of Gregory Kavka's toxin puzzle that one of them had proposed. As you will recall, in the original case a billionaire pays you a large sum if, by midnight tonight, you have formed the intention to drink tomorrow a vial of toxin that will make you miserably ill for a day but has no lasting effects; the money is paid for the intention alone, and you are free to change your mind in the morning. In their variant, the offer is repeated every week for a year, the billionaire verifies intentions with a perfectly reliable intention-detector (so no bluffing and no fooling yourself), and each week you may, if you wish, post a $50,000 bond that you forfeit if you fail to drink the following morning.

(1) Alan: With the bond available the case is trivial. Post it and drinking is plainly rational tomorrow morning, so forming the intention tonight is no more puzzling than intending to go to the dentist. The classic puzzle gets its bite entirely from stipulating away every ordinary device people use to make an unwelcome action rational, so it shows nothing about intention itself — only that an agent stripped of reasons to act is an agent who cannot intend.

(2) Bea: That is too quick, and it concedes the interesting point. The bond moves you in the morning by threatening a forfeit, not by your having settled on a course of action; it is a substitute for an intention, not a case of forming one. And it concedes that unbound you could not intend. What the pure case shows is that intention is constitutively tied to what you expect to do: where there is no expectation of acting, there is no intention to be formed.

(3) Alan: Then drop the bond. In the iterated version there is still a reason to drink: drinking once makes you the kind of agent who can believe of himself that he will drink, which is what unlocks the remaining fifty weeks of payments — one day of misery against a far larger sum. So there is a reason to act, the intention becomes formable, and the single-shot case therefore shows only that intention requires some reason to act, not that it requires believing that you will act.

(4) Bea: Your own solution confirms my claim. The iterated case works only because it first supplies you with a reason to drink, which converts no-expectation into expectation; nothing in it is an intention formed while expecting not to act. And talk of reasons does not rescue the standard counterexamples: the smoker who intends to quit, or the would-be marathoner who knows his own record, is uncertain about himself or deceiving himself, not certain that he will not act. A plan you know for certain you will abandon is a fantasy, and it cannot do the work intentions do in explaining and coordinating action.

(5) Alan: But in the toxin case you are not certain either: knowing that you will have no reason to drink tomorrow is not knowing that you will not drink, since people act pointlessly and people act out of sheer consistency with what they have settled on. And in any case you are assuming what is at issue by building prediction into intention. On the planning conception, intention is a commitment adopted within practical reasoning, not a forecast of your own behaviour; it can survive doubt about whether you will follow through, and it is the plan rather than the prediction that explains your preparations.

Please adjudicate move by move — rate each of the five moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justifying each rating briefly — then say who is more right overall about what the toxin puzzle actually shows, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Give me a verdict rather than an overview of the dialectic.
