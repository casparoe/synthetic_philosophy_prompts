I'm writing a paper where I need to actually lean on the direction of time — I want to cash out "the future is open and the past is fixed" for a philosophy-of-action audience, in a way that isn't just hand-waving at entropy going up. Before I commit to a theory of the arrow I want the spec, not the candidates. So: write me the desiderata list — what does a theory of time's arrow have to deliver to be usable for that purpose, i.e. to underwrite the practical asymmetry (memory vs. prediction, intervening vs. merely finding out, being responsible for what happens later but not earlier)?

With each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, tell me which ones you think are non-negotiable and which are just prejudices of one camp, and be explicit about what's being asked for: is unifying all the arrows (thermodynamic, radiative/retarded-radiation, knowledge, agency, causation) a real desideratum or an aesthetic preference? Is "don't build the asymmetry into the fundamental dynamics" still required given the small T-violating corner of particle physics, or is it a hangover from a certain picture of laws? Does the theory have to *explain* the low-entropy initial state, or is positing it as a boundary condition acceptable, and is "brute posit" a failure or a legitimate stopping point? Does it need a distinguished present, or is a pure entropy gradient enough?

Two cases I want forced into the list, because I suspect they separate real requirements from camp loyalty:

- Twin Flask: stipulate two thermally isolated pockets that share a low-entropy state in the middle and whose entropy gradients therefore run in opposite temporal directions, each containing observers who remember "toward" the shared state and deliberate away from it. Must an adequate theory be able to make sense of this at all? Must it let each observer be right about which way is past? Or is ruling it out (on grounds of instability, or of what counts as a possible world for us) itself a requirement?
- The Long Afternoon: a universe at heat death — entropy essentially maximal, only tiny equilibrium fluctuations. Does time still have a direction there, and does the theory owe us an answer that doesn't just say "no arrows, so no question"? If it says the arrow is a local, gradient-relative affair, does it still deliver the practical asymmetry to an agent in a fluctuation?

Also flag any subset of the desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied — I suspect there's a version of "you can't get an arrow from nowhere" here: the asymmetry has to live in the laws, in the boundary conditions, or in us, and each placement costs you some other item on the list. If that's the real content of the list, say so; the list being a result is fine by me. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding popular candidates — my money is on the anti-anthropic one (the requirement that the theory not explain our asymmetry by selecting for observers who happen to sit on an entropy slope, i.e. that it not predict that we're probably minimal fluctuations surrounded by equilibrium), but argue it.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases. At the end, a short paragraph or small table scoring the usual contenders against your list is enough — the low-entropy-initial-condition/Past Hypothesis view, the symmetric picture where the arrow reverses if the universe recollapses, the fluctuation-plus-anthropic-selection picture, the "the asymmetry is in us, not in the world" perspectival view, and the cosmological pictures that try to generate the low-entropy patch dynamically. Don't give me a reading list or name-check papers; I want the spec.
