My opening move. I think the obfuscated-arguments objection has been misdescribed even by people who accept it. The danger is not that a dishonest debater can bury one bad step in a long argument — cross-examination can localize a flaw, and I will grant you that. The danger is at the level of the judge's own learning signal. On a question hard enough to matter, the judge generally cannot tell whether the winning argument was sound; it can only tell which argument was more convincing. So every bit of training pressure placed on the debaters selects for the persuasive surface of an argument rather than its validity, and every apparent upgrade to the setup — longer arguments, denser technical vocabulary, more intermediate lemmas the judge must accept on trust — degrades that signal further. Call this the substitution problem: the protocol's objective silently substitutes an easy-to-measure proxy, having won the debate, for the target, having led the judge to the truth. If the failure lives in the objective rather than in a class of adversarial inputs, then longer debates, stronger debaters and more compute should make it worse rather than better.

Answer that, and then tell me what, on your account, makes debate anything more than RLHF with extra steps.
