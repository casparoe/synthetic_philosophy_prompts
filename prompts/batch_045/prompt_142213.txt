You are a professional philosopher with a joint appointment in moral philosophy and clinical bioethics, and you have taught a graduate seminar on comparative ethics in which Confucian and other East Asian accounts of the person are read against Anglo-American liberalism. I want you to work carefully and in stages. Before you write anything for me to read, brainstorm: make a private scratch list of every distinct metaphysical, ethical, or merely pragmatic thesis that could in principle explain the pattern of practices described below — aim for six to ten candidates, including ones you will later discard — and note next to each one which of the four data points it explains well, which it explains badly, and which it cannot explain at all. Then write the final answer, and be explicit wherever your reconstruction depends on a sociological or legal claim about East Asian or Western hospitals that I have not supplied, rather than quietly asserting it.

Here is the material. I work in health policy for a system that runs teaching hospitals in both East Asia and North America, and I keep running into the following four things, which I have described as concretely as I can so that you do not need to look anything up.

First: in the clinical setting it remains common for a physician who has reached a grave diagnosis to disclose it to the patient's family first, to discuss prognosis and treatment options with them, and — when the family asks that the patient not be told, or be told less than the full truth — to comply, at least for a time. The family is treated as the natural recipient of the news, the natural decision-maker about treatment, and the party whose consent the physician seeks. Second: the same hospitals, when they enroll patients in clinical research, require the patient's own signature on a consent form addressed to him or her individually, in the first person, with no formal role for relatives; a research ethics committee that learned a son had signed on his father's behalf would halt the study. Third: the family that is said to decide is in practice usually represented by one member — often an elder son or the household's de facto head — who speaks for everyone else; there is rarely any recorded deliberation, any requirement that other members concur, or any mechanism for resolving disagreement within the family. Fourth: several of these hospitals have moved to releasing laboratory values, radiology reports, and clinical notes to patients' phones as soon as they are filed, and in the North American sites this release is mandatory and delayed release is penalized, so that a patient can read a rising tumor marker on a screen days before any physician, let alone any relative, has spoken to him.

Take as a running example a fifty-eight-year-old man with a new diagnosis of metastatic gastric cancer, whose wife and elder son ask the surgical team not to tell him the stage of the disease, and who is simultaneously being screened for a trial that would require his own signature and would make his labs visible to him through the portal.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of whatever theory of the person and the community this pattern of practices presupposes, and an honest account of how much of it is really presupposed. Specifically:

1. Reconstruct the implicit view as charitably as you can — the account of persons, families, interests, and authority on which all four data points make sense together. I have heard terms like relational autonomy, the Confucian conception of the person as constituted by roles, and the idea of the family as a corporate agent; I do not know which of these, if any, the practice actually needs, or how they differ in what they imply.

2. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and tell me where it is underdetermined. In particular: is the family-first practice really committed to any non-individualist *metaphysics* of the person, or could a thoroughly individualist value theory explain it — for instance by treating it as ordinary paternalism plus an empirical claim that truth-telling predictably harms this patient, or as a response to the fact that relatives will bear the caregiving and financial burden? And would a defender of the practice be right to insist that the dispute is about the metaphysics at all?

3. Address the apparent inconsistency between the clinical limb and the research limb: the same institution, often the same patient, treated as a unit with his family in one room and as a solitary authorizer in the next. Is there a reconciliation available that is not merely an artifact of research governance having been imported from abroad, and if there is, does the reconciliation survive the fact that the research form is signed by the very person the family is supposedly protecting?

4. Give me a discriminating test I cannot run myself. I have noticed that the family-first practice seems not to settle whether a family may override a previously competent patient's own written refusal of treatment, or whether a patient who clearly says in advance "tell me everything and tell my son nothing" gets his way. Tell me which answers to which further questions would distinguish the genuinely relational or corporate-agency readings from plain paternalism by whoever holds power in the household, why each answer points where it does, and what exactly I would have to find out in a given jurisdiction to settle it.

5. Finally, say what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by the stricter individualist one — and, symmetrically, whether Western informed-consent practice is really as atomistic as it claims, given its own surrogacy rules, its therapeutic-privilege exceptions, its permission for patients to delegate disclosure, and its use of socially indexed standards for what counts as a material risk. I am suspicious that the contrast my colleagues draw between a relational East and an atomic West is doing less work than they think, but I cannot say precisely where it breaks.

Write this as a memo of about 1,100 to 1,400 words, with short section headings matching my five questions and a one-paragraph bottom line at the end stating what you think the practice is really committed to and what is merely contingent. Do not cite or recommend any reading; I want the argument, not the literature.
