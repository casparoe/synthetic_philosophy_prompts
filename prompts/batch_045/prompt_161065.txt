I am working my way through Bertrand Russell's 1905 paper "On Denoting" and have arrived at a reading of two passages that I want tested before I build an argument on it. Please answer closely and technically: reason from the quoted sentences and from the examples rather than by recalling how the debate about descriptions later went, and if you help yourself to a distinction Russell did not make in 1905, say plainly that you are going beyond the text.

The first passage is from the opening section:

"Thus a phrase is denoting solely in virtue of its form. We may distinguish three cases: (1) A phrase may be denoting, and yet not denote anything; e.g., 'the present King of France'. (2) A phrase may denote one definite object; e.g., 'the present King of England' denotes a certain man. (3) A phrase may denote ambiguously; e.g. 'a man' denotes not many men, but an ambiguous man."

Some paragraphs later, after the schema for 'everything', 'nothing' and 'something', he writes:

"'C(a man)' means ' "C(x) and x is human" is not always false'. This leaves 'a man', by itself, wholly destitute of meaning, but gives a meaning to every proposition in whose verbal expression 'a man' occurs."

My reading is this. Russell is not claiming that an indefinite description is vague about which man is in question; he is claiming that it contributes no object at all to the proposition, and that the only thing to which a meaning is assigned is a whole proposition, by a rule that binds a wholly undetermined variable. Case (3) — the notorious "ambiguous man" — is then just the observation that, since the phrase supplies no object, there is nothing in the proposition of which one could ask "which man?"; the ambiguity is in the relation between words and world, not in a constituent of the proposition. If that is right, the very feature that lets Russell say we can think about a centre of mass we are never acquainted with should also make trouble wherever a later sentence or a later occurrence of the phrase needs to pick up what the first one introduced. I have tried to isolate that trouble in two invented cases; I suspect the first is a genuine defect and the second only an incompleteness of presentation, but I am not confident.

Case I. "A sentry was posted at the north gate. He was found dead at dawn." Suppose two sentries were posted and exactly one was found dead. Both sentences seem assertible. But if the pronoun is itself a denoting phrase on the pattern Russell suggests elsewhere — something like "the sentry posted at the north gate" — uniqueness fails and the second sentence is false. Worse, it is hard to see what else the second sentence could be about: the first proposition has, on Russell's own principle, no constituent corresponding to the phrase, so there is no object for the pronoun to inherit, and meaning is assigned proposition by proposition, never to a phrase that might be shared between two propositions.

Case II. "The president signed the treaty the president had opposed." Suppose one president signed a treaty that an earlier president had opposed. Read with the description's content held fixed, uniqueness fails over presidents at all times and the sentence is false; the true reading seems to require completing the same surface phrase differently at its two occurrences, and Russell's dictum that 'the' strictly involves uniqueness (with the aside that where several sons are in question one should rather say "a son") seems to push us toward the indefinite, which lands us back in Case I.

Please do four things. First, correct my reading of the two quoted passages where it is wrong, and say specifically what "an ambiguous man" is meant to be and what work "is not always false" is doing. Second, for each case, set out what Russell's apparatus actually delivers, in his own idiom where possible and in symbols where that is clearer, and say whether my verdict about the truth value is the one the theory forces. Third, say which of the two cases, if either, is a real defect in the theory rather than a gap in Russell's exposition. Fourth, tell me whether the scope distinction Russell draws later in the paper — the one usually labelled in terms of primary and secondary occurrences — rescues either case, and whether it does any work at all outside negation and outside empty descriptions: consider "The winner might not have won", where the description has a satisfier and the sentence contains no negation.

Use symbols freely; aim for roughly seven hundred words.
