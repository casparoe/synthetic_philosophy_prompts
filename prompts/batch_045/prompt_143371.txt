Working on a short internal memo about what I've been calling commitment races — situations where two or more parties are each trying to be the first to lock themselves into something costly and hard to reverse (a public pledge, an irreversible capital decision, an option signed away), and where what you end up with depends on arriving first and being seen to arrive first rather than on arriving at all. Before I circulate it I'd like someone to check whether the set of things I'm committed to actually hangs together, because I can't tell where the strain is. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) What makes something a genuine commitment rather than a statement of intent is that backing out would cost you something. No cost of reversal, no commitment — so credibility and irreversibility rise and fall together.

(2) The bad thing about commitment races is mostly the racing, not the winning: parties lock in earlier and harder than they would have on their own timetable. So the institutional goal should be to take the prize out of arriving first — simultaneous-move mechanisms, or commitments made early but only revealed or verified later, or flat penalties on early movers.

(3) Given that the others are racing, racing is rational. I don't blame the individual party who joins, and I don't think someone who sits it out and gets taken advantage of has done anything especially admirable.

(4) But I also want to say that in a race like this each party is under an obligation not to join, and that the everyone-races outcome is one nobody should help bring about.

(5) Commitments whose *timing* was forced on you by someone else's race are defective. I want a cooling-off / voidability rule for those, something like how we treat agreements entered into under pressure.

(6) Binding your own future self is autonomy-enhancing rather than autonomy-destroying — tying yourself to the mast is an exercise of self-government, so we should make pre-commitment easier, not harder.

(7) The case I keep testing all of this against: Firm A moves first with a loud and genuinely irreversible pledge; Firm B was quietly going to do more or less the same thing about three weeks later, and instead waits, keeps its options, and free-rides on A's pledge. My verdict is that A should be held to the letter of what it said, and that what B did is the objectionable part of that story.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like it, ordered by how severe the real ones are, and for each real one say what would have to give and what giving it up costs me. Also: what's the single revision that cleans up the most? I don't have a favourite to drop, and I'd rather know if (7) is the load-bearing problem than be told the whole thing is fine.
