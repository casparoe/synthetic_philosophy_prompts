So this has suddenly stopped being an abstract topic for me. My partner works at a human rights org and we had a genuinely bad dinner-table fight about whether they should use the word in a statement, and about a week later my grandmother's people came up, and now I'm the one my dad is arguing with because he's putting together a unit for his high school class and the district's lawyer told him he basically can't use the word unless a court has. My grandmother was Vardani — a highland herding people in Ardania, transhumant, moved flocks up and down between summer and winter pasture, clan councils, sacred groves, a language with no written standard. Everyone in her immediate family physically survived the twentieth century. But between roughly 1958 and 1970 the state collectivized the flocks and settled people in valley villages, sent the kids to boarding schools where they were punished for speaking Vardan, renamed every village and stream, dissolved the clan councils, registered everyone under new administrative surnames, and then flooded the main grove valley for a reservoir. A few hundred people died in the resettlement and the famine after it. There was no massacre. By the 1990s there were maybe a couple thousand speakers left, mostly old, and nobody under forty herds. My grandmother's own grandkids don't speak it.

Here's the thing I keep getting stuck on. The comparator people always bring up: in 1974 the same state shot about five thousand men of military age in one district during a counterinsurgency, and there's no serious dispute that it happened, but the evidence only supports a counterinsurgency rationale, not an aim at the group as such. Nobody calls that genocide. Everyone calls the earlier program genocide, including me, even though almost nobody died in it. So the legal category, as I understand it, requires an intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial or religious group as such — I read that political and social groups got cut from the draft — and the intent standard is applied so strictly that the pattern of conduct has to be pretty much the only explanation that makes sense. But the listed acts aren't all killing: they include deliberately inflicting conditions of life calculated to destroy the group, and forcibly transferring children to another group.

I'd like you to reconstruct what this practice actually presupposes, and I mean the practice as a whole, not the textbook summary. Concretely:

Why have this as a separate crime at all, given that crimes against humanity already cover mass killing and persecution? What does carving out a special name presuppose — that destroying a group is a distinct kind of evil and not just a lot of individual evils, that a group is the sort of thing that can be destroyed while its members go on living, that groups have value not reducible to the welfare of the people in them? Or is the special category really only doing evidentiary, expressive or deterrent work, so that an individualist who thinks only persons matter could sign on to the exact same legal shape? I want to know which parts the practice strictly needs and which parts it merely sits well with, and where it's genuinely underdetermined so several theories would license it.

And is it internally consistent? You can be guilty of it without killing anyone, and you can kill thousands without being guilty of it. Is the object of the crime the group's survival, or is it really a certain hostile will in the perpetrator? Does the list of protected groups presuppose something metaphysical about which groups are "real" — I've seen courts lean on ideas like permanent and stable groups — or is that just a device for keeping the category from swallowing every atrocity?

The test I keep falling back on, which someone at my partner's org half-joked about and which I think is actually serious: a group has been destroyed when there is no longer anyone who could hand on what it was for them to hand on. Call it the handing-on test. Does that capture what the legal practice is really reaching for, or is it a different theory wearing the same word? Where does it come apart from the law as it stands — does it let in things the law excludes, like destroying a political movement or a religious order, or a group that dissolves because everyone emigrates for economic reasons with no perpetrator at all? And what would have to change in how the category is applied if you dropped the group-value presupposition and ran it on the handing-on test instead?

Please be blunt about which of my instincts don't hold together, and please answer in American English — my partner's British spellings are driving me up the wall.
