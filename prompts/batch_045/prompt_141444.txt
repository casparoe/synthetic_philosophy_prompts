I'm writing a seminar paper on the dispute between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function, and I want one section of it argued as strongly as it can honestly be argued. I'd like you to write that section. Please assume the dispute is familiar to both of us and don't spend words restating it: the selected-effects account ties an item's function to a history in which tokens of that item were selected *for* producing that effect, and the systemic-capacity account ties an item's function to the contribution the item makes to a capacity of some containing system we have singled out.

The section should hang on a case I've constructed rather than on the standard ones, because swampman, the first heart, vestigial organs and exaptations all pull in directions the dialectic has already mapped out. Call it the recurrent somatic trait. The stipulations are these:

In a lineage of long-lived colonial marine invertebrates, every individual reliably develops a specialised cell type — call them lancet cells — that fires a toxic harpoon at encroaching competitors and thereby raises the colony's fitness substantially. Lancet cells are not inherited. Each generation they arise de novo, from a somatic mutation at an extremely fragile site that ordinary development re-creates in each individual. The fragile site itself is heritable and is under stabilising selection precisely because it reliably yields lancet cells. But no token lancet cell ever causes the production of another token lancet cell; no lineage of lancet cells has ever been sorted by selection across generations; and the population is genetically uniform with respect to the mutation, so there is no variation at the level of the trait for selection to act on. The arrangement is not pure fantasy — the somatic generation of lymphocyte receptor diversity, followed by selection among the resulting clones within a single animal's lifetime, is a real cousin of it, and I'm stipulating the remaining details only to make the point sharp.

The verdict I want defended is the etiological one: lancet cells have no proper function, and withholding a function here is a virtue of the selected-effects account rather than a counterexample to it. I want the argument to do four things.

First, say explicitly what function attributions are supposed to *do* — what explanatory, classificatory or predictive job they earn their keep by — and show that refusing to attribute a function to lancet cells follows from that job rather than from a bare intuition about the case. Then say what we should say about lancet cells instead: what category they fall into, and why that is not merely a terminological concession that gives back everything the causal-role theorist wanted.

Second, deal with the escape hatch. The etiological theorist's usual move in cases like this is to let an item borrow a function from the mechanism that produces it. That move looks tailor-made for my case, since the fragile site plainly does have the selected function of producing lancet cells. So either show that the move does not apply here, or show that applying it would be ad hoc or would prove too much — for instance by assigning proper functions to items on both sides of the debate agree are mere byproducts. I need this handled head-on, not footnoted.

Third, handle the objection from normativity. The standard pitch for the selected-effects account is that it, unlike its rival, makes room for malfunction: a heart that cannot pump is defective, and defectiveness needs a standard that causal contributions alone don't supply. But if lancet cells have no proper function, then a lancet cell that misfires is not defective, which seems wrong, and I may be sawing off the branch I'm sitting on. Tell me how the position I'm asking for survives that.

Fourth, give the causal-role theorist their best reply and answer it. Their best reply, as I see it, is that function attributions in working biology are answers to how a system currently works, not claims about how a trait came to be there: the field biologist who writes that the function of the lancet cell is colony defence is not making a claim about selection history at all, and my verdict silently reads her as doing so.

Aim for roughly 800 to 1,000 words. Argue from the stipulations and from the internal structure of the two accounts; I don't want any citations, and appeals to what some named author has said won't help me.

Two further requests. Close with a short coda, no more than 150 words: flag the single premise you are least confident in, and say what observation about actual biological practice would most quickly change your mind. And before any of that — if you think the case as I've stipulated it doesn't really separate the two accounts, or that the verdict I'm asking for can only be defended by sliding between two senses of the word function, say so plainly and up front, and tell me what you think the section should argue instead. I would much rather have that than a well-written section defending a thesis you take to be false.
