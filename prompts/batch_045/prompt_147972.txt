I've been going in circles on the special composition question for a couple of weeks — a friend from a reading group we did last year on personal identity (which derailed into mereology somewhere around session three and never really recovered) dared me to write down why nihilism bother me, and what came out is an argument I'm pretty sure proves too much. Here it is:

(1) Composition happens. The particles arranged human-wise in my chair right now compose something, namely me; the cells in my body compose organs; my organs compose me. That much I won't give up.
(2) So the special composition question has a non-trivial general answer: there is some condition C such that, for any things, the xs compose something iff C(xs).
(3) Every candidate C anyone has actually offered — contact, fastening, cohesion, joint participation in a single life, figuring together in one causal-explanatory story — has borderline cases, and not by accident: any C short of always or never will have them, because the underlying relations come in degrees and any cutoff we pick would be arbitrary.
(4) So if C is the answer, then in a borderline case the claim that there is something the xs compose is indeterminate, since C holding is the only thing in the vicinity that could settle it.
(5) But it cannot be indeterminate whether there is a thing of a given sort. For if it were, there would have to be some object y such that it is indeterminate whether y is composed of the xs — an object with fuzzy boundaries — and there is a familiar argument that there can be no such object: if it were indeterminate whether y and z were the same object, then y would have the property of being such that it is indeterminate whether it is z, while z would not have that property, so by Leibniz's law y and z would be distinct after all, which contradicts the supposition that their identity was the indeterminate thing.
(6) So C is either always or never: either every plurality composes something, or no plurality of more than one composes anything.
(7) Not always. On unrestricted composition there is an object made of my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower, and another made of the left half of that shoe and the right half of the moon, and so on without end; positing all of those buys no explanatory power and no genuine unity, and composition stops doing any metaphysical work.
(8) So never: everything that exists is a simple.
(9) But then there are no organisms, no cells, no tables, no galaxies. I am not among the things there are, and every special science is, strictly speaking, about nothing. I can't accept that, and I'd definately rather give up (7) than (1).

The moves I can see, and why none of them satisfies me: he says (2) is the hole and that composition facts could just be brute, with no unifying condition behind them — but then it's a brute inexplicable fact that composition lines up so neatly with what our language happens to pick out, which smells like a coincidence. He also says (5) is the hole, and offers a picture on which there are many equally good candidate objects in the borderline case and no fact of the matter which of them we're talking about, so that what's vague is reference rather than existence. He even puts it as: it can be indeterminate whether there exists an object composed of the xs, even though there is no object that is indeterminately composed of them. That last bit sounds like word games to me — like moving the word indeterminate around inside the sentence and declaring victory — and I cant tell whether it's a real third option, a relabeling of denying (5), or something that dissolves the original question instead of answering it.

What pushed me from idle curiosity to actually asking: I tried rerunning the argument with compose swapped for something like these processes jointly realize one computation, because the case I actually care about is the sharded one — a model split across a few dozen GPUs, or, sci-fi version, an emulation of a whole brain spread over three data centers on two continents. Run the same steps and you get: either every jumble of processes trivially realizes a computation, or none does, or (on his fix) it can be indeterminate how many computations are running in the room. The third one freaks me out in a way that indeterminate tables don't: if it can be indeterminate whether there's one process or several, it looks like it can be indeterminate whether there's one experience being had or a dozen, and I don't know whether to read that as a reductio of the fix or as a cost I should just pay. So: which step goes, why did it look fine, what does each available rejection cost me in general, and does the diagnosis transfer to the computation version cleanly, or is something separate going on there?
