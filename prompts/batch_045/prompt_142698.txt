I'm helping a regulatory agency choose one decision rule for cost-benefit analysis under risk: a single formula that staff economists will apply to hundreds of unrelated projects a year (vaccine approvals, dam safety, wildfire mitigation, biosecurity containment), with the resulting number published and open to legal challenge. Before I look at any candidate rule, I want the specification. What must a risk-weighting rule deliver to count as adequate for that job, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and which of those requirements are genuinely non-negotiable as opposed to the hobby-horses of one camp?

My draft list, with the case I have in mind for each:

1. Take a definite stand on the classic Allais and common-ratio patterns — accommodate them in the formalism, or give a principled account of why they are error. Silence is not acceptable.
2. Stay calibrated across stake sizes: modest, well-documented aversion to a small-stakes coin flip must not compound into refusing plainly favorable large-stakes gambles.
3. Give real weight to low-probability, irreversible, catastrophic losses, rather than letting them vanish because they are unlikely.
4. Avoid fanaticism — a vanishingly small chance of an astronomically large payoff must not automatically dominate every ordinary project — and avoid achieving this by a hard probability cutoff, since any cutoff can be defeated by slicing one risk into many smaller ones.
5. Behave consistently under changes of bracketing: a portfolio of independent projects should not get one verdict project-by-project and the opposite verdict as a single aggregate distribution, or if it does, the rule must say which bracketing is authoritative and why.
6. Say whether it evaluates a single decision or the policy of following the rule repeatedly, and what it recommends when a plan that is optimal from the outset is one the agency will later want to abandon midway.
7. Say whose risk attitude enters the formula when the people bearing a risk are not the people receiving the benefits, and when citizens disagree among themselves; it must not quietly substitute the agency's own attitude for the social one.
8. Pin the risk attitude down by a fixed, publicly specified calibration procedure rather than fitting it to each decision, so that a verdict cannot be retrofitted to an intuition.

Tell me which of these are real desiderata for this job and which I should drop. Flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I think 3 and 4 are jointly unsatisfiable, and that 5 is unsatisfiable together with matching ordinary narrow-bracketed choices, but I want to know if either suspicion is wrong. Name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive candidates, and add any requirement I have missed that would disqualify a rule outright. Then, in a few lines each and no more, score three contenders: expected utility with a concave utility index, a risk-weighted rule that re-weights probabilities rather than only outcomes, and a safety-first rule that maximizes the worst outcome.
