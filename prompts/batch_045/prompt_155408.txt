Write me a two-page prospectus for a term paper. The context: I'm a first-year PhD student in a seminar on the metaphysics of propositions and truth, the paper is roughly 6,000 words due at the end of a single semester, and the seminar leader has asked that the prospectus state a question with a determinate answer rather than a topic to be explored.

My half-formed idea: Bradley's regress is normally presented as a single argument with three exits — deny that relations are among the constituents of a proposition or fact, treat facts as primitive unities that aren't composed of their constituents at all, or hold that some constituents are unsaturated in a way that lets them bind the others together. I suspect two different arguments are being run together: a compositional one (a plurality of constituents is not yet a unity, so something more is needed) and an explanatory one (whatever extra item is offered as the unifier, we can ask what makes it unify those constituents, and any answer seems to reinstall the very structure we were trying to explain). My question is whether any of the standard exits, once the compositional reading is set aside, actually addresses the explanatory reading, and whether they even need to. I also want to take seriously a fourth option the seminar hasn't discussed: an "infinitist" reply, analogous to infinitist treatments of the epistemic regress, on which the unity of a proposition is grounded in a non-terminating chain of further unifying relations with no base level, and on which the chain's being endless is not a defect. The hard part I want the prospectus to face up to is what such a view owes us about why propositions still have determinate truth conditions rather than merely indefinitely many partial explanations of their unity.

Please include: the question stated precisely enough that a reader could tell which way it had been answered; why the existing positions leave it open; what I would actually do, month by month, given that this is meant to be an argumentative paper and not a survey; the range of possible conclusions and which considerations would decide between them; and what would count as the project failing. Be honest about whether this is realistic for one semester, and say what you'd cut if it isn't. Refer to positions and arguments by their usual names if helpful, but no citations or bibliography — the seminar leader has asked us to leave those out at this stage, and I'll fill them in myself.
