im in a seminar on global catastrophic risk governance this term, and last week we did the unilateralist's curse — the idea that when any one of several agents can unilaterally bring about some big, partly irreversible outcome, the outcome that actually happens is the one the most optimistic / lowest-threshold agent was willing to trigger, so the expected result is skewed below what the median agent would have signed off on, and so you want some kind of norm of conformity rather than everyone following their own conscience. (we skimmed the bostrom-ord-tegmark-sandberg paper.) I want to write my final paper on the Asilomar conference on recombinant DNA as a case study, and I'd like you to do the analysis with me rather than summarize either thing at me.

Facts I'm stipulating so you don't need to look anything up (and I don't want the paper to turn on a contested detail): in 1974 a group of molecular biologists led by Paul Berg publicly called for a voluntary pause on certain recombinant-DNA experiments, pending an international meeting; a National Academy of Sciences committee backed that call; roughly 140 people — mostly biologists, plus some lawyers and physicians — met at Asilomar in February 1975 and produced voluntary guidelines that matched containment levels to estimated risk; the NIH turned those into formal guidelines in 1976; and by the end of the decade most of the restrictions were gone, because the risks came to look much smaller than the worst of the 1974 guesses. That's what I'm analyzing.

What I want, in this order:

1. The mapping, done carefully. Who are the "agents" in the curse model here — individual PIs, labs, whole national research communities, funders? What counts as a unilateral act, and what's the shared downside? And how well do the preconditions of the curse actually hold: enough agents, real dispersion of judgment about a common value, irreversibility, nobody able to just block everyone else. Be honest about which ones only partly hold, because I think that's where the interesting part is.

2. What the model predicts should have happened. My guess is it predicts the pause is unstable, the most optimistic lab quietly defects, and the guidelines get set at the lowest common denominator. Say whether that's really what the model predicts and how sharp the prediction is, not just that restraint is fragile in general.

3. What actually happened, and which features of the episode the model explains versus misses or actively misdescribes.

4. What the episode does to the theory — confirmation, anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated. There are three things I want you to actually chew on instead of nodding along:

(a) The restraint version. A moratorium is the mirror image of a unilateral act: it only works if everyone complies, and defection is unilateral. So is there a symmetric curse on the restraint side, where whoever defects captures all the upside and shares the downside? And if there is, does the same structure that generates the curse also make its own remedy unstable — or are "the most optimistic lab acts" and "the most impatient lab defects" genuinely different structures? I can't tell whether these are the same argument in different hats. Also: the restrictions were later lifted because the pessimism looks overblown in hindsight, which makes me wonder whether the 1974 pause was itself drawn from a tail — the most risk-averse voices setting policy for everyone.

(b) Dispersion is endogenous. The curse model takes agents' estimates as given and dispersed and then points at the selection effect. But Asilomar looks like a machine for shrinking dispersion: pooling what little evidence existed, building a shared risk vocabulary and a graded containment scheme. If that's what it was, is the curse really a result about individual rationality, or is it a result about the absence of an institution — and does that change what the remedy should be (a conformity norm vs. mandatory disclosure vs. liability vs. something else)?

(c) Errors vs. stakes. Is the selection effect here driven by agents having different estimates of one shared value, or by agents facing very different shares of the upside and downside (career priority, prestige, exposure)? I think it matters a lot, because if it's the second one, a norm of conformity looks like the wrong fix entirely.

5. Where the model is silent — and I'd much rather get a short "the model has nothing to say about X" than a paragraph of hand-waving. I already suspect it has nothing to say about how thresholds get set in the first place, about timing and how long deliberation should take, about the fact that the pause itself changed who counted as an agent (lawyers, physicians, the public, and later the whole oversight apparatus), and about the possibility that the pause accelerated the field by legitimizing it. Tell me if I'm wrong about any of those and add the ones I've missed.

If there's room at the very end: everyone in my seminar invokes Asilomar as a precedent for AI governance, and I want one paragraph on whether the analysis above makes that analogy better or worse.
