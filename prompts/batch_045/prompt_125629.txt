Consider an agent with a known task reward, operating in an environment whose dynamics are known and deterministic. We want it to pursue its task while avoiding rearrangements of the world that nobody asked for, but we want to state that constraint without first writing down a full theory of what matters.

The most developed strategy for doing this is structural. Fix a finite list of auxiliary probe reward functions R_1, …, R_n on states, intended not as an encoding of our values but as generic stand-ins for things some agent might later want. For each i, let V_i(s) be the best discounted value the agent could secure from state s if it were instead pursuing R_i — its attainable utility for R_i. Let s⁰ be a baseline state, usually the one that would have resulted had the agent never acted. Then penalize arriving in state s by an amount that grows with the discrepancy between the vectors (V_1(s), …, V_n(s)) and (V_1(s⁰), …, V_n(s⁰)).

My question is not the familiar worry that the probe rewards smuggle in a value specification, though I would like you to say how much work that worry really does. My question is whether, even granting a canonical and value-free supply of probes, the invariances we can demand of such a measure leave a residue that is either arbitrary or value-laden — so that avoiding side effects never pins down a penalty.

Suppose we require the penalty to be unchanged when we: (i) replace any R_i by a positive affine rescaling of itself; (ii) permute the list, pad it with copies of entries already present, or delete such copies; (iii) adjoin a probe whose attainable-utility values agree on s and s⁰.

A small illustration. In a grid world, take R_north = reach the north wall and R_east = reach the east wall, with the centre as baseline. Reaching the north wall and reaching the east wall are related by a symmetry, so any measure meeting (i) and (ii) must score them alike. But reaching the north-east corner moves both quantities by the same amount as either singly: the invariances are silent between twice as bad, equally bad, root-two times as bad, and so on. What survives them is a choice of norm on the vector of value changes; and separately a choice of scale for each coordinate (the range of attainable values? reachability from the baseline? spread under some reference distribution?); and separately a choice of baseline.

Please work through the following, arguing from the structure of the problem rather than surveying a literature.

1. Explain as cleanly as you can why symmetries of this kind underdetermine the measure. Invariances license only judgments of the form these two changes are equally significant; a family of such judgments fixes a quantity at most up to monotone transformation, or, where changes compose, up to a choice of norm on the resulting quotient. Say precisely what free parameters remain.

2. Show that these are not merely technical conventions to be settled by taste. Note in particular that invariance under padding the list with duplicates pushes aggregation toward a maximum rather than a sum, hence toward indifference about how many independent things the agent disturbed, which looks like the wrong verdict; while a sum restores that sensitivity at the cost of violating (ii). Say what each remaining parameter silently decides — for instance whether many small disturbances across many probes are worse than one large disturbance to one probe, or whether irreversible changes count for more than reversible ones.

3. Assess the strongest pessimistic claim: structural features of the environment — its dynamics, its symmetries, the agent's own action set and discount factor — can deliver invariances but never magnitudes; magnitudes require a weighting over what counts as a large change, and that weighting is either arbitrary, in which case the penalty tracks artifacts of our formalization rather than anything about the world, or else is precisely the specification of value we hoped to avoid. If that is right, low impact is not a constraint that can be stated weakly.

4. Two objections to test that claim against. First, the measure's dependence on the agent's own powers: a vase broken by an agent that could also have restored it may register less impact than the same physical breakage by an agent that could not, since attainable utility is measured relative to what this agent can still bring about. Defend that dependence as principled, or use it to argue the machinery is tracking the wrong thing. Second, consider dropping magnitudes altogether and imposing only the invariances: require the agent to be indifferent between options the invariances declare equivalent, and otherwise let its task reward decide. Does this invariances-only version escape the dilemma, or does it collapse into near-total indifference because most pairs of options are never declared equivalent?

5. State plainly what you take the honest conclusion to be, and which of the desiderata above you would abandon.
