I am putting together the final session of a graduate seminar on self-knowledge that I co-teach with a senior colleague who has spent much of her career defending introspection as a genuine but sharply bounded epistemic source. I want to anticipate how she will rule on the case I have scheduled, and I want my commentary to engage the strongest version of her view rather than one I have conveniently reconstructed. Her verdicts so far are these; in each she is asked whether the subject's report is a case of genuine introspective knowledge.

A patient with arthritis says, while in pain, that there is a throbbing pain in his left knee: knowledge. A subject in a hallucination condition says that he seems to see a grey square in front of him, with nothing there: knowledge of his experience, though not of his environment. A blindsight patient, pressed to guess the orientation of a grating in his scotoma, discriminates well above chance while insisting that he sees nothing: not introspective knowledge, since on her view his guesses are not reports of an experience at all. Subjects asked to choose the more attractive of two faces and then to explain the choice offer fluent reasons that track a manipulation they cannot detect: not knowledge. In a masked-priming paradigm, yes-reports of having seen the prime track its effect on reaction time and collapse under a conservative response criterion: not knowledge of an experience, and the priming itself shows only subpersonal processing. A subject who performs a memory task under two levels of load and, immediately after each block, says which block felt more effortful tracks the manipulation without being able to say what she is responding to: knowledge. In an experience-sampling study, subjects probed a few seconds after an episode report the word they were just thinking of, while the same subjects asked an hour later fall to chance: knowledge in the first case, reconstruction in the second.

The case I have scheduled uses a design of my own. Subjects view a twelve-object array of common objects for 200 ms, followed immediately by a mask. On each trial they are first asked to name the objects they could identify, and then, after a standardized prompt that instructs them carefully to distinguish what was present in their visual experience from what they could name or recall afterwards, whether the array as a whole was present in experience in full detail. Almost all report full detail. Three further results: cued recall of an object they did not name is at chance even when the cue arrives 50 ms after the array; change detection for an unnamed object across two presentations is at chance; and when the same array is presented for 2 s instead, reports of full detail are unchanged while cued recall and change detection approach ceiling. That last result worries me, because the report appears insensitive to the very manipulation that changes everything else.

Predict her verdict on the 200 ms condition. Identify the rule she is implicitly running and the other rules that fit her first seven verdicts just as well, saying what each predicts here and which precedent is the closest analog. If she rules against the report, say which of her earlier verdicts she would have to qualify or abandon, and if she rules for it, say what she must think the cueing and change-detection results are measuring. Finally, tell me what further case would best separate the surviving rules: I have in mind either the mirror image, where an unexpected object draws the subject's gaze and primes later categorization while she denies having seen anything unusual, or a comparison between naive subjects and subjects with several hundred hours of training in describing their own experience. If neither discriminates, tell me how to redesign the study so that it does, and tell me plainly if my worry about the invariant report is itself confused.
