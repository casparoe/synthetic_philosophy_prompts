Me and my friend spent two hours last night going round in circles about how old nations actually are, and I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. I'm on the modernist side: Anderson's imagined communities, print capitalism, mass literacy, Hobsbawm's invented traditions — nations need that modern infrastructure, so they're a late-18th/19th-century thing, and the deep antiquity nationalists claim for themselves is part of the invention rather than evidence against it. He's with the ethnosymbolists, Anthony Smith's camp: nationalism is modern, fine, but nations aren't, because you can't mobilise people out of nothing — there has to be a premodern ethnie to work with, a named population with ancestry myths, shared memories, some link to a homeland and at least elite solidarity.

My hypothesis is that we're just talking past each other: 'nation' on my definition requires the modern machinery, on his thin definition any named community with an origin myth counts, so there's nothing left to fight about. But it didn't feel that clean, and his strongest move was one I couldnt answer. Suppose some 19th-century enthusiasts cobble together an orthography, a grammar, a few folk epics and a heroic origin story for a region, and eighty years later there's a national movement there. He says that only works because people already had some sense of being a people; I say the movement manufactured that sense and then read it back into the past. Both of us can tell a story about that case, which makes me think the real fight is somewhere else — about whether premodern material does causal work in mobilisation or is just raw material that any old past would have served as, or about which historical cases get to count as data in the first place.

So: is this a verbal dispute, a hidden empirical question, a disagreement about what counts as a case, or two different questions under one name? Argue for your diagnosis using what each side actually claims, and tell me what would count as progress given it — or whether we should expect none.
