I work in machine-learning evaluation, so my working vocabulary is log loss, Brier score, calibration, sharpness, and regret bounds rather than anything from philosophy — I have no formal training in the subject, so please define terms as you go, but do not soften the formalism on my account; I can read expected-value notation and I would rather have a precise statement than a comfortable one.

Here is what I have picked up, and what I want from you. There appears to be a research program that treats a credence function the way I treat a probabilistic forecaster: measure its distance from the truth with a proper scoring rule, then try to derive the norms governing belief — probabilism, updating by conditioning, deference to chances and to evidence — as theorems about maximizing expected accuracy. There also appears to be a rival sensibility holding that the primary objects of epistemic evaluation are not states of opinion but inquiries: which question to ask, which experiment to run, what to attend to, when to stop, whether to reopen a settled question. My impression is that these two camps have been arguing past each other, and I want the document that would end that.

Please draft the joint statement that a committed member of the accuracy camp and a committed member of the inquiry camp (I have seen the label zetetic for the second; use it if that is right) could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Constraints: no citations, no bibliographies, no named authors or papers. I cannot read the literature and I do not want to chase references I cannot check, so give me arguments and formulations only, with positions described rather than attributed. If any premise in what follows is false, or quietly assumes something that is itself at issue, say so inside the document and say what becomes of the argument once it is corrected.

Suggested structure:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section, roughly ten to fourteen items, covering what both camps can sign about the machinery itself: what propriety and convexity buy you, the difference between domination arguments and expected-accuracy arguments, the standing result that free evidence cannot lower expected score under a proper scoring rule, the distinction between synchronic and diachronic evaluation, and what counts as an admissible measure of accuracy. Include a few items stating what neither side is claiming, so the rest of the document is not spent on positions nobody holds.

2. A disagreements table. For each crux, give: the proposition the accuracy camp can sign, phrased as they would phrase it; the proposition the inquiry camp can sign, phrased as they would phrase it; whether the disagreement is genuine or merely verbal, and if verbal, what the substantive question underneath it is; a case, computation, or argument that both camps agree would bear on the crux; and then, written down in advance, what each camp predicts that case or computation will show. If a crux is formal, state what calculation would settle it; if it is conceptual, say plainly that it is conceptual and what kind of argument could still move it.

3. A short closing section on what would actually move each camp — not what ought to move them, but what each would in fact treat as a reason to shift.

4. A short section on failure conditions: what would count as this collaboration having produced nothing, as opposed to having produced a precise disagreement.

Cases I would like used as the shared tests, unless you have better ones:

- The Dogmatist: an agent whose credence in a false hypothesis is essentially one. By her own lights every experiment she could run has zero expected value, so an expected-accuracy argument appears to give her no reason whatsoever to inquire — yet she is, intuitively, exactly the agent who most needs to.
- The Lazy Calibrator: a forecaster who is perfectly calibrated, always reports the base rate, and never runs a discriminating test. Nothing seems wrong with her credences considered as a forecast; the question is whether anything in the accuracy framework can fault her policy of never asking anything.
- The Lucky Guesser: two agents end with identical credences, one after a well-designed inquiry and one by a guess that happened to land. Does the scorecard register any difference between them, and should it?
- The Slow Truth: two inquirers reach the same true conclusion, one in a day and one in a decade. Synchronic accuracy at the end is identical for both.

Cruxes I suspect matter, which you should restructure, split, or replace as you see fit: whether expected-accuracy arguments can only ever tell an agent what is better by her own lights, and what that implies for the Dogmatist; whether the choice among proper scoring rules is conventional, and how much of the program's output depends on that choice; whether norms attaching to products can be norms about processes at all; whether the accuracy camp can recover inquiry norms by moving to a diachronic or time-discounted measure of total inaccuracy, and whether doing so is a genuine convergence or a concession; and whether the residual dispute is about which objects epistemic norms govern rather than about what those norms require.

Aim for something a careful reader can get through in about fifteen minutes.
