Hey — I've been going back and forth on which disputes are really disputes and which are just fights about words, and I keep catching myself applying different standards in different cases. I'd like to know whether there's a principle that actually captures what I believe, or whether some of my verdicts just have to go.

The rough test I've been using: a dispute is merely verbal if you could hand both sides the other person's definition, have them read it, and the fight dissolves — nobody has to give up any belief about how the world is, they just both go "oh, fine, then I agree." Call it the swap test. It handles the easy cases, but I think it misclassifies a few and I can't tell whether to patch it or junk it.

My verdicts:

1. Hotdog/sandwich. Guy at work says a hotdog is a sandwich, I say it isn't. We agree about the bread, the sausage, the hinge, everything physical. Verdict: totally verbal, and kind of a waste of breath.

2. Brain death. My uncle, massive stroke, no brain activity, heart still going on the ventilator. My sister says he's alive, I say he isn't. We agree about every cell in his body. Verdict: not merely verbal, and it obviously matters. But by the swap test it should be verbal — hand her my definition of death and she'll agree he's dead under it, and vice versa. So the swap test is already in trouble, or I have to introduce a category of "verbal but not trivial," which feels like the concept coming apart in my hands.

3. Composition. My friend says there's a table in the room; I've read too much philosophy and say there are only particles arranged tablewise. We agree about all of them and their arrangement. Verdict: I want to say substantive — and it bugs me that I can't say why, because structurally this looks exactly like 1 and 2.

4. Pluto. Astronomer says it's not a planet, my nephew is furious. They agree about the orbit, the mass, the composition. Verdict: verbal. But I also want to say the astronomer's side is *better* — not more true, just better, like the definition earns its keep. I don't know how to fit "verbal, but one side is winning" into a picture where verbal disputes are ones where both sides can be faultlessly right.

5. Race in a medical risk model. One researcher wants the variable kept, the other wants it dropped. They agree on the genetics, the outcomes, all the social facts; neither denies anything the other asserts. Verdict: definitely not merely verbal, there's something real at stake — but the disagreement isn't factual either, it seems to be about which concept we ought to use. Someone told me this is called metalinguistic negotiation. Fine. But then what makes 5 real while 4 isn't? Both look like arguments about which concept to use, and I have opposite reactions.

6. The one that's actually breaking my brain. A says there's a table. B says there are only simples arranged tablewise. C says there is exactly one thing, the whole cosmos. B-vs-C feels genuinely incompatible to me in a way A-vs-B doesn't — like there's a real question about how many things there are and one of them is answering it wrong. And A-vs-C also feels like a real clash. But that makes "merely verbal" non-transitive, or worse, makes it look like I've got two different notions and I'm switching between them without noticing. Is it transitive on your account, and if not, what does that tell me?

7. Empty or mismatched concepts. Two people argue about whether witches exist: one means a woman who made a pact with the Devil and can fly, the other means a practitioner of a certain folk healing tradition. Verdict: I don't want to call it verbal at all — I want to say they aren't disagreeing, and that the first one is wrong about something. Is "not a genuine dispute" a third category, or does any principled account collapse it into one of the other two?

So: can you state one principle, or two or three rival candidates, that gets as many of these as possible? I'd like to know which case forces which clause, what the principle predicts for cases I haven't listed (three I'm curious about: the version of case 1 where the city tax code really does exempt sandwiches but not hotdogs; a dispute between two people who don't share a language and are working through a translation; and a modal one, like two people agreeing on what a given table is made of but disagreeing about whether that very table could have been made of ice), and — most useful part — which of my verdicts it can't swallow, and why you'd rather overturn my verdict than add another clause. Also: is the swap test salvageable by bolting on a "nothing important hangs on it" condition, or does that just smuggle the normative stuff in through the back door?
