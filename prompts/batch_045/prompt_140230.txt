One of my grad students shoved this at me after seminar on Tuesday and I have to say something useful about it on Thursday, and I can't find the bad step — which is irritating, because I don't accept the conclusion and my own view is more or less (1) plus (2).

(1) Karma, on the version worth defending, is a desert claim: a person undergoes a given suffering only because they previously did something wrong, and the suffering is morally fitting for them in virtue of that act.
(2) Desert is agent-relative and proportional: what makes the suffering fitting is the wrong that very person did, and the magnitude of the suffering tracks the gravity of that wrong.
(3) In the classical sources the payoff is deferred, often across lives, and rebirth supplies no memory of the deed and sometimes very little psychological continuity — different capacities, different character, occasionally not even the same species.
(4) So the one who suffers is not the agent who acted, in the sense that (2) requires.
(5) If they are not, then the suffering is not deserved by them.
(6) So karma-as-desert is either false — the consequence lands on a kind of stranger — or it collapses into ordinary within-a-lifetime retribution with the rebirth machinery bolted on as decoration.
(7) And note that (6) generalises: if desert needs that much continuity, then I don't now deserve anything for what I did twenty years ago, and punishing the thoroughly reformed offender is straightforward injustice.

I want to keep (1) and (2). My own move has been to say the continuity is carried by something other than memory: call it trace-ownership. The impression left by the act is itself the thing that later matures into the consequence, so the ownership relation is secured causally rather than psychologically, and the latency isn't an accident of the theory but the point of it. A seperate fallback I've floated is a split version — the debt is incurred by the agent and discharged by the later person, the way an invoice can outlive the employee who ordered the goods. My student says the first just relocates the normative question one step back without answering it, and that the second isn't desert at all but inherited liability.

Where does the argument go wrong? Name the step, explain why it looked fine, and tell me what giving it up costs — including what it costs me if I want to hang on to both the desert reading and the latency story. If (3) is doing more work than it appears to, say exactly what.
