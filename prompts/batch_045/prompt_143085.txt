I've been voluntold into teaching a first-year seminar on dreams and illusions next spring, and I'm trying to get the Zhuangzi butterfly bit under control before I have to stand up in front of eighteen-year-olds and say something intelligent about it. My own work is in philosophy of perception — hallucination, naive realism, disjunctivism, the argument from hallucination, that whole neighborhood — so that's the vocabulary I actually think in, and I'd like you to translate into it rather than give me a survey of the passage.

Here's what I want translated. The story, as I'm reading it: Zhuang Zhou dreams he's a butterfly, a butterfly doing butterfly things, and when he wakes up he doesn't know whether he's Zhuang Zhou who dreamed he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming it's Zhuang Zhou; then a closing remark to the effect that there has to be some distinction between the two of them, and that this is what the transformation of things amounts to. I know there's a long fight about whether the passage is an argument at all, let alone a skeptical one, so don't smooth that over. Do two translations separately: (1) the reading on which it raises the ordinary dream-skeptical worry — maybe my present experience is dreamt, so I don't know the things I take myself to know; (2) the reading on which nothing epistemic is going on and the point is about the instability, or maybe the indifference, of what you are across transformation.

Translate each into my terms: phenomenal character, introspective indistinguishability versus discriminability, the common-kind assumption, the claim that a perception and a hallucination can be subjectively the same, the argument from hallucination, disjunctivism about the two, and whatever closure- or sensitivity-style principle you need to get from can't-tell to don't-know. Be strict about not smuggling: if you catch yourself assuming that dreaming is a determinate kind of conscious episode with its own phenomenal character, flag it, because I think that assumption is doing enormous work and I'm not sure it's in the text. There's a family of views on which dreams aren't experiences you have while asleep at all but something more like reports, or recollections, or a cognitive achievement of the waking subject — I half-remember bits of this from somewhere, but please don't spend time on who said what. I dont need citations and you'd probably get them wrong anyway; just treat it as an option on the table and say what it does to the translation.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue report: which premises of each reading map cleanly onto my vocabulary, which only go through if you distort them, and which have no expression in it at all. I don't want a paraphrase of the story or thoughts about how Eastern and Western philosophy differ in general; I want a translation I can check bit by bit against the text.

Three places where I keep losing my grip, and I want them handled specifically.

First: the two horns aren't waking and dreaming. Dreaming is what the two horns share; the contrast is between Zhuang Zhou and a butterfly — two different creatures living two different lives. So the question looks less like is this experience veridical and more like whose experience is this, or which life am I in the middle of. My vocabulary has a slot for whether an experience is hallucinatory and a slot for which object its of, but nothing for the subject-level question. Is that a genuine gap in the framework, or am I misreading the passage into having that question?

Second: the worry is raised after the fact, about which of two stories is the true one, and it isn't clear to me that it's a demand for a criterion that would settle it from the inside. That's a different shape from the can't-tell-so-don't-know move I know how to formalize. Say whether translating it into that move is honest, or whether it turns the passage into a different argument — and if the latter, what the argument it becomes is, and which bit of the original is left over afterwards.

Third: the closing remark about there being some distinction between Zhuang Zhou and the butterfly. Under reading (2) that seems to be the load-bearing bit, and it looks like an identity or persistence claim rather than an epistemic one. Does anything in it survive being said in perception-vocabulary at all? If nothing does, tell me whether that's because my framework is parochial or because reading (2) is less contentful than it sounds to people who like it.

Two more things. If, once translated, reading (1) turns out to be structurally the same argument as the argument from hallucination, say so plainly and tell me which part of the passage was doing no work. And my own hobbyhorse, which you should feel free to knock down: that the whole thing turns on whether dreaming is being treated as an experiential kind or as a fact about what is really going on, and that the passage is interesting precisely because it never decides. If that's just my own prejudice wearing a philosophy costume, I'd rather find out now than in week three.
