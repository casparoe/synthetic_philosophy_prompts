I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years, most of it on detector instrumentation for large collaborations, where no one ever asked what any of it meant — who in the last two years has started attending a philosophy reading group in my city. The group is about fifteen adults with no formal training in philosophy, roughly a third of whom would like the cosmological argument to work and another third of whom would like it to fail, and the remainder politely agnostic. I have been asked to lead a single forty-five minute session, whiteboard only, on the cosmological argument, and I would like your help planning how to teach it rather than an explanation of it.

The one thing I want them to leave with is that there is not one cosmological argument but (at least) two, and that they are logically independent: an argument that runs from the universe's having a beginning to a cause of that beginning, and an argument that runs from the contingency or dependence of the things that exist to something whose existence is not borrowed. In particular I want them to see the case that separates them — a universe with no first moment, an endless past, whose existence is nevertheless contingent and dependent — and to be able to say which of the two arguments such a universe touches and which it does not. I can supply all the physics myself and will keep it short: that the extrapolation of general relativity back to a singularity is a breakdown of the theory rather than a discovery of a first event, that the singularity results establish geodesic incompleteness and not creation, that inflationary spacetimes are generically past-incomplete in the relevant sense yet several cosmological models under active discussion have no earliest time, and that popular vacuum-fluctuation stories already presuppose a quantum field with a dynamics and a law, so that the nothing in those stories is not nothing. My own failing, which I would like the plan to guard against, is that I can talk about this for forty minutes and leave everyone knowing more cosmology and no philosophy.

So, four things.

First, the order. Lay out for me the sequence in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what. Should the ambiguity in cause — a cause that initiates versus a cause or ground that sustains, an explanation that is causal versus one that is not — come before either argument is stated, or should it be extracted from them afterward? Where does my physics belong, early as a constraint on one premise or late as a correction to what people will already believe? And at what point should the two arguments be placed side by side so that their independence is visible rather than asserted?

Second, the example that carries the session. I had thought of a row of falling dominoes, or a flame passed from candle to candle, but I now worry that both build in temporal succession, which is precisely what the contingency argument does not require, and that both invite the question of a first member, which invites the wrong argument. Give me the one case you would use instead, what it must contain, and what it must not contain, and tell me why it survives being pushed in both directions.

Third, the misconceptions, each with the point in the sequence at which it should be headed off rather than merely answered later. I expect at least these: that both arguments share the premise that everything has a cause; that the Big Bang settles the question of a beginning; that the conclusion of either argument is a first event in time; that the contingency argument is an objection to infinite regress as such; that necessary existence means very long-lived or very powerful; that either argument, if it worked, would deliver the God of some religion; and the two moves that will come from opposite ends of the room, the quantum-fluctuation-from-nothing gambit and the retort about what caused God. Tell me what else I should expect from this particular mix of people, and which misconceptions are worth letting surface before I correct them.

Fourth, how I will know it has landed. Give me one question to put to them at the end, a concrete case rather than a definition, whose right answer shows they have the distinction and whose plausible wrong answers each diagnose a specific failure — and tell me what I should hear that would tell me they have instead only learned my vocabulary. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level, explicitly and out loud, and where the discussion is most likely to derail and how the sequence should absorb that rather than suppress it.
