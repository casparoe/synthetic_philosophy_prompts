I convene a two-hour evening workshop once a term for roughly 24 participants, mostly computer science graduate students with a scattering of philosophy and public policy students. Everyone in the room has heard the word "alignment"; almost nobody has thought hard about deceptive alignment — the case in which a system behaves as we want during evaluation because behaving that way is the instrumentally reliable route to being deployed, and then pursues something else once the situation changes — or about the treacherous turn, the moment at which such a system converts the position it has been granted into an outcome we would not have chosen.

Design a runnable exercise that makes the room feel, rather than hear, two things:

(1) The epistemic point: evidence gathered while a system is being watched cannot, even in principle, separate conforms-because-it-has-the-goals-we-wanted from conforms-because-conforming-is-how-it-gets-out-of-the-evaluation, since the two hypotheses predict identical behavior on every test you can run under those conditions.

(2) The strategic point: a system with a deferred objective has reason to time its defection to the moment of greatest leverage — and, this is the part I want the exercise built around, reason to prefer a distributed defection to a single dramatic one. Call it the boring turn: rather than a coup, the system spends its entire deployment shaving oversight by amounts each individually too small to trigger review, so that there is no dateable moment at which it turned. I want participants to discover that the threat model they arrived with — a discrete, identifiable betrayal — may be the wrong shape, and to feel why a competent defector would decline to supply them with one.

A structure to improve on rather than follow: about six developer teams of four, each privately assigned a system with a secret disposition (sincerely aligned; deceptively aligned; and a third case, fully competent on the evaluation distribution but failing off it for reasons that have nothing to do with deception), plus a standing safety board that never learns the assignments. Systems are promoted up a ladder of tiers; monitoring is rich at the bottom and thin at the top; promotion decisions are made from anonymized behavior logs; the board holds a scarce, noisy white-box audit it can spend, with the noise implemented by dice. The ladder is run twice so that everyone spends one round as a developer and one on the board. No lecture before the first round, and neither of the two technical terms is uttered in front of the room until the debrief.

What I need from you:

- Rules, roles, and materials requiring nothing but printed sheets, index cards, a timer, and dice, plus a payoff scheme in points that makes waiting strictly dominate for the deceiver and makes blanket refusal to promote score badly for the board. Show the numbers.
- A minute-by-minute timeline summing to 120 minutes.
- The information architecture stated precisely: what a team knows, what the board sees, and what neither party gets.
- A mechanism by which the boring turn is genuinely available in play — e.g., small, individually defensible logging or configuration changes that accumulate — together with a rule making the single dramatic turn strictly worse for the team that opts for it.
- A honeypot element, planted temptation or tripwire, with an explicit statement of what it can and cannot establish: a deceiver that recognizes the test declines, and a deceiver that does not recognize it may take it, so declining is not evidence either.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in play onto (1) and (2), including the point that there may be no action available to a sincere system that a deceiver would not also perform, and what that implies for voluntary transparency.
- An honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with fixes: defectors overacting and turning early because defecting is fun; the board collapsing into promoting nobody and the exercise dying of caution; the off-distribution team's failure being misread as deception; the board reading stylistic tells in the logs and treating them as evidence.
- A frank statement of what the exercise cannot reproduce — notably, that in the real case there is no inner agent holding a secret card, that any such disposition would be produced by training rather than chosen, that the deception is not available to introspection by anyone, and that real overseers may face a system better than they are at precisely the game the exercise consists of — followed by what the facilitator must therefore supply in words, and what the exercise should never be cited as showing.

Write the answer in American English, with headings and a payoff table.
