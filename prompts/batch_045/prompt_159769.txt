I've ended up holding a stronger-than-usual version of origin essentialism about people, and before I build anything on top of it I want to know what would count against it.

The core: nobody could have been the product of a numerically different sperm and egg pair. I hold it even when the swap is between gametes that are qualitatively identical — a genetically identical sibling sperm from the same batch, say, or a sperm whose DNA the egg's repair machinery then corrected back to the original sequence. And I go further in two ways. First, what's really essential isn't the gametes themselves but the continuous organism from some threshold onward — roughly the point after which splitting into identical twins is no longer possible, so somewhere around two weeks in — with the gametes mattering only derivatively, because they fix which later organism you are. Call it the Threshold View. Second, I take it as a feature rather than a bug that this makes identity claims about the pre-threshold entity indeterminate: if that zygote had split and only one twin survived, I'm happy saying there's no fact of the matter whether the survivor would have been me.

So what would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a real defender of the view would themselves concede as counting against it, not just what critics say. Some things I specifically want covered:

- The cases that have arguably already happened: embryo biopsy of the sort done in preimplantation genetic testing, where a cell is taken out at the eight-cell stage; deliberate embryo splitting; tetragametic chimerism, where two zygotes fuse into one body; mitochondrial replacement, where the nuclear DNA comes from two gametes and the mitochondria from a third. Does the Threshold View have to say something unacceptable about any of these, and is there a version that survives them without being gerrymandered to fit?
- Whether the gradual-replacement pressure actually works. If swapping one cell, and then in principle all of the matter, doesn't threaten identity, why should swapping the gametes threaten it? Is the causal-historical answer genuinely independent of the matter answer, or does it quietly lean on it?
- The collapse-into-a-rival worry: if organisms are best thought of as processes or events rather than substances, does cross-world identity of the sort I need just evaporate? Is that fatal, or a friendly redescription?
- Modal skepticism: theres a live worry that we have no reliable grip on de re modal claims about ourselves, so my intuitions here and my opponents are both just noise. If that's right, does the dispute become undecidable rather than settled in my favour?
- What the view predicts that its rivals don't. I take it I'm committed to the non-identity problem being real, and to nearly every counterfactual about what my parents did being one in which I don't exist. Are those costs, or confirmed predictions? And is leaning on the non-identity stuff circular?
- Wheather I've qualified myself into unfalsifiability, especially with the indeterminacy move and with the threshold being vague. If I have, is that a genuine problem for a view of this kind, or is every account of personal identity across possible worlds stuck in the same boat?

The cluster that would move me most personally is the twin-plus-biopsy one: if those force me into either obvious gerrymandering or into saying something I really don't want to say about actual children born via those procedures, I'd probably give the whole thing up. But tell me if I'm overrating them.
