Write a sestina — six six-line stanzas in the standard end-word rotation (each stanza taking the previous stanza's end-words in the order 6-1-5-2-4-3) followed by a three-line envoy in which all six words return, two to a line.

The speaker is a retired ship's navigator who, after a head injury, has kept his appointments, his grandchildren's birthdays and his way home in a single ruled notebook carried in his inside pocket; he knows his eldest daughter only by turning to the page where she is described. Take the six end-words to be notebook, hand, skull, street, trust, mind — one for the external vehicle, one for the body's action, one for the boundary everyone wants to defend, one for the world the system moves through, one for the reliability and accessibility conditions that glue the arrangement together, one for the thing in dispute. Let the rotation carry the argument rather than decorate it: the same six words must go on doing the same work in a changed order, so that the form enacts the parity principle from Clark and Chalmers's extended mind thesis — that if a process outside the body plays the functional role of one we readily count as cognitive inside the head, location is not supposed to matter.

In the last stanza let the notebook be taken from him, by fire, theft or hospital admission, and let the envoy be the moment the question sharpens. The poem should be answerable in its own terms to the objection that heavy causal coupling with a notebook makes it an instrument rather than a constituent of the thinker, without either side winning: a reader sympathetic to extended mind should feel embedded mind as a live pressure in the verse. No consolations of the memory-loss genre — no surviving inner self, no dignity. Someone who knows the debate should be able to say, line by line, where the vehicle stops and the agent starts, or why the poem will not let them say it.
