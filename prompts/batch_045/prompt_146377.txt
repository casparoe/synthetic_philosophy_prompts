I'm finishing a thesis chapter on what matters in survival, and I want to attribute a definite view to my doctoral supervisor, who works broadly in the Parfitian tradition but has never published on personal identity — everything I have is from supervisions and a graduate seminar, so there is nothing to look up, and what I need is the argument rather than a reference. Her verdicts so far, each delivered with real confidence: ordinary teletransportation (scan, destroy the original, rebuild from new matter) — you survive, and nothing that matters is lost; fission into two qualitatively identical replicas — you survive as both, nothing that matters is lost, the question of which one is you is empty, and when pressed she will say only that fission is in no way worse than ordinary survival, pointedly refusing to say whether it is better; gradual neuron-by-neuron prosthetic replacement over ten years — full survival, not even slightly diminished; the combined spectrum, where the single resulting person's psychology is at every stage half yours and half someone else's — survival here is partial, roughly halfway, and admits of degrees; a fusion of two distinct people, whose memory traces are both written into one successor body and whose originals are destroyed — each survives only partially, about halfway; a blueprint stored unused for two centuries and then instantiated once — you survive, and although the disorientation and the lost relationships make it a worse prospect, there is no threshold at which the length of the gap turns survival into death. She also insists throughout that the non-branching clause is doing work only in the identity question, never in the question of what matters.

The case I want to put to her is a staggered fission with reconvergence, and I can't work out what her principles commit her to. At t0 you are scanned and your body is destroyed. Replica A is built immediately from the blueprint and lives normally. Replica B is built from the same blueprint forty-eight hours later, so that for two days A is the only continuer; after that the two live in parallel for a year in different cities, having quite different experiences. At the end of the year both are scanned, a single successor M is constructed whose brain carries the memory traces of both streams — M remembers living both lives — and A and B are destroyed. There is also a variant in which the transfer is asymmetric: M receives all of A's traces but only half of B's, the remainder discarded at random. My questions: how much of what matters survives, relative to ordinary survival, during the first forty-eight hours, during the parallel year, and after the merge; whether the forty-eight-hour stagger changes anything at any stage; whether the merge is a gain, a loss or a neutral event; how the person at t0 should distribute prudential concern over A's and B's experiences during the parallel year; and whether, from the standpoint of the person at t0, it would be better for B never to have been built, better for B to be built and merged, or better for the two simply to continue in parallel.

I can see that more than one rule fits everything she has said so far — roughly, a best-candidate rule on which what matters is fully preserved whenever some successor bears full psychological continuity to me, a conservation rule on which the total is fixed and divided among competing branches, and a summation rule on which branching multiplies what matters — but I cannot get them to come apart on her existing verdicts, and I suspect two of them survive the staggered case as well. Please organize the reply in five sections, in this order: (1) "Candidate rules" — the two or three most general principles consistent with all seven verdicts, each stated in a form I could formalize (~250 words); (2) "Predictions" — what each rule implies about the staggered case at each of the three stages, about the asymmetric variant, and about the distribution of prudential concern (~350 words); (3) "Her verdict" — your best guess at what my supervisor would say, with the reasons for preferring one rule, and an explicit note on any of her remarks you are discounting as throwaway (~300 words); (4) "What turns on it" — whether the difference between the surviving rules has any real consequence for anticipation or self-concern, or is mere bookkeeping (~200 words); (5) "The discriminating case" — the single further case I should put to her to separate the survivors, with what each predicts (~200 words).
