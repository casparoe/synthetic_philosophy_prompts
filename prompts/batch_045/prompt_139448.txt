Here's one I've been chewing on since last Thursday and I can't get it to let go of me. I teach the grad seminar on causation most springs (not this one, thank god) and we had a work-in-progress last week by a colleague in the department — call him Dan — whose official topic was supposed to be omissions in tort law but who spent the whole hour arguing that omissions are never causes at all. Not "rarely causes" or "causes only in a derivative sense" — never. Our visitor from the law school went slightly purple. I've reconstructed his chain below as cleanly as I can, and it's starting to annoy me that I can't locate the break.

(1) Causal facts are norm-independent. Whether A caused B cannot depend on what anyone was supposed to do, expected to do, or obligated to do; if it did, the causal story about a death would shift around every time we changed our mind about someone's duties, which gets the direction of explanation backwards — the duties are supposed to track the causal facts, not the other way round.
(2) Omissions aren't events. There's no thing there to push, transfer energy, or produce an outcome; if an omission causes anything it has to be by counterfactual difference-making, so: my not watering the plant caused its death iff, had I watered it, the plant would have lived.
(3) That test is passed by absolutely everyone. For any person P who in fact never watered the plant, had P watered it, it would have lived. So every human being on earth who could have got water to that plant — including people with no connection to me, my apartment, or the plant — comes out as a cause of its death, exactly as much as I do. Dan calls this the profligacy problem.
(4) And there's no non-normative difference to sort them by. Not probability of watering (that's epistemic, and it's the same *kind* of difference either way), not spatial proximity, not my promise to water it, not my role as its owner — promises, roles and ownership are all norms. The only features that separate my ommission from the Queen's are normative ones.
(5) So the options are: accept billions of omissions as causes, or let norms into the causal facts. Given (1), we have to go profligate.
(6) But profligacy isn't a theory of causation, it's a confession. A causal claim that includes every absence of every unperformed preventive act tells you nothing about how the death came about, doesn't tell you where to intervene next time, and leaves "what caused the fire?" answerable only by listing everything in the universe that nobody did. A notion of cause that can't do explanatory or intervention-guiding work isn't the notion we had.
(7) So omissions are never causes in the sense events are. "I killed the plant by not watering it" is shorthand for three separate facts: it died, I could have prevented it, and I was supposed to. Causation drops out entirely.
(8) Corollary he was cheerful about: there is no causal difference between the person who holds a child's head under the water and the person who stands on the bank and does nothing but could easily help. Either neither causes the death, or both do, along with everyone else on earth. The distinction between killing and letting die is purely moral, not causal.

Now: I think (8) is false, or at least that it costs more than the whole rest of the argument is worth, and I'd rather keep (1) than give it up, since norm-dependence really does seem to me like a category mistake about causation. My own move on the day was to say Dan is conflating causation with explanation — that causation is abundant and cheap and includes all those omissions, and that it's *explanation* and *attribution* where our interests and norms do the real work, so profligacy is fine and he should just buy it. He said fine, but then "caused the death" in a criminal statute becomes either trivially true of every bystander or a disguised moral judgment, and the notion isn't doing any work in the place we actually need it. I didn't have an answer to that and I still don't.

So: which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I want you to go through the moves rather than just tell me the right view — if (4) is where it breaks, show me the non-normative difference I'm missing; if the argument is valid and I'm just stuck choosing, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender and what I'd have to accept about killing and letting die, or about the norm-independence of causation, once I've surrendered it.
