hey — I'm a novelist (literary fiction, not a physicist) and I'm about seven months into a book whose narrator is a retired cosmologist named Ines who lost her husband last year and who, in her grief, has started talking herself into a very specific metaphysical line: that because relativity kills the present, nothing is ever really lost, that her husband is still there at his own dates, and that grief is therefore somehow a category mistake. I want that speech to be more than consoling nonsense — I want it to be something a philosopher could say without getting caught out. The trouble is I've been going to a reading group on time and free will with some friends, and I've ended up with a pile of commitments that I genuinely can't tell are a package or just a heap. Can you act as a professional philosopher who specializes in philosophy of physics and has also read a lot of the free will literature, and audit the pile? Please brainstorm a bit first — sketch out what you think the real fault lines are before you commit to an ordered verdict — and then give me the audit in American English. And please don't give me citations, footnotes, or a reading list; I'll chase the literature down myself.

Here's roughly where Ines (and, honestly, I) have landed:

1. The Rietdijk–Putnam style argument works: the business about two people strolling past each other on the street whose "nows" slice through the Andromeda galaxy at wildly different dates shows there's no frame-independent present, and once you take physics at face value instead of treating it instrumentally, all times are equally real — the block universe.
2. But I also think the standard reply to that argument is right: it needs "real for me now" to be transitive across reference frames, and for spacelike-separated events there just isn't a frame-independent now for it to be transitive over — and I currently hold both of these at once without knowing whether the second one quietly kills the first.
3. I'm a compatibilist about free will and responsibility: determinism isn't the threat, guidance control and reasons-responsiveness are what matter, and alternative possibilities were never required.
4. But I also think the block universe takes something away that plain determinism doesn't. Ines keeps saying that if the outcome is already sitting there in the block, her deliberating can't be making a difference, and that feels like a seperate, worse worry than determinism — even though by (3) I shouldn't be able to say that.
5. There's no objective passage or flow of time; "now" is indexical, like "here." Yet I also want to say the asymmetry of agency is a real feature of the world and not just an artifact of memory and entropy — I can affect later things and not earlier ones, and that isn't merely how things look from the inside.
6. On death: I want to keep both that nobody who ever lived has stopped existing, since they're alive at their own times, and that grief is fitting and the loss is real and present.
7. Quantum mechanics gives us objective, irreducible chances rather than mere ignorance, and I've been telling myself that's no problem for the block — the block just contains the one history that happened, chances and all.

One more thing that got thrown at me last week: a physicist in the group said cosmology actually hands us a preferred slicing — the frame in which the cosmic microwave background looks the same in every direction — so there's a privileged global time after all, and doesn't that mean arguments of the Rietdijk–Putnam sort only go through in an empty special-relativistic world and not in the actual one? I have no idea whether that's a rescue for a present-ish Ines or a bullet she has to bite, but fold it into the audit wherever it bears on 1–7.

So: which of these genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do (and why the appearance dissolves), and for each real tension, what's the cheapest revision and what does it cost me — specifically which line of dialogue in the novel I'd have to cut. Rank the tensions by severity instead of flagging all seven as equally bad, say which single commitment is load-bearing for the whole pile (the one whose removal collapses the most), and be explicit about the hinge in each case rather than just announcing a verdict.
