I teach an upper-division undergraduate seminar in metaethics, and I want a diagnostic I can hand out on the first day, before students have encountered any of the labels. The point is to sort them by what they are actually committed to about the relation between descriptive and evaluative claims, not by which position they find attractive once it has a name attached to it. I would like you to design that instrument and to show the logical space it is carved out of.

Deliverable. A flowchart of nine branch questions asked in a fixed order, so that a reader's path through them just is their answer pattern; nine or ten leaves; a scoring key; and short notes on the combinations the tree can generate but that nobody occupies. Each branch question should offer two or three options phrased the way a thoughtful non-philosopher would put the point, and each should be answerable in about twenty seconds. No question and no answer option may use any -ism label. No question may simply ask whether morality is objective or whether there are moral facts: nearly everyone in the room answers those the same way, and they sort nobody. I also want one preliminary question, not counted among the nine, that detects whether a reader can hear the descriptive/evaluative distinction at all — some of them cannot, and I would rather know that than have them guess.

Structure. Build the space before locating the positions in it. The familiar talk of a gap between is and ought conflates at least three separable claims: that no normative conclusion can be validly derived from premises none of which is normative; that evaluative vocabulary cannot be spelled out in purely descriptive vocabulary; and that evaluative facts either are or are not something over and above descriptive facts, with the corresponding consequences for how, if at all, we could come to know them. A student can reject one of these while accepting another, and which one they reject first determines which later questions are live for them. So the tree should separate them early rather than treat them as a single hinge. At least two branch questions must cut across the realism/anti-realism divide rather than along it, sorting realists against realists.

Cases I would like used as nodes — use at least six of these and supply the remainder yourself:

1. Someone fully at home with the concept of maximizing aggregate happiness asks, of an act that does exactly that, whether it is good. Is that question conceptually confused, or does it remain intelligible?
2. An argument with no normative premises validly yields a wildly false normative conclusion, by way of a disjunctive premise whose other disjunct is plainly false. Does this refute the no-derivation claim, or does it show that the claim was never about validity in that sense?
3. Two acts are alike in every descriptive respect. Can one be obligatory and the other not? If not, does that necessary connection admit of an explanation — constitution, identity, something law-like — or is it basic?
4. A person says the words I promise to pay you five dollars. Does the existence of the institution hand you the conclusion that he ought to pay, or does the argument still need a premise that is already normative?
5. Two communities use the same evaluative word while applying it to different descriptive patterns. When one says an act is wrong and the other says it is not, do they disagree about one thing, or are they saying compatible things in different vocabularies?
6. An agent wholly unmoved by moral considerations asks what reason she has to act morally. Is there an answer to her that does not depend on what she happens to want?
7. A sentence in which a moral clause appears unasserted, as in if it is wrong to do that then it is wrong to get someone else to do it: is it the sort of thing that can be true or false, and does its truth require the embedded clause to be?

Leaves. The tree should terminate in at least these: definitional naturalism, flagged as historically occupied but now nearly empty; a posteriori identity naturalism, on which evaluative properties are identical with descriptive ones, necessarily, though not knowable in advance of experience; irreducible non-naturalism with brute necessary covariation; constitutivism, which locates the source of normativity in what is constitutive of agency or of some standpoint; projectivism or expressivism; error theory, split into abolitionist and conservationist variants if you think the split is real; and a quietist or deflationist refusal of the metaphysical question. Add a leaf if a genuine position is left homeless. Each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment, written as something its holder has to swallow rather than as an objection from outside.

Diagnostics. Identify at least three answer combinations the tree can generate but that nobody holds — one of them must be the definitional-naturalism cell, with an explanation of why it emptied out — and say of each whether it is empty because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag at least two pairs of leaves your questions cannot separate, with a note on what further question would be needed and whether that question is one a beginner could answer honestly. In the scoring key, mark the answer patterns that are internally unstable and say what the instability reveals: I would rather learn that a student is pulled in two directions than have the tree quietly force her down one branch.
