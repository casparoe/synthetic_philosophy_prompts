Hey — I'm not a philosopher by training, I'm a backend dev, but I help run a monthly film-and-discussion thing at the public library in my town and we're doing Schindler's List in a couple of weeks, so I'm pulling together some notes for myself.

Here's what I keep circling around. A friend of mine did a PhD in philosophy and when I told him I was doing this movie he brought up something he called the inefficacy objection — the move where you argue that one individual's contribution to some big collective outcome makes no difference, so you're under no obligation to bother. He was talking about stuff like whether you should stop eating meat or whether your vote matters, and he said there's a whole literature on it. I get the general shape: my one purchase or my one vote doesn't change anything, the outcome would have happened anyway, so nothing is up to me.

What I want is a reading of the film through that idea, because it feels to me like the movie is built almost entirely out of the opposite intuition, and I can't tell whether it's actually arguing against the objection or just never noticing it. The obvious case: Schindler ends up with roughly eleven hundred people on his list who are alive because of one guy's choices, so the "my bit makes no difference" move just dies on contact with the plot. But then the ending — him completely falling apart, saying he could have saved one more person and didn't, and that business with the ring and the line from the Talmud about saving one life — seems to be doing something harder than "look, individuals can matter." It reads like it's saying that once you grant your contribution matters, there's no place to stop, and that he's somehow on the hook for the ones he didn't save. That's the part I want straight before I stand up in front of forty people.

A few things I'd like you to deal with specifically.

One: I think there's a distinction that matters and I don't know if there's a standard name for it (honestly I don't care about the name, I care whether the distinction is real). In the cases my friend was talking about — carbon emissions, buying a chicken, voting — my individual act is supposed to be swamped by millions of others, and whether any particular bad outcome happens doesn't hinge on me. But the way the camp works in the film, as I read it, is more like a series of individual thresholds: this person is on that transport or they aren't, and Schindler's bribe or his argument actually flips specific people across a line. Somebody buying a chicken isn't in that situation at all. So is the inefficacy objection really one argument or several, and is the film only refuting the version that depends on your contribution being genuinely swamped rather than the version where it lands on a threshold? I'm probably mangling this, so feel free to tell me my distinction is confused.

Two: the film gives you Amon Göth as a kind of mirror, and I don't know what to do with him. He shoots people from his balcony one at a time, which is individually efficacious in the most literal possible way, and yet nothing about the machine depends on him personally pulling the trigger — if he didn't, someone else would. So he's like a photographic negative of Schindler: the person whose individual acts clearly make a difference to a particular victim and clearly make no difference to the overall death toll. Is the film doing something with that, or am I reading a symmetry into it that isn't there? And is there a version of the inefficacy objection that would let Göth off the hook — which would be a sign the argument is broken — or does he fail for some completely different reason?

Three: the list itself. My read is that the whole emotional machinery of the movie is about turning an anonymous statistical process into named people on a page, and that this is basically the anti-inefficacy move: once the outcome is this name on this list, the "it makes no difference" excuse has nowhere to hide. There's also the girl in the red coat, and the way the film makes her visible and then, later, isn't — which seems to be doing something similar. But I can't tell whether that's a philosophical point or just very good filmmaking, and I'd like to know whether someone who works on this stuff would say the movie is onto something the papers miss.

Four: be honest about where the reading breaks down. My worry is that shoving a Holocaust film into a "does my act produce a difference" frame is kind of gross, and that what the film is actually about is guilt and obligation and not being able to live with yourself, which isn't the same thing as a duty to produce good outcomes. If the movie isn't in the inefficacy business at all — if it's asking a different question — I'd rather say that to the group than twist it into a tidy case study.

So: which scenes should I actually point people to, what's the strongest version of the argument the film is making (or the strongest one it's vulnerable to), and does it end up adding anything to this debate that the philosophy papers can't?
