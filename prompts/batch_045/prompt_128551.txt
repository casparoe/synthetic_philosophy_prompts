Our neighbourhood mutual aid fund is tiny — about forty members, we pool roughly $40k a year and vote on requests at a monthly meeting — and we basically think of ourselves as living in Singer's shallow pond but with an inbox. We've built up a track record of votes, and I want to know what it commits us to on the case we're voting on Thursday. The precedents as best I can reconstruct them: we funded a member's $600 emergency surgery even though she'd ignored the advice that would have prevented the problem, on the reasoning that fault isn't the issue; we funded the same amount for a stranger referred by a partner organization on another continent, on the grounds that distance isn't a moral difference; we turned down a member whose brother would have paid if we said no, because our money would just have replaced his; we refused a $22,000 life-saving request and offered $1,500 instead, because paying it would have cleaned us out for the rest of the year; when two applicants had the same condition and the same price and one had already been funded twice, we funded the other one, saying she'd had her turn; and once we funded a $600 piece of equipment for a local clinic specifically because it would be used on many patients, not just the one who asked for it. That last one is the one nagging at me.

Thursday's case: a member's seven-year-old needs a $600 surgery next month, will die without it, no one else is going to step in (we checked, the way we checked in the brother case), and nobody is at fault. But one of our members who works in hospital billing has convinced half the group that our paying for it has a knock-on: the surgery would happen at the county hospital, and privately funded cases at that hospital reliably push up what it charges uninsured families for the same procedure the following year — enough that over the next three years two or three more families in the same position get priced out of it. Assume for the sake of argument that this is right. I don't want to argue about the empirical claim, though I'm aware we'd never really be able to audit it. So: what should we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and what rule would we be adopting either way?

As I see it, our past votes fit both a rule where we answer the claim in front of us once we've checked that the need is real, the cost is within our cap, and no one else is going to meet it — with fairness and rotation as tie-breakers, and fault and distance explicitly irrelevant — and a rule where we do the most good with the money, counting everyone affected including people who aren't applicants. Those two come apart here in a way they haven't before, because it's the first time the knock-on effects have pointed *away* from the person doing the asking. What I'd like: a straight verdict with your confidence in it; the competing rules spelled out clearly enough that I can read them to the group, including what each one says about those two or three families; which precedent is the closest analogue and why the clinic-equipment decision doesn't just settle it on its own; whether "fund the child but route the money so it never passes through the county hospital" is a genuine third option or a way of dodging the question; and the one further case that would actually tell the two rules apart, since I suspect this vote won't. Please write in American English. And if you think the framing itself is confused — if the precedents don't really underdetermine this, if a fund our size shouldn't be reasoning this way at all, or if the knock-on effect can't do the work I've handed it — say so plainly instead of playing along; I'd rather be told than agreed with.
