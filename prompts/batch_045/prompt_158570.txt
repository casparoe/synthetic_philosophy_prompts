I'm on the research ethics committee for a hospital network, and we're finally getting around to writing guidance for a category of trial that doesn't fit our existing consent forms at all: studies of interventions whose intended effect includes changing what the participants themselves care about. Concretely, the cases on my list are deep brain stimulation for treatment-resistant depression where some patients afterwards describe themselves as a different person (their families agree, the patients usually don't), a year-long immersive training program that turns you into someone who genuinely loves a demanding art form you currently find tedious, psychedelic-assisted therapy for existential distress in terminal illness, first-time parenthood (the usual case from the transformative experience literature), a prelingually deaf adult deciding whether to get a cochlear implant, someone who emigrates with no realistic right of return, and a speculative thing one of our neurologists keeps pushing in every meeting: a two-week "motivation amplification" protocol that durably raises achievement motivation in people who currently describe themselves as contentedly unambitious. The committee's default instinct is to treat all of this as ordinary decision-making under uncertainty — you don't know the payoffs, you assign probabilities, you maximize, done — and I think that instinct is wrong for at least some of these, but I can't get the group to see the difference, partly because the coffee in that room is genuinely terrible and I lose the thread about forty minutes in.

What I want to try instead is scoring the cases on two separate axes, 0 to 10 each, and seeing whether that ordering buys us anything the one we've been arguing about doesn't. Axis A: how much of what's at stake the chooser simply cannot anticipate beforehand — not "unlikely to guess," but the stronger claim, that no amount of description, video, simulation, testimony or armchair reflection gets them there. Axis B: how much the pre-decision standpoint becomes unavailable to them afterwards, so that even a well-designed retrospective check ("do you still consent to this? do you want us to stop/reverse it?") can't do the work, because they can't recover who they were, or they no longer care to. My suspicion is that our existing guidance implicitly assumes B is always near zero, and that's exactly what fails in the DBS cases and arguably in the motivation-amplification case, if that neurologist ever gets his protocol funded.

So: give me the table. Score each of the seven on A and on B, combine them however you think is right (tell me the weighting and defend it), and rank them. Then tell me three things. (1) For which of these is the committee's "just treat it as unknown payoffs" line actually fine, and where does it break — and what specifically is doing the breaking? (2) For which cases does surveying people who've already been through the thing genuinely solve the problem, and for which does it not, and why the difference? (3) If we can only write proper new guidance for two of the seven this year, which two, given that what we actually care about is preventing someone from being enrolled in a way they'd later have reason to regard as a mistake? Feel free to tell me the two axes are the wrong cut, that one of them collapses into the other, or that one of my cases is boringly vanilla and shouldn't be on the list. My sister, who's a GP, says the whole exercise is philosophers inventing a problem that clinicians have been handling fine for decades, and I'd like to know whether she's right about any of it.
