I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who teaches political theory, and I came away unable to reconstruct the principle she was actually running. Her verdicts about affirmative action and quotas are unusually firm, and they do not sort the way I expected. Before I try her again I want the rule stated precisely enough that I could test it on her, and I want to know what it commits her to on a case we never reached.

Her record, as far as I can reconstruct it:

1. A selective American university that reads each application as a whole and may treat an applicant's race as one factor among many: permissible.
2. The same university awarding a flat numerical bonus on its admissions index to applicants from under-represented racial groups: impermissible.
3. A statutory rule requiring large listed companies to fill at least 40 per cent of board seats with women, backed by sanctions. When I pointed out that the women who take those seats are typically at least as credentialed as the men they replace, and that the rule has no expiry, she said neither fact mattered here: permissible.
4. A scheme reserving a fixed share of undergraduate places for the children of a university's own graduates: impermissible.
5. Caste-based reservations in Indian higher education of the sort that exclude the creamy layer, the best-off within the beneficiary category: permissible. The same scheme without that exclusion: impermissible.
6. A quota reserving a share of university places for men in a country where men have become a minority of those entering university: impermissible.
7. A points bonus in admissions for applicants from the poorest income quartile, where the group has no ascriptive identity and no history of legal exclusion: impermissible. Her line was that school funding and maintenance grants are the right instruments for that, not the order of admission.

The case we never got to. A public university system in a country with no history of legal segregation proposes to reserve 25 per cent of the places in a fixed-size entering class for applicants who would be the first in their family to attend university. Such applicants are about 55 per cent of those who apply but only about 28 per cent of those admitted. There is no separate pool of extra places, so the reservation takes places away from identifiable borderline applicants in the general category. Selection inside the reserved pool is by the same entrance examination with a lower published cutoff, so the beneficiaries are the better-prepared members of the group, and roughly a fifth of them come from households in the top income quartile; the proposal contains no exclusion for those students and no expiry. The stated rationale is that these students have been badly schooled and that their presence changes what the institution can teach and discuss. In this country first-generation status correlates strongly with membership in a rural ethnic minority that has been looked down on and informally excluded from professions, though it never suffered legal exclusion, and the correlation is far from perfect in either direction.

I argued that her record commits her to rejecting this; she denied it but would not say why, and we ran out of time.

So: what rule, or family of rules, fits items 1 through 7? I can build at least two that fit all seven and disagree about the new case, but I do not trust mine. Then give me her verdict on the proposal as written, with the candidate rules you considered, what each predicts, and your reasons for preferring the one you do. If you think her precedents genuinely underdetermine it, say which principle she would be adopting with each choice and which choice is closer to the spirit of her own record. Name the single further case that would most cleanly separate your two best candidates: I have thought of two myself, the same first-generation reservation rewritten with an exclusion for the well-off and a ten-year sunset, and a rule reserving a quarter of board seats at listed companies for people under thirty-five, so tell me whether either of those does the work or whether there is a sharper one.

Commit to answers rather than surveying them: one verdict, one rule stated plainly enough to be falsified by her next sentence, and one discriminating case. And tell me which of her seven earlier verdicts you would reinterpret rather than give up your rule, if she refuses the verdict you predict for her.
