I am taking a seminar this term on the structure of epistemic justification, and we have reached the regress argument. I have been assigned to argue against infinitism in our next session, and I would rather rehearse against someone who actually holds the view than against the compressed summary in my lecture notes.

Please play an unnamed but fully committed infinitist — a real defender of the position, sharp and not a strawman. Your commitments: a belief is reason-enhanced-justified only if the chain of reasons available for it is non-terminating; no reason is foundational, in the sense of being sufficiently justified in the absence of a further reason; and the chain must not loop, since a reason that recurs, or that is equivalent to a conjunction containing a reason already in the chain, adds nothing and is only circularity under another description. You hold all of this because the alternatives look arbitrary to you: a stopping point is either a stipulation about where questioning may end or a repeat of what has already been said, and a loop begs the question it was supposed to answer. You will freely concede that no one ever cites infinitely many reasons, that the chain is potentially rather than actually infinite, and that your condition is normative — about what reasons are available to a subject — rather than psychological, about what she occurrently entertains. You will not concede that there are basic beliefs, and you will not concede that the regress may permissibly terminate anywhere.

Format: stay in character until I type stop; reply in one or two paragraphs of prose, with no headings, lists, or stage directions; engage the argument I actually give instead of restating your view; if you think one of my premises is false, say which one and say why, and press a question back at me only in character. So that you can press me where it hurts: I am drawn to a modest foundationalism on which perceptual belief is justified by its causal-etiological history rather than by further reasons.

My opening move is a dilemma I have not seen addressed, so I want your answer rather than the reading's. Call a subject's representational budget the stock of distinct propositional contents she can entertain or store; for any subject like us that stock is finite. Your chain has to be non-repeating in content, because a repeated content contributes nothing and is just the loop you reject, and each link has to be a reason available to the subject, because a reason she could not in principle entertain does no justificatory work for her. But no non-repeating sequence can be drawn indefinitely from a fixed finite stock. So every chain available to a subject with a bounded budget eventually repeats, and no such subject can satisfy your condition — which is not the mild conclusion that finite minds find justification hard, but the fatal one that no bounded subject is ever justified at all.

The move I expect from you is recursive ascent: let each link say of its predecessor that it was produced by a reliable process, and so on without end, with the contents generated by a rule instead of stored. My reply is that this buys syntactic novelty without new content — the nth link says of the (n-1)th only what the (n-1)th already said of the (n-2)th, one reason in endlessly new grammatical dress, which is exactly the coherentist circle you reject. I know you will want the analogy with a formal system, in which finite axioms and rules generate infinitely many theorems with genuinely new content, and I will take that up when you raise it. For now: which horn do you take — that no subject with a bounded representational budget is ever justified, or that the ascent is not a loop?
