Please defend the following thesis about the epistemic force of the scale argument for including insects in welfare prioritization.

Background and formalization. The scale argument, in its strongest form, is not the claim that insects are probably sentient, but a claim about what follows if we are uncertain. Write the expected welfare gain of a candidate intervention affecting a population of size N as N multiplied by the expected value of two further factors: a credence factor, our probability that members of the population have valenced conscious states at all, and a sign factor, the signed per-capita net welfare difference the intervention produces, averaged over the individuals it affects. The argument draws its force from the fact that N is astronomical for insects, so that even a small expected value of the product of the two factors yields a very large expected gain. Defenders of the argument typically add that our uncertainty about insect sentience is shared across the population rather than independent per individual: either the kind of organism in question has the relevant capacity or it does not, so the credence factor must not be diluted by assigning each insect a small independent chance of mattering.

The thesis to defend, call it the Redirection Thesis. Population size does no independent work in settling what a decision-maker ought to do, because the sign factor, not the credence factor, is where all of the action-guiding content of the argument lies, and the sign factor is not one that numbers can settle. Concretely: for population-scale interventions on insects, our uncertainty about whether the intervention makes things better or worse is close to symmetric, since the effect runs partly through how many individuals exist and partly through how much suffering per individual, and these run in opposite directions. Multiplying a roughly sign-symmetric quantity by an astronomical N does not convert it into a reason for action; it converts it into a very large quantity of unknown sign, and the recommendation then flips with arbitrarily small changes in the credences one assigns to the two sides of the sign question. What the numbers legitimately do is something else: because the stakes scale with N, the expected value of resolving the sign question also scales with N, and for insect interventions that resolve it by measurement is close to unavailable. The scale argument is therefore best reconstructed as an argument for buying information and for preferring interventions whose per-capita sign can be verified at small scale, and on that reconstruction it does not specially favor insects, since the interventions that pass the verifiability test are, as things stand, largely ones directed at animals about whose welfare states we can gather evidence.

Requirements for the defense.

First, give the formalization explicitly and say exactly where N enters, so that the reader can see which term of the decision the thesis is about and which term it concedes.

Second, explain what the claim of sign symmetry amounts to, distinguishing uncertainty about whether insect lives are on balance good or bad from uncertainty about whether a given intervention raises or lowers the number of such lives and the suffering per life, and argue that the symmetry is not merely a reflection of present ignorance that further evidence would straightforwardly remove, or, if it is, say what follows.

Third, do not avail yourself of the two standard escapes. You may not treat the credence factor as a per-individual independent probability in order to dilute it. You may not defeat the scale argument by appealing to a general prohibition on low-probability, high-stakes reasoning, to worries about fanaticism, or to the thought that astronomically large stakes are suspect in themselves; the thesis is meant to hold for someone who accepts expected-value reasoning in full generality.

Fourth, steelman and answer the two strongest objections. The linearity objection: expected value is linear in the stakes, so if one has any view at all about the sign, the numbers do the rest, and a decision-maker who declines to act on a large expected gain of uncertain sign is simply refusing to use the information she has. The learning objection: large-scale intervention is itself a way of resolving the sign question, so the thesis's own appeal to the value of information may recommend acting rather than investigating.

Fifth, restate the argument in its mixture form. Replace the single credence factor with a distribution over competing accounts of what makes an organism sentient, each of which returns a different verdict on insects, and show either that the thesis survives this generalization or say precisely how it must be qualified.

Sixth, give the constructive upshot for an allocator who must choose between a large insect-directed intervention and a smaller, better-evidenced intervention directed at vertebrates, and be explicit about whether the thesis yields an asymmetry, a tie, or a recommendation to defer.

Seventh, state what would falsify the thesis: identify one empirical finding or one conceptual point that, if granted, would make it false.

Constraints. Write in a formal register, around nine hundred to twelve hundred words. Define each term as you introduce it. Argue from the structure of the decision problem rather than from empirical results in insect neuroscience, and rely on nothing more specific than what a generally educated reader knows about insect life history. Do not cite, name authors, supply a bibliography, or refer to any particular paper, book, or research program; the argument should stand on its own. Where a step depends on a contestable empirical assumption, flag it as a premise rather than asserting it.
