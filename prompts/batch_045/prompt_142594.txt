For a metaphysics and philosophy-of-religion seminar I'm TAing this term, I'm assembling a short packet of "applied theodicy" material. We've just finished Leibniz's optimism and then his most famous caricature, and every student paper came back as the same joke about a certain tutor, so I want something that forces them to engage the machinery rather than the meme — a piece that Leibniz himself would concede was fair to him before he noticed he was being laughed at. What I have in mind is a document written from inside the operation: an internal bug-triage report of roughly 800 words, in the house style of a large technology company's issue tracker, authored by the team that ran the possible-worlds optimization and shipped the actual world.

Give it the shape of a single ticket. A header first: ticket ID, severity, component, status resolved as "Working as Intended / Won't Fix", assigned to something like the Compossibility Review Board, with a subject line in the terse style of someone who has closed this ticket many times before. Then the reporter's original complaint — no more than about 120 words, in plain, furious, wholly non-technical language: a parent whose young child has just died of an aggressive childhood cancer, demanding to know why that outcome was shipped and what it would cost to patch it. Then the substantive reply. Then a footer listing auto-linked duplicate tickets and a customer-satisfaction survey.

The reply is where the work is, and I want the comedy to come out of the doctrine: please include an explicit statement of the objective function (not a sum of happiness, but the richest stock of phenomena compatible with the simplest, most unified laws — maximum variety per unit of law-complexity); an explanation of incompossibility, in which the team patiently points out that selection operates on whole maximal packages rather than on individual items, so the survival cannot be ordered à la carte — and, further, that the "same child who survives" is not a well-formed request at all, since a complete individual notion carries its entire history with it, so the survivor in any alternative package would be a different individual; the privation move and the permission rather than the willing of evil; and the argument that pain is load-bearing engineering, cheap, robust and infinitely scalable. Clothe all of this in the idiom of the genre: an ablation study, a constraint budget, a Pareto frontier, guardrails, a severity downgrade, a "known issue." Add at least one threaded internal comment from a reviewer in a neighbouring department (grace, foreknowledge, concurrency of causes — pick one) who objects in corporate-speak and is politely overruled. Two constraints: I want at least one moment where the reply is genuinely correct, where a reader who has read the theodicy thinks "yes, that really does block that complaint" — the satire has to be fair rather than a strawman — and one sentence somewhere that lets slip something damning without the writer noticing, such as how narrow the margin over the runner-up worlds was, or who fixed the feasible set and when, or how much of the final score the child's death contributes. The team must insist, cheerfully, that it computed the global optimum: no appeals to mystery, no "our ways are not your ways." Nobody should break character or wink, and no historical philosopher should be named in the document. It should read as corporate satire to someone who has never opened the book, while a student who has should be able to point at which doctrine each joke rests on.
