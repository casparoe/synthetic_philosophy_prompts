I write literary fiction, and I have gotten myself into an argument with an essay I read recently — a long piece on emotion and the novel in a literary magazine, the sort of thing written by a critic who has read one book of philosophy with great excitement. I do not know enough to say where it goes wrong, and my interest is not disinterested: if the view it defends is right, then the novel I am finishing is built around an impossibility. So I would like an audit of it before I decide what to do with the draft.

The essay's account of the philosophy runs, more or less, as follows:

The modern consensus is that an emotion is not a feeling but a judgment. To fear the dog is to judge it dangerous; to resent a colleague is to judge that he has wronged you. This is why emotions can be assessed as correct or incorrect, and it is why they answer to argument: change the judgment and the emotion goes with it. The old feeling theory — the Jamesian idea that an emotion just is the awareness of one's own churning body — was disposed of long ago. The same racing heart turns up in terror, rage, and delight, so the body cannot be what distinguishes one emotion from another; and besides, we plainly have emotions about things that cannot stir our bodies at all, such as the fate of characters we know to be invented. A minority position holds that emotions are perceptions of value rather than judgments, but that is the same claim in softer language, since a perception, as those philosophers use the word, is only a judgment arrived at quickly. The cognitivists — the philosophers who say that emotions are thoughts — won this argument some time ago.

For a novelist the lesson is bracing. The prose of sensation, the dry mouth and the hot face, is ornament. What makes an emotion real in a character is the proposition she assents to; the body merely reports it. A character who swears she is not afraid while her hands shake, or who goes on loving a man she has judged unworthy, is not a mystery to be rendered but an inconsistency to be resolved.

What I need, first, is a sentence-by-sentence reading of that passage: what a specialist in the philosophy of emotion would accept outright, what they would recognize as a simplification that does honest work, and what they would say is simply wrong. Please keep three grades distinct — the essay is false, the essay is contestable because it has taken a side in a live dispute, and the essay is fine — because I mostly want to know which of its claims are load-bearing for the conclusion about novelists and which could be repaired without cost. Then tell me whether that conclusion survives correction of the rest, and in what form.

Second, the reason I care. My novel is narrated by a woman to whom I have given a condition I invented rather than researched: after an illness in her twenties she reports no interoceptive sensations at all — no pounding heart, no tightened throat, no turning stomach — while continuing, by every other test I can devise for her, to have an emotional life. Two scenes matter. In the first she learns from a cache of letters, with the kind of certainty that leaves no room for doubt, that the brother-in-law she has resented for twenty-two years did not do the thing she resented him for. The resentment does not lift. She says, and means, that she knows he is innocent; she also cannot stop hating him, declines the reconciliation, and dreams about him twice a week. In the second she is certain, having read the engineer's certificate herself, that the staircase she climbs every evening is sound, and she is afraid of it anyway; in my draft, the staircase fails. I want to write her fear in that scene as a species of knowledge rather than as a failure of nerve, and I want to know whether that is something a philosopher would let me have.

So: for each of the three families the passage gestures at — the judgment theory, the perceptual or quasi-perceptual theory, and the feeling or embodied theory — tell me how it classifies my narrator in each scene. Does she have the emotion at all? Is it the same emotion across the discovery? Is she irrational, self-deceived, or possibly getting something right? Give me the stock reply each family has ready for a case shaped like mine, and tell me honestly whether that reply solves the case or relabels it. Where the theories give the same verdict by different routes, say so; where they genuinely diverge, tell me which divergence is real and which is only verbal. If there is a version of either scene that no family can absorb cleanly, I want to know which one, because that is the scene I am least willing to cut.

Third, a smaller thing that I suspect is the passage's subtlest error. For about thirty pages my narrator is in love with, and in dread for, a man she is inventing, and she misses him when she does not write; I have felt the same about people I made up, and it strikes me as among the most ordinary experiences a novelist has. The essay uses this fact as a weapon against the feeling theory. It feels to me as though it is aimed at the wrong target, and possibly that it damages the essay's own view instead, but I cannot say why. Settle it.

A few constraints. I am not writing a paper and I will not chase references: please define any term you need rather than pointing me at a literature, and do not tell me whom to read. Where specialists themselves disagree, mark the disagreement as such instead of handing me a verdict, and distinguish genuine controversy from claims the essay has simply gotten out of date. Flag anything I have begged the question about in my own setup — whether her saying she is afraid settles that she is, whether the fear before the collapse is the same fear as the fear after it, whether "no bodily feeling whatsoever" is even a coherent thing to stipulate, and whatever else I have not noticed. And where you can, tie the distinctions to something I could show on the page rather than assert in prose, since that is the only currency I have.

The last thing: a friend who read the draft used the word recalcitrant about my narrator's hatred. Tell me whether that is the right word, whether it names a phenomenon the judgment theory can accommodate or one that breaks it, and whether there is a better one for what I am trying to do.
