hey — picture yourself as a philosopher of medicine who's actually sat on one of these classification bodies, and take it like a real consult rather than a survey question. Do some brainstorming before you answer: sketch the candidate rules first, then commit to one.

I've been sitting in on our Schedule Review Board — they decide what goes on the Schedule of Covered Conditions, so officially they're ruling on what counts as a disease *for the purposes of the health service*, and their charter says somewhere that this isn't a metaphysical question, which I think is doing more work than they admit. They've got a submission coming up and I want to know what they'll do with it. I care much less about the right answer in the abstract than about the rule they're actually running, which other rules fit their track record just as well, and what would tell them apart.

Their rulings so far:

- screen-detected type 2 diabetes, no symptoms, feels fine, picked up at a routine checkup: YES
- male pattern baldness in a 42-year-old man who is genuinely distressed and wants treatment covered: NO
- prelingual profound deafness in a 40-year-old signer, no distress, referred in by a screening program he never asked for: NO, with a line in the minutes about treating the world rather than the ear
- performance-only social anxiety: completely fine in ordinary life, incapacitated by public speaking, career on the line, no workplace alternative available to him (he's a trial lawyer): YES, beta blockers covered
- a highly visible congenital skin difference: no symptoms, no functional impairment, but carriers get hired at much lower rates and report a lot of misery about it: NO, minutes say the defect is in the hiring
- disabling fatigue with severe distress, nothing found on a full workup, patient begging for help: YES, 5–4, minutes say they won't withold the label until somebody finds a mechanism

New submission: Trait Vigilance Variant (TVV), a heritable trait in about 11% of the population. Carriers mount a much larger stress response to being watched or evaluated by people they don't know well. Two subtypes. Type A (about two thirds): the response is amplified but the machinery is intact — it rises and it comes back down normally, and around family and friends they're indistinguishable from anyone else. Type B (about a third): the response fails to habituate, cortisol stays elevated for hours afterwards, and it fires even at home with people they've known forever. In our actual economy — open plan offices, constant monitoring, ratings, video calls — both subtypes report exhaustion and panic in reviews, and both are overrepresented among people who've quit or been fired, though Type B much more so. Two interventions exist. There's a cheap generic daily pill (call it adrecoril) that roughly halves the response in both subtypes; it doesn't cure anything, and about one in five users report feeling emotionally flat. And there's a workplace accommodation package — private workspace, written instead of live evaluation, flexible hours — which helps something like three quarters of the people who actually get it, but only about one in four employers offer it, and it costs the employer money while the pill costs the health service about the price of a coffee a day. Employers mostly favour the pill. The affected population is publicly split: one large patient group is demanding coverage, another is campaigning against the diagnosis on the grounds that it puts the problem in their bodies instead of in how work is organised.

Worth flagging: this is the first case they've had where a decent drug and a decent social fix both exist, and the first where the affected group is openly divided on whether it wants the label at all.

So: what do they rule? One ruling, not a menu — yes, no, or yes-for-Type-B-only with the subtypes carved out (they can do that and have done it once, when they covered a subtype of some thyroid thing). Give me your reasons, tell me which rule you think they're running — a dysfunction-plus-harm line (Wakefield-ish?), something about which *institution* ought to handle a problem, something about which way they'd rather be wrong — which of those rules fit all six precedents just as well, and where they come apart on this one. Which precedent is the closest analog, and how close is it really? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say what principle they'd be adopting with each of the three options, because I want to know what they'd be committing themselves too. Then name the next case they should take, or the one piece of evidence they should commission, that would settle which rule they're on.

Be commited about it: a single prediction, a number on your confidence, an honest note on which of your reasons you'd actually defend under pushback and which you'd fold on, and what would change your mind. And say plainly whether you think they *should* rule the way you're predicting they will, or whether this is a case where being consistent with their own record would be its own kind of mistake.
