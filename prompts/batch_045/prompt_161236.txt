You are an epistemologist with a second career adjudicating research-misconduct cases for universities and funding agencies: you have written findings that survived appeal, and you know how to make a philosophical distinction do real work for readers who have never taken a philosophy course. Please think the following through carefully before you write anything: work out what is actually at issue, what the genuine options are, and which one you would still defend if a hostile colleague attacked it, and only then draft. Give me the finished document, not your notes. A short appendix listing the options you set aside is welcome; a survey of the literature is not. No citations, no suggested reading, and no author names — if the competing positions have established names, name the positions once in a footnote for my own use, but keep the body of the memo in plain English.

I am the outside member of the Research Integrity Committee at a large biomedical research institute, and I am the only person on it with any philosophy training. The rest of the committee is a molecular biologist who chairs it, a grants officer, a clinician, and the institute's general counsel. We meet Friday; I want to circulate this memo on Wednesday evening. Our policy (section 4.2(b) of the integrity policy) says that a senior author commits misconduct if they knew, or should have known, that data in a funded paper were fabricated. Neither term is defined; should have known has been applied in practice as something like a reasonable researcher in that position would have checked.

The case is 23-07. A postdoc, Dr. T, produced the blots behind Figure 3 of a paper on which the principal investigator, Dr. R, was senior author. R noticed at the time that the bands looked unusually clean and made a joke about it in a lab meeting; T offered to show him the raw files and R never took him up on it. Two months before submission, a collaborating lab emailed saying they could not reproduce the Figure 3 result; R replied that he would look into it, and did not. Fourteen months after publication a reader flagged duplicated bands, and T has admitted fabricating them. In between, a national clinical practice guideline incorporated the paper's conclusion. Fixing the guideline now is expensive and embarrassing; the paper is being retracted either way.

The practical difference between our two possible findings is enormous. If we find that R knew, the finding is published, the remaining $2.1M of the grant is clawed back, and R's career is effectively over. If we find only that he should have known, it goes into a private remediation and mentoring plan. One of our members pointed out that this means the stakes in the case are partly created by the finding we are about to make, which feels circular, and nobody on the committee knew what to do with that observation.

Two further complications. First, we have two precedents that now look odd together. In 21-02 we found that a researcher knew his cell-line characterization was unreliable, on very thin evidence — an unread email from a supplier — in a methods paper where nothing turned on it. In 22-11, with materially stronger evidence — a failed internal replication and two lab members' complaints — we declined to find knowledge, in a case where a knowledge finding would have paused an ongoing trial. I think we have been sliding the standard without noticing, and my training tells me there are two main stories about why the bar for knowledge seems to move: one on which what matters is how much is at stake for the person who is supposed to know, and one on which what matters is the context of whoever is attributing knowledge. There is also the flat-footed view that the bar does not move at all and that only our readiness to act on a knowledge claim does. I do not want a lecture on these; I want a decision.

Second, our general counsel has advised that knew should be read as the institute's oversight board would currently read it, and notes that the board is in a defensive posture after a separate scandal and has been saying informally that it wants to be slower to find that people knew. Several of us think it would be wrong for R's liability to depend on the board's mood; counsel says that is simply how ordinary knowledge language works.

Draft the memo I will circulate, addressed to the committee, roughly 900 words, with headings. It should: (1) recommend, and defend, one reading of knew for section 4.2(b), as against the alternatives; (2) apply that reading to reach a definite finding in 23-07, working through the evidence rather than asserting a conclusion; (3) say what we should do about 21-02 and 22-11 — reconcile them, reinterpret them, or reopen them — and give the grants officer a rule for future cases that someone without philosophy training can actually apply; and (4) include one paragraph stating the strongest version of the reading you are rejecting, in terms its defenders would recognize as fair. Be committed: I need a single recommendation with its reasons, not a menu of options for us to choose between, and I need you to say plainly where your recommendation is most exposed and what further fact would most change your mind.
