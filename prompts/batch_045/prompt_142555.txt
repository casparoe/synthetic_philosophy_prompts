I teach an upper-division seminar called *Rational Choice and Games* — 16 students, cross-listed between philosophy and economics, one 90-minute session, a whiteboard and a room with movable tables but no projector, and small real stakes available (chocolate, or a token worth one point on the next problem set). The students have seen Nash equilibrium and mixed-strategy equilibrium, can compute an expected utility and can grind through a 2×2 indifference calculation, but at least four of them quietly stop writing as soon as an expression with a variable in it appears, and none of them, in last week's session, spontaneously asked whether a row might be beaten by *a coin flip between two other rows* rather than by some single row. That is the gap I want to close, and I want to close it without lecturing first: I want them to run into the phenomenon, name it themselves or nearly name it, and only then see the formal statement. So I need a runnable activity — a game with rules, roles, materials, payoff matrices and a payout scheme in small integers and probabilities like ½ — in which the participants are making actual choices against actual randomly generated moves (drawn cards, coins, a bag of tokens), not just filling in matrices on a worksheet. Assume no laptops and no calculators beyond phones used as coins.

Three things in particular should show up in what *happens*. First and most basic: a strategy of theirs that no single pure strategy beats, but which is beaten by a mixture of two others — and I'd like the mixture to be something they can literally implement by randomizing, so that the elimination feels like a recommendation they could act on, not a fact about a matrix. Second, once they have the feel of strict dominance by mixtures, a short contrast phase in which the same group discovers that *weak* dominance behaves badly: the order of elimination changes the answer, and in the worst case the game empties out entirely. I want that contrast to land as a difference in the rules they are playing under, not as a warning from me. Third, and this is the part I am least sure about, I have a half-memory of a gap that only opens up with three or more players: in a two-player game, I believe, "strictly dominated by a mixed strategy" and "never a best response to any probabilistic belief about what the other player will do" coincide, but with three players a strategy can apparently be a best response to a *correlated* conjecture about the other two players' moves while never being a best response to any pair of independent randomizations. If that is right, I want them to feel the difference with objects on the table: a correlated conjecture is exactly what you get by drawing one card from a shared deck that tells both of the other players what to do, whereas an independent conjecture requires two separate randomizers that can't see each other. If my half-memory is wrong, or if the honest statement is subtler than this, tell me so plainly and give me the nearest true thing instead — and if the example needs more than two moves per player or probabilities other than ½, say so explicitly. Along the same lines: I have never been clear whether it matters that the dominating mixture is allowed to put weight on strategies that were themselves eliminated in an earlier round, or whether the procedure has to restrict the mixture to the survivors; if you can make that choice visible in the activity, please do, and if the answer is that it makes no difference, tell me and I will drop it rather than fake a puzzle.

What I want back is the activity as I would run it: the materials list, the rules and roles, the payoff matrices (with the numbers chosen so nothing depends on arithmetic beyond halves and thirds), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes including how groups are formed and rematched, and the debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific parts of the idea — including the one-sentence formulation of what iterated elimination actually claims, which I'd like them to be able to state at the end (something about what common belief in rationality rules out, rather than about an algorithm). Then an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: the student who spots the dominance in thirty seconds and announces it, the student who wants to know why they shouldn't just take the safe option and ignore the whole apparatus, the group that confuses a dominating mixture with a mixed-strategy equilibrium, the algebra-averse students who disengage, and what I should do in each case. Finally, be frank about which feature of this material the activity cannot reproduce and which part only I can supply in words afterwards — I would rather know the limits than have the activity pretend to prove something it only illustrates. Please don't send me to papers or reading: I want the matrices, the rules, and the reasoning, and I want any claim of mine above that is false to be corrected rather than politely accommodated.
