Two colleagues of mine in the mathematics department fell into an argument after a seminar last week, and I agreed to referee it. On reflection I don't think either of them is obviously wrong, but I also can't reconstruct precisely where each of them stops being right, and I have no stake in the outcome. I'd be grateful for a careful adjudication.

Background. The seminar speaker was defending the standard style of heuristic argument in prime number theory: treat the statement *n is prime* as an event of probability 1/log n, as the prime number theorem suggests; presume that these events are independent across different values of n; read off a conjecture. Applied to pairs n, n+2 and corrected by the usual product over odd primes, ∏ p(p−2)/(p−1)² ≈ 0.660, this yields the Hardy–Littlewood conjecture for twin primes. One of my colleagues — call her R — argues this way routinely. The other — call him T — works in probability and is uneasy about it. What follows is their exchange afterwards, slightly tidied and numbered.

(1) T: The presumption of independence is not merely unproved; it is straightforwardly false in the very case in front of us. Take the prime 3. Independence predicts that the chance that neither n nor n+2 is divisible by 3 is (2/3)(2/3) = 4/9, while a moment's thought gives 1/3, since of the three residue classes mod 3 only n ≡ 2 survives. The ratio is 3/4, which is exactly the p = 3 factor of the correction product. So the speaker was not presuming independence at all. He was presuming independence *after conditioning on the residue of n modulo every small prime* — a different claim, and one which, taken to the limit, has to be stated as a uniformity or level-of-distribution assertion that is precisely the hard, unproved part. Calling it a presumption of independence conceals the entire content of the argument.

(2) R: That mislocates the content. The presumption is not an empirical claim awaiting confirmation; it is a normative default. Fix the marginals that the prime number theorem hands you and nothing else, and the product measure is the maximum-entropy, least-committal joint distribution. To insert correlations would be to claim information we do not have. On that reading the correction product is not a fudge fitted to the data: it is what you get when you condition on the arithmetic facts we do know, and the fact that the resulting constants match the numerical counts to many digits is evidence that the conditioning was done correctly, not evidence that independence was ever true.

(3) T: Two problems. First, maximum entropy is a policy for not over-claiming, not a route to true point predictions; it is sensitive to how the sample space is set up, and in a setting where every event is in fact deterministic, choosing the entropy-maximizing measure is choosing a representation rather than describing the integers. Second, and worse: the model is not merely unconfirmed, it is refuted. Maier's theorem shows that Cramér's model — the same independence presumption, applied to short intervals — gives the wrong answer: for fixed λ > 1, the number of primes in intervals of length (log x)^λ, normalized by its expected value, has no limit, the limit superior exceeding 1 and the limit inferior falling below it. Pintz obtained the same verdict for the mean square error in one form of the prime number theorem. And Granville's refinement, which conditions on divisibility by small primes, replaces the constant 1 in the strong form of Cramér's gap conjecture by 2e^(−γ) ≈ 1.1229. So here the model was wrong, and we learned which conditioning had been missed only from a theorem.

(4) R: But that is the same lesson again, not a refutation of the method. Every failure you cite is a failure to condition on enough; every repair is a finer conditioning. Maier's phenomenon and Granville's correction are themselves consequences of the structure of small-prime divisibility, which is exactly what the correction product encodes. So there is really one schema — presume independence only after conditioning on all arithmetic information up to some level z, then let z grow slowly relative to x — and the question is always which level. That is a research question inside the method, not an objection to it.

(5) T: Then the method is unfalsifiable, because the level gets chosen after one has seen the answer. Here is the criterion I would put in its place, to make it answerable: call a heuristic prediction *licensed* only if it is entailed by some uniformity statement about the sequence in question that is not currently known to be false, and entailed in such a way that if the prediction failed the uniformity statement would fail with it. On this criterion a twin-prime prediction is licensed only to the extent that it follows from a level-of-distribution input, and Cramér's strong gap conjecture is not licensed at all, since the uniformity it requires is refuted by Maier's theorem. But notice what licensing costs: a licensed prediction contains no more information than the unproved lemma it is deduced from. So a heuristic can only ever be a restatement; it can never be evidence.

(6) R: Your criterion misclassifies the best heuristic we have. By the parity phenomenon, no level-of-distribution input of the Bombieri–Vinogradov or Elliott–Halberstam kind suffices to produce the twin-prime asymptotic: sieves cannot separate primes from products of two primes, which is why bounded gaps came from Goldston–Pintz–Yıldırım and from Maynard and Tao rather than twin primes themselves. So whatever warrants the Hardy–Littlewood prediction, it is not a uniformity statement of the sort your criterion permits. If your test demotes that prediction to unlicensed while its constant matches the counts to high precision, the test is wrong. The alternative is to admit Möbius randomness, or something in that family, as admissible arithmetic information to condition on — but that is the heuristic again, one level up, which was my point in (4).

(7) T: Perhaps. But note the asymmetry: at every stage in this history the repair was supplied by a theorem — Maier's, Pintz's — not by the hierarchy correcting itself from the armchair. The refined heuristic became credible only once a theorem had said which conditioning had been missed. Heuristic arguments are therefore evidentially parasitic on theorems in a way that limits how much independent confidence they can generate, however well their constants fit the data.

Please go through the seven moves and tell me, for each, whether it is clearly right, right but with an important caveat, wrong but with a real point inside it, or simply wrong — and, more usefully, which specific inference in the move is doing the work and whether that inference holds. I am particularly interested in the following: whether the arithmetic in (1) really shows that *independence* is the wrong name for what the heuristic assumes, or only that the name needs a gloss; whether the maximum-entropy defense in (2) can carry the weight R puts on it, given that the predictions at issue are point-valued asymptotics rather than qualitative expectations; whether Maier, Pintz and Granville are best read as refutations of Cramér's model or as refinements of it, which is what (3) and (4) disagree about; whether the licensing criterion in (5) is even coherent, and whether the parity objection in (6) genuinely defeats it or merely relocates it; and whether the parasitic-on-theorems point in (7) is a real limitation of heuristic evidence or only a truism about non-deductive methods generally.

Then, in a closing section, say what you take both of them to be missing — some distinction neither draws, or a way of formulating the presumption of independence that would dissolve part of the dispute — and give an overall verdict on who is more right, with some indication of how much more right.

Two constraints on the answer: please spend nothing on citations, references or suggestions for further reading, since I have a library and it is the adjudication I want; and if I have misstated any of the mathematics above — a constant, a theorem, an attribution — say so in a line and then adjudicate the corrected version of the exchange rather than the version I wrote down.
