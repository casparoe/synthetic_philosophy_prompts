Long time lurker, first time actually posting one of these.

I've spent about two years reading every ruling out of the Chamber for Religious Affairs in a jurisdiction I'll keep vague — picture a German-style state-collected church tax grafted onto a US-style no-establishment clause, with a French-style neutrality rule inside the schools. I know that combination isn't stable, that's sort of the point, and I know the real-world analogues so I don't need them named. I'm trying to predict what they'll do with an application that's genuinely pending, and me and a couple of friends have three different theories about what rule they're running and we can't seperate them.

Their track record as I read it:

1. "Recognized bodies" get a surcharge collected by the tax authority from self-declared members and remitted back, minus 3% for admin. Recognized so far: the historic majority church, the old Jewish community, and — four years ago — an Orthodox diocese with no historic presence in the country at all, mostly recent immigrants. Refused: a pagan association (reason given: no stable structure, too few members) and a mailing list that applied as a joke.

2. No public money for buildings of worship. They turned down a construction grant for a mosque but gave it the same nonprofit loan guarantee any registered association gets.

3. Pastoral care in public hospitals and prisons is funded per-capita and delivered through the recognized bodies. For everyone else the state pays per-visit honoraria to whatever minister the patient or inmate asks for.

4. Schools: the state pays the salaries of religious-education teachers nominated by recognized bodies. Parents can withdraw kids into an ethics-and-citizenship class, taught by state teachers on a state syllabus. Two requests to have an outside group's own state-paid teachers for its own classes — one from a Muslim association, one from a humanist league — were both refused, and both times the stated reason was just that the applicant wasn't a recognized body.

5. Heritage: the state pays upkeep on listed pre-1900 churches and abbeys, including keeping them open for worship, and funds the cathedral choir school, which is open to any child.

6. Old one: a freethinkers association applied for recognition and was refused, but the refusal basically said they didn't do any of the things the scheme funds — no regular assemblies, no rites of passage, no pastoral visiting — and wanted to spend the money on advocacy and a magazine. So the categorical question was never reached.

The pending application is from a non-theist congregation, about 14,000 self-declared members (more than the Jewish community), which does basically the whole package: weekly Sunday gatherings in a rented hall with singing, a reading and a short talk; trained celebrants who do namings, weddings and funerals and visit members in hospital and prison; a written curriculum for a non-religious worldview class in schools. No deity, no prayer, and they describe the gathering as shared attention rather than worship. About a fifth of their declared members are also registered members of the majority church, and the congregation says it's fine with those people paying the surcharge twice. They want four things: (a) recognized status and the tax mechanism, (b) state-paid teachers for their worldview class on the same per-pupil basis as recognized religious education, (c) entry into the standing per-capita pastoral scheme instead of the honoraria route, (d) their celebrants authorized to conduct legally binding marriages.

What I want isn't really the four verdicts, it's the rule. Give me the two or three candidate rules that fit 1 through 6 about equally well, what each predicts on (a) through (d), your best guess on the actual outcome with the reason you prefer that rule, and then the one further application — not one of these four — that would actually tell the rules apart. If two of the rules come apart nowhere on (a) through (d), say so and say which pair. My own guess is they grant (a) and (c) and deny (b) and (d), on something like: the state pays for care and for state-syllabus instruction but never for the formation of conviction, and the pass-through isn't state money anyway. But I genuinely can't tell whether that's their rule or just mine.
