I'm writing a paper for a seminar on moral cultures — the three-way contrast between honor–shame cultures, dignity cultures, and the victimhood culture that some sociologists argue is now displacing both — and I want to use Fred Zinnemann's *High Noon* (1952, screenplay by Carl Foreman, from John W. Cunningham's 1947 story "The Tin Star") as my central case rather than as a passing illustration. What I need is a reading of the film through that framework, and I'd like it to be a critic's reading rather than a plot summary with labels attached.

Let me say how I'm currently holding the three categories, since my use of them may be idiosyncratic. Honor cultures make standing depend on a publicly witnessed reputation for willingness to retaliate, and they accord low moral status to the party who absorbs an insult without answering it. Dignity cultures hold that standing is equal and not at stake in slights, that the insulted party is expected to forbear, and that redress, when it is needed at all, goes through impartial third parties. Victimhood cultures invert the honor gradient by conferring the highest moral status on the injured party and treating the public recruitment of third-party sympathy as the normal remedy. That last one is the hardest to find in a Western, and part of what I want to know is whether it is in this film at all.

My tentative thesis, which I want tested, is what I've been calling the delegation thesis: dignity does not abolish the honor logic so much as outsource retaliation — to courts, to employers, to the state, to public opinion — and it forbids self-help precisely because the delegate is supposed to be reliable. On that account, dignity's claim to be morally superior to honor is not unconditional; it is a claim conditional on institutional reliability, and where the delegate defects, dignity should collapse back either into honor or into complaint. Hadleyville looks like a laboratory for exactly that: the judge who sentenced Frank Miller flees and tells Kane to do the same, the mayor advises flight, the saloon and the congregation refuse to be deputized, and the man who insists on staying ends up fighting alone in an empty street. So my first question is whether the film bears out the conditional reading or refutes it — whether Kane is best understood as a dignity-culture actor holding to a standard no institution will back, or as the last honor-culture actor in a town that has quietly converted, in which case the film's sympathy for him would be nostalgia rather than moral argument.

I also want the reading done at the level of specific scenes, because I think the details pull in different directions. Harvey Pell's bargain — he will stand with Kane only if Kane puts in a word for him with the town fathers — looks like honor brokered through patronage, but his resentment is also a procedural grievance of exactly the sort a dignity culture recognizes; is the film unfair to him, and does my framework require me to be? The church scene is the one I keep returning to: townspeople who invoke the town's good name and its property values against Kane are using reputational reasoning, the characteristic currency of honor, as an argument for doing nothing, which is the characteristic tactic of a restraint culture. Sam Fuller hiding behind his door while his wife lies about it, and Herb Baker withdrawing when he finds he is the only volunteer, look to me like forbearance degrading into covert avoidance and then into desertion — a genealogy the dignity-culture literature tends not to draw. And Amy, the pacifist, finally shoots Pierce from behind, an act the honor code should despise and the film rewards; I don't know whether that makes the film's moral verdict independent of honor logic or merely shows honor logic granting an exemption for defense of others. The dropped star at the end is where I most need help: is it a repudiation of the honor economy, or its final and most expensive gesture?

Two constraints, please. Where the film resists the framework — where calling it a study in moral cultures flattens it into a genre piece about a marshal and a clock, or into the familiar parable about a community's cowardice — say so plainly, and say what the resistant remainder is. And I'd rather have your own candidate for what the film adds to the debate than a restatement of the categories: if there is something about moral cultures that a real-time drama about waiting, audiences and witnesses shows and an argument does not, that is the sentence I'm trying to write.
