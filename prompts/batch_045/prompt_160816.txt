I'm putting together a problem set for a third-year seminar on rational choice, and I want one question that does more than rehearse the textbook money pump. The version my students have all seen is the cyclic case: the agent prefers A to B, B to C and C to A, a trader offers a sequence of swaps for a small fee, and she ends up back with A and out of pocket. What I want instead is a case built around preferences that are never intransitive. At every time the agent's ranking of the items is complete and transitive; her beliefs about matters of fact are probabilistically coherent and never change unexpectedly; she has perfect foresight of her own future rankings; and she never acts against her current all-things-considered judgement. The one unusual thing about her is that her ranking of the two items flips back and forth over time — the way a preference for the sunny flat flips to a preference for the quiet one as the year turns — and, crucially, the intensity of the preference varies from one occasion to the next, so that on some occasions she would happily pay more than the fee to swap and on others she would not. She foresees all of this and regards every change as legitimate: no stage of her thinks any other stage is in error.

What I need is an ordinary, realistic scenario — a neighbour, dealer or acquaintance who happens to own the other item and offers to swap it for a modest fee, repeatedly over a year or so; no science fiction, nothing a normal person could not face, no predictor better than a shrewd acquaintance, no lies or hidden information, and the fee must be pure profit from her point of view rather than payment for anything she would list as a benefit. Keep the scenario itself to a single short paragraph. And I need it engineered so that three ways of handling the sequence give three different results. First: someone who simply re-optimizes at each offer, taking the swap whenever her current ranking favours it, must be drainable without bound — for any amount you care to name, a long enough sequence of offers leaves her poorer by more than that and holding something she could simply have kept from the outset. Second: someone who foresees the whole sequence and works backwards through it must lose only a bounded amount, and I want the bound stated exactly, together with the reason it holds. Third: someone who at the outset commits, costlessly and with full knowledge, to a plan covering every future offer must end up holding the item her earliest self ranks highest, and must pay no more than that same bound. Then the part I actually care about: I want the comparison between the second and third chooser to reverse depending on whose verdict we ask for. By the lights of her earliest self the committed chooser does strictly better; by the lights of her final self, counting everything she has spent rather than treating it as sunk, the look-ahead chooser does strictly better. The verdicts have to fall out of the preferences, the offer structure and the three rules — please do not simply stipulate what each chooser does, and do not smuggle in a mistake, a deception or a charge for a real service to make the numbers work.

After the scenario I would like: the backward-induction working for all three choosers, with the amounts made explicit; a statement of which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed — if the intensity of her preference were constant across occasions, if the ranking never flipped at all, if she could not foresee the flips, or if commitment carried even a small realistic cost; and the strongest reply you can construct from someone who holds that diachronic rationality demands nothing beyond being rational at each time and updating by conditioning, since on that view no stage of this agent has done anything wrong and there is nothing here left to explain.
