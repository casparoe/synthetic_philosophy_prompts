I write literary fiction — three novels, and I teach an advanced workshop most autumns — and for the last year I have been reading, slowly and with a dictionary, about how economics gave up on measuring happiness and replaced it with something supposedly thinner and harder: the doctrine that an agent's wants are nothing over and above what her choices disclose, that the numbers assigned to those wants have no unit and no common scale across persons, and that any claim about the inner magnitude or the felt texture of a desire is, strictly speaking, idle. My formal training is in no relevant field; I lasted one term of a philosophy-and-economics survey before switching to English, and while I can use words like ordinal, budget set, and maximisation in a sentence I could not define them under pressure. So I do not want an explanation of the debate so much as a translation of it. The vocabulary I actually think in is the vocabulary of craft: show-don't-tell, subtext, in-character and out-of-character, objective and obstacle, the arc, voice, the scenic menu of live options a scene puts on the table, and a private document I keep for each novel called the want ledger, in which I write down what I take each character to want and revise it as the draft goes on.

Here is why I am asking. I am finishing a novel whose narrator, Margot, spends a decade turning down a promotion she has plainly been positioned for, saying each time that she never wanted it, and then, in the last third, litigates furiously for a version of the same thing under another name. My readers and my editor agree on the facts of her behaviour and disagree about everything else. Some say she'd never do that, which I take to mean not that the action is improbable but that it proceeds from nothing. Others say the litigation is the truest thing about her and the refusals were the evasion. I have found myself using the word real about her want in a way I cannot cash out, and I have noticed something else: whether Margot reaches for the thing in a given scene depends on which other options I have left on the page. Remove the alternative and she does not move at all; put a worse alternative beside it and she suddenly wants it badly. In workshop I call this the cut-option test, and I use it to decide whether a want is load-bearing or an artefact of the scene, but I have started to suspect that the test is doing the same work as a technical apparatus I cannot read. I also do, on every page, something I am told is impossible: I weigh the intensity of Margot's desire against her brother's and decide whose loss the chapter is really about, and I do it not by measuring anything but by allocating sentences.

So please do this. Restate the revealed-preference picture of agency, and the quarrel about what the term utility means once it has been prised loose from happiness or felt satisfaction, as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary — tell me what a budget set is in terms of a scene, what the consistency conditions on choice amount to as constraints on character, what it would mean to say that a utility number is only an indexing of behaviour rather than a quantity of anything. Then give me the residue report. Which of their claims translate cleanly into craft terms, which translate only if you quietly import an assumption that is mine or theirs rather than common to both, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all? I am especially interested in three places where I expect the translation to fail: my conviction that as her maker I know what Margot wants prior to and independent of any choice of hers, in a way no observer could; the phenomenon in which a want survives its own renunciation, so that the refusal is evidence for the desire rather than against it; and my practice of comparing the force of one character's want with another's by compositional means. For each, say whether the residue indicts my vocabulary or theirs — whether it shows that the craft terms are doing real work economics has no room for, or that what I call knowledge of a character is less clear than it feels from inside the writing of it. And then tell me, practically, what I should do with Margot.
