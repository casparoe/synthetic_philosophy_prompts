I'm writing a paper for a philosophy of science reading group on the epistemology of long-run social predictions, using Malthus as my central case study, and I've drifted into a position I want stress-tested rather than confirmed. The position has three parts: first, that the Malthusian mechanism — in an organic economy, durable improvements in productivity or real income get converted into additional people rather than into higher living standards, so per-capita income is pinned near a subsistence floor and technical progress shows up as headcount rather than as wages — was a correct description of the world before roughly 1800; second, that the divergence after 1800 is explained by an exogenous change in the constraints (fossil energy and mined or fixed nitrogen, then the modern epidemiological and fertility transitions) rather than by the mechanism's being false; and third, that a verdict of right-but-early, or better right-but-scope-restricted, is therefore legitimate rather than a face-saving rescue. A friend in the group replies that the whole thing is unfalsifiable, because the positive check can always be deferred: the theory only ever says eventually, and every decade in which famine fails to arrive is quietly treated as more time in which it may. What I want from you is a map of my exposure, not a verdict on who wins.

Concretely, I'd like three things. First, separate the theory's discriminating content from its merely true content. I've been working with four candidate signatures of a Malthusian regime: long-run flatness of real wages alongside large improvements in technique; a positive response of nuptiality and fertility to real wages, and of survival to real wages; productivity shocks being absorbed by population rather than by income; and ceiling-like behaviour in which population, not living standards, takes the strain. Which of these would a Boserupian, an institutionalist, or a Smithian commercialisation story predict as well, and which are genuinely diagnostic? Second, give me the counterfactual record: what would the pre-industrial data have had to look like to count against the view — sustained improvement in living standards in an organic economy, fertility or marriage unresponsive to income, a positive long-run association between wages and population — and how much of that has arguably already been observed? Third, here is the case I keep circling and would find most damaging if properly made: in rich contemporary societies the income–fertility gradient has not merely flattened but inverted, with fertility falling below replacement at high incomes. Is that a failure of the mechanism, or a reversal of the channel (a quantity–quality substitution) that the mechanism can tolerate, and how would I tell the difference in principle? I'd also like your judgement on a test I've been toying with and cannot tell is fair. Suppose a permanent and abrupt removal of the industrial inputs to agriculture — synthetic nitrogen, mechanisation, fossil-fuelled transport — with all relevant knowledge left intact. My view seems to predict a fairly swift snap-back towards a much lower carrying capacity with living standards near subsistence; an induced-innovation or Boserupian view seems to predict something quite different. Does that experiment discriminate at all, or does the stipulation that knowledge is retained stack it against one side? And is there any observed historical case of an endogenous escape from the trap that was not preceded by an energy or disease windfall?

Finally, I want the meta-questions handled bluntly. Rank the defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and in doing so say which ones a competent defender of my position ought to concede rather than explain away — I care more about what the view risks than about what its critics allege. Distinguish the kinds of revision open to me: restricting scope to organic economies; adding an auxiliary mechanism (Malthus himself added moral restraint when he expanded the essay, and one might similarly add human-capital substitution today); or switching the target observable from famine to wages to fertility — and tell me which of these revisions are progressive and which merely degenerating. Then assess whether the structure of the case, and not the evidence, is doing the real work: a prediction about a two-century transition gets essentially one trial, so early and never are hard to separate in real time, and my position may be protected less by what has been observed than by the option value of deferral. If it is so protected, is that a defect here in a way it is not in long-run climatology or geology? And if the theory turns out to bind only in a regime we have every reason never to re-enter, does counting it as right do any work at all, or is the honest conclusion that it survives as a how-possibly model of a closed system rather than as a live constraint on us?
