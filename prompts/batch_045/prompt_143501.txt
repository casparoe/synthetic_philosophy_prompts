I am drafting the framework chapter of a book manuscript on cultural membership and the limits of liberal legitimacy, and before I build the rest of the book on it I would like a structural audit of the commitments I have assembled. My suspicion is that at least one of them cannot bear the weight I am putting on it, but I cannot locate the fault myself, and I would rather find it now than in review. The commitments as I currently hold them are these.

1. Anti-voluntarism about the self. I reject the picture of the agent as able to step outside all of its ends and attachments and select among them. Persons are partly constituted by memberships they did not choose, and a political theory that treats such membership as a preference the agent happens to have — one ingredient in a life plan rather than a condition of there being a life plan to revise at all — is defective at the level of the person it assumes.

2. Neutrality as a doctrine about justification. Coercive law is legitimate only if it can be justified to each person subject to it on grounds that person could not reasonably reject; in particular, the state may not justify coercion by appeal to the intrinsic superiority of some conception of the good life. I mean this as a constraint on the grounds of justification, not on effects: I am not committed to the claim that a legitimately neutral state has neutral consequences.

3. Group-differentiated rights. I defend self-government rights for minority nations, land and language rights for indigenous peoples, and exemptions from generally applicable law for religious minorities, and I defend them on the ground stated in (1): participation in a continuing cultural structure is constitutive of its members' identity, so the interest at stake is not comparable to an interest in a practice or a preference, and its loss is not compensable by transfers of other goods.

4. An asymmetry verdict. I hold that the case for cultural accommodation is available to minorities and not, in the same form, to majorities. A sub-state nation may restrict the language of schooling and of commercial signage within its territory; a majority nation may not appeal to the protection of its own way of life in order to restrict immigration or to impose a curriculum designed to secure a thick national identity in its children.

5. Autonomy-facilitating education as a constraint on (3). I also hold that the state owes every child a genuinely open future, and that this is enforceable against the child's own community: compulsory common schooling to sixteen, including instruction in the sciences and in the range of ways of life available in the wider society, over the objection of parents and communities who would withdraw their children earlier or shield them from that instruction.

6. Method. Two claims that I have been treating as a package: (a) a principle has critical purchase on a political community only if it can be articulated as an interpretation of the shared self-understandings already present in that community — a principle that cannot be stated in the moral vocabulary actually available there is inert for it, however sound it is in the abstract; and (b) the account I am building is intended to hold for any society marked by reasonable pluralism, not only for the two or three societies I know well.

7. The device I have been using to reconcile (1) with (5), which I will call the non-alienating inheritance condition. The thought is that autonomy need not be a presupposition of agency — which (1) denies — but only a condition on an unchosen attachment's being genuinely the agent's own rather than something that has merely happened to them: an inheritance counts as non-alienating only if the agent retains a real capacity to revise or repudiate it. On this reading (5) does not compete with (1) but completes it, by supplying the test for when constitution has degenerated into alienation.

What I want is an audit rather than a treatment of the subject. Go through the set and say, for each pair that looks to be in conflict, whether the conflict is genuine or merely apparent, and rank the genuine ones by severity. I expect them to differ substantially in how much damage they do, and a list of tensions weighted equally would not be useful to me. For each genuine tension, state precisely what would have to give — a restriction of scope, an added premise, a change of justification, or the abandonment of a commitment — and say what that revision costs somewhere else in the set. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what it would leave me unable to say.

Three further requests about how you proceed. First, say explicitly which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why, since I would rather not give up something I can keep. Second, tell me whether the non-alienating inheritance condition actually performs the reconciling work I am asking of it in (7), or whether it is a redescription of a claim that (1) and (2) jointly rule out. Third, do not settle anything by appeal to what is standard in the literature or to what some theorist holds; I can read that myself. I want to know what my own commitments commit me to, including where they commit me to something I have not noticed.
