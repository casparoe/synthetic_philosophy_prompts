I'm reading a popular book about how we know anything about the past (it sort of gestures at Foucault and at people who write on colonial archives, but never really names names), and it sums up the whole "archives and power" idea like this:

"Archives are not neutral containers of the past, they are the paperwork of government. A record exists because somebody in power needed to write something down in order to tax, police, or manage the people it describes, so the archive is less a memory of the past than a toolkit for ruling it. That means the silences in an archive are not accidents — they are the marks of what power found inconvenient, and behind every missing document there is somebody's decision. This gives the historian a clear method. Suppression has to be carried out by people working inside a bureaucracy, so it leaves traces: a gap in a numbered run of files, a sudden change in what a clerk bothers to write down. Read against the grain and you can recover the voice that was being suppressed, in something close to its own terms. It also settles the question of digitization: every page scanned and put online is one less thing an archivist can hide from you, so mass digitization is straightforwardly on the side of the historian. And appraisal — the archivist's word for deciding what to keep — is basically censorship with a nicer name. The uncomfortable corollary is that some people show up in the archive only as objects of somebody else's paperwork, a name in a jail register or a line in a relief roll, and there we are not recovering their voice at all, we are just reading our own assumptions off a document someone else wrote. If that is all we have, the honest thing to do is say nothing about them."
