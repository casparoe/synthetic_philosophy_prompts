I'm TAing for an intro political philosophy course and one of my students handed in this as a paper proposal. It's better than I expected and I still think it's wrong, but I can't put my finger on where.

(1) The harm principle: coercion is legitimate only if it prevents harm — a setback to someone's interests — to someone other than the person being coerced.
(2) Whether an action sets back interests depends on its expected effects given the whole causal situation, including how other people will in fact react; whether the agent intended or foresaw those effects is irrelevant to whether the condition is met.
(3) Call it uptake drift: on any networked platform the audience a message actually reaches is not the one the speaker addressed. Any public utterance has some chance of being relayed, screenshotted, quoted or translated into a context where it foreseeably triggers harmful downstream reactions — riots, harrassment of a named individual, copycat acts. So every public utterance carries nonzero expected harm.
(4) Once you index the speech act to the audience it actually reaches, there's no stable difference between harmless and harmful speech: the same sentence in a private group chat and on the front page of a national paper is a different act then, with different expected effects, and the difference is made by other people's choices rather than the speaker's.
(5) So the harm principle can't carve out any protected class of speech: for any utterance there is some audience it could reach such that banning it satisfies the principle. And it's worse than that, because the permissibility of the ban is self-fulfilling — whether the thing is banned changes who ends up seeing it and how they respond, which changes its expected harm.
(6) So either the harm principle constrains nothing about speech regulation, or it has to be read as a balancing test — weigh expected harm against the costs of suppression — which is just consequentialism, the very thing the principle was supposed to rule out.
(7) Hence there is no stable middle position on speech between near-absolutism and straight consequentialism.

I want to keep (1) and (2), and (3) looks to me like it's just true about how platforms work, but (7) is obviously false — there are plenty of sensible middles. Which step do you reject and what does rejecting it cost you? And one thing I care about in particular: whatever you do to (4), I want to still be able to keep the heckler's veto out of (1), i.e. I don't want harms that consist in other people's wrongful reactions to count as the relevant harm. Tell me whether I can have both, and if not, which one is cheaper to lose.
