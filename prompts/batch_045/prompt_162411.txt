Next Saturday I'm giving the six-minute invited address at a naturalization ceremony: around two hundred people taking the oath, their families, a good many small children, the federal judge who will administer it, and a local TV crew that will cut the whole thing to a single sentence for the evening news. The judge asked for the theme "the widening circle," asked specifically that the speech contain the word "welcome," and asked for no jargon and no politics. I work on the history of moral change, and I can't give the speech that theme seems to call for. I want it built around two distinctions. First: concern has historically widened less through people discovering that outsiders were "really like us" than through ceasing to make resemblance the condition for concern at all — which makes the standard ceremony line both condescending and, I think, false, and leaves intact the authority of whoever is doing the recognizing. Second: being inside the circle is a practice rather than a status, so the speech cannot say "we now owe you the same concern we owe each other" as if owing followed automatically from admission. And "welcome" has to be recovered from the fact that welcoming is what hosts do to guests, which after the oath these people will not be. Further constraints: the widening must not be described as a steady arc, since several people in the room fled countries where it ran backwards; the country must not be flattered, since flattery repeats the original exclusion in a softer voice; the room must not be scolded or lectured; and the speech must be written so that no single sentence lifted out of it would read, on the six o'clock news, either as national self-congratulation or as a slight to the people being sworn in. Please write it.
