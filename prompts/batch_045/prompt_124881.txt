I convene a graduate reading group on well-being, and I am preparing a one-page handout that sorts readers on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: what, if anything, the many states we call pleasures have in common in virtue of which they are all pleasures. I want the handout built as a taxonomy rather than as a survey of named positions, and I want it built from the logical space first, with the actual views located in that space afterward.

Constraints on the instrument:

1. Begin with three or four branching questions, not with a list of theories. Each branch must be a concrete case or a claim about a case that a first-year graduate student can answer without knowing which view it leads to, and no question may ask anything of the form do you think pleasure is a feeling, or otherwise invite self-identification by label. A reader who answers honestly should land on the view they would defend under pressure.

2. The following cases must all be load-bearing somewhere in the tree, because I have chosen them to block the easy moves: a climber absorbed for hours in a hard route who attended to nothing about her own experience at the time and reports no feeling when asked; the relief that follows the stopping of a toothache, which seems to consist in an absence; delight at a fictional character's escape, where the object is not believed to obtain; the dry satisfaction of closing a long proof, with no distinctive sensory phenomenology anyone can locate; mild background contentment that the subject would not have reported unless prompted; the first experience of a harshly astringent food, enjoyed and disliked at once; and a case in which a subject's measurable reward-system response and his own reports of enjoying come apart, where the question is which of the two has evidential priority here.

3. At least seven and no more than ten leaves. The following must appear: the view that pleasures share a distinctive felt quality; the view that pleasure is a way or mode of undergoing an experience rather than an experience of its own; the view that pleasure consists in a pro-attitude toward an experience or its object; a hybrid of the previous two; the view that pleasures share only a functional or common-currency role in learning and action; the view that pleasure is a determinable whose determinates share nothing qualitative, or the disjunctivist variant on which the class is a gerrymander; and the eliminativist option on which serious psychology and ethics should stop using the category. Each leaf carries its name, or unnamed if nobody holds it, one sentence of statement, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

4. One branch must be the double-duty constraint: whether whatever unifies the class must also be what explains why pleasure benefits the person who has it, or whether unity for classification and unity for value can come apart. A related branch must separate the theory of what pleasure is from the theory of what makes a life go well, so that no leaf wins by changing the subject.

5. After locating the named views, list at least three combinations of answers that the tree allows but that nobody defends, and say for each whether it is empty because incoherent, because unattractive, or because merely unexplored. No leaf may be defined so as to exclude the disagreeable pleasures or the phenomenologically silent ones by stipulation; those two are data for my purposes, and a view that handles them only by denial should say so at its leaf.

Close the handout with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion to that section: the five pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, and for each the further question that would separate them, or the reason no question could.
