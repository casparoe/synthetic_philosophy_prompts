hey — i've been going round in circles on the thing I keep seeing labelled the special composition question (roughly: what has to be true of some things for there to be something that they compose?) and I want a number out of you rather than another tour of the options.

First, two cases so we're talking about the same world. Call it the Lattice: an infinite cubic lattice of qualitatively identical, changeless simples, perfectly evenly spaced, no minds, no language, no interests, nothing anywhere that could care about how things are carved up. Question A: in the Lattice, does any collection of simples compose something, and is there a fact of the matter either way? Now Call it the Speck: the very same lattice except that exactly one simple has a slightly different intrinsic property — a marginally different charge, say — and that is the only asymmetry in the whole of reality. Question B: does that single blemish change your answer to A, and if it does, what does that tell you about what your view was really resting on?

Now the number. Give me your credence that the unrestricted answer to the SCQ is true — that any two or more things, however scattered, gerrymandered or short-lived, compose something — decomposed. I want rough sub-probabilities on at least: (1) that the SCQ has a determinate, mind-independent answer for every arrangement at all, as against its being vague, or relative to interests and purposes, or settled by which conventions we happen to adopt; (2) that, conditional on there being such an answer, it is the unrestricted one rather than the never-one (no composites ever) or the life-based one you see defended in the literature; (3) that a restricted answer couldn't merely be stipulated as a long disjunctive list of special cases — contact, cohesion, life, whatever — and still count as a genuine answer to the question rather than a refusal to give one; (4) whatever else you think belongs in there. And separately: how much of your headline number is carried by the Lattice, and does the Speck move it?

Then show me how the sub-probabilities combine into the headline figure, tell me which single one your final number is most sensitive to, and tell me what would move it most — a new argument about vagueness, something about how our object-concept gets off the ground in infancy, a cost-benefit on ontological simplicity, or something else entirely.
