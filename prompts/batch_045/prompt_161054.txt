ok so this might be a dumb question but here goes. In debate club we had a round about where the school's activity budget should go: keep funding the tiny robotics team that like 8 people are completely obsessed with, or put it into a school-wide spring festival that way more people get something out of. Afterwards my friend told me about this thought experiment with a "utility monster" — a being who gets, like, a thousand times more happiness out of everything than a normal person does, so any view that says "maximize the total good" seems to have to hand over basically everything to it. He said that's the standard objection to that slogan. But when I brought it up at practice someone said "no real person is like that, it's a dumb sci-fi case" and I couldn't tell whether that was actually a good reply or just a way of not answering.

So: what are the precise things "greatest good for the greatest number" could be telling you to do? My friend listed a few and they sound genuinely different — one where it means maximize total happiness, one where it means help the most people (which seems like it could give a different answer in our round? if 8 kids care enormously and 400 people are mildly into the festival, do those two versions disagree?), maybe something about averages, maybe something like "get everyone above a decent minimum first" — and I want a menu of the readings with verdicts: which are obvious to the point of being useless, which are real substantive positions someone defends, which are weird or arguably just false. And for each one, say whether the utility monster actually bites it, and whether it would have settled our round either way.

Two cases I made up that I want run through the same menu: (1) instead of one monster, imagine a huge group of people who are each only slightly better than average at turning resources into happiness — nobody individually looks like a monster, but the "maximize the total" version still funnels most of the stuff to them and you can't point at anyone and say he got too much. (2) a "reverse monster" — someone whose suffering from even small losses is enormous compared to everyone else's gains. Is that the same problem or a different one?
