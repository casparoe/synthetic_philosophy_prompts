I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist — forty years, mostly experimental, mostly staring at noisy data — and since I stopped teaching I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university. That's how I ended up spending the better part of two years on the question whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems have any consequences outside mathematics, and I cannot get anyone in that literature to move an inch.

Here's the shape of it as I see it. Everyone agrees on the mathematics: no consistent, sufficiently strong axiomatization of arithmetic is complete, such a system can't prove its own consistency, end of story. Then you get: (1) the Lucas–Penrose style argument that a human mathematician therefore isn't a machine, so mechanism about mind is refuted; (2) the standard rebuttals — the argument needs you to identify your own formalization and know that it's consistent, which you can't do, and anyway the machine gets exactly the same move, hand it its own Gödel sentence and it just adds it; (3) people who insist the theorem is a narrow result about formal systems and says nothing whatever about minds, physics, or anything else — and who then turn around and use it to argue that physics can never have a finite "theory of everything"; (4) the physics strand, which is the one I actually care about: a final theory would be a finite axiom set with a computable consequence relation, so there are questions in its own language it can't settle, plus the more careful version about whether some physical quantities might be non-computable.

What I can't work out is what kind of stalemate this even is. My hypothesis, and I want you to take it apart: none of it turns on the mathematics, all of it turns on what you're allowed to hold fixed while you "see" that the Gödel sentence is true, and once that's made explicit the argument either becomes trivial or becomes an empirical question about what bounded reasoners can actually do, for which nobody has any data. Two things push me that way. First, the moving-target point: S can't prove its Gödel sentence, but S together with the statement that S is consistent can, and so can any machine with a fixed rule for extending itself; to keep the diagonal argument alive you have to fix the update rule as well, and then you're off into transfinite progressions — ordinal logics, forever adding reflection principles — where what's growing isn't the machine's power but what we keep handing it. Second, a point I almost never see addressed: for a theory anyone could actually write down, the Gödel sentence has to encode that theory's own proof predicate, so the popular "we just see it's true" is a fixed point over the whole encoding, not the two-line insight it's made out to be, and I have no idea what survives when you put finite time and memory back into the picture.

So: diagnose it. Is this one disagreement or several going under one name? Is it verbal — about "mechanical", about "know", about what makes a reasoner the same reasoner over time — or is it a genuine empirical question about cognitive capacity wearing a logical costume, or a difference about what would count as a warrant, or just an ill-posed question? And in the physics strand specifically: is there any coherent notion of a physical theory's being "incomplete" in play here at all? Physics predicts by computing and measuring, not by proving; is "unprovable in the theory" quietly being traded for "undecidable" or "unmeasurable", and does that swap do all the work?

Two requests about how to answer. First, be committed: give me the diagnosis you actually think is right and your reasons for it, not a map of the options ending in a shrug — I can get the map from the reading group any Thursday. Tell me which strand has a live question in it and which one is heat; I'd much rather you said "this one is empty, here's why" than be even-handed about all four. And if anything would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a thought experiment about bounded reasoners, a better-stated question — say what it is and how much it would actually settle. Second, don't send me to papers: no bibliographies or reading lists, I'm not going to chase them down and I can't tell a good citation from a plausible-looking one anyway.
