I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in formal epistemology. My students have Bayes' theorem, strict conditionalization, and Jeffrey conditionalization with the rigidity condition, but no decision theory beyond that, and I want one 45-minute final-exam question on uncertain evidence that cannot be answered by writing down Jeffrey's formula and defining rigidity.

The question should turn on a principle I introduced in lecture and want them to test rather than apply. Call it the Coarsest-Cell Principle: when an experience leaves you uncertain rather than certain of anything, update by Jeffrey conditionalization on the coarsest partition of your algebra whose cells that experience discriminates, namely the partition generated by the proposition whose probability shifted, holding fixed your prior probabilities conditional on those cells.

Ask students to test this principle against two kinds of case and to say which, if either, it handles correctly. The first is a case in which the cells of the evidential partition are themselves what the experience is about: someone examines a cloth under poor lighting and comes to be 0.8 confident it is green rather than blue. The second is a case in which the experience is the receipt of a report from a channel whose reliability is itself uncertain and may be correlated with the hypothesis under test, such as a lab result, an informant's testimony, or an instrument readout. Students should be forced to say exactly what work rigidity is doing in each case, what has to be true of an evidential partition for its use to be legitimate at all, and what follows for the status of the rule itself, whether it needs a supplement, a restriction, or replacement by plain conditioning inside a richer algebra.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong. Make explicit what separates an A from a B answer: I expect the A answer to explain why a constraint on the new probability of a single proposition, as opposed to a whole partition, fails to determine any update, and what that underdetermination shows about whether there is a genuine problem of uncertain evidence at all, whereas a solid B answer may be right about everything else and still miss this.
