I sit on the committee rewriting how a large hospital system classifies and protects patient data, and one feature of the current practice keeps bothering me, because we are about to write it into policy.

DNA is carved out. A genetic test result gets its own consent form, its own retention rule, its own prohibition on use by employers and insurers, and its own vocabulary: the phrase in every statute and consent document is genetic information, never merely information. A family history of the same disease, a cholesterol panel, an imaging finding that predicts the same outcome far better than any genotype, a protein level — all of these are handled as ordinary clinical data, filed and shared under the general rules. Meanwhile, in the same institutions, the stretches of the genome that police, immigration authorities and paternity labs treat as maximally informative about who a person is are, as far as I can make out, short repeated sequences that vary enormously in copy number between people, lie outside the regions whose sequence spells out proteins, and are used precisely because they appear to do nothing and are therefore free to drift. And when we do look at the regions that spell out proteins, the reports come back hedged: variants of uncertain significance, incomplete penetrance, a change that tracks with disease in one family and not in another.

So the practice seems to run on two different notions of what makes a stretch of DNA informational, and on a metaphor — the genome as code, blueprint, instruction book, something read and now edited — that I cannot tell is load-bearing or decorative.

What I want is the implicit theory. What view of genetic information does this carve-out presuppose? Does it genuinely presuppose that DNA is a code in a substantive sense, a text with content and a reader, subject to a convention, carrying a message about the organism? Or would something weaker do: DNA as one causal predictor among others, distinguished only by being cheap, stable, effectively unwaivable, and revealing about people who never consented — my sister, my children? Does the practice quietly rule out the rival view that the sequence is only one developmental resource among many, on a par with the molecular and structural machinery already present in the egg? And are the identity strand and the disease strand actually resting on inconsistent commitments, or are both licensed by a single theory I am failing to see? Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where it is genuinely underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would license exactly the policy we already have.

Then tell me what would have to change. If we replaced the code-and-text picture with a plainly causal or predictive one, DNA as one more input to an actuarial estimate, what becomes of the carve-out: does the special category collapse into a general rule about predictive information, so that family history, proteomics and imaging have to be pulled inside it? If instead we took the code picture seriously, what else would have to count as genetic information — a methylation profile, a transcriptome, an edited cell line — and what would we do about the fact that the stretches most heavily relied on in criminal and immigration cases carry, on that picture, no message at all? I would also like your view on the one hard fact usually cited at this point: that the mapping from codons to amino acids is chemically arbitrary and is implemented by adaptor molecules rather than following from the chemistry of the bases themselves. Does that settle anything about whether DNA is a code, or only something considerably smaller than people take it to settle?

Be explicit about your own confidence: mark which claims you would defend to a hostile reader and which you are inferring or reconstructing, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
