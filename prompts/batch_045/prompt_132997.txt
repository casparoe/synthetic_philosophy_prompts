so me and my friend got into about a two hour fight last night about whether you have to follow local conventions when your just visiting somewhere, and we got nowhere because we kept sliding between three diffrent questions. So before I pick it up with him again I want the spec, not a verdict.

the fight: he tips everywhere. we were in a country where nobody tips and he left money on the table and I said that was actually worse than not tipping, and he said the only rude thing available is stiffing someone. then it moved to queueing — he says cutting a line is wrong everywhere, I said in a place where people don't really form lines you're not cutting anything, you're just standing somewhere — and then to jaywalking, where he crosses on red in a city where literally everyone crosses on red and I don't, and neither of us could say why that one feels different from the queue one. then he said something I couldn't answer: he said the real rule isn't comply or don't comply, it's that whoever was there first gets to set the convention, so what a newcomer owes isn't imitation, it's not unilaterally renegotiatng the terms. on his version you MAY hand over extra cash in a no-tipping place, because nothing changes for anybody else, it's just a strange gift, but you may NOT start a queue where there wasn't one, because now everybody standing behind you is suddenly a line-cutter. he calls it something like normative squatters' rights. I kind of hate it and it also splits the cases exactly the way I want, which is why I'm here.

so: write me the desiderata for a theory of convention-generated obligation. adequacy relative to a purpose, and here's mine — I want a theory good enough to tell me what I owe as an outsider in a place whose conventions aren't mine, and simultaneously what a host can legitimately resent and what a travel guide should actually print. concretely:

- the cases it has to handle, each with the reason it's a requirement: the visitor who complies; the visitor who doesn't; the visitor who complies badly or ostentatiously; the visitor who quietly becomes a resident; the visitor who is one of ten thousand (stag parties, cruise ships, remote workers staying six months — at which point "visitor" stops being a stable category at all); the case where the convention and the law point opposite ways, i.e. the thing is illegal and everyone does it or legal and nobody does it, with jaywalking as the test case; and the case where the traveler genuinely cannot tell what the local convention is. add one he threw at me late: a card machine in a no-tipping country that shows a tip screen, so the norm gets imported by the hardware — who's the outsider then?

- the distinctions it's not allowed to collapse: convention vs law vs morality; having a reason to comply vs being required to vs being blameworthy for failing to; convention as a coordination device vs convention as a signal of respect vs convention as a tax on people who don't know better; "the convention of the locals" vs "the convention of the place"; and whether the wrong, if there is one, is done to a person or to a practice.

- the stuff from social science and game theory it can't contradict: that a lot of conventions are arbitrary solutions to recurring coordination problems, so the content is contingent and the stability isn't; that conventions shift when enough people defect, which is how visitors ever change anything; and that tourists are systematically bad at detecting local conventions and their noncompliance is often completely invisible to the host.
