Write an essay of 1,200 to 1,800 words on the following problem in social philosophy and decision theory. Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, but finish by defending a single verdict: a survey of how the arguments balance is not an ending. Keep the essay self-contained — argue from the setup given below, and do not appeal to citations, to the contents of particular publications, or to what some named theorist is reputed to have shown.

**The calibration problem.** In decision theory a single person's utility (or welfare) function is standardly unique only up to positive affine transformation: if u represents someone's attitudes, then so does a·u + b for any a > 0 and any b. This is enough for choosing under risk for one person, and not obviously enough for choosing between people. To weigh a gain for one person against a loss for another, a planner seems to need at least a common unit of welfare; to weigh a life against no life at all, a planner seems to need a common zero. Call the difficulty of supplying either the calibration problem, and call the thesis that the problem has no solution worth having the incommensurability thesis.

**The device.** Suppose a technology, which I will call the transference collar, removes part of our ignorance. For any two living subjects i and j, the collar can place i, for a fixed short interval, in a state that is phenomenally and motivationally exactly like j's present condition, while i retains the knowledge that the episode is induced and temporary. Afterward, i reports comparisons on i's own intrapersonal scale, and not merely ordinal ones: i can report ratios of differences, of the form "the step up from j's condition to the best day of my life is about four times the step up from j's condition to the worst." Assume the reports are stable, honest, and obtainable for every ordered pair in a finite population. Assume also, for the sake of the argument, that the welfare of a whole life is the sum of the welfares of its temporal parts, so that a short induced episode can carry information about a life spent at that level. Note what the collar cannot do: it cannot be used on anyone who does not exist, and it cannot induce an episode of never having been born.

Write E_i(x) for the number that i's own scale assigns to undergoing an episode of situation x, where x may be the actual condition of some other person. Suppose that when the collar is run for every ordered pair, the extended scales turn out to agree completely: all judges order all situations alike, and all judges agree on every ratio of welfare differences between situations. That result — call it ratio-scale commonality — is the piece of new evidence whose significance your essay must assess.

**Two indeterminacies, easily confused.** (α) Each judge's own scale is, as always, measured only up to positive affine transformation. (β) Even if all the judges' extended scales turn out to be positive affine transforms of one another, the common scale they share would itself be fixed only up to a single global positive affine transformation. Settle which of these a planner is left with, and why the difference matters. Note that it matters a great deal: if (β) is all that remains, then for a fixed population the policy that maximizes total welfare is insensitive to the residual indeterminacy, since Σ(a·w + b) = a·Σw + n·b with a > 0 and n fixed; whereas if a separate indeterminacy survives for each person, summation is not well defined at all.

**The question your essay must answer.** Does ratio-scale commonality solve the calibration problem — does it hand the planner the interpersonal comparisons that aggregative moral reasoning needs — or does it only produce something that looks like those comparisons? In answering, address at least the following.

1. *Work out what the agreement delivers.* Does agreement on all ratios of differences force the different judges' extended scales to be positive affine transforms of one another? If so, is what remains a single global indeterminacy or one indeterminacy per person? Show the reasoning rather than asserting the result, and say exactly which aggregation rules each answer would license.

2. *Constitution or simulation.* Suppose the agreement is real. Is it best described as the discovery of an interpersonal welfare scale, or merely as a striking psychological fact — that creatures with our shared psychology respond alike when placed, from the inside, in one another's conditions? Notice that the first description makes welfare a property of situations, while the ordinary notion makes it a property of a person-in-a-situation; say whether you are willing to revise the notion that far, and why. Then consider the dissenting judge: one subject whose extended scale agrees with everyone else's on easy cases but not on hard ones. Does her existence show that the agreement among the rest was never interpersonal comparability but shared psychology? Does your answer change if her psychology is merely unusual rather than defective?

3. *The zero.* By hypothesis, ratio-scale commonality fixes ratios and not an origin. Assess whether a common zero is available anyway. Two candidates: the disagreement or status quo point, which each person can locate intrapersonally; and the neutral level, the level at which a life is exactly worth living, which each person might also locate intrapersonally. Does locating either one person by person yield an interpersonally meaningful zero, or does it require a further concordance that ratio-scale commonality does not entail — for instance, that everyone's neutral level is the same situation? Say what the absence of a common zero costs, and to which choices.

4. *Rank the rules.* Given your answers, rank these aggregation rules by what the collar's output actually supports: the sum rule; the rule that maximizes, over feasible policies, the product of everyone's gain above a specified disagreement point; the maximin rule, which maximizes the position of the worst off; and the leximin rule, which maximizes the position of the worst off, then the next worst off, and so on. Note that the sum rule is sensitive to rescaling one person's utility function alone, while the product-of-gains rule is not, since rescaling each person separately multiplies the product by a positive constant. Then assess the objection that the product rule's equal-treatment condition is itself a comparability assumption in disguise, and take a position on it.

5. *Two pressure points.* (i) Full agreement is a very strong assumption: state the weakest degree of inter-judge agreement your verdict actually requires, and say what collapses if agreement holds only for orderings and not for ratios, or only within families of similar situations. (ii) Policies also change who exists. Does anything in the setup help with choices that alter the size or identity of the population, given that a common zero does the heaviest work there and that the collar cannot be used on people who never exist? If your verdict does not extend to that case, say so plainly.

Finally, flag the assumption you have borrowed that does the most load-bearing work — the additivity of episode-welfare into life-welfare is one candidate, the honesty and stability of reports another, the completeness of agreement a third — and say what would happen to your verdict if that assumption failed.
