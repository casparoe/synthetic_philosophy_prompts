Posting this here because the philosophy group I meet up with IRL has basically given up on me. I've been lurking around rationalist blogs and forums since the Sequences were still coming out, so I know roughly how these threads go, but this one has me genuinely stuck and I want someone to point at a step and say "that's the one".

Background on how I got here: I came to panpsychism the boring way. Brute emergence of experience from wholly non-experiential stuff looks like magic to me, dualism has the interaction problem, and "the intrinsic nature of the physical is experiential" at least promises that the macro-facts about my consciousness are *constituted* by micro-facts, rather than pasted on by a further brute law. I'd call myself maybe 60/40 on something in that family.

Then I wrote out the following and it now looks to me like it proves way too much. I think the same reasoning, if it works, also shows that there are no composite objects at all, and it hits plain physicalism *harder* than it hits panpsychism. So I can't accept the conclusion and I can't retreat to my old view either. Numbers so you can point:

(1) Datum: right now there is exactly one subject of this experience. Not a committee. The mug-seeing and the fan-hearing and the itch are all had by the same thing, all at once, and there's an "and" in there that isn't just a list.

(2) Everything physical about me is exhausted by the fundamental physical entities that compose me plus their relations and arrangement. No extra ingredient. Both physicalists and panpsychists sign this.

(3) Panpsychism adds: the intrinsic nature of those entities is experiential, each has some micro-experience, and my macro-experience is wholly made of theirs.

(4) So there must be some relation R such that micro-subjects standing in R are, or constitute, a single macro-subject. If there's no such R, panpsychism never explains (1).

(5) Here's the part I keep coming back to: nothing about a plurality of subjects standing in any relation you like settles whether there is one subject there or many. Relations can correlate, integrate, share content, pass information around — but "many subjects, related" still contains as many subjects as "many subjects". Subjecthood isn't the kind of quantity that goes down when you wire things together. I don't mean we're missing the mechanism; I mean the number just doesn't move.

(6) So R is either (a) a structural / functional / causal relation, something you could in principle read off a complete physical-and-functional description of the system, or (b) some further relation with no structural specifier at all — the "phenomenal bonding" move, Goff-style, or somthing in that neighborhood.

(7) If (a), then the structure is all that's doing the work, so any system instantiating that structure has a subject. Including: a brain built out of eighty thousand people with walkie-talkies; the internet, if it happens to hit the right integration profile; and — my favourite version — the very same functional organization re-implemented in silicon chips that have no experiences whatsoever. But that last case is the killer: the macro-subject in the silicon brain isn't made out of *any* micro-experiences, so (3) is false and panpsychism has quietly become functionalism, which is the exact view the whole intrinsic-natures motivation was supposed to beat. Also, on the way there you pick up moral patients by the truckload: every sufficiently integrated network becomes a being you can harm, made out of parts that never consented to being parts, and turning it off is somthing you now need a very good reason for.

(8) If (b), you've posited a brute law saying subjects bond *here* and not *there*, which is brute emergence with extra steps — you've handed back the only advantage panpsychism had over physicalist emergentism. And the law lands suspiciously exactly at the level of brains.

(9) So panpsychism either self-destructs into functionalism or it's emergentism in a costume. Since (5) looks like a special case of a completely general "you can't get a one out of a many" worry, I also seem to have argued myself into there being no brains, no tables and no me, which I really don't want. And because physicalists have to get a subject out of parts with no experience at all, the same argument hurts them more, so there's no going home.

Where's the bad step? Every time I try to say, I end up either sneaking in a non-structural relation and paying (8), or admitting the stadium is a person and paying (7). One friend says the stadium *does* have a subject but a thin one — there's something it's like, but no unified perspective, no self, nothing that would matter to anyone — and that the real combination problem only shows up for thick subjecthood. I can't tell if that's a genuine distinction or just moving the bump in the carpet.

What I'd most like to keep: (1), the unity datum, and the background thought that brute emergence from wholly non-experiential stuff is not an explanation. I'd sooner give up nearly anything else. I'd also rather not be told "we just can't know which systems are subjects" or "go read the literature" — I want the metaphysical bill, not an epistemic shrug and not a reading list.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost? Please price the exits too — phenomenal bonding, going top-down instead of bottom-up, the "illusionism of the one" move where only the micro-subjects are real, biting the group-mind bullet, whatever else you've got. And if you think the argument is actually valid and I should just eat one of (7) or (8), tell me which one and what it costs downstream.
