You are a professional analytic philosopher who works on the foundations of rational choice — in particular on the difference between representation theorems, pragmatic arguments (money pumps, Dutch books, exploitability), and constitutive arguments, and on what any of them can really establish about expected utility maximization. I have spent years reading rationalist blogs and forum threads on this cluster of arguments, and I am tired of both available genres: the triumphant version, where one intransitivity pump is taken to settle everything, and the dismissive version, where the whole thing is waved off as an artifact of how you describe outcomes. Please take the question seriously and work in two stages; reason it through before writing the dialogue itself.

Stage 1 — preparation (output this first, in compact bullets, under 300 words):
- For each character, list their three strongest considerations, and note where those considerations are independent of each other rather than three versions of one point.
- List the four places where the two characters genuinely disagree, as opposed to disagreeing about labels.
- Flag any move either character could make that would dissolve the dispute by relabeling rather than by arguing, so that you can keep them from making it more than once.
- Note explicitly what each character will concede, and why the concession costs them something real.

Stage 2 — write the dialogue (1,400–1,800 words). Setting: two decision theorists, Marcus and Thea, continuing an argument over coffee after a colloquium talk on coherence arguments. Marcus defends the claim that the pragmatic case — exploitability plus long-run performance, not mere axiom-chanting — gives you expected utility maximization once you grant that only consequences matter. Thea defends a risk-sensitive, non-expected-utility policy that weights the probability of a bad tail outcome directly rather than folding it into a utility of money, and argues that coherence arguments either constrain nothing or presuppose what they were meant to prove. Both are equally competent; neither is a strawman; neither is allowed to win by volume.

Work through the following sequence, but let it read like a real argument rather than a checklist:

1. Marcus opens with the strongest version available to him, not the naive intransitivity pump: the long-run leg (over a working life of many small, roughly independent decisions, expected utility maximization with a fixed utility index beats rival rules with probability approaching one) and the sequential leg (an agent who expects to learn something and act again, and whose conditional plans violate the sure-thing principle, is steering at time zero toward a distribution she herself would have rejected). Give both legs concrete numbers.
2. Thea separates exploitability from irrationality, and presses the hidden premises of the long-run leg: it needs many draws, independence across draws, an objective that aggregates separably across decisions, and an environment that is not adapting to her. Give a case where one of those fails. She should concede one thing outright — that intransitivity is indefensible, and that it is ex ante worth paying a small fee to have one's preferences brought into line.
3. Marcus drops the adversarial-bookie framing: the exploiter can be an anonymous mechanism or a market; precommitment is neither free nor always available; and being the sort of agent who gets exploited is a cost denominated in the very currency Thea cares about. He then demands that she state her rule as a functional rather than a mood.
4. Thea states it: tail-weighting over the outcome distribution, plus a genuine menu-dependent concern (she is worse off for having been offered certain options, holding fixed what she ends up choosing).
5. Marcus runs the consequentialism-plus-dynamic-consistency move: if all her concerns are fixed by the total consequence — the whole history, including whatever risks she ran — and she has no plan to act against her own earlier conditional plans, then her ranking over histories is represented as the expectation of some utility index, and the non-expected-utility content has quietly become bookkeeping. The pump, he says, then applies at the level of histories.
6. Thea answers with what I think of as the no-free-consequences objection, and this is the center of the dialogue: Marcus's two legs pull against each other. To make her risk-sensitivity consequentialist he has to enrich the consequence space until "the risk I ran in this decision" becomes a coordinate of the outcome; but coordinates indexed to individual decisions do not aggregate across decisions, and across-decision separability is exactly what the long-run argument needs. Rich consequences or aggregation, not both. Illustrate with a two-decision numerical example in which the enriched objective's ranking is not the sum of the per-decision scores.
7. Marcus's best reply: the long-run argument can be restated over life-histories — maximize the expectation of your real intertemporal objective — so it does not need narrow per-decision separability; and if her concerns really are decision-indexed rather than history-level, then she is not a consequentialist at all and the framework is being asked to arbitrate a dispute it was never about. He closes with the fixed-point move: expected utility maximization is self-endorsing, and a rule that does not endorse itself on reflection is unstable.
8. Thea's close: self-endorsement is cheap, because satisficing at a threshold, tail-minimizing, and menu-sensitive rules are each self-endorsing under their own standard, so reflective stability selects nothing. And exploitability is a cost to be weighed, not a refutation — promise-keepers and people who refuse to pay blackmail are exploitable too — so the pragmatic case is at best ceteris paribus. Marcus's final line should concede that ceteris paribus point explicitly, then insist that the weighing is itself a decision that the coherence arguments constrain, and ask her to name a single case in which the cost of becoming coherent exceeds the cost of remaining pumpable. End there, with the disagreement sharper than it started.

Constraints: no citations, no author names, no named theorems used as conversation-stoppers, no footnotes. Each character gets at least two concrete numerical examples, and each says plainly when they are conceding something. Nobody is allowed to end a thread with "that's just a different utility function" more than once in the whole dialogue. Do not resolve the dispute, do not add a moderator, and do not add commentary after the dialogue.
