I'm a PhD student in political theory working on the normative foundations of conditionality in social assistance, and I would be grateful for an outside reading of an exchange I keep turning over. It took place at a small workshop last spring: the paper under discussion was on workfare-style conditionality, and the argument continued over dinner between two participants I'll call R (a political theorist) and S (a labour economist who has worked on benefit administration). I have reconstructed it as faithfully as I can, and I have tried not to improve either side.

The case on the table was a municipal pilot for unemployment benefit: claimants had to attend a fortnightly work-search interview, and a missed interview triggered a four-week suspension of payments unless a caseworker accepted that there was "good cause."

1. R: The case for conditionality is a reciprocity case. The benefit is financed by people who work, so someone who could work and will not is asking others to carry them. Obligations of mutual aid run in both directions, and the "undeserving poor" label, whatever its history, marks a genuine moral difference: between those who would reciprocate if they could and those who will not.

2. S: The pilot's own figures undercut that. The people actually sanctioned were not predominantly the will-not-work group. Missed interviews tracked chaotic housing, undiagnosed mental-health problems and caring duties, and the largest single sanction category was non-attendance rather than refusal of a job offer. So whatever the reciprocity principle justifies, it does not justify this regime: the population it punishes is not the population it is about.

3. R: That is an objection to the administration, not to the principle. A principle does not stop being true because it is badly applied; the remedy is better casework, not abolition.

4. S: The gap is not accidental but structural. Conditionality attaches where monitoring is cheap rather than where desert is absent. You can observe whether a claimant walked into an office; you cannot similarly observe whether the recipient of a tax relief or of a publicly financed degree is reciprocating. The targets of conditionality are selected by administrative cost. And once you see that, the retrospective claim that the sanctioned are the undeserving looks like a rationalization rather than an application of the principle.

5. R: Every enforcement system is bounded by what it can observe. The criminal law does not punish unobservable mental states, and nobody infers from that that murderers are not really blameworthy. You are deriving a conclusion about desert from a fact about evidence.

6. S: Those cases differ in a checkable way. In criminal law the observable proxy is corroborated: we have independent reason to think it tracks whatever grounds blame. Here the proxy is not merely noisy but anti-correlated with its supposed base. The groups most likely to be sanctioned — limited work capacity, caring responsibilities, unstable housing — are precisely the groups we would antecedently excuse. A proxy that moves inversely to what it is supposed to track is not evidence-constrained enforcement of a desert claim; it is a sign that the desert claim is doing no independent work.

7. R: Then fix the proxy. Require acceptance of a suitable job offer rather than attendance at N interviews. That is something within the claimant's control, and it tracks reciprocity far more closely.

8. S: Two problems. First, suitability is assessed by the caseworker, and the claimant bears the cost of the caseworker's errors. Second, control over refusing an offer is not control over what matters: an offer can be nominally suitable and still set the claimant back, by displacing a training place or a caring arrangement. The control the regime demands is control over a menu the claimant did not choose, and how many suitable offers exist at all is fixed by labour demand, which no claimant controls. Taken seriously, though, that control point cuts further than you want: what your own labour earns also depends on demand, so if desert is to govern transfers, you owe an account of why it does not also unsettle market incomes.

9. R: I can accept that. My claim was never that the sanctioned are blameworthy, only that they lack a positive claim on other people's labour — failing to be deserving is not the same as being undeserving, and a state may attach conditions to a redistributive transfer even to the merely non-deserving. I would add that the discourse is not pure artifact: the deserving/undeserving distinction shows up in poor-relief systems under very different economic arrangements, which is hard to explain if it is only an ideological reflex of one particular mode of production.

10. S: The positive-desert version still has to say what level of cooperation generates a claim, and the natural answer — enough to be a net contributor over a working life — would strip most people of a claim during any recession, which nobody accepts. So the version that survives scrutiny is the one with no specified threshold, i.e. the unfalsifiable one. And as for recurrence: the form of the distinction recurs, but what fills it does not. Which group counts as undeserving shifts with the state of the labour market and with what employers need at the time, and that variability is exactly what you would expect if the category were doing work for labour discipline rather than tracking a moral difference.

Could you adjudicate this exchange move by move — grading each of the ten moves as clearly right, partly right, or mistaken, with a brief justification in each case — and then say who is more right overall, if that question has an answer? I would also like your view on what both parties are missing. My own suspicion is that the whole exchange may be conducted in the wrong currency: if an unemployment benefit is better understood as social insurance than as poor relief, the relevant worry about conditionality would be moral hazard rather than reciprocity, and several of the moves above would look differently. But please do not let my suspicion organise your answer if you think the more important missing consideration lies elsewhere.
