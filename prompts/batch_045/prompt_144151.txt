The claim I want you to argue for: the standard rationalist story about Moloch / multipolar traps is a misdiagnosis, and the misdiagnosis is doing practical damage. In the usual telling — many agents, everyone would prefer the cooperative outcome, everyone defects anyway, so what we need is better coordination technology — the trap is modelled as a giant many-person prisoner's dilemma and the implied remedies are commitment devices, enforcement, norms against defecting, "just build the mechanism already." Argue that in the cases people actually point to (arms races, publish-or-perish, the attention economy, capabilities races between labs) this is the wrong model, because the agents in them are not tempted defectors at all: they are agents whose own marginal contribution to the bad outcome is imperceptible, both to themselves and to everyone else. Call this marginal opacity. Under marginal opacity the live consideration isn't "I gain by defecting" but "nothing I could do makes any difference," which flips both the diagnosis and the prescription: the participants are closer to paralyzed cooperators than to rational defectors, and the coordination-toolbox remedies systematically underperform or backfire, because moral exhortation and stigma land on people who were already trying not to and commitment devices have nothing to bind, the relevant act being below the threshold at which anyone can detect it. What has leverage instead is making the marginal contribution visible, aggregated, or thresholded.

Then do the honest part and steelman the one objection you think really bites. My candidate: if it isn't a dilemma, why does it feel like betrayal, and why do participants themselves reach for Moloch language to describe it? Deal with that, and with the worry that "marginal opacity" is only a redescription of the same old prisoner's dilemma with measurement noise stapled on, in which case you haven't said anything new. If you think the claim is only half right, say which half and why.
