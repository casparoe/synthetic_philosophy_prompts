I sit on the committee that sets the research agenda for a small theory group — roughly six postdocs for three years, plus a visitors' budget — funded to work on the conceptual foundations of cooperation between artificial agents. The group does formal and philosophical work only: game theory, logic, decision theory, perhaps small computational experiments; no training runs, no empirical ML. What I need from you is a defended agenda, not a map of the terrain, so please skip the reading list and the citations: where you think a question is already settled, say what the settled answer is and how that changes the ordering of everything else.

The area is the one opened by the following construction. Take a one-shot prisoner's dilemma in which each player submits, not a move, but a program; the two programs are given each other's source as input and each outputs a move. In the familiar constructions, a program cooperates just in case it can in some sense establish that the other is a cooperator of the relevant kind — a syntactic copy of itself, a program provably equivalent to it, a program it can prove cooperates against it — and mutual cooperation then shows up as an equilibrium of the program game, despite the underlying game being played once and the parties being strangers. This idea is appealed to constantly in arguments about whether advanced AI systems could be expected to cooperate with one another, and I do not trust the appeals. Some of the questions I think the group might work on:

First, the recognition problem. Cooperative programs have to decide which other programs to cooperate with, and the usual constructions are either extremely narrow (cooperate only with a copy) or extremely demanding (cooperate with anything provably cooperative, at unbounded proof cost). In a population of independently written cooperative programs, does any interesting cooperative equilibrium survive, or does the cooperative set collapse to whichever implementation happened to spread? What exactly has to be true of the epistemic relation between two agents before the construction gets off the ground, and does the answer change if that relation is syntactic inspection, proof, simulation, or reputation?

Second, a worry I cannot get a handle on and which I suspect is underdiscussed. Real agents are trained rather than written. What can be exchanged between them is weights, an API, a natural-language specification, a behavioural transcript, or an audit report. Is there any genuine analogue of reading source code for a system whose policy is an opaque function approximator, or does the whole approach rest on a transparency condition that current and foreseeable systems fail? And there is a second half to this: two programs can be mutually legible at the level of syntax while attaching different meanings to the same terms, so that each correctly predicts the other's outputs and neither gets what it took itself to be bargaining for. Call this the semantic handshake problem. Is it a real obstacle, a special case of something already understood, or a confusion?

Third, the delegation gap. The equilibrium is an equilibrium among programs, but programs are written and deployed by parties whose objectives may differ from what the program encodes, from what the deploying party is willing to avow, or from what it would want under reflection. What has to hold of the delegation relation for cooperation among programs to translate into cooperation among principals, and is that condition ever checkable from inside the program game?

Fourth, a conceptual question about what is even being claimed. Is a program equilibrium a genuinely new solution concept, or just the ordinary equilibrium concept applied to a richer space of actions? If the latter, does the construction tell us anything about what rationality requires, or only about what commitments can do for agents that can make them? Is that contrast a substantive dispute or a verbal one, and how would we tell?

Fifth, selection and brittleness. The program game has many equilibria, including thoroughly bad ones, and the famous constructions are sensitive in specific ways to small perturbations of the other program's code, to proof budgets, to time limits, and to misreadings. How should robustness here be formalised, and does the cooperative outcome survive the formalisations that matter for systems that actually exist? Is there a principled refinement that selects among program equilibria, or is the cheerful result an artifact of attending only to the good one?

Sixth, scope. What happens outside the symmetric two-player prisoner's dilemma: bargaining over divisions, games with partial conflict of interest, many-player settings, settings with more than one round but continuing opacity, settings where one side can rewrite the other's code? Where does the construction generalise, and where does it break in a way that should change someone's expectations about AI cooperation?

Seventh, the downstream question. Does any of this cash out in advice — to engineers, to regulators, to anyone deciding whether AI systems should be able to inspect one another's internals — or is it a beautiful toy? Is make commitments legible a real recommendation, and if so, legible to whom and at what cost?

Rank these, or replace them. I want to know what you would actually put six people on for three years and in what order, on what criteria — importance for the downstream question, tractability with the tools this group has, neglectedness, how many of the other questions depend on the answer — and how you weigh those criteria against each other when they conflict. Say which questions are upstream of which, which you would drop because their answer would not change what anyone does, and what result or piece of evidence over the next two years should move an item up or down. Finally, if you think the framing itself is wrong — the mandate, the three-year horizon, the restriction to non-empirical work, the idea that the group's output should be judged by whether it changes engineering or policy practice — say so, and tell me what you would put in its place.
