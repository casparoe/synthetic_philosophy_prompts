My friend Ada is finishing a PhD thesis on Kant and lying, and she's been consistant enough that I've started using her as a kind of oracle at parties. Her verdicts so far: murderer at the door asking whether your friend is hiding upstairs — you may not say he isn't, full stop, though she says you can slam the door, refuse to answer, or physically block the doorway; a dying patient who asks "am I dying?" — you may not tell her she'll recover; a bluff in a poker game and an actor's lines in a play — fine, not lies at all; a literally true but misleading answer to a mugger about where the valuables are — permitted, because nothing false was asserted; hiding your friend's coat where the murderer will find it and draw the wrong conclusion — she says that's fine too, or at least it isn't the thing she objects to.

The case I actually want is this: a hospice patient with full capacity, already told her condition is serious, says to her daughter, "I don't want the truth and I know you'd normally owe it to me. I'm releasing you. Please just tell me I'm going to be fine, and I mean that as a real waiver, not as a hint that I want reassurance." The daughter says "you're going to be fine." What does Ada say — permitted, forbidden, or something weirder?

Don't bother with what any commentator has argued; I want the shape of the reasoning, not the literature, and I can't check references right now anyway. Give me exactly six numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no conclusion: your prediction; the two or three rules that fit all five of her earlier verdicts equally well; what each of those rules predicts about the waiver case and why; your best guess with the reason you prefer it, plus which of her five verdicts is the closest analog; one further case of your own devising that would separate the surviving rules cleanly if she ruled on it; and one line on what you'd believe yourself, noting whether that's the same as your prediction about her.
