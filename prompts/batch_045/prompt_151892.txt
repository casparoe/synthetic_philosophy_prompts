I am writing a novel in which a scholar of Bertrand Russell, hired as a consultant by an AI company, has to explain to the engineers why a chatbot's confident biography of a novelist who never existed is a problem in the philosophy of language rather than an item in a bug tracker. I want her to say something Russell himself could recognizably have said, extended to a case he never faced, and I would rather she not mouth anachronistic jargon.

So: how would Russell — take the Russell of On Denoting — treat sentences that a large language model emits, that no speaker asserts or endorses, and whose definite description has no referent? Three cases, please. First, the model outputs the present King of France is bald. Second, it produces a fluent life of a wholly invented novelist, complete with titles and dates, having been asked about her. Third, it produces a description that fits two real but obscure people equally well, so that no one could say which was meant. For each: does the output express a proposition that is false, does it express nothing at all — given Russell's principle that a proposition one can entertain must be built out of items one is acquainted with — or does the 1905 machinery simply have no slot for it? Attach a rough confidence to each verdict, and say which Russell you are channeling.

Point of pressure for my scene: the engineer's reply is that a theory of denoting built for speakers with referential intentions has nothing to say about text that no speaker stands behind, so the whole apparatus stays silent. Tell me whether Russell, or a scholar speaking for him, has a real answer to that rather than a shrug. Then tell me the one argument or consideration most likely to make you reverse yourself, and where your extrapolation from Russell is weakest.
