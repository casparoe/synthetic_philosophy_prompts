I am revising the final lecture of my upper-level course on freedom and responsibility, in which I set aside the question whether free will is compatible with determinism (I leave that open all term) and ask instead the pluralist question I take from the subtitle of Dennett's *Elbow Room*: which of the varieties of free will on offer is the variety worth wanting? I want a single case I can put on the screen and run several accounts past in sequence, so that the class can see the varieties come apart in one agent and one action rather than across a syllabus. Design that case for me to the following specification.

The scenario: one agent, one action, one scene, contemporary and ordinary — a workplace, a family, a clinic, a department meeting; no one dies, and the moral weight is moderate but real (a betrayal, a refusal of help, a small sabotage). One paragraph, under 150 words. Banned furniture: no neuroscientists, implants, chips, demons, predictors, or exotic physics; no manipulation of the agent by another person; and, importantly, no counterfactual intervener of the sort standard Frankfurt-style cases employ — no backup mechanism standing by to make her act if she wavers. The case must generate its verdicts from the agent's own motivational economy, and you may not settle anything by stipulating that the universe is deterministic or indeterministic.

Required verdicts, each of which the case must earn rather than announce:

1. **Leeway / regulative control** — fails. She had no alternative possibilities, and the absence of alternatives is internal: given what she most wanted and what she took the circumstances to be, she would not have done otherwise even had she tried to stop herself. Convince a theorist who thinks agents sometimes have categorical alternatives that this agent did not.
2. **Guidance control / moderate reasons-responsiveness** (Fischer-style) — satisfied. Her mechanism was receptive to a suitable range of reasons, including moral ones, and reactive to at least some of them, and the action is one she can be held responsible for on that account. Specify the pattern of reasons you are relying on; do not just assert receptivity.
3. **Answerability** (the reactive-attitudes criterion) — satisfied. Her action expresses a quality of will toward another person such that resentment is warranted, and withholding it would read as condescension rather than charity.
4. **Hierarchical identification** (the Frankfurt-style mesh condition) — fails. What actually moved her is a motive she repudiates at the higher order; she does not want to be moved by it and would not endorse it on reflection.
5. **Diachronic addressability**, a variety of my own devising that I want to test — satisfied. I mean this: the action is one that a later stage of the same agent could intelligibly be asked to answer for, because the desire or policy it issued from is still available to her afterwards as something she can take up, explain, or disown in the first person. This is deliberately not the same as identification at the time; an agent can act from a motive she disowns and still be someone to whom a demand for an answer can later be addressed. Show that she is such a person, and say what the case would look like if she were not.

After the scenario, give me: (a) the condition each variety applies, the verdict, and the reasoning, in the order above; (b) an honest account of which single feature of the case is doing the work in producing the split between 1–2 and 4, and precisely which verdicts collapse together if that feature is removed; (c) a minimal perturbation — change one feature, not the whole story — that flips the hierarchical verdict to satisfied while leaving the other four unchanged, together with your judgment whether anything about her responsibility or the fittingness of blame moves with it, and, if no clean perturbation exists, which verdict blocks it and why; (d) the strongest objection available to the defender of whichever variety comes out worst in your case (I expect that to be the identification theorist, who will want to say your case shows she was not free rather than that resentment was warranted), plus a two-sentence reply I can use; and (e) one sentence on what the class should conclude about the phrase "worth wanting" — whether it is interest-relative, and if so, relative to which practices.

What counts as cheating, given the use I will make of the case: no verdict may be imported by a phrase like "she really couldn't help it" or "of course she is to blame"; no appeal to a mechanism whose inner workings you decline to describe; and no resolving the disagreement by making the disagreement about determinism after all. The point I am after is that the varieties answer different questions, so the case has to make them disagree inside one life.
