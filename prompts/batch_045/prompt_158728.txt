Two research traditions have spent years arguing past each other about whether the Neolithic transition — the shift from foraging to the cultivation and storage of staple crops — improved or degraded the lives of the human beings who lived through it. Call them, purely as labels, the primitivist camp (foraging was the affluent, leisure-rich, egalitarian baseline; the transition was a trap that traded freedom and health for grain, population density, and rulers) and the productivist camp (the transition was a forced or rational response to pressure, it was not chosen as a package by anyone, and whatever its costs it is the precondition of everything that makes human life more than precarious). Assume they have agreed to an adversarial collaboration and have produced the document below. Write it.

Deliverable, in three parts.

Part 1 — Agreed claims, numbered. Include the shared factual background both can sign (the transition was gradual, regionally staggered, and in places reversed or partial; it occurred independently in several regions; the ethnographic record of foragers is drawn disproportionately from marginal environments; almost everyone alive now lives under agricultural states). Include the shared conceptual claims both should be able to sign: for instance, that "mistake" requires both an agent and a counterfactual, that the comparison must be fixed to some population level to be well defined, and that no single welfare metric is obviously correct. Each claim must be substantive enough that one could imagine a reasonable outsider denying it — no "the truth is probably in the middle" filler.

Part 2 — A disagreements table. Columns: the crux; the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; the primitivist formulation, in wording a primitivist would accept; the productivist formulation, in wording a productivist would accept; the evidence, case, or argument both accept as bearing on that crux; and each side's prediction about it, written down in advance. Cruxes I expect to see addressed, plus any you think are more central:

1. The currency of efficiency. Foraging earns high returns per hour of work and low returns per unit of land; agriculture is the reverse. Which currency is the right one depends on whether land or labor is the binding constraint, and that in turn depends on population density — which is itself an output of the subsistence regime. Say what each side thinks the correct counterfactual density is.
2. The definition of affluence: satisfaction of few wants, objective bodily welfare, leisure, security against famine, or autonomy. Do these come apart here, and if so which one is supposed to be doing the work?
3. Whether there was a decision at all, and hence whether "mistake" is even the right predicate on the transition. If you think this part of the dispute is verbal, say so and say why.
4. The ratchet. Once a population sits at agricultural carrying capacity, reversion implies mass mortality, so "was it a mistake to adopt" and "would it be a mistake to abandon" can come apart. Is the ratchet evidence of a trap, or merely path dependence of a sort any large change produces?
5. Exit cost as a welfare component: how costly it is for a household to leave — a band versus fields, granaries, and a tax claim. Does autonomy belong inside the welfare comparison or on a separate political axis?
6. The identity-dependence of the complaint. If the transition changed which individuals ever existed, then the people who might have been made worse off by it may have no bearers, and the primitivist grievance may be unstatable in person-affecting terms. Both sides should say whether this dissolves the complaint, transforms it into a claim about the value of states of affairs, or leaves it untouched.
7. A concrete empirical crux both accept: for instance, what regionally and contemporaneously matched skeletal evidence from the first generations of cultivators and from neighboring foragers would show, or how common genuine abandonment of agriculture should be if each side is right. Record the competing predictions.

Part 3 — A short section sorting the residual disagreement into verbal disputes, disputes about which welfare currency matters (value disagreement), and genuinely empirical disputes, flagging any crux for which no shared test can be constructed and explaining why.

Constraints. Steelman each camp; no side gets a straw version. No citations, bibliographies, or appeals to what some named authority said. Define any term you coin. Keep the document roughly nine hundred to thirteen hundred words, and prefer precision over breadth: a shorter table whose cells are genuinely load-bearing beats a longer one padded with hedges.
