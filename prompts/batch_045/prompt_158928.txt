I'm a PhD student TAing for our upper-level normative ethics seminar, and I'm marking the midterm answers to the question below. I'd like a second opinion before I commit to a grade, since one answer reads very smoothly and I don't want to be carried along by it.

The question (20 marks, 500-word limit):

The doctrine of double effect (DDE) is often said to explain why it may be permissible to divert a runaway trolley onto a track where it will kill one workman rather than five, but impermissible to push a heavy stranger off a footbridge into its path so that his body stops it. Consider the following pair of cases.

Case A (Sandbags): A runaway trolley will kill five workmen unless you divert it onto a spur. The spur ends in a pile of sandbags, which on their own are sufficient to stop the trolley before it reaches the five. A single workman is standing directly in front of the sandbags; he will certainly be killed, but his body contributes nothing to stopping the trolley.

Case B (Sandbags Plus): Everything is as in Case A, except that the sandbags on their own are not quite sufficient: the trolley will stop short of the five only if the collision with the workman's body also slows it. There is no other way to save the five.

(a) Set out the conditions DDE imposes, distinguishing the good effect from the merely foreseen bad effect, and explaining what it is for a bad effect to be a means rather than a side-effect. (6 marks)
(b) Apply the doctrine to both cases. If your verdicts differ, identify exactly which condition does the work. (8 marks)
(c) How much work, if any, is done by the bare comparison of numbers — five lives saved against one lost — in the doctrine's verdicts on these cases and on the footbridge case? (6 marks)

Student's answer:

DDE holds that an action with both a good and a bad effect is permissible only if (i) the action itself, apart from its effects, is not wrong; (ii) the agent intends the good effect and merely foresees the bad one; (iii) the bad effect is not the means by which the good effect is produced, but a side-effect of producing it; and (iv) there is a proportionate reason for acting. The doctrine is designed to capture exactly the contrast we care about: in the switch case the one death is foreseen but not aimed at, whereas on the footbridge the man's body is what stops the trolley, so his death is the means of saving the five and the act is excluded.

Case A is straightforwardly permissible. The good effect, five lives saved, is produced by diverting the trolley into the sandbags; the workman's death plays no part in producing it, so it is a foreseen side-effect and conditions (ii) and (iii) are met. Condition (iv) is met too, since the comparison of numbers is straightforward: five lives outweigh one, and that favourable balance is what makes the reason proportionate — it is the same comparison defenders of the doctrine rely on in the original switch case.

Case B is impermissible. Here the five survive only because the workman's body helps to slow the trolley: the good outcome causally depends on his death, and so his death is the means to it. Structurally this is the footbridge again, and DDE rules it out for the same reason, namely that the man is being made to serve as a brake.

I should say why I reject the alternative reading of condition (iii), the agential one, on which a bad effect counts as a means only if the agent's plan or practical reasoning builds it in as a step towards the good effect. That reading should be rejected because on it whether the death is a means depends on what the agent happens to be thinking or saying to herself as she acts, which is a private psychological matter and cannot affect whether the action is right. The causal reading is therefore the only reading with moral content, and it yields the correct result in both cases.
