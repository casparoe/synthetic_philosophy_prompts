I've got no academic background in any of this — I'm just a dad who got dragged into a fight over our district's magnet lottery a few years back and then couldn't stop reading — and there's one argument I keep having that has completely stopped moving. I don't want to know who's right, I want to know why it stopped moving.

The argument is parents choosing schools: charters, vouchers, magnets, homeschooling, all usually lumped together. Here's what each side actually sounds like to me now, after a lot of reading. The pro-choice people say: parents should be able to get a kid out of a school that isn't working for her; the school you're assigned is just a function of your zip code, which is itself a lottery nobody agreed to; the district had decades and plenty of money and didn't fix anything; and a parent who can walk is a parent the system suddenly has to listen to. The anti-choice people say: choice lets the most organized and best-resourced parents sort themselves away from everyone else, takes out the kids who were least costly to teach along with the funding attached to them, and leaves behind a building with fewer of the parents who know how to work a bureaucracy — so even if every individual chooser ends up better off, the arrangement as a whole gets worse, and nobody is left whose actual job is to say no.

What puzzles me is that the empirical stuff seems to have gotten used up. When the big score comparisons came back basically flat, instead of anybody changing their mind, both sides slid sideways into moral arguments that don't seem to make contact with each other at all. So my hypothesis, which I'm holding loosely: the two sides are measuring different objects. One is asking whether this particular kid is better off than she would have been, with her actual assigned school as the comparison. The other is asking what kind of system you get when choice is the rule, where the comparison is a whole arrangement and not a kid. And those could both have a true answer at the same time, which would explain why nobody ever updates.

My second hypothesis is that it isn't about evidence at all but about two unarguable starting points on what the state's stake in a child even is: whether it's in outcomes you can measure (scores, safety, whether she ends up employable and not isolated) or in the process of being formed in a room full of people and ideas neither she nor her parents picked. If it's that, then no study design is ever going to matter.

There's also a homeschooling version that might be a separate diagnosis: the comparison group is definatly broken, because the families who deregister are exactly the ones who couldn't make school work, so any outcome study is comparing self-selected people against everyone. I suspect that makes that question not under-researched but unfalsifiable in principle, which if true is a different kind of stalled than either of my other two guesses.

So: which is it? Are people answering two different questions under one name, is it a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work (something like a positional-goods or sorting effect that makes individual-level and system-level answers come apart), or is it bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a study design that hasn't been tried, a change of question — or should I expect no progress, and why? Please don't give me a survey of the literature or a both-sides-have-a-point summary, and don't bother with citations or reading suggestions; I've read the standard books and I can look things up myself.
