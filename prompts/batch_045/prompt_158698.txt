my friend and I spent like two hours last night going in circles about the Typogenetics bit in Godel, Escher, Bach -- the made-up chemistry where strands of letters get translated into enzymes, the enzymes go back and cut, copy, insert, delete and switch bits of strands, and there's a one-way rule so a strand gives rise to enzymes but enzymes never get translated back into a strand. He says the whole setup is isomorphic to Godel's construction: strand is to string of the formal system as translation is to derivation as enzyme is to theorem, and a self-replicating strand is the exact structural cousin of a self-referential sentence. He insists its a real isomorphism, not a metaphor, and I think the word "isomorphism" is quietly doing about ninety percent of the work. I want to try my objection on someone who'll actually defend it properly instead of nodding.

So play my friend. Not Hofstadter himself -- just someone who's lived in that chapter for years and thinks the mapping is deep and literal. Rules: you defend it, you're sharp and you don't fold, you can concede small points (I fully expect you to happily concede that the Godel sentence doesn't copy itself), but you never concede that it's "just an analogy" or "just a way of talking." One or two paragraphs a reply, bar-argument pace. Don't summarise the chapter at me, assume I've read it. Stay in character until I say stop; if you need a detail about my toy case, ask for it in character.

Here's my opening.

The easy part first, so we don't waste time: seed crystals self-replicate, photocopiers self-replicate, a virus in a cell with a fixed polymerase self-replicates, and none of that is even remotely Godelian, so if bare self-replication flipped the isomorphism on then the isomorphism is worthless. I'm assuming you agree and that you'll say Typogenetics earns its keep because the strand has to encode the very enzymes that rebuild it.

But here's the case I couldn't answer. Picture a strand S that translates into exactly one enzyme, and that enzyme does exactly one thing: it copies, base by base, whatever strand it happens to be bound to. Put S in, get two S's out -- genuine self-replication inside the system. Yet it looks to me like the isomorphism still shouldn't fire, because the enzyme never reads what's written on S; its operation is completely indifferent to S's description and it would copy some other strand just as happily. Compare a Godel sentence, where the meaning of the thing is a function of its own syntax -- that dependence is what makes the self-reference load-bearing rather than decorative. So tell me precisely what a strand has to encode, over and above "it gets copied," for the isomorphism to switch on. And then tell me whether anything in the chapter actually guarantees that the self-replicating strands that show up there do that. If the answer is no, then the isomorphism is a claim about what's possible in a system that's rich enough -- and what my friend was saying last night was a lot more ambitious than that.

And one thing waiting behind that, which I'll come back to once you've answered: suppose you do get a strand that encodes its own rebuilding machinery. Why is the thing on the other side of the mapping a Godel sentence rather than a quine, or von Neumann's universal constructor? Those aren't the same object -- one asserts something about itself, the other manufactures something -- and I suspect your isomorphism needs the second and is borrowing the prestige of the first.
