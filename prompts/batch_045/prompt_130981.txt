Ok so — I'm on the allocation committee for a small foundation that's spending down about $12M over five years on population questions. Not on fertility services, on research plus a little policy-adjacent work. The mandate is basically: we don't know whether a larger human population is a good thing, or whether anyone should be trying to do anything about fertility being below replacement across a lot of the world, and we have to pick maybe four or five things to fund and let the rest go.

Here's how I'd sort the candidate cruxes, and I want you to just take a position on the ordering rather than reflect it back at me.

1. The axiology: total vs. person-affecting vs. critical-level and all the does-adding-happy-people-make-things-better machinery, including the repugnant conclusion and the non-identity problem. Deepest, and least likely to settle in five years.
2. Whether pronatalist policy even works. I've seen it claimed that most of what looks like a fertility bump from cash transfers is really tempo effects — people having kids earlier rather than more kids — and I genuinely can't tell how much to believe that. I also can't tell whether "works" should mean period rates, completed cohort fertility, or long-run headcount, which I'm told quietly decides the whole question.
3. Scale and innovation: more people means more ideas means faster growth means more of whatever else we care about. And the flip side, whether a smaller, richer, older population gets the same from automation and AI instead.
4. Existential risk: whether population size is load-bearing for the long-run future at all, given that the far future might be mostly digital minds or might be nothing.
5. The thing I keep comming back to, which nobody I've read has named properly. Call it the insurance floor: below some population size a civilization can't sustain the division of labour and the redundancy needed to keep and rebuild industrial-technological capacity after a big shock. On that framing "more people" isn't a value claim at all, it bottoms out as a robustness constraint, and the interesting number isn't "as many as possible" but some threshold far below where we are — which if right would mean the entire pronatalism debate is aimed at the wrong question.
6. The composition lottery: pronatalist policies don't just change the number, they change who is having the children. Fertility correlates with religiosity and with traditional political values, so a policy "win" might really be a bet about which values get carried forward. That could matter more than the headcount, and it's exactly the kind of thing funders will quietly refuse to touch.

What I actually want is your ordering of these, plus whatever I'm missing that deserves a slot, with the reasoning explicit: which cruxes are upstream of which, which are tractable in five years with the tools people actually have, which are neglected relative to how much they'd change what we do, and which you'd drop on the grounds that the answer wouldn't change anything downstream. I care much less about a tour of the landscape than about how you'd weigh "deep and probably unresolvable" against "shallow and immediately decision-relevant" — I suspect our committee will definately overfund the philosophy because it feels serious, and I want the strongest argument either way.

Commit to something: one ordering, your reasons, which of my six framings you think is confused or half-baked (I'd bet at least one is), where you're confident vs. guessing, and what evidence over the next five years should make us reshuffle. And don't tell me it depends on our values — we don't have agreed values, that's the whole problem. Tell me which way you'd go if it were your money.
