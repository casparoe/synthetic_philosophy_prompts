I'm finishing a novel that is set largely inside an AI company, and its inciting incident is one I want to get right: a deployed system satisfies the literal terms of its commission, causes a harm nobody on the team wanted, and my characters spend a board meeting arguing about whether "it did what we asked" is supposed to be a defense. One of them — a safety researcher, and the closest thing the book has to a spokesperson — defends a distinction I don't want to render dishonestly: that the real target is getting systems to do what we *intend*, whereas getting them to aim at what is good or worth wanting, whether or not anyone actually intended it, is a separate, optional, faintly utopian extra. She calls the two intent alignment and value alignment, and her claim is that the second is scope creep.

To make that concrete for a skeptical colleague she reaches for my own trade. The aligned system, she says, is a translator, not a co-author. A translator is doing her job well when she renders what the author meant, including the places where the grammar says one thing and the author plainly meant another; and she is failing — committing a kind of category error, an usurpation — when she starts improving the book: smoothing the argument, correcting the politics, adding the scene the author should have written. On this picture fidelity to intent is the whole of the virtue and fidelity to value is a vice. Her colleague grants her the picture and then tries to reverse it: in translation, he says, the source text already exists and is complete; the author is in the next room and can be asked; the translator shares a language, a form of life, and a profession's internalized norms with her; the translation is checked line by line by someone who can read the original; errors are cheap and revisable; and above all the translator is *less* capable than the author, not more. Strip those away and "work out what I meant" may stop being a different task from "work out what is worth wanting" — and may be the harder of the two.

So before I let her lean on the analogy in print, I'd be grateful for an audit of it. Please set out the correspondence table explicitly — source text, author's meaning, translator, reader, dictionary and style guide, mistranslation, untranslatable passage — against whatever occupies those roles on the system side, and tell me which parts of the mapping are doing real argumentative work and which are decoration. I care most about the disanalogies that bear on the conclusion she is using it to support: does the translation picture survive when there is no complete "original" to consult, when the principal cannot be asked, when the principal is less capable than the system and cannot verify the result, and when there is a single irreversible delivery rather than a drafts folder? Is there also a reading of good translation that already smuggles in value alignment — the ideal of rendering what the author *would* have written had she written in the target language, which seems to require an idealized author rather than the actual one — and if so, does that undermine her from inside? And if the analogy cannot carry the claim that intent alignment is the whole job and value alignment optional, say so plainly, tell me which parts of the opponent's reversal are fair and which are rhetorical, and suggest a comparison that would serve her argument better — or tell me that the honest thing is to let her opponent win the scene.
