hey — you're a political philosopher who's spent way too long in the equality-of-what debates and who, weirdly, also did a couple of years of practical mechanism design for public agencies. Before you write anything polished, do some messy brainstorming and take your time: I want you to actually think about what belongs on this list, not recite the standard four views with their standard objections. Where you think a requirement is genuinely non-negotiable, say so flatly; where you're unsure, say why.

Context, because adequacy is relative to a job. I'm on a committee that has to build an actual allocation procedure. We have a fixed, too-small supply of a one-shot intervention — call it a boost — with four features that matter: you get it at most once, its effects are permanent, it cannot be sold, given away, banked or transferred, and what it does for you depends heavily on your body and your circumstances. There's no secondary market that could reallocate it afterwards and no way to undo a decision. We need two things: a currency (one measurable quantity in which we compare how well off or how advantaged each applicant is) and a rule for what to do with those comparisons (equalize, prioritize the worst off, get everyone over some floor, whatever). I'm asking only about the currency.

Before I let anyone on the committee propose a metric, I want the spec. What does a candidate currency of egalitarian comparison have to be able to do to count as adequate for this job? For each requirement give me the case or consideration behind it — the case, not just the label. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable for us or as a preference that really only belongs to one family of views. And flag any subset of the requirements that you think can't all be satisfied together: if the list is jointly unsatisfiable, then the interesting result is which requirement we should drop, so tell me which pair or triple is doing the excluding and what each one costs to give up.

Cases I already know the currency has to handle, including a couple I invented because our intake really does produce things like them:

- Same bundle, different bodies: two applicants with identical income, insurance and housing, one of whom needs dramatically more of all of it to reach the same level of mobility, or of being able to work at all. Does the currency track what people have or what they can do with what they have, and does it have any answer when the conversion rates differ for reasons nobody chose?
- Expensive tastes on purpose: someone who, after our rules were published, deliberately trained themselves to need something rare and costly so as to count as worse off.
- Sour grapes: someone whose reported satisfaction is very high, but only because their expectations collapsed to fit a deprived life twenty years ago.
- The coupled case (mine, but we've had two): two applicants whose physiologies are linked such that a boost given to either one partly improves the other, and neither can be treated without affecting the other. What is the unit the currency is measured over, and does the currency have anything to say when the units aren't cleanly separable?
- The time-unit problem: a 22-year-old and an 82-year-old; a patient who is fine now and will be badly off in five years; someone who was badly off their whole short life and died at 30. Is the currency summed over a whole life, averaged per period, evaluated at a moment, or discounted somehow? Does a short bad life count as a smaller inequality than a long one?
- The unresponsive applicant: someone in a permanent coma, no advance directive, no proxy, and we still have to assign them a number.
- The not-yet-there applicant: a pregnant person, where part of the benefit would land on someone who doesn't exist yet, and a budget we're setting now for people born in 2060.
- Risk: we must rank people before we know who actually responds well to the boost, and later we find out. Does the currency want to be ex ante or ex post, and what breaks if it has to be both?

Constraints that are mine rather than philosophical, and that I want you to treat as candidate requirements rather than as mere practicalities you can wave away: it has to be computable from information our intake can actually get (no invasive health audits, no demanding that people disclose things that would humiliate them, no home visits for 40,000 applicants); it has to survive applicants strategically shaping what we can observe; and it has to be stable enough that two different intake officers give the same applicant roughly the same number.

Things I want argued rather than assumed: whether sensitivity to brute luck or to responsibility is a genuine requirement or a sectarian taste of one camp; whether any no-envy condition belongs on the list at all; whether the currency has to be the same one we'd use if supply were unlimited — i.e. whether equality is the whole story here or just triage on top of something else; whether the metric has to avoid ranking whole lives as better or worse in a way that demeans the people being ranked; and whether administrability is a real desideratum or just a grubby concession to the fact that we're a committee with a deadline.

At the very end, and briefly — one short paragraph or a compact table, no bibliography and no citations, I know the names and I don't want a reading list — score the big families of views against your own list: resources or primary goods, welfare or opportunity for welfare, capabilities and functionings, access-to-advantage and other luck-adjusted views, the relational or no-one-gets-to-dominate view, and plain sufficientarianism. Then tell me which single requirement on your list does the most work in knocking candidates out. Keep at least 80 percent of the answer on the criteria themselves and their motivating cases; the scorecard is an afterthought.
