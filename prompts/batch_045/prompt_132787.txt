I'm the student rep on my university's animal ethics committee, which mostly means I check that forms are signed, and I've started noticing how weird the boundary of our jurisdiction is. If someone wants to work on mice, or zebrafish, or even a single octopus, there's a protocol, a harm-benefit analysis, the whole replacement-reduction-refinement song and dance; if someone wants to gas fifty thousand fruit flies or boil a beetle, there's literally no paperwork, and when I asked why, the chair said something like "well, they're invertebrates, we don't do ethics for insects" in the same tone you'd say we don't do ethics for pipettes. The same shape is everywhere once you look: an exterminator will sell you a "humane" mouse trap and then spray a wasp nest without blinking, insect protein gets marketed as the ethical alternative to beef partly on the assumption that the insects don't count at all, and my colleague who signed the thing about boiling lobsters alive told me that yes, trillions of insects are killed in agriculture and insect farming every year, but they almost certainly don't feel anything so who cares. The bit that won't let me go is that an octopus is about as far from us on the tree of life as a honeybee is, and honeybees do some pretty fancy learning, so it can't really be similarity-to-us or intelligence doing the work — and then there's the number thing: my committee would treat a protocol that killed fifty thousand mice as vastly worse than one that killed one mouse, but a protocol that kills fifty thousand ants isn't even a form, so somewhere the numbers have stopped mattering.

A friend made a point that I think is doing more work than it looks like: the uncertainty about insects is one big question, not billions of little ones. Either bees are the kind of thing that has experiences or they aren't — it's not twenty percent of each individual bee that's conscious — so the uncertainty is correlated across all of them and doesn't wash out across the enormous headcount the way independent risks would. Which means the tempting expected-suffering calculation, roughly number-of-animals times probability-of-sentience times welfare-range times intensity times duration, doesn't behave the way people assume: the huge N doesn't make the P go away, and the P doesn't shrink just because there are a lot of animals. So what I actually want is the implicit theory behind the practice of treating insects as outside the moral frame entirely. Does it presuppose a binary, threshold view of moral status with the burden of proof on whoever claims sentience? Does it presuppose that aggregating a small probability over an astronomical number is illegitimate — a ban on the Pascalian move — or is it just that people quietly treat any probability below some vague cutoff as zero? Does it presuppose that sentience is a taxon-level natural kind, so that the answer for "insects" is one fact and not billions? And is it secretly commitment to something about institutional tractability rather than anything about the animals?

Please reconstruct the strongest version of that implicit theory for me, and be sharp about which parts the practice strictly needs and which parts it merely sits well with. I'd also like: where the practice contradicts itself or is at least strained (I've given you the octopus and the fifty-thousand-ants cases, but use better ones if you've got them); where it's philosophically underdetermined, i.e. which genuinely different theories would all license the same hands-off treatment; and then the replacement test — if we swapped the implicit theory for the expected-value-plus-precaution view, what would my committee concretely have to start doing, and where does that view bite back or turn absurd? Don't give me citations or a reading list, I won't follow them up; just the argument. Sections in this order, please: "The implicit theory" (~350 words, in its strongest form, not a strawman), "What it strictly needs vs. what it merely fits" (~250), "Where the practice pulls apart" (~250), "Underdetermination" (~250, other theories that would license the same thing), "The replacement test" (~250, including what would get worse).
