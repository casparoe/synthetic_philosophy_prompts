I run a once-a-month meetup, just a bunch of us in a library meeting room — usually about 14 people, 90 minutes, a mix of a software person, a nurse, a couple of students, a guy who works in insurance. We're not academics and most of them won't do written arithmetic, so keep any numbers to whole percents or coin counts. We also can't use real money (library rules), but we can use chips and a stupid prize.

We've been chewing on the thing I've seen called the no-trade theorem: that two people who start out agreeing about the world can't profitably trade with each other just because they know different things, and that the moment it's common knowledge that both of you want to take the other side, you should both back out — because the fact that he said yes is itself evidence against you. Bit like the old joke about not wanting to belong to any club that would have you as a member.

Design me an activity for that group where they run into this themselves. I've tried building it around a jar of marbles with private draws and it always goes one of two ways: either it turns into an arithmetic lesson, or everyone just laughs and trades at random, and the point never lands.

What I want them to feel, in order:

1. First a phase where trading seems obviously fine. Everyone has some private scrap of information, prices move, people feel smart, some of them make chips.
2. Then a flip. Somehow make acceptance common knowledge rather than private — I dont know whether that's a public round of yes/no, or both hands up, or offers posted on the board before anyone commits — and have the trade dry up, and have them feel in their gut why, before I name it.
3. Then my own hobby horse, which I half believe and want tested rather than flattered. I think the theorem only bites when the thing being traded is a bet whose payoff both sides can already write down over a shared list of possible outcomes. Most real trading, I suspect, is on stuff like whether some technology turns out transformative, or whether a company's culture is broken, where nobody can even list the outcomes — so there isn't a prior to share in the first place. I seperate three kinds of disagreement: different evidence, different priors, and different ways of carving up the world (frame disagreement, I call it). I'd like a third phase where trade comes back and they can feel that the third kind isn't a disagreement about numbers at all. But maybe I'm wrong and the real story is just vague payoffs, or that people hate uncertainty they can't put a number on — I'd rather the activity could surface that than smuggle in my conclusion.

Give me rules, materials, the setup per phase, a timeline across the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto each part of the idea. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (someone will trade just to be friendly, someone will try to game the game, someone will say it's rigged) and what I do about each. And be straight with me about which part of the no-trade idea an activity like this just can't reproduce, so I know what I'll have to say out loud at the end.

Last thing: I'd like them to walk out with the sense that this is a claim about knowledge, not about finance — that the interesting bit is what two minds can and can't do with each other's information. If you can end on a question they can't settle in the room, even better.
