Here's the situation, with the details you'd otherwise be missing:

The last application in the pile is a large cross-national study of what people call the gender-equality paradox — the finding that in countries scoring highest on gender-equality indices, women make up a *smaller* share of science/engineering graduates and occupational choices look more gendered, not less. The applicant (a psychometrics group, PI is a well-known differential psychologist) reads this as evidence that when material constraint and social pressure drop away, men's and women's average occupational preferences diverge — i.e. what we're seeing is partly what people freely choose, not just what they're pushed into. Their proposal has three parts: (1) re-analysis of the existing cross-national data with better controls; (2) a new 12,000-person survey in six countries using what they call an "aspiration-similarity index" — how close the stated ideal-job profiles of 15-year-old boys and girls are within a country, which they argue is a cleaner measure of "how gendered are choices" than degree counts, since degree counts are contaminated by who gets to attend university at all; (3) a trade-press book and a media push, with a stated commitment to report whatever they find.

The thing that has blown us up is a pilot result they included in the appendix. They re-ran an earlier instrument in two of the six countries fifteen years after the original wave. In both, gender equality rose over that period *and* the female share of STEM entrants rose with it — i.e. within a country, over time, the sign is the opposite of the sign you get when you compare countries at a single point in time. The PI treats this as a puzzle to be explained and says the study will adjudicate between "the cross-sectional pattern is a real expression of preference" and "it's a between-country artifact of some sort." Two of our members think that admission is fatal and that the whole literature is built on a broken measure — their counter-proposal, which we could fund instead (about a third of the budget, no new data collection), is a pure methods audit: reanalyse everything with the compositional confounds and the index-construction choices varied systematically, and see whether the paradox survives. Others think the pilot result is exactly the reason to fund the big study, since only new data can tell these apart.

And then there's the third fight. Two members want to decline on the grounds that whatever this finds will be weaponised — either as "see, gender equality is self-defeating" or as "see, the differences are innate, stop trying" — and that a council whose remit includes contributing to social cohesion shouldn't bankroll it. Another member says that's a heckler's veto, that we funded plenty of research on the social causes of the same gaps and can't now refuse to fund the uncomfortable hypothesis, and that a norm of pulling money because of who might quote you will rot the discipline from the inside. I don't think either side is being silly.

The council's rules let us: fund as requested; fund with conditions (which we do set fairly often — preregistration, data-deposit and openness requirements, approval rights over dissemination, milestone reporting); defer pending revision; or decline. We can also split the money between the two proposals, though the audit group says a half-funded audit is pointless.
