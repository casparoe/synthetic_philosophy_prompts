Me and a friend got into this after a public lecture on cosmology and we couldn't find the broken step, and its been bugging me for a week. His argument, cleaned up a bit (numbering is mine):

(1) An observation confirms a hypothesis only if the observation would have been unlikely — or at least not guaranteed — had the hypothesis been false. Evidence that was coming either way is no evidence for anything.

(2) Take any multiverse theory M that's big and varied enough that every physically possible observation gets made by somebody somewhere.

(3) Given (2), then for any observation report E whatsoever, the probability that some observer somewhere sees E is 1.

(4) But "some observer sees E" is all the information we ever actually have. All I can report is "I see E", and whoever the observers turn out to be, each of them will say the same thing about themselves. The word "I" doesn't add any extra fact to the report.

(5) So M assigns probability 1 to every possible observation.

(6) A theory that assigns probability 1 to everything predicts nothing, so nothing can confirm it. So no multiverse theory can be confirmed by observation. That's the bit he wants.

(7) Now run the same argument with M swapped for "the universe is just extremely large and the same laws hold everywhere". If it's large enough, every physically possible observation occurs somewhere in it too. So by (1)-(6) plain old Big Bang cosmology is unconfirmable as well.

(8) And nothing in the argument really used anything special about the multiverse. It only used the fact that we can't locate ourselves among the observers a theory says exist. So the argument shows that nobody who is uncertain which observer they are can ever confirm anything, which is nuts.

(9) So (1)-(6) prove way too much and something in there is wrong.

I'd rather keep (1) and (3) than swallow (9) — (1) is just the bare idea of evidence and (3) looks like arithmetic. My own guess is that (4) is the culprit: "I see E" carries something "someone sees E" doesn't, something about how typical an observer in my situation is in the world the theory describes. But my friend has a reply I cant get past: if you patch (4) with a typicality assumption, you've only moved the problem up a floor, because the claim about what fraction of observers are in my situation, and how to count them when the number is infinite, isn't itself something you can check by looking. He also says this is the same shape as the look-elsewhere effect that particle physicists deal with all the time, and that they deal with it fine — so either the ordinary fix works and (3)-(5) never got off the ground, or it doesn't work and cosmology as a whole is in trouble.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the fix really is a typicality assumption or a measure over observers, I want to know honestly what kind of claim that is and where a person could be entitled to it, and whether leaning on it is any different from the "no alternatives" style of non-empirical argument people use to support string theory without data.
