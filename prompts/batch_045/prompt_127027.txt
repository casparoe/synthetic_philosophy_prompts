I'm a cognitive-science PhD student, and I've been asked to give a forty-five-minute guest lecture, whiteboard only, to roughly forty second-year computer-science students in a Foundations of AI course. They have had one machine-learning course and a good deal of linear algebra, no linguistics and no philosophy, and they were assigned Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences for the week; several of them have already told me they think the book's thesis is obvious. I don't want you to explain the thesis to me — I want a plan for how I present it. Start with a question I genuinely can't settle: should the strong version of the claim be on the board at minute zero, or withheld until they have been led somewhere surprising? My worry about stating it up front is that it will sound trivially true to them in the weak form and obviously false in the strong form before I have earned the strong form, and once they have settled on one of those two reactions it is hard to dislodge. So give me the dependency structure: what has to be in place before the claim that categorization is itself analogizing — rather than that analogy being a device we occasionally reach for — can even be stated without sounding either trivial or wrong, and what breaks if I reorder it. When I first worked through the material myself I oscillated for days between "of course" and "surely not, we also apply rules," and I would rather the students pass through those two reactions in a sequence I chose than at random.

Second: the carrying example and what it has to be. I am tempted to open with the desktop, folder, window and trash vocabulary of a graphical interface, precisely because the analogy there has become invisible to these students — but I suspect that very invisibility is also the argument against it, since they will say that is a metaphor in the interface, not a fact about thinking, and I will have spent my best example on a case I have to talk them out of. My own guess at the constraints is that the example must have an analogy doing real, load-bearing work before anyone notices that there is an analogy; that the contrast between what travels and what gets left behind must be visible in a single sentence; that it must not depend on any fact a student would feel the need to check; and that it must not be a four-term比例 puzzle or a literary metaphor. Correct that list, add to it, choose the example, and tell me why the GUI case does or does not clear it. Then the misconceptions, each with the point in the sequence where it should be defused and a reason it belongs there rather than ten minutes earlier: that analogy is a deliberate comparison of two things already understood and so cannot be constitutive of understanding itself; that this is just nearest-neighbour similarity in a vector space and the ML course already covered it; that if all categorization is analogical then any object could belong to any category, which I am fairly sure can only be answered after the surfaces/essences distinction is on the board and not before; and that large language models already do exactly this, so the question is settled. Give me the two or three I have missed as well — I expect there is one about rules versus analogy and one about whether any of this is a claim about neural machinery.

Finally, the ending and the cuts. I want the last ten minutes to land on something the students cannot walk out having already agreed to, and my idea is to finish with what I have been calling the coinage threshold: the point at which a category stops stretching to absorb a new case and speakers coin a new word instead — cases like "mail" stretching to cover electronic mail while on-demand audio got a new word rather than an extension of "broadcast." Does that belong at the end, does it genuinely depend on everything before it or could I open with it, what example should carry it given that I cannot make any historical claim the students would want to verify, and does it reframe the thesis or merely decorate it? For the check on whether any of it landed I want a transfer task rather than a comprehension question, and my candidate is to hand them something fresh — whether treating a chatbot's persona as a friend is an analogical extension of friend or a change in what the word picks out, and what would settle it — but tell me what a correct answer and a merely fluent answer would look like there, and what the probe would leave me unable to tell. And close by saying what I should openly refuse to explain in forty-five minutes, announcing the refusal as I go: I am thinking the neural-implementation question and anything about whether deep learning vindicates or refutes the picture. Tell me also which single thing I should cut even though it is my favourite part.
