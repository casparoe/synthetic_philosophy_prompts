My sister is a sculptor, and last month I helped her crate a fired-clay figure for a gallery, which started an argument with the shipper that I have been turning over ever since. The manifest listed one item; my sister's insurance schedule listed one work; but the conservator's condition report described "the sculpture" and "the clay body" separately, noting a hairline crack in the body while declaring the sculpture itself undamaged. That sent me off reading about material constitution on my own — I have no academic background in philosophy, just whatever popular books I've managed to find — and there is one chapter whose summary of the statue-and-the-clay puzzle I can't tell whether to trust. As best I can reconstruct it, it goes roughly like this: on Monday a sculptor buys a lump of clay; on Tuesday she shapes it into a statue, and we call the clay Lumpkin and the statue Goliath. The two fill exactly the same space, weigh the same, and at every moment are made of exactly the same matter, yet they cannot be identical, because they differ in their properties — Lumpkin existed on Monday when Goliath did not, Lumpkin would survive being rolled into a ball while Goliath would not, and Goliath could have been cast in bronze while Lumpkin, being clay, could not. Identity is necessary and identical things share all their properties, so there are two objects here, and ever since, nearly everyone who works on this has accepted that constitution is not identity: Lumpkin constitutes Goliath without being the same thing as him, and the only remaining question is what sort of relation constitution is. The price of that view is that the same reasoning applies to us: a person and her body would be two things in one place, and since both would seem to qualify as thinking, the view commits you to two thinkers sitting in your chair, each with its own thoughts. That absurdity is what pushes eliminativists like Peter van Inwagen to deny that statues or lumps exist at all — on his view only simples really exist, and talk about statues is useful fiction.

My own instinct, which I put to the shipper and which he thought was obvious nonsense, was that there is just one thing in the crate and "statue" and "lump of clay" are two ways of describing it, with the difference being in how we talk rather than in what is there. The chapter shook me on that, but not entirely, because of something it never addresses: my sister's figure was fired in a kiln, and once clay is fired it isn't clay any more, it's ceramic. So if Goliath was constituted by a lump of clay on Tuesday, what constitutes him on Friday — does Lumpkin simply go out of existence and a new portion of stuff, which was never a lump of clay, take over the job, making the statue's survival depend on a change in its constitution that the summary never mentions? And what should the crate manifest say, one object or two, or is that the wrong kind of question?

Could you go through that summary a claim at a time and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what they'd call a simplification or a slide, and what is straightforwardly wrong? In particular: are the three differences it lists (the Monday/Tuesday one, the squashing one, and the bronze one) really three versions of the same argument, or do they do different work? Is it true that nearly everyone has settled on constitution-is-not-identity? Does the two-thinkers point actually refute anything, or is it a known cost that defenders think they can pay, and is it really what motivates the eliminativists? Is the book right about what van Inwagen's positive view is? Does my "one thing, two descriptions" instinct have a name, and what is the standard reply to it? And is my firing case, or the conservator's cracked-body-but-undamaged-sculpture, a genuine difficulty for the apparatus the chapter sets up, or does that apparatus handle them without strain?
