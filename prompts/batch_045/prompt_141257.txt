I'm a PhD candidate in philosophy working on the epistemology of thought experiments, and I keep being stopped by a piece of departmental folklore that no one will spell out: "You can't answer an intuition pump with an argument — only with a better intuition pump." (Dennett's term has gone generic in my department; any vivid hypothetical gets called a pump, and he collected his own favourites in *Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking*.)

The occasion matters, because the precisification I need is the one that argument required. I presented a paper attacking a widely used case in my subfield. My objection was not a rival case. I did what I call a pump audit: I built a minimal pair, varying the original scenario along exactly one feature I claim is philosophically irrelevant to the verdict, and then gave both versions to two groups of undergraduates in different orders. The majority verdict flipped with the order of presentation. I concluded that the case's pull depends on an incidental feature of its packaging, and that it therefore can't bear the weight the literature puts on it. A senior philosopher replied with the line above, and the room treated that as disposing of my paper — my objection, being an argument and a pilot study rather than a hypothetical, was said to be the wrong kind of thing entirely.

Please set the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing out as a menu, ordered from the near-trivial to the radical, and give verdicts rather than picking one. For each reading I want: the claim stated sharply enough that it could be false; a classification (platitude about how people are moved, empirical claim about actual audiences, normative claim about what counts as evidence, or claim about what philosophical theses are); whether it is defensible; and whether it would really do the work it was made to do in my case — that is, whether it licenses dismissing a non-narrative objection without engaging it.

Three things I'd like handled inside that menu. First, disambiguate "better": more persuasive, more vivid, closer to the target concept, more reliable as a guide to the thing being investigated, or more diagnostic of what is doing the work in the reader. These come apart, and the reading chosen seems to decide the whole issue. Second, grade the readings against a taxonomy of possible responses to a case: a rival case (a counter-pump), a pump audit of the kind I ran, a stability test that varies framing or order, and a bridge attack on the step from "we would say X here" to the general or modal claim the case is used to support. For each reading, say which of these four is ruled out as the "wrong kind of thing" and which remains available. Third, flag any reading on which the slogan is itself in trouble: if a pump audit is just another pump, the slogan becomes unfalsifiable, and if no argument can ever bear on a case, then this one-liner about pumps is oddly positioned to tell us anything.

Finish with what you take to be the most charitable reading, and with one sentence I could say in reply next week that neither concedes the point nor picks a fight.
