I am assembling a set of stress cases for a graduate seminar on the explanatory move usually called the strange inversion of reasoning — Darwin's version, in which apparent design is explained by a mindless, purposeless filter, and its Turing-machine analogue, in which apparent reasoning is explained by a formal procedure that no one needs to understand — and on the associated claim that competence can exist without comprehension.

The cases I already have are ones where the mindless system wins because it is cheaper, faster, or more tireless, and my students dismiss them correctly: such cases show that comprehension is expensive, not that it is dispensable or that it can be outperformed. I need one case where the mindless system is genuinely better, and where the reason it is better is constitutive of what comprehension is rather than an overhead comprehension happens to carry.

Specifications for the case:

- Two systems, A and B, working in the same domain on the same task. A has genuine and correct comprehension of the domain: it grasps the structure that makes the correct answers correct, and can give the reasons why. B has none of that. B has competence without comprehension, and that competence is produced by one of the two inversions named above — you should say which, and why it is that one.
- On the ordinary run of cases, of the kind that a competent practitioner, A included, would treat as routine rather than adversarial or contrived, B is reliably more accurate than A. Not faster, not cheaper, not able to handle more volume: more accurate, on a metric both parties antecedently accept.
- A's comprehension must be correct. The margin cannot come from A's having a false belief, a gap in its knowledge, or a merely partial model of the domain.
- The margin cannot come from any of the usual suspects: computational cost, speed, memory, attention, fatigue, choking, self-conscious monitoring, distraction, or time pressure. You may not make A tired, rushed, nervous, or overloaded.
- Realistic setting: a clinic, a farm, a factory floor, a trading desk, a logistics hub — somewhere a present-day practitioner could actually be, using technology that exists now. No superintelligent systems, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics.
- No gerrymandering. The regime of cases that reveals B's advantage must be the ordinary one, and whatever feature B exploits must be reliably present in the environment as it actually is, not an artifact of a biased sample or of how the evaluation set was assembled.

One route you may find useful, though you are not required to take it: consider whether comprehension is essentially tied to the ability to cite reasons of a grain that can be stated and generalized, and whether that requirement excludes from consideration exactly the fine-grained, idiosyncratic, but statistically tighter structure that a mindless system can exploit. If you use this route, say precisely which step of it is doing the work.

Deliverables, in order:

1. The scenario itself, in no more than 150 words, including one sentence on how B came to have its competence.
2. A one-sentence working criterion of comprehension that you will be using — operational, not an appeal to consciousness or to what it is like to be the system — together with a demonstration that A satisfies it and B does not.
3. What the competence-without-comprehension account says about the case, and why, on that account, B wins.
4. What a comprehension-first theorist — someone who holds that genuine understanding is what makes flexible and reliable competence possible — says about the case, and where that theorist's prediction goes wrong here.
5. Name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and show that removing that feature, and only that feature, collapses the case back into one where comprehension wins.
6. Say whether B's competence is generated by Darwin's inversion or by Turing's, and whether substituting the other inversion would break the case; if it would, say what the other inversion cannot supply.
7. Say what would happen if B were additionally given comprehension of the domain, deriving the answer from your criterion in item 2 rather than stipulating it.
8. The strongest objection a comprehension-first theorist would raise against the case — the best version of it, not a straw man — and your reply.
9. A final sentence: does the case support the strong thesis, that comprehension is nothing over and above a particular arrangement of competences, or only the weak thesis, that comprehension is not required for the highest grades of competence?

A case that simply stipulates that B is more accurate, or that builds the verdict into the description of the systems rather than deriving it from the two accounts plus the details of the scenario, fails the assignment.
