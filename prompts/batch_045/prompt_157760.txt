Suppose Borges — who died in Geneva in 1986, having been blind for roughly the last three decades of his life — had lived to 2036 and was being asked, as he so often was, for his opinion of novelties. Three of them bear directly on what he wrote about time.

First: the reading of quantum mechanics that was still a marginal curiosity at his death, on which every outcome of a measurement is realized in some branch and the branches are all equally actual, is now a research programme with a canon, a literature on how to recover probabilities within it, and a well-rehearsed popular presentation. In 2033 a laboratory in Kyoto announced what it called the Pên protocol: a quantum computer carrying a fine-grained simulation of a single household's day, arranged so that every decision the simulated agent might make is realized in some branch. The press release described it as Ts'ui Pên's novel realized at last, and the physicists quoted in it said the branches are all equally real.

Second: a Geneva company sells a mortuary service. Your waking experience is recorded at neural resolution from the day you enrol; after your death the recording is run, at real speed, into an emulated brain fitted with no memory of any earlier pass; the contract permits an arbitrary number of passes. Each pass is subjectively first-personal — the subject in it does not know that she is being replayed.

Third, for completeness: a generative system that, given any paragraph, emits every admissible continuation of it — a mechanical Ts'ui Pên for prose, running unattended, producing far more than anyone could read.

Now my question. The easy answer is that the forking-paths story anticipated the multiverse, that Borges would have been enchanted by the Pên protocol, and that the rest is commentary. I don't believe that answer, and I want the one that survives contact with what he actually argued. In the essay in which he extends Berkeley's denial of matter and Hume's denial of the subject to time itself, the move that does the work is a repeated perception — the same perception twice, in one life or in two — which is offered as enough to break the linear sequence: if the self is not a substance and succession is not real, there is nothing for a branching tree to branch. Note also that on the standard Everettian picture the branches are not temporally successive at all; they coexist, and nothing becomes. So if anything vindicates him here, it is the atemporal, nothing-happens reading of branching — not the every-possibility-is-lived reading that the popularizers sell, and not the image in the story, where the forking is a property of a book, of an artefact, which Albert calls an incomplete but not false image of the universe.

I'd like, then:

- his likely verdict on each of the three, and which of them he would treat as confirming something he held versus merely supplying a new image for it;
- what the replay case does to his view, given his treatment of recurrence, of doubles, of the man who dreams a son and discovers he is himself dreamed, and of total memory in the story of the man who remembers everything and therefore cannot think: is the second pass the same life, another life with identical content, or nothing anyone could care about, given that he doubts there is a subject to care;
- where his commitments pull against each other and what he would have said if forced to choose;
- what short story he would have written about each of the first two;
- and, for each device, the change in the setup that would flip his verdict — the feature whose presence would make the thing either worthless or genuinely terrible, and the feature that would make it matter.
