You are a professional philosopher working in normative theory and the philosophy of action, specifically on the question of whether a person's reasons are fixed by her actual desires and choices, or by what she would endorse if she deliberated well, or by facts about her good that reduce to neither. Before you answer, please think the problem through slowly: begin by sketching, informally, two or three accounts of what it is for someone to have a reason, then test each of them against the practices I describe below, and only then settle on the account you find most defensible.

I sit on a hospital ethics committee, and I keep tripping over the same asymmetry in how we treat a person's earlier self and her later self, and I want you to reconstruct the theory our practice implicitly relies on. Three features of it. First: in many jurisdictions a psychiatric patient who, while well, signed a so-called Ulysses clause authorizing involuntary treatment during a future episode will be treated over her contemporaneous protest once the episode arrives; we discount what she now says precisely because she is frightened, manic, or psychotic, and we treat her earlier, calmer signature as the more authoritative expression of her will. Second: in the very same hospital, a competent patient's refusal of a life-saving blood transfusion is respected even when her reasons rest on beliefs the staff regards as plainly mistaken, and we say that the quality of her reasoning is not ours to police. Third: the law lets the earlier self bind the later self only within narrow limits — consumer rules let a buyer cancel within a few days a contract signed at her own front door, courts will not order someone to keep performing a personal service she has promised, and jurisdictions that permit assisted dying typically insist on repeated contemporaneous requests rather than accepting a single authorization given years earlier, however lucidly. A colleague of mine says this pattern only makes sense on an indirect view: the reasons you have are not given by what you actually want at the time of action, but by what you would want, or would advise yourself to do, if you deliberated from full information, calmly and coherently. I am not convinced the practice really needs that. What does it strictly presuppose? Which rival views would license the same three features just as well — a plain desire-based view, an objective-list or object-given view, a view that distinguishes a person's critical interests from her merely experiential ones? And are the three features mutually consistent? If they are not, tell me which one should give way and why.

The immediate reason I raise this is an amendment now before our committee. Under current policy, any revocation of a Ulysses clause is discounted while the patient is in an episode. The proposal is that a revocation should be honored even mid-episode, but only if the patient can, when asked, reconstruct in her own words the considerations she cited when she signed the directive — the thought being that a revocation counts if she still has cognitive grip on the earlier reasoning she is rejecting, and does not count if she has lost touch with it entirely. Tell me what this amendment presupposes that current policy does not. In particular: does it assume that the authority of an idealized deliberator over the actual agent depends on the actual agent's being able to recognize that deliberator's reasoning as continuous with her own, and is that a genuine constraint on indirect views or merely a practical safeguard against misidentifying whose reasoning it is? Does the amendment confuse procedural idealization, which only adds information and removes fear, with substantive idealization, which corrects the desires themselves? And finally, if we abandoned the indirect picture altogether and said that a person's reasons at a time are simply given by her actual contemporaneous valuing, or instead by facts about her well-being that her actual valuing can fail to track, what exactly would have to change in the three features of our practice I described above?
