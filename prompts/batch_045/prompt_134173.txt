Suppose Paul Grice, who died in 1988, had instead been handed, in the present year, a few hours with a frontier chat assistant plus an honest engineering account of how it works: next-token prediction over a large corpus, then preference tuning against thousands of anonymous human raters, then deployment behind a system prompt written by a company. I want his considered verdict on one question: does such a system ever *mean* something by what it emits, in his sense of utterer's meaning — roughly, producing an output with the intention of inducing a belief in an audience by means of that audience's recognition of the intention, as against the timeless meaning of the sentence type — or does it only ever emit word-shaped output that we read as meaningful, the way an actor on stage merely makes as if to say something?

To force a determinate answer, give him two cases. First, a model fine-tuned on the complete surviving writings of a single dead novelist and prompted to continue her correspondence: no living person intends anything, but there is a dead one whose intentions the outputs track. Second, the deployed assistant, where the candidate intentions are distributed across raters, deployers, and end users who want different things from it. Which case would he take to be more hospitable to genuine speaker meaning, and where would he try to locate the intention — in the model, in the training process, in the deployer, in the interpretive stance of the user, or nowhere at all?

Two follow-ups he would not let drop. When the assistant confabulates a confident falsehood, is it lying, is it violating a maxim it was never able to follow, or is Quality, for such a system, only a statistical regularity rather than a norm? And given his standing aversion to multiplying senses of words beyond need, would he conclude that *meaning* has turned out to be ambiguous between our case and the model's, or that the model case simply fails the test and the word applies to us alone? End with the one future development in AI that would most likely move him off whichever verdict you give.
