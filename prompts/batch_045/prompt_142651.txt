I'm in a graduate seminar on metaphysics and mind, and this week we worked through the three-stances framework — physical, design, intentional — together with the account of a *real pattern* that is meant to accompany it. Two people came away from the same pages with opposite conclusions. One said the view shows that beliefs and desires are perfectly real and that the anti-realist about the mind simply loses. The other said it shows that taking the intentional stance is a cheap prediction trick, and that calling a pattern real here amounts to nothing more than calling it useful to us. Both were citing the same criterion, which we were given roughly as follows: a pattern is real in a body of data just in case there is a description of that data more efficient than the bit map — shorter than a point-by-point listing of it — whether or not anyone has happened to think of it.

I have tried to write out the argument behind the first reading and then push it until it breaks. It breaks, and I cannot tell where to get off.

(1) On the criterion as stated, whether a pattern is real in a given body of data is settled by whether there exists a description of that data which is more efficient than the bit map.
(2) Whether such a description exists does not depend on anyone's having found it, on anyone's interests, or on what anyone can compute in the time available.
(3) So realness, so defined, is an objective feature of the data rather than something conferred by our taking up a stance toward it: the intentional stance is not merely instrumental.
(4) Consider any system for which the intentional stance pays for itself: ascribing beliefs and desires to it, and predicting on the assumption that it will do what it has most reason to do, yields better predictions more cheaply than the physical stance — available in principle, hopeless in practice — and more cheaply than the design stance, which needs to know what the system was built to do and whether it is in working order.
(5) A description that outperforms its rivals as a prediction scheme is, in the relevant sense, a more efficient description of the system's behavioural data than they are. So, by the criterion, the pattern it finds is real.
(6) So for any such system, its beliefs and desires are not mere as-if posits; they are as real as anything the physical or design stance turns up, and the realist wins.

Now the part that is meant to show that (1) cannot be what we wanted.

(7) Take any body of data and any arbitrary way of carving it up — say, the class of observations recorded on dates containing the digit 7. There is surely some coding scheme in which that class receives a one-symbol name, and in which the data therefore admits a description shorter than the bit map.
(8) Generalizing: for any finite body of data and any carving of it whatsoever, one can choose a description language in which that carving is compressed as far as it can be compressed. So if the description language is allowed to float, every carving of every finite data set marks a real pattern, and the criterion excludes nothing at all.
(9) The only way to keep the criterion from collapsing is to fix the description language in advance. But which language is fixed is not itself a fact about the data; it is a choice, and different defensible choices can disagree about the behavioural data in (4).
(10) So (3) is false. Realness on this account is relative to a coding scheme, hence to something on our side of the divide — which is exactly what the friend of (6) needed it not to be.
(11) So the apparatus cannot do the mediating work it was introduced to do. It either certifies every gerrymandered class as real or makes realness a function of our representational choices, and either way the original dispute is not settled but relocated.

The step I trust least is (8): it feels too easy, and we were told that the existence of a compression was supposed to be the objective part. But I cannot say what is wrong with it.

Two further things I cannot place. First, I tried two repairs to (1). Call a pattern *load-bearing* if exploiting it is strictly cheaper than any alternative route to the same predictions; call it *canonical* if its shortest description is in a language fixed antecedently for the domain rather than tailored to the carving at hand. Does either repair let me keep (4) through (6), and what does each one cost me? Second, a test case. Call it the Ledger: a device whose complete physical description is an enormous table of input–output pairs, compiled by recording the behaviour of some ordinary agent over a large but finite range of situations, with enough idiosyncratic gaps left in it that nothing compact reproduces the table exactly. The physical stance predicts the Ledger perfectly if you have the table. The design stance yields almost nothing, since there is no function to reason from beyond read off row n. The intentional stance does find a pattern and beats the design stance, but it loses to the physical stance, because the table covers situations the intentional story gets wrong or does not reach. Does (5) say the Ledger has beliefs? If it does, I take that as a further reason to doubt the argument. If it does not, then whether a system's beliefs are real apparently depends on how good our physics happens to be, which is its own kind of trouble for (3).

Which step should I reject, why did it look sound, and what does rejecting it cost? I would much rather keep (4) through (6) than (1) through (3), if that helps you price the options.
