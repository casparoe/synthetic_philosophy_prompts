ok so for my metaethics semster paper I have to hand in a one-page statement of where I've landed on the Frege–Geach stuff before we write the actual essay, and I've been trying to write mine down and honestly I can't tell whether I've got a position or just a pile of things I like the sound of. Can you audit it? Here's the list:

(1) I'm an expressivist. No stance-independent normative properties, no normative beliefs; what makes 'stealing is wrong' mean what it means is that it expresses a certain non-cognitive attitude (roughly, being against stealing), and that's the whole semantic story.

(2) I think Frege–Geach is the real objection and that the only serious answer is a properly compositional semantics: the meaning of a normative sentence has to be the same in embedded contexts as it is asserted, negation has to be one uniform operation, and I want the logic that comes out to be classical. I use excluded middle, double-negation elimination, proof by cases and contraposition in ordinary moral argument all the time and I don't want to be told I'm not allowed to.

(3) Negation is not attitude reversal. 'Stealing isn't wrong' does not express approval of stealing, it expresses withholding, or tolerance, and I want that to be a genuine third state — not just the absence of an attitude, and not something definable as some higher-order attitude about attitudes. This is the bit I think saves me from the usual complaint that expressivists can't handle negation at all.

(4) I'm not a deflationist about truth. I don't think we earn the right to call moral sentences true just by playing the disquotation game, and I'm happy to say they aren't truth-apt. But I also want moral disagreement to be robust: when me and my uncle disagree about something moral, one of us can be straightforwardly mistaken, and it is not like me liking chocolate and him liking vanilla.

(5) Fixed point I won't move on: torturing a kid for fun is wrong, and it would be wrong no matter what anyone thought about it. That's the sentence I'm least willing to lose.

(6) My own idea for how (4) is meant to work without truth: the correctness standard isn't anybody's actual attitudes, it's the norms constitutive of the practice of moral judgment itself — universality, taking everyone's interests into account, consistency, whatever the real list turns out to be. A judgment is mistaken when it violates those. I like it because the standard ends up being neither my attitudes nor a stance-independent fact.

(7) I want to be able to say moral thinking is genuine reasoning — arguments can be valid or invalid, and you can be irrational in how you revise a moral view — even though there are no moral beliefs.

Here's the case that's been bugging me. Take a famine where I think stealing the bread is positively required, not merely permitted. Intuitively I'll say 'of course it isn't wrong, it's required'. But on (3) that utterance has to express withholding or indifference about the stealing, and I'm not indifferent in the slightest, I'm fully for it. And if I let 'not wrong' cover both being-indifferent and being-for, then negation doesn't pick out a single attitude any more and I've lost the uniform story I wanted in (2).

So: wich pairs on this list are actually in tension and which only look like they are? Rank the real ones by how bad they are, and for each one say exactly what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please don't give me names or a reading list or tell me who in the literature holds which view — I've got all that separately and I'll add it myself. I just want the shape of the thing. I'm most attached to (5), and I think (6) is the clever part, but I've got a feeling the actual problem is somewhere else entirely and I can't see where.
