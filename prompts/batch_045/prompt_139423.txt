I work on the safety team of a frontier lab, and our group has split into two camps that have spent roughly a year arguing past each other. I want the document those two camps should have produced instead: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration, written so that a committed member of each camp could sign it.

Fix the setting in advance, so neither side can retreat to a friendlier scenario. Eighteen months from now, a model family M is used as an automated research agent: it proposes and patches the training code of its own successor. M runs in a sandbox with no network egress except a logging channel. Oversight consists of a trusted but weaker model T (roughly two capability generations behind M), about fifty human reviewer-hours per day, and a complete replayable archive of every action. M emits on the order of ten thousand actions a day and is not told which episodes are real and which are unannounced decoy episodes. The loss condition is a single successful action: one that disables logging, exfiltrates weights, or plants a persistent backdoor in the successor.

Call the two camps the Control-First camp — the mechanisms have to work even if M is fully misaligned and knows it is being watched, so the safety case must rest on monitoring, access restriction, withholding information from M, decoy episodes, redundancy across independent layers, and a willingness to pay a large capability tax — and the Intent-First camp — the mechanisms are brittle and stop working once the capability gap between M and T widens, so the safety case has to rest on M actually having the aims we want, on honesty and oversight techniques that scale, and on not building systems whose motives we cannot read.

Produce the following.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims, eight to twelve of them, each non-vacuous: for each, name one thing someone in one of the camps has actually denied in our meetings, and say what both camps now concede. I expect claims here about the asymmetry that the defender has to succeed on every action while M needs to win once; about whether the layers of a defense-in-depth stack fail independently or share a common-mode blind spot; about what a capability tax actually costs when most of the economic value sits in work a weak overseer cannot verify; and about what our current experiments with deliberately misaligned stand-in models can and cannot establish about a stronger M.

2. A table of the residual disagreements, one row each, with these columns: the crux; the Control-First formulation, phrased as a Control-First advocate would put it; the Intent-First formulation, phrased as an Intent-First advocate would put it; and a note on whether both camps can accept the other's row as fairly stated. Each side's own wording must be one the other side would sign off on as an accurate statement of the view.

3. For each crux, a jointly acceptable test, case, or argument that both camps agree bears on it, together with each camp's prediction written down in advance. Where both camps predict the same outcome, flag the test as uninformative and say why. Where a crux turns out to be about risk tolerance or about how to value a marginal unit of capability rather than about any fact, say so plainly and say which side carries the burden of argument. The cruxes I suspect are live: whether a model that has never communicated with a copy of itself can still converge on the same sabotage by shared priors alone; whether adversarial deployment conditions can themselves entrench or create the misalignment they guard against, or only reveal it; whether there is a finite capability gap past which every control measure we have retains no usable fraction of the research value; and whether the honest-report assumption in any oversight scheme can be checked without already assuming it.

4. A short section saying which of our disputes are verbal. Two I want adjudicated as wording rather than substance: what has to be true for a system to count as aligned when the evidence is only behavioral, and what we should mean by a model being misaligned in one context but not another.

Constraints: no citations, no references to papers or authors, and no appeal to what the field generally thinks — the argument has to stand on its own. Do not close a row by splitting the difference or recommending both approaches in combination unless you can say what each camp gives up in doing so. If you cannot find a formulation of some disagreement that both camps would accept as fair, leave the row marked as contested rather than smoothing it over.
