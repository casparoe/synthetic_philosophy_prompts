You are a decision theorist and philosopher of practical reasoning who has spent years advising grantmakers on how to allocate money under uncertainty. Please work in two stages. First, brainstorm privately: the different precise things each of the three factors in the importance / tractability / neglectedness framework could be measuring (importance per dollar or importance of the whole problem? tractable by whom, with what, at what margin? neglected by whom, relative to what benchmark?), the ways those factors can pull apart, double-count each other, or turn into evidence against one another, and the specific ways a three-factor score can mislead a small committee that multiplies numbers in a spreadsheet. Only after that should you draft the document described below.

I'm a retired accountant with no academic background who volunteers on the grants committee of the Halstead Family Foundation, a small family foundation in Cascade County. Our annual giving is about $1.9 million, but the donor's will restricts one pot to "relieving suffering in Cascade County in ways government cannot or will not," and each year we award a single $250,000 grant from it. A couple of years ago I found a website about choosing a career for maximum impact that scored causes on importance, tractability, and neglectedness, so I built us a spreadsheet: each trustee rates the finalists 1 to 5 on the three factors, we multiply, and the highest product wins. This year two finalists survived, and the multiplication has produced a stalemate that I don't think is really about the numbers. Finalist A is an in-school reading tutoring program built on a model with strong evidence behind it; it is already roughly seventy percent covered by a state literacy grant and a national donor, and our money would close the remaining gap to serve four more elementary schools. One of our trustees, who sits on a county budget advisory group, says the state was likely to top that program up anyway and our check would simply free state money to be spent elsewhere. Finalist B is retrofitting ventilation and filtration in the eight oldest school buildings in the county, aimed at indoor air quality, asthma, attendance, and possibly test scores — an area where the county facilities office has said it can do the work but no other funder will touch it, where nobody has measured whether the retrofit changes any outcome we care about, and where the school boards would have to keep buying and changing filters for a decade. The trustee who proposed it says its cheapness to us is exactly the point; another trustee replies that the fact that nobody else funds it is itself evidence against it, the "if it were any good, someone would already be doing it" point. In the middle of this I invented a fix, and I want it stress-tested rather than flattered: I added a fourth column, a crowding-out haircut that discounts a cause's neglectedness score by how likely other funders are to fill the gap regardless of what we do, and I also scored B higher partly for what I called option value — that $250,000 might buy us the information to fund it properly later if it works, which a ten-year commitment to tutoring never would. Half the committee now suspects the fourth column is just me rigging the outcome in favor of the more exciting proposal, and I'm not sure they're wrong.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the other five trustees before we vote on the fourteenth, roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words, written so that non-philosophers can read it in ten minutes. It should open with a concrete bottom line — fund A, fund B, split the money, or defer the decision in a specified way — and a time frame for whatever you recommend. It should tell us plainly what our three factors actually mean at the margin and how they should be combined when the underlying judgments are uncertain and not really on comparable scales, and it should take a clear position on the two things I invented: whether a crowding-out adjustment belongs in the framework at all, and whether option value is a legitimate tiebreaker or a way of smuggling in enthusiasm. It should also say what our donor's "cannot or will not" clause does to the neglectedness question, since the rest of us have been arguing past each other on that. I need an honest treatment of the strongest argument on whichever side you come down against — if you recommend B, engage the crowding-out and evidence-quality worries head-on; if you recommend A, engage the argument that we are just buying something that would have happened anyway. Close with what specific evidence, obtainable before or shortly after the fourteenth, would change your recommendation, and with what our scoring rule should be for next year. Do not cite studies or hand me a reading list; none of us can look anything up, and what I need is the reasoning itself.
