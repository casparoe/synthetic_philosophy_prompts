i'm drafting a bit of a paper section on whether inference to the best explanation even gets off the ground before its question is fixed, and i need a case built to spec to make the point land rather than just assert it. here's what i want:

an ordinary, realistic situation — a hiring panel, grad admissions, a grant committee, a restaurant picking a supplier, whatever, nothing science-fictional and no skeptical machinery — in which the very same evidence supports two different explanations depending on which contrast you ask about. so: ask why P rather than Q and explanation A is clearly the loveliest thing on offer; ask why P rather than R (or just why P, full stop) and explanation B is clearly the loveliest. and A and B have to be genuinely incompatible, not two compatible factors that one narrative could happily cite both of — if A is why-Dana-rather-than-nobody, then B can't also be why-Dana-rather-than-Mo, and vice versa.

constraints, in no particular order:

- one paragraph of scenario. realistic: the kind of thing someone could tell you over coffee. no committee of gods, no lotteries, no predictors.
- both contrast questions have to be ones a real person in that situation would actually ask out loud. none of that why-did-the-puddle-form-here-rather-than-in-the-Andes business.
- identical evidence across both questions. you don't get to quietly add a fact when you switch contrasts.
- nothing ad hoc and no bad lot: each winner has to be a decent explanation on its own terms, not just the least bad item on a sad menu. so you can't get the flip by making A obviously false or by leaving the true hypothesis out of the set.
- the incompatibility shouldn't come from stipulation. it should fall out of the setup — the two stories disagree about something like how high the bar was, or whether the mechanism was local or global — with genuinely mixed evidence, some pointing one way and some the other, such that each contrast question makes a different half of the evidence load-bearing.
- readable by someone with no background in the literature, so set up whatever needs setting up inside the paragraph.

then, after the paragraph:

1. name the two contrast questions, the two candidate explanations, and the evidence, with the evidence separated out from the story so i can see it's the same list both times.
2. walk through each question: say what has to be explained in each case (that P happened at all, versus that P happened rather than this specific nearby alternative), and why the explanation that wins one is a poor answer to the other. rough likelihood talk is fine; invented numbers that look fake are not.
3. tell me which structural feature of the case is doing the work. i want to be able to delete it and watch the flip disappear, so say explicitly what i'd have to change to collapse the two questions back into one.
4. a Bayesian who just conditions on the evidence gets one verdict, not two. say which of your two IBE verdicts that lines up with, and whether that's supposed to be a rescue of IBE or an indictment of it.
5. finally, the objection i'll have to answer: a defender of IBE will say there's no problem here at all, since explanation always answers a question with a contrast built into it, and you're simply asking two different questions. give me the strongest version of that reply you can manage, then the best thing my side can say back — ideally something that shows the reply costs IBE its role as a rule about what to believe.

i'd rather have a case thats slightly messy and real than a clean one that cheats by stipulating the answers. if some part of this spec turns out to be unsatisfiable together with the rest, tell me which bit and why instead of quietly dropping it and hoping i won't notice.
