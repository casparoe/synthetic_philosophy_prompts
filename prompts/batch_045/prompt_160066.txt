I help run a volunteer open-source project — around 3,000 members, everything governed by one mutable document — and for the last couple of years anyone can propose a change to it as a plain-text diff, which passes if it gets whatever the document currently says it needs (usually 2/3 of active members). The twist, and the reason I'm writing: the rules about how rules change are inside the document too, so they get changed as well, and we keep ending up in situations where nobody can tell what the document now says. Last month someone passed, under the old 2/3 rule, an amendment requiring unanimity for all future amendments — and then couldn't get a single typo fix through, with half the group arguing the unanimity rule is void because it was enacted by a procedure it itself declares inadequate, and the other half saying that's just what amending the amendment rule means. A month earlier a cleanup motion passed by simple majority that deleted every rule containing the word restrict, which took out a clause labelled permanent, amendable only by 4/5, and also deleted the sentence defining active member, so we're not even sure who was entitled to vote on it. And we currently have two precedence clauses in force: an older one saying the section it sits in overrides anything added later, and a newer one saying the most recently added rule wins. Apparently there's some game and maybe a philosophy literature about this (Nomic?) but I've never read any of it, and I don't want references or a reading list — I can't look anything up and neither can my moderators, so everything has to be in the procedure itself.

What I want is a review procedure my moderation team can actually run: the ordered set of tests to apply to any proposed diff before it goes to a vote, what counts as a satisfactory answer to each test, which tests end in a flat reject as malformed, and which ones hand the decision to a human because it's a values call rather than a question of fact. Realistic constraints: rotating volunteers with no logic or law background, about fifteen minutes per proposal, and the output is a few sentences of justification in the thread. Please keep it honest about where a step is doing real work and where it's just forcing us to write down a choice we'd otherwise make silently. The failure modes I'd want it to cover, because these are the ones that keep biting us: rule identity — we number rules and amend them in place, so after a diff is it rule 17 or rule 17-prime, and when is a change so extensive that it's really a repeal-and-replace, and why should we care; priority and precedence conflicts, including clauses that claim precedence over later or earlier rules; entrenchment, i.e. whether a clause saying this cannot be amended or only by 4/5 is genuinely binding or is just text an ordinary majority can delete; self-consuming and self-modifying rules, like sunsets, one-shot rules, and rules that rewrite themselves when triggered; definitional feedback, where a rule changes what counts as a member, a quorum, a majority, or a valid rule at all; and irreversibility — deletes, merges and redefinitions that destroy information, where we couldn't get back to the prior state even if everyone agreed they wanted to (we do have git history, so this isn't about losing the text, it's about whether the community can actually return to a prior normative state). Then say plainly what the procedure cannot settle even in principle.

Please also run it on three live-ish proposals so I can see how it behaves. First: a proposal that redefines active member to include people who have only read the forum in the last 90 days, and wants those people counted toward the quorum for the vote on that very proposal. Second: a contributors' rights section stating it may only be amended by a 4/5 vote, with no change to the amendment rule itself — I'm personally in favour of this one and assume it's fine, so if your procedure says it does nothing like what we think it does, say that straight and don't soften it; that's the answer I most need. Third: a spam emergency rule banning one specific tactic that deletes itself after thirty days, plus a seperate housekeeping rule saying any rule unchanged for two years is automatically repealed — which, as far as I can tell, would eventually eat the amendment rule itself. If any of these should be split into two proposals or rewritten rather than rejected, show me the rewrite; and if the honest answer in some case is this is a decision about who has power, not a decision about drafting, tell me that too, because I'd rather know that than pretend the checklist settled it.
