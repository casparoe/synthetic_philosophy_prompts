I'm a novelist, two messy drafts into a book narrated in close third by a woman who, after brain surgery, has something like pain asymbolia: she can tell you exactly where the pain is, how strong it is on the scale, whether it's burning or stabbing, and she'll insist, quite cheerfully, that she doesnt mind it. My problem isn't the medicine, it's the page. Every time I write her, readers (and my own editor) assume she's repressing, or being brave, or lying to herself, and I can't find the words to make "hurts and doesn't care" read as one plain fact instead of a puzzle. I know the philosophy only secondhand, through a friend who works on the badness of pain, and I want it in my vocabulary rather than hers: focalization, free indirect style, narrative distance, scene versus summary, voice, alignment, what's on the page versus what the reader infers, unreliable narration, and the ordinary craft distinction between rendering a sensation and rendering a character's attitude toward it. For calibration: I'm fluent in all of that stuff, I teach the occasional workshops on it, and I've never taken a philosophy class — so please don't explain the views to me in philosopher-speak first and then translate them. Do the translation directly, and if you need a term of art from your side, define it in one clause.

Here's what I want translated, one at a time. (1) The view that the badness of pain just is its sensory phenomenology, so that if she really is feeling the same burning, something has to give — either her report or the view. (2) The view that pain is more like a perception of the body's being damaged, and the badness is part of what the experience represents, so the "this is bad for me" can come apart from how much she minds. (3) The view that what makes pain bad is that it's the kind of thing you want to stop, so the badness lives in the aversion rather than in the feeling. (4) The view that the badness is a matter of reasons — pain is the thing that gives you a reason to get out of the situation — where the reason is supposed to survive even when the wanting is gone. I've got a device I use as a test, which I call stereophonic narration: I write the same scene twice, once with the sensory channel wide open (the grain of the sensation, precise location, duration, exact adjectives) and the affective channel muted, and once the other way round (almost no sensory detail, but the prose is all recoil and flinching syntax), and I treat whatever I had to change in the second version as the thing the philosophers are pointing at. So for each of the four: what does it look like written my way, which of my tools do you reach for, and where does my stereophonic test help and where does it mislead me?

Then the part I actually care about: the residue. For each view, tell me what translated clean, what you had to bend to fit into my vocabulary, and what has no counterpart on the page at all. My own suspicion is that the normative bit is the homeless part — prose can show indifference beautifully, but "this is bad for her" isn't a narration problem, it's a claim about the world, and I don't think I have a craft word for it. Is that a real limit of my vocabulary, or a sign that one of these views is a thinner difference than it looks? Related: my readers' stubborn assumption that she must be repressing feels like evidence about how ordinary people read reports of pain, and I'd like you to say whether that does any work in the philosophical argument or whether it's just a fact about my readership. If it helps, do the translation on a line or two: give me two versions of her sentence "it burns and I don't mind it," as each view would have me write it, and then tell me which of the four views a reader could even tell apart from the prose alone — I suspect at least two of them sound definately identical on the page, and I want to know whether that tells against the distinction or against me.
