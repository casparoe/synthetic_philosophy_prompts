I'm TAing an intro epistemology course and we did the new riddle of induction last week, the grue/bleen emeralds thing. Discussion went exactly the way it always goes: half the class decided the whole puzzle is just ordinary skepticism about the future wearing a fake nose, and the other half said Goodman already answered this, it's entrenchment, next topic. I want to build a case for section on Thursday that makes both of those comfortable reactions unavailable, and I'm finding it harder to write then I expected, so I'd like you to build it for me.

What I need is a grue-style case — two hypotheses that fit every observation available in the story perfectly and that disagree about one specific, actually-checkable next instance — built to these specs:

- contemporary and realistic: a lab, a warehouse, a fishery, an auction house, a hospital, whatever. No time machines, demons, or perfect predictors, and the deciding test has to be something the people in the story can really carry out, not something a thousand years or a billion dollars away.
- no temporal ordering doing any work anywhere. The partition that all the current evidence sits inside can't be observed-before-T / not-observed-before-T, and it shouldn't be temporal in any sneaky way either — split the domain by place of origin, supplier, instrument, batch, site, species. I want to be able to tell my students: there's no past and future in this story, only here and there, and the riddle still fires.
- the gerrymandered predicate has to be the one the people in the story actually use, and it has to be load-bearing for them — something they'd lose money or look stupid without. Real life gives you the template, since plenty of working trade categories come out disjunctive when you translate them into scientific terms. The natural predicate should belong to an outside expert who shows up later. I want this because it blocks the reflex answer that the weird predicate loses on entrenchment.
- there should be a real decision hanging on it, with money or safety on the line, so nobody can walk out saying the disagreement is merely verbal.

Then do the work on it: state both hypotheses in full and show the evidence fits each; say what the entrenchment reply now has to say, given that familiarity points toward the gerrymandered predicate — and be honest with me about whether used constantly is even the same thing as entrenched in Goodman's sense, since entrenchment is supposed to track actual successful projections, not just frequency of use; say what a simplicity or shortest-description approach says; say what someone who thinks natural kinds are metaphysically primitive says; and tell me which of the three handles your case best and which worst. Finally: which single feature of your scenario is doing the work, does the case collapse if I delete that feature, how would someone who thinks there's no fact of the matter here attack it, and — the bit I actually care about — is this just Goodman's emeralds with the serial numbers filed off, or does taking time out of the picture change anything philosophically? If it does, say what; if it doesn't, just say so.
