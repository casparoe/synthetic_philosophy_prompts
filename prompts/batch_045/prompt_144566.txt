I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background; I ship ML systems for a living. Over the last couple of years I've drifted into a position I now find myself defending in internal design reviews: that Solomonoff-style induction is the correct formal statement of Occam's razor, and that this is, at bottom, why simple hypotheses generalize — so that the working rule we actually apply, prefer the hypothesis with the shorter description, is principled rather than taste. As I understand it, there is a single prior over computable environments that dominates every computable rival up to a constant depending only on that rival, and whose total expected prediction error is bounded, so a predictor built on it cannot be beaten in the long run by anything anyone could implement.

Two things make this more than armchair for me. First, I've bet engineering effort on a corollary: if description length is what matters, a crude off-the-shelf compressor should be a usable stand-in for the ideal — and in our internal tests, a nearest-neighbour classifier built on compression length has held up surprisingly well against a fine-tuned embedding model on some text-classification tasks, at a small fraction of the cost. Second, I lean on the simplicity story to explain something I otherwise can't: that heavily overparameterized networks, which can fit randomly relabelled training data perfectly, still generalize on real data. My gloss is that SGD isn't finding shortest programs but is biased toward simple functions in a way that approximates the same result.

What I want is the strongest case that I'm wrong, and an honest map of how exposed I actually am. Please write it in the following sections:

- **Bottom line** — two or three sentences stating how much of my position survives contact with the best objections, and which part is in the most trouble.
- **What I'm actually committed to** — about 120 words separating the load-bearing claims from the decoration, so I know what I'd have to give up.
- **What would be decisive** — about 300 words: observations, arguments, or formal results that should move me, ranked fatal to merely serious, including results that already exist. In particular: are there computable environments where the universal predictor does badly for long enough that the convergence guarantee is worthless at any scale I care about? And is the variant that charges for computation time as well as description length a repair of the same theory or a different theory wearing its clothes?
- **What would merely be awkward** — about 150 words: things critics cite that don't really bite.
- **What defenders concede versus dismiss** — about 200 words: which objections a sympathetic theorist would grant as counting against the view, and which they'd call a category error. I especially want the dependence on the choice of reference machine or description language treated honestly, given that the constants involved do not wash out at the data scales I work at.
- **My two bets** — about 150 words: does the theory predict that a compression-length classifier should work, or am I reading that into it after the fact? And is my SGD gloss something the view entails, or could an unrelated bias toward smooth or local functions explain the same evidence equally well? If there's a measurement I could make on our own training logs within a week that would discriminate between those explanations, name it.
- **Is the view insulated?** — about 120 words: between incomputability, unspecifiable constants, and the fact that nobody can run it, has this been qualified into something no evidence could touch? If so, say plainly whether that is a defect in a claim of this kind or a reasonable cost of aiming at a universal guarantee.
