Hey — I'm a distributed systems PhD student (CRDTs, local-first replication, that whole world) and I got dragged into a reading group with some political philosophers, which has gone about as well as you'd expect. Last week we did Sen's Paretian liberal result: two people, one copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover, Prude decisive over whether he reads it vs nobody reads it, Lewd likewise over his own pair, Pareto on the remaining pair, and the social preference comes out cyclic. I know it at roughly that level and essentially nowhere beyond it — I also have a foggy memory that someone in the 70s argued you can get out of it by modelling rights as control over a game form rather than as unconditional pairwise decisiveness, but I can't place the reference and I might have dreamt it.

What I actually want is a translation into the vocabulary I think in, as faithful as you can make it: a global state as a replicated object; each person as a node with write access to its own key; a preference ordering as that node's total order over global states; the social decision function as a deterministic merge/resolution policy that takes a profile of local orderings and returns a global ranking; unrestricted domain as that policy being total over arbitrary concurrent inputs; Pareto as never ranking anything above an alternative every node prefers to it; and so on, filling in whatever I've left out (acyclicity/convergence, whether the output is a ranking or just a winner, liveness vs safety). Then the residue report, which is the part I care about: what translated cleanly, what you had to fudge to get it in, and what has no expression in that vocabulary at all. Two things I suspect are where the action is, but don't take my word for it: (i) in my world a node having preferences over other nodes' keys is basically an anti-pattern — access control exists precisely to prevent that — while the classic three-alternative cycle only gets going because Prude has an opinion about what Lewd reads, so is the theorem saying anything about systems built the way I'd build them, or is it a theorem about meddlesomeness? (ii) is a right here a write permission (you get to set the value) or an invariant no merge is allowed to violate (a safety property)? Those feel like different things and I doubt Sen's formalisation captures both; if the second is closer to what people mean, does the impossibility even touch it?

And then the bit I'd actually get use out of: I've been trying to dodge the cycle by weakening minimal liberalism instead of Pareto, since in the app I'm building the jurisdiction really is local. Attempt one — give each node decisiveness only over pairs of global states that differ solely in its own coordinate. I definately thought at first that this kills the cycle; I now think it doesn't, because the three-alternative example already satisfies it — am I right, and can you say why in a line? Attempt two, which I'm much less sure about: require different nodes' private spheres to be disjoint as sets of alternatives. I have a scribbled construction that rebuilds the cycle anyway using nine global states (three possible acts each, so the two private pairs can be kept apart), but I can't tell whether I've smuggled something illegitimate into it. Can you write that construction out explicitly and check it, or explain what condition I'd actually need to block the cycle? And then the punchline question for a blog post I'll probably never write: if some weakening like this does work, is it a genuine answer to the paradox, or does it only look like one because rights in my vocabulary were never what Sen was formalising in the first place?
