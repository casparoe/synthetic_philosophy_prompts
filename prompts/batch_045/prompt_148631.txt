I've been asked to do one of those "where I stand" sessions at the grad workshop next month — twenty minutes on my own package, then half an hour of people taking it apart — and I typed up my commitments on a plane last night. Before I say any of this out loud in front of to many people who would enjoy dismantling it, can you audit the set for me? Here's roughly where I've landed on welfare, aggregation, and the monster material.

(1) Welfarist consequentialism about distributions among people who already exist: whether one outcome is better than another is a function of individual welfare levels and nothing else. No desert, no rights, no equality as an independent ingredient.

(2) I've made my peace with the repugnant conclusion. A vast population of lives barely worth living really is better than a small population of excellent lives, and honestly I don't think it's close.

(3) I take Nozick's utility monster as a reductio of the straight total view — no argument, I just trust the verdict. I've also convinced myself the extreme version is a red herring. Two variants: the "modest monster," who converts resources into welfare only about ten percent more efficiently than the rest of us, gets exactly the same verdict under constant returns, and I find that just as unacceptable; and the "miserable monster," same conversion edge but starting out very badly off, still gets the same verdict from me. So whatever is broken here, it isn't about how extreme or how well off the creature is.

(4) I'm a prioritarian: a fixed gain in welfare matters more the worse off the recipient is. I got here because it looked like the cheapest way to keep aggregating without being indifferent to distribution.

(5) Interpersonal comparisons of welfare are coherent, but in practice we are close to completely ignorant of who is an efficient converter, and I don't think that licenses paralysis. Work out expected welfare on your best evidence and maximize that; don't refuse to aggregate just because the measurment is bad.

(6) Two case verdicts I'm confident about. ICU case: the last dose goes to ten patients who are each much worse off than the eleventh and who would gain 25 units apiece, not to the eleventh patient who would gain 200 — and I use this case to show people I'm not a bare total-maximizer. And the "sink": someone already very well off who converts resources into welfare terribly inefficiently — I have no compunction about giving them nothing and handing everything to people who convert better. That this is structurally the mirror image of the monster does not bother me at all.

So: which of these actually fight each other, which only look like they do, and what's the least damaging revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one tell me which commitment has to give and what it costs me somewhere else in the set. I'd especially like to know whether (4) is doing any of the work I'm implicitly relying on it to do in (3), and whether (5) leaves my rejection of the monster with any practical grip at all. American English please, and keep it concrete — I want an audit of my position, not a tour of the literature.
