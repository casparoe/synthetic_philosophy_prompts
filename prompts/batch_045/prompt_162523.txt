I've got myself into a corner defending a version of the Keynes uncertainty thing in our reading group, and I want to know how much trouble I'm actually in: what would count against what I say?

The view, as sharply as I can put it. In the 1921 Treatise on Probability Keynes isn't just making the boring point that some probabilities are hard to estimate. He's saying something structural: probability is a logical relation between evidence and a conclusion, and it's a separate quantity from the weight of the argument — roughly, how much relevant knowledge you have versus how much relevant ignorance. The two come apart, because new evidence can lower the probability of a conclusion while always raising its weight. My addition, which is the bit where I've gone out on a limb, is that this does real decision-theoretic work: a thin-weight 70% and a thick-weight 70% are not the same thing to act on, and expected-utility maximisation has no slot for the difference, so it quietly treats them as identical. And I claim it cashes out empirically too — that it's what "animal spirits" is a placeholder for, and that risk aversion plus asymmetric information plus adjustment costs don't get you to cash hoarding, investment freezes and herding on their own.

So: what would defeat this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and split into the things I'd have to take seriously versus the things a Keynesian would just wave off. Does weight collapse into something Bayesianism already has — a second-order probability, or a set of priors in an imprecise-probability framework — in which case have I just invented vocabulary for a special case of the view I claim to be rejecting, or is that collapse itself contested? Does treating probability as a logical relation commit me to the claim that two people with the same evidence must assign the same probability, an Aumann-flavoured commitment that persistent real disagreement makes look silly, and is weight my escape hatch or a dodge? If probabilities are often only partially ordered — Keynes's own example is that on some occasions it's neither more likely than not nor less likely nor as likely as not that it rains, so it's rational to let caprice settle the umbrella question — then what does my view actually tell anyone to do in a concrete case, and is "then use maximin, or minimise regret" an answer or a bail-out? And empirically: if a rival explains hoarding, freezes and herding without weight, how much does that hurt me given that I said weight was doing the explaining?

Two more things. I'd personally be moved most by a worked case where a weight-sensitive rule and a Bayesian with one wide-but-precise prior give genuinely different advice, or by an argument that they provably never can — argue whichever you think is true. And be willing to tell me the whole thing has been qualified into unfalsifiability, plus whether that's fatal or fine for a claim of this kind. Write it as plain continuous prose, no headings, bullets, bold or tables, like an email — I read this stuff on my phone and formatting turns into soup.
