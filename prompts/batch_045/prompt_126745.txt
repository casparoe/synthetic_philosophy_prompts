I am in the third year of a PhD in philosophy, writing on the axiology of population size, and I need to characterise my supervisor's view accurately enough to attribute it to her in a chapter. Over the last two terms she has given me off-the-cuff rulings on a series of cases, and before I build the chapter around them I want to know what she will say about the case I am about to put to her, and how much of her view her own verdicts actually pin down.

I will use the following convention. Welfare is measured on a scale on which 0 is the neutral point — a life neither worth living nor worth having — 100 is the best life a person could have, and negative numbers are lives not worth living. A life at 40 is decent but unremarkable; a life at 60 is good; a life at 1 is barely worth living. I write n@w for a population of n people each at welfare level w. Where I say that one population contains "a further" group, I mean that the original people exist with the same welfare under both options and the further people exist only under one of them. Her verdicts so far:

(1) 1,000@100 versus 1,000@100 together with a further 2,000@60. The larger population is better.
(2) 1,000@100 versus 1,000@100 together with a further 2,000@30. The smaller population is better. I pressed her on this: she said the addition makes the outcome worse, not merely that it fails to improve it.
(3) 1,000@100 versus 1,000,000,000@40. The larger population is better, and by a wide margin.
(4) 1,000@20 together with 1,000@80 versus 2,000@50. The two are exactly equally good.
(5) 1,000@100 versus 1,000@100 together with a further 1,000@−10. The smaller population is better.

Two features of this list strike me as important. The first is that it excludes the two simplest comparisons I can think of: (1) is hard to reconcile with ranking populations by average welfare, and (2) is hard to reconcile with ranking them by total welfare. The second is that in every case so far in which the number of people differs, one of the two populations has been 1,000@100. That is what worries me, because my own reconstruction and a colleague's reconstruction seem to differ only in cases where the anchor is somewhere other than 100.

My reconstruction: she is measuring each life by how far it stands above some fixed level and adding those surpluses up, with the fixed level lying somewhere above 30 and below 60. My colleague's reconstruction: she is weighting total welfare by something like a power of population size, so that numbers count but with diminishing returns. On the verdicts above I cannot tell these apart, and I do not trust my own arithmetic enough to construct the separating case reliably.

The case I want to put to her is this. Under policy I, 10,000 people exist at welfare 40. Under policy II, those same 10,000 people exist at welfare 40 and a further 10,000 people exist at welfare 28. Every life in question is worth living; the second policy creates additional people whose lives are decent but well below those of the people already there, and leaves everyone else exactly as they were. Is the second outcome better than the first?

What I am asking for, concretely:

- Which rules, stated precisely enough that I can compute with them, are consistent with all of (1) through (5)? Please give each as a formula, and give the range of parameter values that her verdicts permit for it. If her verdicts leave a rule's parameters only loosely constrained, say where the loose constraints bite.
- What does each candidate rule say about my case, and is any of those verdicts sensitive to where the rule's parameters sit inside the permitted range? I care about robustness here: I would rather know that a rule says "the second outcome is better" for every setting her track record allows than that it says so for most settings.
- Your best guess at her actual verdict, with the reason for preferring that rule over the others that fit her record equally well — including any reason that comes from which rule is more likely to be something a person would arrive at informally, rather than from fit alone.
- I have one supervision left before I write this up. If more than one rule fits her verdicts and agrees with the others about my case, give me the single further comparison that would do the most work in telling them apart, and say what she would have to say about it for each rule to be confirmed.

Two last things that may bear on this. She has told me more than once that she has never felt the force of the repugnant conclusion as an objection; yet her ruling in (3) has, as far as I can see, the same shape as the repugnant comparison, merely at a higher welfare level than the standard one. I would like to know whether that shows she has only relocated the problem rather than avoided it, and whether it counts for or against either of the two reconstructions above. And I should say that I am not after a label for her view; a name would be useful to me only insofar as it comes with a formula I can apply and a statement of where the view's edges are.
