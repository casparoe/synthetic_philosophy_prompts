I'm in a graduate seminar on rational choice and decision theory, and this week we covered Good's theorem: the result that for an expected-utility maximizer who updates by conditionalization, the expected value of costless information is never negative, because any act available before the signal arrives is still available afterwards, and one can simply resolve to act as one would have acted without it. Two people in the seminar stayed behind and got into an argument about whether that result is substantive or close to empty. I've tried to reconstruct the exchange faithfully below. I have no stake in who wins, and I'd rather you work the argument out than send me to readings, since I can't chase references this week.

1. Marcus: The proof is one line — for each possible signal, take the act that maximizes conditional expected utility, and observe that the unconditional optimum is still in the menu in every cell, so the expected value with the signal is at least the value without it. But the second half of that is just the remark that you are free to walk away from something free. Nothing about inquiry, evidence, or science follows. The floor is zero by construction and the theorem never says the value is positive. Worse, it looks unfalsifiable: any claimed counterexample gets met with the reply that you've quietly violated a background assumption.

2. Lena: Two things. First, the floor is exactly what makes the economics of experimentation work: since gross value is nonnegative, the only question about any experiment is whether its cost exceeds its gross expected value, and the theorem decomposes where the value comes from — strictly positive just in case there is positive probability of a signal that would change what you do. That's a mechanism, not a floor. Second, it is falsifiable: it fails for agents who update non-Bayesianly (someone who will treat a weak signal as conclusive does worse by looking), and for agents whose utility depends on what they know. So it's a coherence constraint on a package — expected utility, conditioning, costlessness, and purity of the signal — not a tautology.

3. Marcus: The still-in-the-menu premise is much stronger than it looks, and routinely false. Case one, mandated disclosure: I'm offered a free genetic test for a condition that affects what I'd pay for the insurance I was about to buy, and I'm legally required to disclose the result if I take it. The act I had planned — buy at the pooled rate — no longer exists after I learn. Case two, knowing is itself an outcome: for an untreatable condition, having it and knowing you have it differs in utility from having it and not knowing, and you cannot select the latter once you've been tested. In both, the signal isn't merely informative: conditional on the state, whether you receive it depends on what you do, or receiving it changes the outcome space. So the theorem is restricted to signals that are pure, and the interesting real cases of should I find out generally aren't.

4. Lena: Granted — but the right conclusion is about modelling, not refutation. Enlarge the state space so that testing carries disclosure as a consequence and so that knowing is part of the outcome, and the inequality is restored over the enlarged space. Notice what that costs me, though. Over an enlarged space the theorem holds for any joint distribution you like, which turns it into a claim about your model rather than about the world. Once anything awkward can be relocated into the state space, no case can test it. So the vacuity worry comes back in a sharper form: the result is either substantive and scope-limited, or fully general and untestable. I'd take the scope-limited version.

5. Marcus: Here is a case that is neither a mis-modelling nor a failure of consequentialism, and in which acting as you would have is perfectly available: a calibration oracle. It tells you nothing about which hypothesis is true. It tells you that your credence function is miscalibrated — that your ninety-percent intervals contain the truth sixty percent of the time, that you should shrink every log-odds by a fixed factor. There is no proposition here to conditionalize on, and no way to act as you would have acted, since what is being revised is the probability function you use in drawing up every plan. If Good's result cannot even be stated for this signal, it isn't a general result about the value of information; it is a result about the value of partitions of a possibility space you already possess.

6. Lena: It can be stated: introduce a variable ranging over which recalibration of your credences is the correct one, put a prior over its values, and treat the oracle's report as a likelihood over them. The theorem applies again and the value is nonnegative. But the absorption exposes the real presupposition: the theorem needs a prior over everything the signal might bear on. And its silence is precisely where the action is — a signal that reveals that a possibility you had not represented at all is real, so that you expand your hypothesis space rather than reweight within it. Good's theorem says nothing about that, and that is the case the philosophy of science actually cares about.

7. Marcus: Then we agree more than it looked. The theorem is diagnostic, not justificatory. If free information looks worthless or harmful to you, it tells you where to look: cost, impurity of the signal, preferences that aren't consequentialist, updating that isn't Bayesian, or a prior over an impoverished space. It keeps the books. It doesn't tell you to open them.

8. Lena: Agreed, with one asymmetry worth keeping. The diagnostic only ever tells you that one of the assumptions failed; it never licenses the conclusion that information itself is bad. That residual content is small but it isn't nothing.

Please adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the eight moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification, and say who is right about what overall. I specifically want your verdict on three things. First, whether the impurity cases in move 3 are genuine counterexamples to Good's theorem or changes of subject, and whether Lena's reply in move 4 concedes more than she admits. Second, whether the calibration-oracle case in move 5 can really be absorbed by enlarging the prior as Lena proposes in move 6, and what that enlargement costs. Third, whether there is any reading of the theorem on which it is genuinely substantive rather than either trivial or heavily conditioned — and if there isn't, what its status is. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing.
