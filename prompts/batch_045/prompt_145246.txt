I work in insurance claims, not academia, but I got sort of obsessed with this question of what we're allowed to do with what people say about their own experience, after a medication reaction a few years back left me saying things to my doctor that I'm pretty sure weren't quite true. So I've been working my way through a researcher's blog — he's a self-described heterophenomenologist, and the way I understand his method is this: you collect everything a subject says or writes or signs about what it's like for them, you treat it like a text, kind of like a novel, and you build up the world those reports describe. But you never treat the reports as the last word about what's actually going on in there. The reports are data, not authority. (I'm not sure I've got that right, so correct me if I've mangled it.)

What I've been doing is tracking his rulings on what counts as data in the first place, because he's ruled on a bunch of cases and they don't all feel like they come from one rule.

His verdicts so far, as best I can list them:

- A subject in a normal lab task says "I saw a brief flash on the left." Counts.
- A subject in one of those studies where she's shown a photo she didn't actually pick, and she confidently explains why she picked it. Counts — he said confabulation is still data, it just describes a world that didn't happen.
- A patient who insists "I saw nothing" while reliably picking out the target anyway. The denial counts, and so does the picking, and he said the denial doesn't settle whether there was any experience.
- A patient who insists she just moved her paralyzed arm. Counts, same reason.
- An actor in a film reading the line "I feel a stabbing pain in my left shoulder." He said no, that isn't data about the actor. It's a text, but it's the writer's fiction, not her report.
- A locked-in patient spelling out answers letter by letter with eye movements, including things like "I'm in pain." Counts — he said the medium doesn't matter, it's still her saying it.
- A dog yelping and limping. He said no, there's no heterophenomenology for the dog: no reports, no text, nothing to build a world out of. He added that the yelp is obviously still behavior you can study some other way.
- Someone describing a psychedelic experience in detail years later. Counts, though he said he'd treat it as a different grade of data than something said in the moment.
