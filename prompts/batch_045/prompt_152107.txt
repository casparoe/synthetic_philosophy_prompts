Take an eighteenth-century object and read it through a twenty-first-century distinction, and tell me in the end which of the two comes off worse.

The object is the chess-playing automaton that Wolfgang von Kempelen built and first displayed at the Habsburg court in 1770, and that Johann Nepomuk Mälzel later bought and toured through Europe and America: a life-sized, turbaned figure seated behind a cabinet with a chessboard on top, which beat most of those who played it — Napoleon Bonaparte and Benjamin Franklin among them — and which was exhibited for more than eighty years before it was destroyed in a fire in 1854 and described in print three years later by the son of its last owner. The facts I want on the table are these: the pieces were moved by a concealed human chess master working by candlelight, using magnets, levers and a sliding seat; the cabinet was engineered so that every door could be thrown open in front of an audience without exposing him, with dummy clockwork sliding into place as he moved; Kempelen also set a small wooden box on the cabinet during play and appeared to consult it; the machine was widely suspected of being a hoax throughout its career but was never accurately described while it still existed; Edgar Allan Poe published an essay arguing that a man was hidden inside it; and one contemporary account has a spectator retreating to a window seat as far from the cabinet as she could get, convinced that a spirit had taken up residence in the machine.

The distinction is Dennett's, from his late writing on artificial intelligence: not between machines that think and machines that do not, but between real people and counterfeit people, modelled on counterfeit money. A good counterfeit bill is not worthless — it looks right, it passes, it is a remarkable artifact — but it is not money, and the danger it poses is not that it is junk. It is that it circulates as money, that it can be made cheaply and in volume and so drive out the genuine currency, and that it corrupts the practices of trust that depend on our being able to tell the two apart. The defect, on this account, is not behavioural: a perfect counterfeit is by hypothesis indistinguishable from the real thing under any test you can run on it, and what separates them is provenance — where the thing came from, what produced it, what it is licensed to stand in for. The corresponding worry about AI is not that systems will become conscious but that they will become good enough at the surface to be accepted in roles — friend, lover, mourner, confidant, teacher — where what we are owed is the presence of an actual person.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not a general essay, and it has to run in both directions. First, say what the counterfeit is supposed to be here: the figure alone, the machine-plus-operator composite, or the performance as a whole. Note the awkwardness that the composite contains a real person, so that the Turk may be less a counterfeit person than a genuine person disguised as a mindless machine, the mirror image of Dennett's case — and say whether the category still does any work, and on what, if so. Second, say what the frame gets right: the deliberate engineering of inspectability, doors built to be opened and dummy gearing to be seen through, looks like exactly the counterfeiter's art of surviving scrutiny, and the falsification of provenance was part of the performance rather than an accident of it; the chess itself, on this reading, was beside the point. Third, say what it misdescribes or cannot reach: that the audience's error was about where agency was located rather than about whether mind was present, that playing strong chess is not one of the roles whose occupant Dennett thinks must be real, and that the concealed operators — real, exploited, and for the most part unnamed — are people to whom the framework seems to have nothing to say. Where the theory is silent, say it is silent rather than making it speak.

Then turn it around. Say what the theory implies the participants should have done: Kempelen and Mälzel as makers of counterfeits, the spectators as parties to an exchange whose currency was admiration and credence, and the debunkers most of all — does a view on which the distinction matters enormously but no behavioural test settles it give them any reason to expose the machine, or does it undercut the exposure, given that what the machine delivered, a hard game of chess and a genuine wonder, was never fake? Consider also the economics: Dennett's counterfeits are dangerous partly because they are cheap and numerous, whereas the Turk was singular, expensive and hand-built, and took a concealed master to operate — does eighty years of successful deception make it a dangerous counterfeit, or does the case show that the harm needs volume, and that an object like this is closer to a curiosity? And then give me a verdict on the theory itself, choosing between three readings and defending your choice against the other two: that the episode corroborates it at a deeper level, because what does the damage is not absence of mind but the unlocatable source of behaviour; that it is an anomaly for it, because here there was a person inside and the harm is plainly ordinary fraud, leaving the special machinery about mind and trust idle; or that it is a case the category never anticipated, and that the real moral is a more general one about a mismatch between the stance we adopt and the true source of the behaviour we are responding to, with Dennett's counterfeit people only one species of it. Finally, tell me whether a plainer frame — fraud and deception, or the intentional stance without the moral — handles the case better, and whether any part of your analysis rests on a detail of the Turk's history that is genuinely contested.

Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter to someone who knows both the machine and the argument: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no citations.
