hi — I write novels (three published, none of them bestsellers; the last one is about a woman in 1870s provincial Russia who mostly lives inside other people's letters). I've been using one of the big text models for about a year, mostly to generate deliberately bad first drafts for me to react against, and I've got myself into a loop about whether it understands anything. I'd like an audit of my own position rather than an essay on the topic. Two ground rules: I can't get at paywalled papers right now, so please don't send me to any reading or name-drop anyone's article — just argue it out — and could you write the whole thing in American English (publisher's house style, long story).

Here's where I think I've landed. Sorry, it's a long list.

1. Understanding needs stakes. Nothing understands a thing unless it's the sort of thing for which things can go badly — for which the thing can matter. This is why I want to say my character doesn't really understand that her marriage is over until the letter actually arrives; before that the words are just noise in her head.
2. Substrate doesn't matter. Get the organization right and it understands, whether it's neurons or silicon or clockwork. I want no part of any argument that turns on what the stuff is made of.
3. The question isn't merely verbal. "Do they understand or not?" has a real answer, and I think people who answer "it depends what you mean by understand" are stalling.
4. I'm a behaviorist about characters. There's no inner light and nothing behind the text. Everything a reader gets about whether someone understands is what they say, what they notice, and what they do next. My working test while drafting: a character understands X only if I can write a scene where X turning out otherwise would surprise them, and the surprise feels earned. If I can't find the surprise, they don't have it. (I notice the model does seem to get surprised, in the sense that some continuations are wildly unexpected to it.)
5. Text is a fine route to understanding. I've never been to 1870s Russia, everyone who was there is dead, and whatever understanding I have came out of letters, diaries and novels. Testimony got me there.
6. A few years back I wrote a scene with a woman recovering from a stroke: she produces long fluent grammatical sentences, understands almost none of what's said to her, and can't follow a simple instruction. Readers tell me it's the scariest thing in the book. I think that scene is evidence about something.
7. I say "the model knows what a subpoena is" all the time and would never say "the model understands what it's like to be served one," and I don't think I'm being careless.
8. I also want "it's only predicting the next word" to be a criticism, but I keep thinking that predicting the next word this well must involve having internalized an awful lot of structure — and structure-grasping is most of what I mean by understanding.

My editor says the woman in 6 and the model are the same case and I should stop feeling superior to it. I don't know whether she's right. What I want: which of these actually fight with which, ranked by severity, and for each real fight, what I'd have to give up and what it would cost me. Also flag the one or two that only look like they clash and say why they don't. Then tell me the single revision that would clean up the most. I genuinely don't know which one I'd drop — that's rather the point of asking. And please don't open with "it depends what you mean by understand"; see 3.
