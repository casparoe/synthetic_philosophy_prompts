I write software for a living and have no formal philosophy background. I keep catching myself assuming that the mind is to the brain roughly as a running program is to the machine it runs on: same program, different hardware, same mental life. I want a thought experiment engineered to break exactly that assumption, for a talk I am giving to other programmers, and I want it built to specifications rather than borrowed from the literature.

Build me a case involving two systems such that:

- They are functionally identical in the only sense a working engineer would care about: for every input either could receive over its whole life, it produces the same output, passes through the same internal state transitions, and reports the same things about its own experience.
- The case nonetheless generates the intuition that they differ mentally: same functional organization, but different mental states, or mentality present on one side and absent on the other.
- It does not get that intuition from any of the usual moves. The second system genuinely has the functional organization; it is not a precomputed table of responses; no one gets to say it is merely shuffling symbols and nothing in it understands anything; and the trick is not environmental or historical, so no switched surroundings and no suspicious causal history. No exotic physics, no panpsychism, no systems the size of a country.
- The difference between the two systems should be one that functionalism itself admits is real but declares irrelevant, so the case is not simply denying functionalism from the outside.
- Ideally the case leaves multiple realizability looking plausible in general, while making the functionalist verdict about this particular pair look wrong.
- Keep it realistic and near-future, one paragraph, nothing a working engineer could not imagine being built.

Then show the design works. State what machine-state functionalism says about the pair and why it says it; state what someone who thinks the physical realization itself matters says and why; name the single feature of the case doing the work; show what happens to the intuition if that feature is removed, and in particular whether the case then quietly collapses back into one of the excluded moves above; and give the strongest reply available to a functionalist who refuses to give up the software/hardware picture.

Finish with a final line of the form \answer{...} naming, in at most eight words, the one feature of the case that carries the argument.
