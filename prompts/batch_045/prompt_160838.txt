I've been arguing with a friend about animal minds for a couple of months now — started over beers, has since migrated into a group chat with my brother, who works in ML and keeps trying to steer every example toward neural nets, which I don't want. What I've noticed is that I have firm gut verdicts on individual cases and no idea what principle they add up to. Somewhere in the back of my mind there's a taxonomy I half remember from a book I read years ago — creatures sorted into Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and a fourth kind named after some psychologist whose name I've completely lost — but I don't trust the memory, and I don't want the four labels handed back to me as the answer. My own stab, which I can't make do any work, is that it matters whether a creature can be wrong in advance of getting hurt, and whether the space of things it can try out was built by it or built for it.

Here are the cases. The digger wasp that starts its whole burrow routine over from the top if you move the cricket while it's inside surveying (I've been told the anecdote is disputed, but assume it's true): no mind worth the name, just a program tuned by selection on its ancestors. A rat learning to press a lever for food: real learning, but dumb — shaped by consequences after the fact, nothing foreseen. A bird that eats one foul-tasting butterfly and never touches that colour pattern again: same verdict, one trial instead of forty, still not foresight. A crow that takes a straight bit of wire, bends it into a hook, and fishes a grub out of a tube, apparently orienting itself first: something has changed here, it looks like the move got tried out somewhere internal before it got tried out in the world. A chimp that stacks crates to reach a banana: same verdict as the crow. A dog that watches where a human points and goes to the right bowl: I flip-flop constantly — it isn't hard-wired and it isn't consequence-shaped in the rat's way, but I resist crediting it with the crow's foresight, and I can't say what the difference is supposed to be. A honeybee swarm settling on a nest site out of its scouts' dances: I want to say something is going on at the level of the swarm, and I also want to say that no individual bee has it and that the swarm isn't a creature for these purposes, which I suspect is cowardice. A whale calf that learns its pod's song from its elders and sings only that dialect for life: I count this as a different thing again, not cleverer individually, but the behaviour piles up across generations. And here's the case I think catches me out: a rat that learns in one trial to avoid a food that made it sick feels like less of a mind to me than a pigeon pecking a key for grain over fifty slow trials, even though the rat's learning is faster, more durable and more useful. My reason is that the rat came wired to connect nausea with food in just that way, so the cleverness was done by selection rather than by the rat — but when I say it out loud it sounds like cheating, since the two animals differ only in how their brains got to be that way, and I claimed earlier to care about what the creature can do in its lifetime.

So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? I'd like it stated precisely enough that I can see what it delivers on cases I haven't listed — a creature that learns a trick and whose descendants many generations later are born doing it without ever learning it; a person who can do a sum out loud but not silently; a system trained on everything humans have written that solves problems it has never seen by recombining patterns and never suffers anything at all. Tell me which clause each of my verdicts is what motivated, where your principle has to overturn me, and whether it ends up being a threshold on one dimension or several things at once. And if it doesn't line up with those four -ian labels, say which of them it splits or collapses. Please write it as straight prose — no headings, bullets, bold or tables, I'll be reading it on my phone.
