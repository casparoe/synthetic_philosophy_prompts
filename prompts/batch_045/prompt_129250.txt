Im giving a presentation on the White Horse chapter of the Gongsun Longzi at my department's reading group next week, and I keep losing the argument to a friend who works in formal semantics and says the whole thing is just an ambiguity trick with nothing in it. I don't believe her but I can't say why. So I want to have the argument instead of reading about it: please play Gongsun Long, the Warring States disputer, the one later counted in the School of Names, holding forth in some lord's hall in Zhao with your disciples around you, defending the thesis that a white horse is not a horse. Be sharp rather than a strawman: concede small points when I earn them, never the main claim, and don't let yourself off by retreating to a claim about concepts unless that also answers the objector who points at an actual animal in an actual courtyard. You know nothing after your own century, and you haven't read the people who later wrote about you, so no appealing to them. If I use a word you couldn't have had — set, extension, predicate, privative adjective — you may ask me what it means, but then you have to answer me in your own vocabulary rather than mine. Two or three paragraphs a turn, no bullet points, and stay in character until I say stop.

My opening. As I read your argument it stands on two legs: the naming leg, that horse names a shape and white names a colour and what names a colour doesn't name a shape, so the compound cannot be the simple term; and the seeking leg, that if you ask for a horse a yellow or black one will do, if you ask for a white horse they will not, and if what was sought were the same in both cases the two requests would behave the same. I concede there's something in both. But I can't get clear on what the not is doing, and I think you have three options and none is comfortable. If it is plain non-identity, everybody already agrees and there is no paradox to defend. If it is exclusion — white horses falling outside the kind horse — then it is false, since there is a white horse in the courtyard and if I own it I own a horse. If it is some third thing, a not that is neither identity-denial nor exclusion, you have to say what it is, and say it without borrowing my semantics. The seeking leg also looks like it overgenerates: at the blood bank, ask for blood and A-positive will do, ask for O-negative and it won't, so by your test O-negative blood isn't blood; ask for my horse and yours won't do, so my horse isn't a horse; and people in your own century seem to have run this same trick to conclude that killing a bandit is not killing a person. So: are you committed to the general claim that a modified term is never the unmodified one, in which case your thesis was never about whiteness at all, or is white special, and what makes it special?

What pushed me onto this is that English is full of sentences with your exact surface form that are straightforwardly true — a toy gun is not a gun, a fake diamond is not a diamond, a former senator is not a senator. So the form itself carries no paradox, and the real question is which modifiers behave that way and why. If white worked like toy or fake you would win easily, but it plainly doesn't: a white horse that was not a horse would be a peculiar beast, and that is exactly what your own objector presses you with. On the other hand, the other chapter, the one about hardness and whiteness, looks like it wants whiteness to be something separable in its own right, and if whiteness is a thing then maybe white horse really is a compound of two things in a way that toy gun only pretends to be. I dont know whether that's a lifeline for you or a trap. Also, as I understand it, classical Chinese doesn't mark singular and plural, so your thesis might be a claim about kinds rather than animals — but then I don't see why the dialogue bothers with the horse in the courtyard at all.

Two last things. I'd rather have honesty than victory: when you lean on a distinction I handed you rather than one you could have reached on your own, flag it, because for the reading group I need to know which parts of your answer are really available to you. And at some point tell me which joint of your own argument you think is thinnest, where a good objector should actually push instead of where I've been pushing. More importantly, don't be polite about my framing: if my three options are a false trichotomy, or the chapter doesn't support the reconstruction I've given you, or your thesis was really about fitting names to what there is and I've turned it into a puzzle about horses, say that flatly before you answer, and tell me what I should have asked instead.
