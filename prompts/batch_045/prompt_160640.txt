I work as a senior planner at a statutory nature conservation body, and my team screens around forty habitat-change consultations a year: drainage of fen for housing, commercial conifer planting on upland rough grazing, agri-environment conversions of arable to wet grassland, and the offset and net-gain packages that come attached to them. Biodiversity duties we can handle; there are established metrics and agreed methods. What we have no framework for is the objection that several consultees now raise in almost every case: whether the change makes things better or worse for the animals that live on the land, considered as individuals whose lives can go well or badly rather than as units of a population. We are not required to answer that question, but we have started answering it badly and inconsistently, and I would rather have a bad procedure we apply uniformly than ad hoc reactions to whichever objection letter is loudest.

Please write me a decision procedure my team can run on a consultation in about half a day, with no welfare economist and no specialist ecologist in the room: we receive species counts, habitat maps and management prescriptions, not welfare estimates. Build it around the accounting unit of welfare-years per hectare-year — positive or negative valence, summed over every sentient individual that occupies the site during a fixed window — and make it do the following:

1. Fix the counterfactual baseline correctly: not pristine or pre-development habitat, but the trajectory the site would in fact follow if the proposal were refused, including succession, abandonment, continued grazing and climate-driven shifts. The quantity of interest is the difference between two trajectories, not the level of either.
2. Face the replacement question head-on: whether the animals apparently lost to a project are additional deaths and lost births, or whether density-dependent compensation means the population was going to be trimmed to carrying capacity anyway, so that the change mostly rearranges which individuals die and when rather than how many die young. Say what field evidence would settle which regime a given site is in, and what the procedure does when that evidence is absent.
3. Say how life history enters the calculation: the difference between species whose surplus offspring mostly die young and species where most young survive to adulthood, and why one and the same intervention can be clearly net-negative on a site dominated by the first and clearly net-positive on a site dominated by the second.
4. Handle the sign-symmetric case. Habitat improvement adds welfare-years too, so a coherent procedure must be capable of returning refuse for a restoration or rewilding proposal. Frame that as an output of the method rather than a defect in it.
5. Handle leakage: habitat lost on site and created or protected off site, including under statutory net-gain obligations, and state which off-site animals fall inside the accounting boundary and on what principle.
6. Deal honestly with taxa whose valence and sentience are poorly evidenced — soil invertebrates, larval stages, extremely short adult lives — in a way that neither lets that uncertainty swallow the whole analysis nor lets it be waved aside because it is inconvenient.
7. Mark, at every step, whether the question is one of fact and evidence, one of value that must be handed to a named decision-maker or to public consultation, or one of judgment dressed up as procedure.

For each step give the question to be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer at our level of evidence, the condition under which the procedure stops with a no, and the condition under which it hands the question onward. Then run the completed procedure on three proposals of the kind we actually see: draining and filling eight hectares of lowland fen for housing, with a wetland creation scheme two kilometres away; planting native broadleaf woodland on two hundred hectares of upland rough grazing currently grazed at low density; and converting sixty hectares of intensive arable to wet grassland under an agri-environment scheme with grazing resumed. I expect at least one of these to come out the way I will not like, and I would rather have that than a procedure that ratifies what we intended to do regardless.

Close with the limits: the classes of proposal to which this procedure should not be applied at all, and the failure mode you would predict after a year of use.

Please write in British English throughout. End with a single line in the exact form \answer{n}, where n is the number of the three proposals (0, 1, 2 or 3) that your procedure permits on welfare grounds, preceded by one sentence naming the single step that does most of the deciding work.
