Act as a comparative political scientist who has also done serious conceptual work in the philosophy of social science: someone equally at home with measurement validity, essentially contested concepts, and the gap between a concept, a definition, and an indicator. Think the problem through carefully before you write. The context is an argument I had with a friend last night that I could not finish. He claims that democratic backsliding is nothing more than a label people attach to institutional reforms they already dislike, and he made four points I could not fully answer: that every canonical case is disputed, with Hungary, Poland, Turkey and the United States all producing confident and opposite verdicts from different observers; that the concept is applied asymmetrically, so that a governing majority enlarging a supreme court counts as backsliding when conservatives do it but as repair when progressives do it; that there is no canonical institutional design, so there is no baseline level from which a country can be said to have slid; and that when voters elect a government that promised these changes, calling the result erosion is just elitism. I conceded the second and I half-conceded the third. I did not concede the rest, and I would rather settle it by building something that works than by arguing.

Here is the setting in which it has to work. I am one of four coders on a small team that maintains a quarterly country-level monitoring dataset. We screen roughly a hundred institutional changes a year, about fifteen to twenty minutes each. Two coders work each case independently and a senior editor signs off on disagreements. Our codings are published, they feed a donor review process, and they get quoted by journalists, so both false positives and false negatives are costly. Most coders have uneven knowledge of the country in question and English-language summaries only. What I need from you is a decision procedure my team can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a not-backsliding verdict, the point at which it stops with a contested-do-not-code verdict, and the points at which it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts.

The procedure has to confront the following, and I want you to decide how each is handled rather than list options: the baseline problem, including a rule for when disagreement about the baseline is itself outcome-determinative; the prior-state requirement, since a case that was never at a higher level on the dimension in question seems to me to be non-consolidation rather than erosion; the line between changes in the rules of the game and ordinary outputs within them, drawn tightly enough that a policy I dislike is not automatically a constitutional one; a symmetry test that would still disadvantage a reform's enactors were they in opposition, since that is the only guard I can see against the concept turning into a partisan label; a distinction between a reversible tilt and a ratchet, keyed to how hard reversal is and to who controls the referees who adjudicate reversal; an aggregation rule for the salami-slicing problem, where each of several individually defensible changes is minor but the sequence is directional, specifying what counts as one lever of control and over what window changes are pooled; the role of intent, given that we cannot observe it and must not infer it from outcomes we disapprove of; and the two hard families of case, namely reforms that improve one democratic good while degrading another, and reforms enacted with an electoral mandate and a manifesto promise.

Then apply the finished procedure in full to three cases. First, a governing supermajority rewrites the appointment rules for the constitutional court and extends sitting judges terms so that its own appointees hold a majority past the next election. Second, a state legislature moves authority over election administration from a bipartisan commission to a partisan officeholder and tightens mail-ballot rules, citing uniformity and fraud prevention. Third, a stylized composite I want you to be honest about: a newly elected reformist government, whose agenda has been repeatedly blocked by a supreme court packed by the previous majority, expands that court from nine to fifteen seats and fills them at once through an open public selection process, exactly as it promised in its manifesto. My instinct and my team's instinct is that the third case is democratic repair, and I suspect your procedure will tell me it is backsliding. If it does, say so plainly, and then tell me whether the defect is in the procedure or in my instinct. Close by stating which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, what the procedure cannot do, and whether, on your own showing, it answers my friend.
