Hi — I've somehow ended up giving the opening remarks at the first session of a two-day symposium on constructive analysis, thrown for my old supervisor's 70th birthday. She is in the front row. For thirty years she has rung a small brass bell at anyone who used the law of excluded middle out loud, and she will definately do it to me.

The room is roughly a third constructivists who will audit every clause, a third classical analysts and number theorists who mostly think this is a parlour game and who will die slowly if the whole thing turns into in-jokes, and a third her family — her sister, her partner, two grandkids — who know none of it.

So: write me the remarks. Rules she'd enforce, and I'd like you to actually follow them rather than gesture at them:

- No disjunction you can't decide. No "either her programme flourishes or it doesn't," no "the field will or won't remember this," no "her central conjecture is true or false."
- No double-negation elimination. I can't praise her by saying nobody has ever refuted her main claim, therefore it stands. And "her work isn't forgotten" is not a compliment I'm allowed.
- Her one open conjecture has to be left genuinely suspended: not proved, not refuted — but also not "we don't know yet," because she thinks that phrasing smuggles in a determinate answer sitting there waiting to be found. Find some way to leave it open that isn't a hedge and isn't a eulogy for a truth value.
- Every existential claim needs its witness in the same sentence. Not "many of her students went on to do important work" — which one, and what.

And I want one joke in the middle that works for both the constructivists and the classical people, built on the intermediate value theorem: constructively you can't in general hand over the exact point where a continuous function crosses zero, only arbitrarily good approximations to it. Something about her and approximations would land well, since she spent her life being annoyingly precise about what exactly you'd handed over.

Under 500 words. Prose only — no bullet points, no bracketed stage directions, no footnotes, and no winking asides that explain the rules or the joke to the room.
