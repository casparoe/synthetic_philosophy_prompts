I'm in the final year of a PhD in economics — my own work is on dynamic games under incomplete information, and I've spent a lot of time on costly signalling and on credibility and commitment problems — and I've recently been reading the science-fiction novel that gives the dark forest hypothesis its name, i.e. the suggestion that the reason we see no evidence of extraterrestrial civilisations is that every civilisation's optimal policy is to stay silent and to destroy anyone it detects before they can destroy it. I keep being told that this is just the security dilemma, or a Hobbesian state of nature in space; that comparison is what made me curious, not the thing I want handed back to me. What I want is a translation into the vocabulary I actually work in, at the level of detail where I could sit down and try to write a model: type spaces and priors, higher-order beliefs and common knowledge, cheap talk versus costly signalling, separating and pooling equilibria, credibility and commitment devices, off-path beliefs and refinements, first-mover advantage, and the difference between an equilibrium explanation and a dynamic/selection explanation. I'm fluent enough in all of that that you can use notation where it helps, though I'd like the prose to carry the argument on its own.

For concreteness, here is how I currently reconstruct the argument, so you can tell me if I've got it wrong before translating it: there are two background postulates — that survival is any civilisation's first need, and that civilisations tend to expand far faster than the fixed resource base they can draw on — and two mechanisms that do the real work. The first is what the book calls a chain of suspicion: I cannot tell whether you are hostile, I cannot tell whether you believe I am hostile or whether you believe that I believe you are hostile, and so on, and the distances involved make communication far too slow to settle any of it, so ignorance is resolved in favour of pre-emption. The second is the possibility of discontinuous capability jumps: a civilisation that is harmless today may be an existential threat within a few centuries, so the option value of waiting is low and the value of striking first is high. Together these are supposed to yield total concealment as optimal policy, and to explain the observed silence. If you think this reconstruction is itself a distortion, say so and correct it as part of the translation.

The reason I'm asking rather than doing it myself is that I tried and got stuck in two places, and I suspect they are the interesting part. First, when I try to write it as an ordinary Bayesian game, the mechanism seems to live in exactly the assumptions the framework buys its tractability from: I need uncertainty about others' dispositions that never gets resolved by common knowledge of rationality, and I need a situation where a common prior over a commonly known type space is precisely what the participants lack; I also have an unknown and possibly huge number of players, and types that change endogenously over time because of the capability-jump mechanism, rather than a fixed type space drawn once. Second, I can't tell whether the hypothesis is really an equilibrium claim at all or a selection claim in disguise — whether, that is, the silence we would observe is the prediction of a game everyone is playing optimally, or merely the residue left after every civilisation that broadcast was killed, which would make our own survival a survivorship artefact rather than an outcome the model predicts. Those strike me as two different theses that the story may be running together, and I'm not sure equilibrium analysis can even express the second one. Relatedly, signalling theory exists largely to show that communication remains partly possible even under severe conflicts of interest, so I'd like to know whether there is a formal reason no separating equilibrium exists in which a weak civilisation credibly signals that it is not worth destroying, or whether the impossibility of credible communication is a separate empirical premise — some asymmetry in the costs of hiding, listening, and striking — that a model would have to stipulate by hand. The plot's own endpoint, in which a threatened broadcast of the coordinates of both the threatener and the threatened is used as a deterrent, looks to me like a doomsday-device move, and I'd like to know what credibility would actually require there and whether the story supplies it or helps itself to it.

So the deliverable I'm after has three parts. One: a component-by-component translation, saying for each piece — the two postulates, the chain of suspicion, the capability-jump mechanism, the pre-emption conclusion, the deterrence move — what maps cleanly, what maps only if you quietly add an assumption that wasn't in the original, and what has no expression at all in this vocabulary. Two: the residue report proper — for each thing that doesn't survive, your view on whether that indicts the toolkit (its habit of fixed player sets and fixed type spaces, its preference for equilibrium over dynamics, its reliance on common priors) or whether it shows the original argument was doing less work than its confidence suggests. Three: the reverse residue, which I care about just as much — is there anything my vocabulary has that the dark forest appears to have no use for, such as repeated interaction and reputation, institutions or mediators, correlated equilibria, or the possibility of building rather than assuming commitment? I should add that I'm away from my university library for the summer and can't chase references, so please argue from the structure of the models rather than pointing me at papers.
