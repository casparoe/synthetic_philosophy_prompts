Hey — I need you to draft the joint statement for an adversarial collaboration I'm trying to set up between two camps that have been talking past each other for years. I want the document they could both actually put their names to, not a transcript of them arguing and not your verdict on who wins.

The camps, as I'd like them defined:

- **Virtual realist:** the objects in a persistent online world — a sword, a house, a plot of land, a currency balance, a guild's treasury — really exist, as a distinctive kind of dependent, artifactual object made out of server state plus a practice. Damaging, taking, or destroying them can therefore be a harm *in its own right*, not merely a cause of distress or of downstream real-world loss.
- **Derivative-harm fictionalist:** the "sword" is at bottom a bit of server state plus a fiction licensed by it, closer to a prop in a game of make-believe than to a piece of property. Nothing is harmed except persons. The genuine wrongs are the familiar ones: distress, wasted effort and time, lost real-world opportunities, broken real agreements, damaged real relationships.

To keep them arguing about the same thing, set both in one stipulated world: **Longworld**, running continuously for twenty years, items genuinely non-duplicable because the engine won't allow it, a player-run court system that settles disputes and enforces punishment, participation voluntary under a terms-of-service that permits banishment at the operators' discretion, an economy that trades against real money unofficially, and a sizeable subset of players for whom most of their friendships and standing now live inside it.

Cases I want both sides forced to answer, at least in passing:

1. A player's items are deleted by another player and she never finds out, suffers nothing, and loses no real-world opportunity.
2. "Walling-in": a griefer builds around someone's house so they can't get out. Nothing is destroyed; only virtual options are removed.
3. Migration: Longworld is ported to a new engine. Item records are rebuilt with new identifiers, the physics differ slightly, everything looks the same to players. Is it the same sword?
4. Permanent banishment, exercised under the terms-of-service the player agreed to.
5. The sealed world: everyone stops logging in for good, the servers keep running, and someone then wrecks the builds.
6. A generality check both sides should have to survive: Monopoly hotels, poker chips, a tampered scoreboard. Whatever criterion each side is using, does it sort these the way they want?

Structure:

- A **numbered agreed-claims section** — the things both can sign: what's genuinely at stake, which intuitions are contested rather than obvious, where the analogy to ordinary fiction does and doesn't hold, how much work consent actually does, and anything else you think they'd converge on.
- A **disagreements table**: one row per issue, with a column for the wording the realist would sign, a column for the wording the fictionalist would sign (each phrased so that side would accept it as *their* view, not a caricature), and a column marking whether the disagreement is substantive or — be honest if so — partly verbal, with a sentence on why.
- A **cruxes section**: for each crux, the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; the case, argument, or finding both agree would bear on it; and each side's prediction written down *in advance*. Where a crux has an empirical edge — what players say about it, willingness-to-pay, distress measures, what people actually do after a migration or a ban — record what each side expects to find.

Two more things. First, keep the two headline questions apart: are virtual objects real, and are virtual harms real? Say explicitly where they come apart, and if the most defensible package combines a realist answer on one with a fictionalist answer on the other, say that rather than forcing the two camps into tidy boxes. Second, include as one crux whether acts directed at an avatar — harassment, unwanted simulated sexual contact — involve a distinctive kind of wrong, or whether the wrong is exhausted by the distress and the real-world relational facts. If you think some crux can't be settled by anything both sides would accept as evidence, say so and explain why; that's a useful result too.

Work from the cases, no citations or reading needed, and don't resolve it — I want the disagreement made exact, not dissolved. Aim for roughly 900–1200 words, and keep the prose plain enough that both sides would recieve it as fair.
