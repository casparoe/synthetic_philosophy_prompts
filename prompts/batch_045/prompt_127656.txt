I am grading the written final for an undergraduate course on rational choice and decision theory. Below is one exam question, followed verbatim by one student's answer to it. Please grade the answer as I would have to: carefully, and with comments I could paste into a grading sheet.

Exam question (20 points total)

A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time. If the first head occurs on toss n, the player receives 2^n dollars. Call this the Petersburg gamble P.

(a) [5 points] Show that the expected monetary payoff of P diverges, and explain in two or three sentences why this is consistent with the fact that the player is very likely to win a very small amount.

(b) [8 points] Daniel Bernoulli's classical response replaces expected monetary payoff with expected utility, taking utility to be logarithmic in final wealth. State the response, and then evaluate it: does the logarithmic proposal actually secure a finite expected utility for the gamble as specified? Support your answer with a computation (you may simplify by supposing that the payoff constitutes the agent's entire final wealth). Then say whether that settles the paradox, or whether the difficulty reappears in some modified form.

(c) [7 points] Consider two modifications of P.

(i) The capped game C_N: the house tosses at most N times. If the first head occurs on toss n not exceeding N, the player receives 2^n dollars; if no head occurs within N tosses, the player receives nothing. Compute the expected payoff of C_N.

(ii) The audited game A_h: before each toss an independent audit takes place, and with probability h (where 0 < h < 1) the game is cancelled at that point and the player receives nothing. Compute the expected payoff of A_h as a function of h.

Then, in a short paragraph, say whether these two modifications show that the paradox is merely an artifact of infinite expectations.

Student's answer

(a) The probability that the first head falls on toss n is 2^{-n}, and the payoff in that case is 2^n, so the expected payoff is the sum over n of 2^{-n} times 2^n, which is 1 + 1 + 1 + ... and diverges. Each outcome contributes exactly one unit to the sum, so no single outcome is responsible for the divergence. There is no conflict with the fact that the player almost certainly wins very little: with probability 1/2 the payoff is 2 dollars, and with probability 3/4 it is at most 4 dollars. The expectation is infinite only because a tail of very small probability carries very large payoffs, and an expectation is sensitive to how large the payoffs are, not only to how likely they are.

(b) Bernoulli's idea is that a rational agent maximizes expected utility rather than expected money, and that utility is a concave function of final wealth, the logarithm being his own proposal. This does not get us out of the difficulty. The logarithm is unbounded above, and the gamble offers unbounded payoffs, so for a logarithmic agent the expected utility of P is itself infinite, exactly as expected payoff was, and such an agent should again be willing to pay any finite entry fee. Menger's theorem is the general form of this point: for any utility function that is unbounded above there exists a Petersburg-style gamble whose expected utility diverges; rescale the payoffs to 2 raised to the power 2^n, for instance, and even a logarithmic agent's expected utility blows up. So no unbounded utility function is safe, and the only stable resolution is to take utility to be bounded above, which is to say to postulate enough risk aversion. Once we do that, the willingness to pay is finite and the paradox is over. Whatever remains is a question about what people actually do, and that is psychology rather than rational choice.

(c) (i) For C_N the expected payoff is the sum from n = 1 to N of 2^{-n} times 2^n, which equals N. (ii) For A_h, to be paid at toss n the player must survive n audits, which has probability (1 − h)^n, and must first get heads on toss n, which has probability 2^{-n}. The expected payoff is therefore the sum over n of (1 − h)^n times 2^{-n} times 2^n, which is the sum of (1 − h)^n, or (1 − h)/h; for small h this is about 1/h.

Neither modification dissolves the paradox, and that is the interesting result: both show that the infinity was never the essential thing. The cap makes the point most sharply, because the cap barely changes anything. The probability of ever reaching it is 2^{-N}, so truncating the game removes a contribution of order 2^{-N} from the expectation; with N = 40 that is roughly one in a trillion, and the expected value of the capped game is for all practical purposes still infinite. The paradox therefore cannot be a puzzle about a divergent series. It is a conflict between the value the theory assigns to the gamble and the amount anyone would actually pay for it.
