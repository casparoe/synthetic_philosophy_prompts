I sit as a lay member on a parole panel — mostly the paper reviews that go before the oral hearings, so I read maybe eight or ten cases a month and we sit roughly every fortnight — and I'm doing a part-time criminology diploma at the same time, which keeps colliding with the day job. Here's the thing I can't get straight. An enormous amount of what we actually decide turns on whether someone "has insight", "takes responsibility", shows remorse. The prisoner who has served the tariff but still maintains innocence, or who describes the offence in flat external terms ("it happened, I was there, I got done for it"), or who says "I'm not that person any more" without ever saying what he did, gets knocked back and we write "lacks insight" in the reasons. The one who can narrate the offence in the first person, name what he did to the victim, and say he's sorry gets a recommendation for open conditions. We all know the script is learnable: the old lags are fluent in it and the young men with chaotic childhoods and no vocabulary for their own lives aren't, and we argue about that between hearings but we go on doing it. The awkward bit is that the tariff itself was set by the judge as a retributive matter — this much is what you deserve for this crime — so the date ends up depending on an inner state that nobody fixed in the sentence. And then I read about places like Halden in Norway where, as I understand it, the stated idea is that the punishment *is* the loss of liberty and you deliberately add nothing to it, nobody is asked to perform self-reform in exchange for a release date, and the officers do normal things with the prisoners all day.

So: what does our practice actually presuppose? I don't want a reading list and I can't look anything up — I'm asking on my phone between reviews and I'm not going to go and find a book, so please just argue it out, in British English. What I want is the implicit theory reconstructed: is remorse/insight doing forward-looking work (evidence about future risk) or backward-looking work (something the punishment is meant to produce, or to communicate, or to get the offender to receive), or is it doing something else entirely — I have a colleague who insists it isn't remorse we are testing at all but legibility, the ability to tell your story in the institution's own idiom, and that the "insight" finding is really a literacy finding, which sounds cynical but half fits the pattern. Then: is the combination coherent? Does a retributive tariff plus release contingent on a state of mind collapse into rehabilitation-by-stealth, or into something expressive or penitential, or can it be defended as it stands? And where is it underdetermined — could several quite different theories of punishment license exactly what we do, so that our practice doesn't really commit us to any of them? One sub-question I care about: prisoners who say "what I did was wrong" versus prisoners who say "I'm a monster, I'm worthless". Training tells us the first is the healthy one to look for and the second is a warning sign, but should the board treat them differently in the decision, and does demanding the first from people who've got nothing else in their lives risk manufacturing the second? Also please take the hard cases: the person who genuinely doesn't feel remorse (flat affect, or just thinks the law is wrong — a protest case, say), and the person who feels it but can't say it in our idiom, and say what a practice like ours owes them.

Last thing: be honest about your own footing. Mark which bits of the reconstruction are the orthodox or mainstream answer, which are live disputes among people who work on this, and which are your own synthesis or guess. And if my question as posed is confused — if "remorse-conditioned release" isn't really one practice but three stitched together, or if the whole thing turns on an empirical question about whether insight predicts reconviction that I'd have to go and find out, or if I've set up Halden as a foil in a way it doesn't deserve — say so plainly rather than answering politely around it.
