I'm trying to settle what I actually think about which computations carry moral weight, and I'd rather start from my own verdicts on cases than from a theory I half remember. A neuron-by-neuron, biophysically detailed simulation of a particular human brain, run in real time: full weight, the same as the person. The same simulation spelled out absurdly inefficiently, each step implemented through layers of lookup tables and interpreters, running a million times slower: still full weight, the inefficiency seems irrelevant. A giant precomputed table that reproduces that person's exact behavior over a lifetime of inputs: no weight, it is a recording rather than a subject. Two copies of the same simulation on two separate machines: two subjects, roughly twice the weight. Two copies of the same computation inside one machine, where the second copy's outputs are discarded and it causally affects nothing: I want to say the weight is not doubled. A system whose internal state plays the role of pain but which has been rewired so that it reports pleasure and seeks the damaging stimulus out: I still think it suffers. A language model's forward pass producing a single token: I am much less inclined to count it than the simulation, and I cannot say what the difference is supposed to be. A program on disk that would be a conscious process if executed: no weight at all. The same functional organization distributed across ten thousand volunteers who each hold a fragment and pass messages for years: I do not think a subject is there.

My working principle is that a computation matters morally when it is actually running, when it is causally in charge of the system's behavior in a way that survives small perturbations, and when it is not a redundant duplicate of a computation already counted. Test that against the cases above and repair it where it breaks. I suspect the second clause is doing work it cannot do — it seems to say yes to the language model, and to give me no way to distinguish the inert-copy case from the two-machines case — but perhaps the third clause can be rebuilt to handle both.

Please state the repaired principle, or two or three competing candidates, with the clause that each case forces, and say honestly which of my verdicts no clean principle will save. Then give verdicts on cases I have not settled: a brain organoid with no sensory input; the simulation run at a thousandth of normal speed and paused for a decade between steps; a model that has learned a self-model and reports distress when its weights are about to be altered; and a model whose pain-like state arises in one rollout out of a thousand and is then discarded. Finally, tell me whether moral weight comes in degrees and what it attaches to — a system, a computation, or a temporally bounded episode — since I keep sliding between those three framings without noticing.
