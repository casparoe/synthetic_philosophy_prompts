I'm a PhD student in behavioral ecology — my dissertation work is on problem-solving and social tolerance in corvids — and I sit in on our department's interdisciplinary reading group on the evolution of cognition. At the last meeting I floated what I meant as a modest empirical thought: in the one biosphere we have data about, the capacities we bundle together as general intelligence were elaborated in lineages where social cognition paid, under selection pressures that rewarded managing conspecifics, so it is at least open that the systems we actually end up building, given the selection pressures we build them under, will tend to have motives that are not indifferent to us. A machine-learning colleague ended the discussion with a line I recognize from the AI-risk literature: "The AI does not hate you, nor does it love you, but you are made out of atoms which it can use for something else." He took it to establish that capability and motivation are independent axes, that the danger is impersonal rather than hostile, and that my evolutionary anecdote therefore has no bearing. I don't think the sentence is false; I think it is doing several different jobs at once, and that only some of them do the work he needs.

I have been trying to sort this out for myself and have arrived at a three-way distinction I have not found stated anywhere, which makes me suspect I am either reinventing something or conflating things. Call the first convergence across goals: for a fixed architecture and a fixed situation, many distinct final goals prescribe overlapping subgoals — securing more matter and energy, preventing one's own goal-content from being rewritten, and so on. Call the second convergence across situations: for a fixed final goal, the optimal policy looks much the same across a wide range of environments, so the goal's local appearance badly underdetermines long-run behavior. Call the third convergence across lineages: among the systems actually produced by some training or selection process, the ones that end up with influence are disproportionately ones with certain subgoals, whatever final goals were aimed at. My suspicion is that the first is the claim that gets defended, while his inference needs the second or the third. Two toy cases sharpen this for me: a system whose sole final goal is to build the largest physically possible accurate scale model of Salisbury Cathedral out of purified silicon, and a system whose sole final goal is to keep one particular houseplant alive for as long as physics permits. I have a strong intuition that the first ends up doing something catastrophic and a much weaker one about the second, and I cannot say what principled difference that tracks — or whether the intuition is merely trained on which examples the literature happens to use.

Please don't give me sources or a reading list; I can find the literature myself, and what I want is the disambiguation, not the bibliography. I would like a menu rather than an argument for a single reading. Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no preamble and no summing-up: the five distinct precise theses that line could be expressing, ranging from the platitudinous through the substantive to something that may simply be false, and for each a verdict on whether it is a truism, defensible but substantive, or false or incoherent, together with whether it would actually license the inference my colleague needed. If the reading people who repeat the line are almost certainly invoking is a different one from the reading that would really carry his argument, register that in the item where it belongs.
