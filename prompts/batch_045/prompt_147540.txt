I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar on the ethics of information: eighteen students, one ninety-minute session, and a room in which roughly a third of them grew up somewhere other than Germany or the United States and arrived with very different instincts about what the state is entitled to know. The session falls late in a unit on privacy and the state. The assigned reading is already handled; I do not want a fourth discussion of whether privacy is dead, and I do not want a debate about which country does it right. I want something they do.

The thing I want them to run into is a distinction my students reliably collapse. They arrive treating transparency as a single dial: how much the state can see. I think there are at least three separable variables, and that most of the real disagreement lives in the space between them:

- downward legibility: how much of a citizen's conduct reaches a durable record the state can consult;
- upward legibility: how much of that record — and of the rule that turns it into a consequence — the citizen can inspect, contest, and correct;
- automaticity: whether a bad record produces consequences on its own, or only through a human decision that someone can be asked to justify.

Call the gap between the first and the second the reciprocity gap, and call the finite quantity of conduct a person can keep off the record in a given week — the errand paid for in cash, the message not sent, the visit not made — their opacity budget. My suspicion, which I would rather they discovered than were told, is that a polity can be extremely sparing in what it collects and still be harder to live under than one that collects far more, provided the reciprocity gap is wide and the consequences are automatic; and that the labor of keeping a second-order file on the state's file is itself an unequally distributed tax that nobody budgets for.

So: design a game or structured simulation for that room and that clock. What I need:

- Participants making real choices under real (small, symbolic) stakes — points, tokens, standing, a trivial prize. Nothing that costs me money and nothing I have to grade.
- No lecture first, and ideally no vocabulary: the three variables, the reciprocity gap, and the opacity budget should go unnamed until the debrief, and I would prefer the words Germany, United States, and China not be spoken before then either. If you use my three national labels at all, treat them as the caricatures my students walk in with. The game must still work if I rename them three fictional cities, and it must not depend on any sociological claim about any of them being true.
- Roles or secret cards that give different participants genuinely different reasons to want different settings of the dials, plus a mechanism — a veil, a rotation, a secret ballot, or something better — that stops the room converging on a mushy moderate compromise. I specifically want the instant at which someone who voted for high downward legibility discovers what it costs them, and the instant at which someone discovers they cannot correct a record they are not allowed to see.
- A timeline with minutes attached, a materials list (index cards, a printed board, dice — I have a photocopier and no budget), and rules I can read aloud in under three minutes.
- A debrief that maps named moments in play onto the variables and onto the two coined terms, and that uses at least one student's own inconsistency as its evidence rather than my assertion.
- Your honest predictions of what will go wrong the first time I run it, with a remedy for each. I am especially interested in how to keep this from degenerating into an optimization puzzle, and in what to do with the student who refuses on principle to play the registrar.
- A frank note on what the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in my own voice. My guess is that it is something like the slow effect on what people dare to want, together with the fact that in actual systems almost no one is ever shown their score.

Aim for something I can really run on a Tuesday afternoon: around a thousand words, prose and short lists, no citations, no reading list, no legal detail — any of that I will provide myself.
