Roughly: keep the aversive signal — its gradedness, the way it grabs attention, the way it gets stamped into memory — but cap how bad it can get. A hard ceiling somewhere around uncomfortable, noticeable, doesn't run your whole mind. Worst case for a human being becomes roughly what a mild sunburn is now.

Here's the shape of what I can't stop thinking about, use it or throw it out. The thing that actually made those three weeks bad wasn't the unpleasantness as such, it was the colonization — that there was no rest of his mind left, no stepping back from it, no room for anything else, and that it could always get worse without limit. If that's right, then the morally urgent target is saturation and imperiousness rather than aversiveness itself, and you can remove the first without touching the second. Which matters because the second is load-bearing in a way the first isn't: it's how learning gets its error signals, and — this is the bit that gets me — it's the thing that would notice if the fix degraded. Delete the aversive channel and then, when the intervention drifts, or the maintenance regime for the next generation gets sloppy, or the political will to keep funding it evaporates, nothing hurts. Nobody's bothered. Same goes for the value itself: the first generation that never felt anything bad has no grip on why any of this mattered, so what transmits? Under thinning you keep the sensor.
