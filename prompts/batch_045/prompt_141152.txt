I'm auditing a graduate seminar on population ethics this term, and we spent two of the last three sessions on what happens to axiology and to consequentialist decision-making once the number of people is infinite. I came out of it with a pile of case verdicts I can't get to line up. A friend in the math department tells me my trouble is just that infinite sums don't behave like finite ones, which I take to be true but not to settle anything, since the question I actually care about is which changes to an infinite world I have *reason* to bring about. So I'd like you to do reflective equilibrium on me. Please assume throughout that an infinite population is genuinely possible and that the cases are coherent: I want the axiology and the decision rule, not an argument for finitism.

Here are my verdicts. The baseline world W has countably infinitely many people, indexed 1, 2, 3, …, each at welfare +1, and every change I describe is a one-off that affects only the people named. (1) Add one extra person at +1: no reason to do it; nothing of value is at stake. (2) Raise person 1 from +1 to +100: yes, a real improvement and a real reason. (3) Person 1 starts at −1000 and I can bring them to +1: yes, and a stronger reason than in (2). (4) I can either leave W alone or bring person 1 down to −1000 while giving every other person a tiny gain, so that there is no finite bound on the total welfare added: I have no stable verdict — sometimes the unbounded gain looks decisive, sometimes wrecking one life for grains of welfare looks plainly wrong, and this is the case I most want a principle to speak to. (5) Compare W with a world in which the very same people are each at +2: the +2 world is clearly better. (6) Compare W with a world containing infinitely many people at +10 and infinitely many at −1: my verdict slides around depending on whether the +10 people are presented to me as half the population or as a sparse subset, and I regard that slide as a defect in me rather than a feature of the world, so I end up with no verdict at all and resent it. (7) Now run the same menu with a billion people instead of infinitely many: adding one happy person is a small but genuine improvement, and the analogue of (4) looks to me clearly worth taking. So my verdict on pure addition flips somewhere between a billion and infinity, and I can't tell whether that is a deep fact about infinity or just inconsistency on my part.

The thing I've been groping toward has three stages, and I'd like it tested, repaired, or replaced: first, compare worlds by looking for a one-to-one matching of the people in one world to the people in the other such that everyone is at least as well off in the better world and someone is strictly better off; second, where no such matching settles it, treat the proposed change as a finite disturbance against a fixed infinite background and evaluate only what it does to the people it touches, ignoring the untouched infinity; third, where even that is silent, stop aggregating and fall back on non-aggregative considerations — constraints, priority for the worst off, whatever does the work — rather than pretending the sums mean something. I don't know which clause is carrying the weight, and I suspect the second and third are doing more than I'd like to admit.

Please write your answer in five titled sections, in this order. **My verdicts, restated** (about 120 words): a compact list or table of the seven cases with the verdict your account must capture, so I can check you've read me right, including explicit note of the two cases where I have no verdict and the one where my verdicts are discontinuous. **The principle** (about 250 words): one or two candidate principles stated precisely enough to be applied mechanically, with the case that motivates each clause, and an indication of which clause is load-bearing. **Verdicts on cases I haven't settled** (about 250 words): apply your principle to (i) a world containing infinitely many agents each holding a button that tortures one person if pushed, where I know that whatever I do infinitely many people push and infinitely many don't, so my own act makes no difference to the total; (ii) a world with infinitely many people at +100 and infinitely many at −100, compared with everyone at 0, where the total is not merely infinite but undefined; (iii) a variant of case (4) in which the tiny gains go only to a sparse infinite subset of the population rather than to everyone else, to see whether density matters to you. **Verdicts I'd have to give up** (about 150 words): which of my seven verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, stated plainly, and whether the discontinuity in (7) is defensible or an artifact. **What would change this answer** (about 150 words): the one argument or consideration that would flip your verdict, where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail, whether the disagreement bottoms out in a technical question about how to sum or in a substantive moral one, and how confident you are in each part.
