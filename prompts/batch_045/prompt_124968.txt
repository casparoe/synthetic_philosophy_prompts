I've somehow ended up on the standards commitee of the Serena Valley cheese consortium — the little body that decides which producers get to put the "Val Serena" name on their wheels — and we owe written decisions before the spring assembly at the end of next month. Two files are stuck. The Baldinis' pastures sit on the long gentle rise where the valley floor stops and the "slopes" begin; our spec says the milk has to come from herds grazed on the slopes above the valley, but nobody ever surveyed where that starts, the ground just keeps going up continuously, and any contour we pick is going to look arbitrary to whoever is two hundred metres below it. The other one: a bigger outfit has been ripening wheels since 2019 in chambers it cut into the hillside under an old quarry — same rock, same humidity, but the temperature and airflow are held constant by a computer rather than by the hill. Our spec says "ripened in natural caves" and says nothing about whether mechanical help disqualifies you. And making it worse, one of the producers who'd fail under a strict reading has held certification for 31 years and sells most of their output under our label, so losing it is a genuinely serious hit to a real family.

I think this is a philosophy problem wearing a rubber boot, and I'd rather admit that than fake our way through it. My partner did her PhD in philosophy and has opinions about everything; over dinner she said our problem has a name, vagueness, and that there are two standard stomachs for it. One says there is a fact of the matter about exactly which contour counts as "the slopes" and exactly how much climate control turns a cave into a room, we simply have no way of finding out, so any line we draw is a guess at a truth that's already there. The other says the word is genuinely unsettled across that middle band and any line inside the band is as good as any other, so we aren't discovering anything, we're stipulating. She also said the second view gets into trouble when you ask where the band itself begins and ends — which was roughly the point at which I lost the argument and went to do the washing up.

So please write me the memo I can circulate, and please don't make it mush — I need a document the committee could actually vote on next week. Sections, in this order, roughly these lengths:

- "Recommendation" (about 150 words): bottom line first — one clear call on each of the two cases, plus what we do about the rule itself.
- "The two cases" (about 200 words): restate them in the terms the decision actually turns on, not in the order they got argued.
- "Why we should admit we are drawing the line rather than finding it" (about 300 words): make the case that our job here is stipulation, and say what follows from that about how loudly we should claim to be right.
- "The strongest thing the other side gets to say" (about 200 words): steelman the view that there's a real boundary we're approximating however badly — and that we should keep our reasoning in the file rather than publishing it, because the moment we set the contour at 380 metres somebody will build a barn at 381 and the whole point of the designation quietly dies.
- "What we publish and what we keep in the file" (about 150 words), including whether the exact numbers go in the public version at all.
- "Timetable" (about 100 words), with dates pegged to the spring assembly and to the 31-year producer's recertification deadline.

One more thing I definately need handled, because the two retired lawyers on the committee will raise it and I'll look like an idiot if I haven't thought about it: whatever line we set has its own borderline cases right at the edge, and borderline cases of those. Say what we do about that, and be concrete — a sunsets clause, a safe harbour, a standing review, whatever you think works — rather than just noting that the problem exists.
