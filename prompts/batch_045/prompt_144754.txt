our book club is doing The Road next month and ive somehow ended up leading it, and I dont want to spend an hour on is it too bleak. I've been reading a bit of care ethics — Gilligan's different voice stuff, Held on care as both a practice and a value, and Tronto, including the four phases she lays out (attentiveness, responsibility, competence, responsiveness, if I've got them right) — and it feels like a much better fit than the usual virtue-ethics reading people give it, but I want it done properly, not just care ethics = the guy loves his son, discuss.

The bit I keep going back and forth on: the paradigm case for care ethics is an adult responding to someone dependent, with the dependency running one way, and in this book it doesn't. The boy is the one dragging his father back toward the people they pass, and the father is the one who has narrowed all his care down to a single person and treats everyone else as a threat. So who's the care ethicist here? Is the boy's concern actually the kind of generalized impartial concern Gilligan and Noddings are suspicious of — the thing that looks like morality but is really abstraction — or is that the point, that a caring relation is supposed to produce someone who cares beyond it? And is the father's failure a failure in one of the phases rather than in the amount of care? He looks to me very high on responsibility and competence — he's extremely good at keeping the boy alive — and pretty bad at responsiveness, since he mostly can't hear what the boy is actually distressed about. Is that a fair use of the framework or am I bending it to fit?

Other places I'd like it to bite, roughly: the mother, whose exit is in a sense a refusal of the whole job of caring, and who more or less accuses him of being attached to an idea rather than to people — I cant tell whether the novel is offering a critique of care there or just describing burnout and the loss of it. The cellar with the people being eaten alive, where there's no care-receiving left to be responsive to: does care ethics have anything to say about people who are past the point of being cared for, or does it go quiet, and is going quiet a defect? The scene with the thief they strip (and whatever happens after — I genuinely don't remember if they go back), where the boy's distress is about a stranger the father has decided is nothing.

And please tell me where the lens breaks, since these readings always break somewhere and that's the useful part. My worry is this: Tronto's later work is explicitly political, about how whole societies organise care, and there's basically no society left in the book — so maybe applying a partly institutional view to two people on a road is a category error, or maybe the novel shows that an ethic of care with no social infrastructure collapses into exactly the anxious hoarding the father does. Which scenes would you actually point the group to? And what, if anything, do you think the novel adds to the care ethics debate that the papers don't — something about care under absolute scarcity, maybe, where there really isn't enough to go around and the tradeoffs stop being hypothetical?
