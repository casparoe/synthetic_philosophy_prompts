write me a short story (2000-3000 words, or whatever it actually needs) about a small team of experimental philosophers — just call them x-phi people, they'd call themselves that — who travel to a valley community to run the standard battery of thought-experiment surveys: a couple of Gettier cases, the one with the name Gödel / the man who actually proved the theorem, a trolley variant, maybe a Kripke-style case where a speaker uses a name they picked up from someone else. Invent the culture, the language, the place and the season. Don't set it anywhere real, and don't turn them into wise natives with a special relationship to truth; they should be as annoying, funny, busy and internally divided as any other group of people.

Here's the thing I want the story built around, because I've been going back and forth on it and can't settle it.

In your invented culture, being asked by a relative stranger what you'd say about an imaginary case — with nothing riding on it, no decision hanging in the balance — is a marked and slightly childish kind of question. Roughly the equivalent of asking "do you promise?" when nothing is being promised. So the team's numbers wobble in a very specific way: they get results that look a lot like the Western samples when the interview is framed as a game (say, a judging contest with cowrie shells or bottle caps as counters, run in a courtyard), and something noticeably different when the identical question is put as a plain enquiry, sitting under a tree, in the ordinary questioning form.

The lead researcher is preregistering all of this and the preregistration has to be quietly abandoned about two thirds of the way in. There should be a real argument with a translator over one single word, and the translators should be right about something the team isn't.

Then the part I care most about. One of the local research assistants — give her her own ambitions, she's not there to teach the visitor a lesson — makes a distinction the team's instruments literally have no box for. Something like: there's what a person will say about a case when asked, there's what they'd be willing to have repeated about them in the market the next day, and there's what they'd actually act on when the case stops being hypothetical. Call them whatever you want. Her claim is that the philosophers have only ever been collecting the first one, and that the first one is the most detatched from anything you'd want to call an intuition — it's a response to the situation of being asked, by this person, in this register, for this purpose. She proposes some other way of putting the question that the survey can't code.

End with a scene, not a speech. I'd like the final beat to be the lead researcher trying to ask herself one of her own cases in the local language, late, and finding she can't produce an answer that feels like an answer — but don't let the story tell me whether that's because the intuition is gone, because the translation failed, or because the whole project was measuring the wrong object. Leave it genuinely open which of those it is; if the ending secretly settles the debate I'll be annoyed. No narrator paragraphs explaining the upshot, no footnotes, no philosophy-sounding asides from characters who'd never talk that way.
