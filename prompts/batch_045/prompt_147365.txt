I am a novelist, and a character in the book I am currently writing — a colour scientist called Hallam — has constructed an argument I cannot refute and cannot believe. I need to know which step to cut loose, because the whole third act currently rests on it. I would be grateful for a diagnosis rather than a survey of the literature; I have no access to a library here, and in any case what I need is the shape of the error, not who first committed it.

Hallam begins with the familiar case, stated as neutrally as I can manage:

(1) Before her release, Mary knows every physical fact.
(2) On being released, she comes to know what it is like to see red, which she did not know before.
(3) Knowing is factive: if a subject comes to know that p — or comes to know what it is like to undergo E — then there is a fact that the subject did not previously know.
(4) So there is at least one fact that Mary did not know before her release.
(5) Since by (1) she already knew every physical fact, the fact in (4) is not physical, and physicalism is false.

My own contribution is a modification I invented for plot reasons. I needed a character whose experience changes without her ever noticing that it has, so I devised the following: a subject, Iris, is raised from birth in a chamber whose illumination is rotated slowly through hue space, by the smallest perceptible step, one step each day. The arrangement has two features. First, on each day Iris sees a hue she has never seen before: any two of the hues, presented side by side, are discriminable. Second, no two of the hues are discriminable at a day's remove: Iris cannot remember the hue of yesterday well enough to compare it with today's, and at the end of a year she cannot, by introspection or memory, tell any of the 365 hues apart from any of the others. Let us also stipulate that the chamber's operators possess a complete physical description of Iris throughout, so that no physical fact about her is unknown to someone.

Hallam then generalises:

(6) The reasoning in (1)–(5) trades on nothing peculiar to Mary. What does the work is this: when a subject who has not undergone E acquires, by undergoing E, a phenomenal concept of E — an ability to recognise, remember, imagine or re-identify E — the subject thereby acquires knowledge of a fact she did not previously know. So the inference holds whenever that condition is met.
(7) On each of the 365 days, Iris undergoes an experience she has not undergone before and thereby acquires a phenomenal concept she did not previously have.
(8) So on each day Iris learns a new fact; after a year she knows 365 facts she did not know at the outset, and by (5) none of them is physical.
(9) But by construction, Iris cannot at the end of the year distinguish any of these 365 facts from any other: not from the outside, since every physical fact about her was known all along and is the same in every relevant respect, and not from the inside, since her introspective and memorial position with respect to the hue of day 200 is exactly what it is with respect to the hue of day 201.
(10) So if the argument is sound, there are facts that are invisible from every epistemic position there is — not merely unknown, but in principle undetectable, differences that make no difference from any point of view.
(11) Yet phenomenal facts were supposed to be precisely the facts that acquaintance with one's own experience puts one in touch with; the entire appeal of (1)–(5) is that release is the one case where a subject is in a position to tell that something new has been learned. A conclusion that renders the newly learned facts undetectable from that very position withdraws with one hand what the argument offers with the other.
(12) Therefore either (6) is false — acquiring a phenomenal concept or ability need not be acquiring a fact — or else (1)–(5) was never sound in the first place, and what Mary acquires on release is no more a fact than Iris's 365 are.

Hallam has a reply to (9)–(11), and I cannot tell whether it is deep or evasive. Facts, he says, are not the sort of item that must be distinguishable from somewhere. That the chamber contains 10^80 atoms differs from its containing 10^80+1, and the difference is a genuine difference in the world whether or not anyone could ever count them; likewise Iris's 365 experiences involve 365 distinct properties, and the facts about them differ accordingly. The knowledge argument requires only that Mary come to know a fact, not that the fact be discriminable by anyone, and certainly not that it be discriminable by Iris at the end of the year.

Someone has told me that there are standard names for the manoeuvres available here — that one can deny that Mary's gain is a fact at all and call it an ability, or grant that it is a fact but deny that it is a non-physical fact, or insist that the case cannot be set up as (1) describes. If any of these is the right response to the argument above, I need it stated as a response to these twelve steps, and I need to know what it costs.

Specifically: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I should say where my sympathies lie, since that constrains the answer. I want to keep (1) and (2) if at all possible: that Mary genuinely learns something, and learns it by having the experience, is the thing the novel is about, and I would surrender almost any other premise to preserve it. If you reject (3), tell me what account of knowing what it is like is supposed to replace the factive one, and whether it survives the fact that Iris's case strikes me as, if anything, a clearer instance of learning than Mary's: she acquires something daily, incrementally, and never loses it. If you reject (6), give me the restricted form of the generalisation that covers Mary and not Iris, and say what that restriction costs the anti-physicalist — whether it leaves the argument anything more than a single anecdote. If you think the flaw is already in my description of the chamber — that the two stipulations about discriminability cannot both hold, or that their holding together makes (7) false rather than (9) — say exactly which reading of experience and of hue does the damage. And if you think Hallam is right, that the argument is sound and the conclusion in (10) must simply be accepted, tell me what I would have to believe about facts in order to believe it, and whether that belief is more expensive than giving up (1).

Please end your reply with a single machine-readable line in the form \answer{(n): label}, where n is the number of the step you reject and label is at most four words naming what is wrong with it; if you hold that the argument is sound and its conclusion should be accepted, write \answer{none: conclusion accepted}.
