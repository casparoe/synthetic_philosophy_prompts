I host a monthly "concepts night" in my living room — ten or so people, mostly programmers and EA-adjacent types with a couple of philosophy majors mixed in, all of whom have read a fair amount of LessWrong and Slate Star Codex but almost no formal ethics. Next month I'm on the hook for a forty-minute slot on the procreation asymmetry: the claim that there is no moral requirement to bring into existence a person who would have a life clearly worth living, but that there is a strong one not to bring into existence a person whose life would be worth not living. I have my own half-baked view about where it goes wrong, but what I need from you is a plan for teaching it, not a defense or a critique of it.

Start with the order of ideas, since that's where I expect to get it wrong. I'm fairly confident that the move "no one is made worse off by being created" has to be on the table before the four-cell table — create a good life, create a bad life, don't create a good life, don't create a bad life — will do any work, but I'm unsure where to place the distinction between an act's being permissible and its being required, and where to place the point that the asymmetry is a constraint on one kind of choice rather than a general account of which outcomes are better. Give me the dependency structure with the reason for each ordering decision. Then the carrying example. The case I've been using with friends is a "hatchery" machine with two dials: one dial sets whether the mind it produces has a life clearly worth living or clearly not worth living, and a second dial can add a fixed amount of well-being to one of the people who already exists. The point of the second dial is to force the question of whether the asymmetry has any verdict at all in a choice between two permissible options — I suspect the honest answer is that it doesn't, and I suspect that will be the thing my group resists hardest. Tell me whether that example can carry the whole session, what it must have and what it must lack in order to work (does the counterfactual of non-existence need to be made vivid, and does the case need to be scrubbed of the politics of reproduction, of uncertainty, of identity-affecting timing, of flow-through effects on third parties?), whether you would replace it and with what, and what to do with a third dial I've also toyed with — one that painlessly deletes an already-existing contented person, as a probe for whether the asymmetry is really about existence or about being made worse off.

Third, the misconception map, in the order you'd expect them to arise and with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off — before it can form, or after it has formed and can be named. The ones I anticipate: that the asymmetry is just antinatalism in a lab coat; that no one is worse off is the same claim as no one is harmed and settles the matter; that the asymmetry is really a threshold view about lives being worth living plus something about risk; that it must be total utilitarianism with the serial numbers filed off; and that it must say something in the second-dial case. Correct my list, add what I'm missing, and tell me which of these are worth deliberately letting surface so they can be killed in front of the room. Close with one question I can ask at the end that distinguishes having understood the asymmetry from having merely been talked out of arguing with it, and with an explicit accounting of what I should refuse to explain in forty minutes — I'd guess at least the non-identity problem and the view that coming into existence is always a harm to the person who comes into existence, but tell me what you'd cut and how I should say out loud that I'm cutting it rather than dodging it.
