I should be grateful for your help with a talk I have to give in three weeks. One stipulation first: I have read a good deal of the recent animal-ethics literature and I have no library access where I now live, so please give me the argumentative shape of the problem rather than citations, references, or suggestions for further reading. If a reply of mine depends on a number, say so, and say how much of my conclusion rides on it.

By way of background: I spent a little over thirty years in vertebrate ecology, mostly small-mammal population dynamics, with about a decade of applied work on rodent damage in cereal and pulse systems. Since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy, which is how I ended up on this council.

The occasion is a joint meeting of our county Food Policy Council and the local chapter of a conservation NGO, convened to settle a consumer-guidance plank. The room will be: a retired county extension director in sustainable agriculture who chairs the council and is sympathetic to regenerative grazing; a rancher who finishes grass-fed beef for the local market and whose livelihood is entangled with the plank; a public-health nutritionist interested in protein transitions; a conservation biologist from the NGO who holds that working agricultural land provides habitat and that habitat is what matters; a farm-animal-welfare advocate whose entire concern is broiler welfare — growth rate, lameness, stocking density; a county commissioner who wants something that can pass without a fight; and a handful of local-food advocates in public comment. None of them are philosophers. Their shared instincts are localism, a deep suspicion of industrial livestock, a presumption that wild animals get along well enough on their own, and discomfort with adding up suffering across individuals.

My thesis is that the draft plank is backwards. It tells consumers who want to reduce animal suffering to move from intensively reared chicken to locally raised grass-fed beef. I want to argue that this advice fails once wild animals are counted: per gram of protein, grass-fed beef occupies far more land than broiler chicken, and both land occupation and crop production carry their own burden of wild-animal death and suffering, in small mammals, ground-nesting birds, herptiles and invertebrates. On my accounting — expected deaths per hectare-year, multiplied by hectares per gram of protein, with invertebrate sentience handled as a credibility range rather than as a yes-or-no — the upgrade to beef plausibly increases expected suffering by considerably more than it decreases it, and the honest recommendation is the reverse: move beef consumers toward the most land-efficient protein available, and stop treating pasture as though it cost the wild nothing.

I should also concede, before anyone raises it, that my own numbers contain an uncomfortable non-monotonicity. Nearly every measure that spares field animals per hectare — less intensive tillage, no rodenticide, wider margins, lower pesticide loads — also lowers yield per hectare; and at constant demand the shortfall is made up by bringing additional land into production. So the obvious spare the mice intuition can be self-defeating at the margin unless it is paired with reduced demand. I would rather this be used against me properly than have it discovered halfway through the question period.

What I need from you is a Q&A preparation sheet. Give me the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this room, in roughly the order they will arrive, phrased the way these people would phrase them rather than the way a philosopher would: I do not need the question of whether eating animals is permissible in the first place; I need the question of why I am telling a rancher in this room to go out of business, and the question of whether I am really about to say that industrial chicken is fine. For each objection I want a two-minute reply, an explicit note on where that reply is soft, and a judgement about which two or three objections have real force — force enough that I should change my recommendation or my framing rather than merely improve my answer. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks anything, and identify the single reply that has to land if the proposal is to survive the meeting at all.

Finally: name the question I am hoping nobody asks. I have my own candidate in mind, and I will say only that it concerns what my accounting implies about the value of habitat itself, rather than about food. I would like to see whether you arrive at it independently.
