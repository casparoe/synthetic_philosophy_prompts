I host a fortnightly discussion evening in the back room of a pub — 12 to 16 adults, mostly regulars, ages from early twenties to mid-seventies: two working programmers, a retired maths teacher, a couple of art students, a nurse, and one man who has read Roger Penrose and will mention it within the first five minutes. Two hours, of which I want to spend no more than the last quarter actually talking. Paper, pens, index cards, envelopes and a whiteboard; no laptops, no phones, and I would strongly prefer that the words incompleteness, theorem, proof and machine stay out of the room until the debrief. Almost nobody there has done any formal logic, though most can follow a rule of the shape: if a string is marked, you may mark the same string with a dash on the end.

The thing I want them to run into rather than be told is a distinction one of the programmers put to me after the last session. I had been repeating an argument I picked up from reading about the philosopher John Lucas and from Penrose's popular books: that a fixed stock of rules for churning out mathematical truths will always leave some truth it cannot reach, that a person standing outside those rules can see which one, and that this shows a mind is not a rule-following machine. My friend replied that the argument only works for someone standing outside the rules, and that from the inside — which is where I am with respect to whatever my own brain is doing — I can no more see that fact about myself than the machine can. I think he is wrong but I cannot locate where he is wrong, and more to the point I do not want to win the argument. I want the room on Tuesday to feel the difference between the outside view and the inside view in their bones, and to discover for themselves that the confident closing move, the one where you just see that your sentence is true, is leaning on something they have no way to check. If the evening ends with people disagreeing, that is a good evening.

What I need from you is a runnable activity. My own attempt, which I cannot get to work: split everyone into teams of three, hand each team a sealed envelope containing three or four rules for marking strings of letters and strokes (a is marked; if s is marked then saa is marked; if s is marked then sb is marked; something along those lines), let them generate marks for a while until they have the feel of working mechanically, then ask each team to write down something that is true of the marks but that their rules will never mark, and see what they come up with. Two problems: I cannot work out how the sentences in the envelopes get to be about the rules themselves without me standing at the front giving a lecture on coding, and I suspect the two programmers would just write this sentence is unprovable, feel clever, and learn nothing. I have also been toying with something sneakier — printing half the envelopes with a fifth, hidden rule that lets the machine mark literally everything, so that at the debrief the room splits down the middle over whether the unreachable sentence is true, and everyone realises they were trusting in their own consistency. Tell me whether that is a good idea or an unfair trick, and what to do instead if it is the latter; I am not attached to my version and will happily throw it out if you have something better. Please give me the setup and materials, the roles and rules and any scoring (points, not money), a minute-by-minute shape for the two hours, exactly what the facilitator says and — just as importantly — what the facilitator does not say, and a debrief that maps the moments people actually had onto the two readings of what is going on. I have already read the easy-to-find material on this, so do not spend your answer summarising the debate or pointing me at books; I want something I can run on Tuesday. And tell me four more things: what will predictably go wrong the first time (I am guessing the arithmetic of keeping four teams in sync, and somebody building a rule-set too feeble to say anything about itself); which part of the real argument the activity simply cannot reach, so that I will have to say it in words myself; where your design quietly takes a side in the dispute rather than leaving it open, and why you have taken that side; and one thing — a consideration, or an outcome on the night — that would make you scrap this design and build the evening around a different idea instead.
