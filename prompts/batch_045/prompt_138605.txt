I have no training in philosophy. I run a small haulage company, and I came to this by way of sitting on hiring panels and being sent on a bias-awareness course, which left me with a question the course never answered. In arguments about anything moral — what a firm owes its long-serving staff, whether it is acceptable to hire a friend's son, whether a rule should be bent for one person — one side accuses the other of bias, and the other side answers that what they have is not a bias at all but a genuine conviction or preference. I cannot find a test that settles who is right, and the more I turn it over the more I suspect that the tests I have do not agree with one another.

The two I have ended up with are these. The history test: is the judgement there because of something that is not a reason for it — something that raised its credibility by a route that has nothing to do with whether it is fair or true? If yes, call it a bias. The steady-state test: look at the judgement as the person now holds it — does it move when reasons are given, does it sit consistently with their other commitments, do they act on it when acting costs them? If yes, call it a preference. Usually the two deliver the same verdict, because a judgement with a tainted history also tends to be held rigidly. I want a case where they come apart, and I want the difficult direction: the history test says bias, the steady-state test says preference.

What I would like you to do is construct that case. The constraints:

- An ordinary setting — a workplace, a family, a committee, a club. Nothing futuristic, no hypothetical machines, and nothing that only works if someone in the story is unusually reflective or unusually dense.
- One paragraph of scenario, and no more.
- The distorting factor in the history must be one that everyone, including the person holding the judgement, would accept is distorting: the order the options were put in, whether the meeting ran past lunch, who happened to be in the room. It must not be something whose status as a reason is itself contested — no religion, no party politics, no disputed moral claim doing the work in the background.
- No self-deception, no motivated reasoning, no weakness of will. If the person is deceiving themselves, the verdict has been built in and I will have learned nothing.
- The steady-state endorsement has to be earned inside the story rather than asserted: the person should later act on the judgement at real cost to themselves, so that the reply that they are merely rationalising is not available to my colleague.

After the scenario I would like: (1) each test run in its strongest form, with what it returns and why; (2) the single feature of the case doing the work, named; (3) what happens to both verdicts if that feature is removed — it should collapse into either an ordinary bias or an ordinary preference, and I want to see which; (4) the best available reply from someone who thinks the whole distinction is only a polite way of calling other people's views biased, and why your case survives it.

If you can manage it without straining the story, I would also like the case to defeat a third test I keep playing with — the mirror test: if the arrangement had been the other way round from the start, and the person had grown up under the opposite one, would they still hold the judgement they hold? Ideally the mirror test returns something useless here, and I would like to know whether that shows my third test is simply a bad one. And if you think either of my first two tests is the wrong test, say so and replace it — but then build the case for the replacement.

The case is for a conversation with a colleague who claims that any moral view you cannot argue someone into is just a preference, that any view they cannot argue you out of is a bias, and that there is nothing further to say. So it cannot work by stipulating the verdicts: the tests have to generate them out of the facts of the story, and both of us have to be able to check the working.

Please commit. Tell me whether the case actually works, whether the two tests really come apart in the direction I asked for, and what you take the case to show about the distinction itself — and if you think no such case can be built, say that plainly and explain why, rather than giving me a case that half works followed by a list of caveats.
