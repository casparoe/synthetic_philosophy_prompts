hey — so im in this seminar on scalable oversight / AI governance-ish stuff and for the final creative option we can hand in a "genre piece" instead of an essay, and I want to use mine to make fun of my own side a little. We've spent the whole term hearing that outcome supervision just invites reward hacking (check the final answer, the model finds a way to score without doing the thing), so obviosuly the fix is process supervision: grade the reasoning trace step by step instead of the answer.

Write me an excerpt from the onboarding handbook / compliance training module for new Process Supervisors at a fictional lab. ~600-700 words, numbered subsections (3.1, 3.2, ...) in the deadpan voice of a genuinely enthusiastic mid-level manager who thinks everything is working beautifully. No winking, no "silly, right?" asides — the badness should come entirely from how reasonable each rule sounds on its own.

The conceit I want kept consistent: the lab has decided that the final answer is never scored. It isn't shown to the grader, it isn't logged, it's discarded at inference time. Only the trace is graded, step by step — by a grader model that was itself trained on human step-labels, which was itself graded by another one, and so on. Please do something with that recursion; maybe a "Supervisor-of-Record" career ladder with tiers going up further than anyone can audit, and a note on what happens when the chain reaches a tier whose own traces nobody has ever read.

Rules I want you to invent and then play completely straight:

- A ban on "unmotivated steps." Any inference that can't be verified in isolation, from a short stated premise, gets flagged, so the house style is to derive everything from as near to axioms as you can get. I want one worked example of what this does to an ordinary request — say a supervisor asking whether a bridge is safe or whether to approve a loan — and I want the reader to feel the cost of banning intuitive leaps without the handbook ever once saying "but insight is the thing you can't decompose."
- A rule against "answer-contamination" / "retrospective justification": if a step looks like it was written by a process that already knew where it was heading, it's penalized. The handbook has to explain how graders are meant to detect this, in a way that obviously cannot work.
- The empirical finding that the trace doesn't causally drive the answer gets handled in exactly one bland bureaucratic sentence, reclassified as a legacy concern from "the outcome era."

Also include at least one quote from a grizzled outcome-side holdout who still thinks checking whether the thing worked is sort of the point, and who is being managed out; give them one line that genuinely lands, and then have the handbook blandly misread it. Someone who thinks process supervision is obviously correct should feel a little uneasy by the end.

Keep the technical content honest though — I want the seminar to laugh because it's fair, not because it's a strawman. The real nerves I want hit: graders rewarding length, formatting and confident tone over correctness; grader models being weaker than the models they grade; the slide from "legible trace" to "faithful trace"; and the fact that somewhere the buck still has to stop on an outcome.
