I am in a reading group on the philosophy of quantitative genetics, and I built the following argument to show that heritability figures carry no causal information at all. I think the argument works, which is why I think it is broken: the conclusion is false. I would like you to locate the break and price the repairs.

Two stock replies I already know and do not want. First: heritability is a population-relative statistic, so it licenses no inference about individuals or about the causes of differences between groups. I grant that within-group heritability underdetermines the causes of a between-group difference in means, and the argument below is constructed so as not to depend on that point. Second: genetic does not mean immutable. I grant that too. Both claims are true and neither touches what I am after, so please do not restate either as the diagnosis; in particular, telling me that heritability is population-relative is a slogan, not an answer, unless you say which counterfactual a heritability figure does and does not encode.

The argument:

(1) Setting aside measurement error and the details of twin, family, and adoption designs, a heritability figure for a trait T in a population P states the proportion of the variance in T across members of P that is attributable to genetic differences among them as opposed to differences in their environments. To say h²(T) = 0.7 in P is to say that 70 percent of the variance in T there is genetic.

(2) That is a causal claim, not a merely predictive one: it says that genetic differences are part of the reason the members of P differ in T. If it were only a regression summary, nobody would cite heritability in disputes about what causes differences, and nobody would bother contesting the estimates.

(3) The only way a claim of the form "F accounts for share s of the variance in Y" can be causal at all is for it to be a difference-making claim: if you were to intervene so as to remove the differences in F, holding everything else fixed, the variance in Y would fall by fraction s. A causal claim with no counterfactual content is a correlation with a causal gloss painted on it.

(4) Now consider a compensatory intervention. Instead of equalizing environments indiscriminately, match each person's environment to their genotype so as to cancel out the genetic differences. Newborn screening for phenylketonuria followed by a low-phenylalanine diet for those who have it is a real instance; genotype-guided dosing and risk-score-targeted prevention would be others. Such an intervention changes nobody's genes; it is environmental in the ordinary sense.

(5) If such an intervention drives the variance in T to nearly zero, then by (3) the environment accounts for nearly all of the variance in T: h²(T) ≈ 0. Nothing about the genes has changed; only their consequences have been offset.

(6) There is a symmetric intervention: tailor each person's genotype or gene dosage to their environment so as to cancel out the environmental differences. If that intervention drives the variance to zero, then by (3) the genes account for everything: h²(T) ≈ 1.

(7) Neither sort of intervention is impossible in principle for an arbitrary heritable trait. The difference a genotype makes is always mediated by developmental processes that are in principle sensitive to some environmental variable, and the difference an environment makes is in principle compensable by some genetic or medical variable. We may not be able to design such regimes today for every trait, but nothing in the concept of heritability rules them out.

(8) So by (3), every heritable trait has h² ≈ 0 and also h² ≈ 1, which is absurd. Since (1) is a definition and (4)–(7) are modal possibilities, the culprit must be (2) or (3).

(9) Either way I get a conclusion I cannot accept. If (2) goes, heritability is a purely predictive statistic and can never be evidence that any difference has a genetic cause; if (3) goes, "due to genes" has no counterfactual content and heritability can support no prediction about what would happen under any intervention. But heritability seems to do both: it caps how much of the variance in that population could be contributed by environmental differences uncorrelated with genotype, and it predicts response to selection through the breeder's equation, which is a prediction about an intervention.

My own suspicion is that something slides between (4) and (5) — an intervention that removes variance by compensating for a cause does not show that the cause was not operating, and in the phenylketonuria case the success of the diet seems to confirm rather than refute the genetic causal claim. A friend says the rot is already in (3), and that if I repair (3) I will have made heritability unfalsifiable. I cannot tell who is right, and I would most like to keep (1), (2), and the claim that a heritability figure constrains environmental explanation within the population it was measured in. I would give up (3) if forced, but only if you can tell me what replaces it.

Which step do you reject or repair, why did it look sound, what would a defender of that step say in its favor, and what does giving it up cost someone who still wants heritability to do explanatory and predictive work?
