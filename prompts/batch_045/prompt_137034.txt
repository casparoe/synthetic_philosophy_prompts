So I've got two people who've been arguing about the phrase 'Western values' for about a decade and keep talking past each other, and I want the document they should have produced by now.

Call them the tradition realist and the coalition theorist. The tradition realist holds that the phrase names something real: a historically continuous, internally quarrelsome lineage of argument and institution — a cluster of commitments that hang together causally rather than by checklist, where the unity is genealogical and the disputes inside it are part of what makes it the thing it is. The coalition theorist holds that the phrase is mainly a persuasion gadget: a way to build an alliance and mark an opponent, whose apparent content is set by whatever it's currently against, and whose listed items are either shared by plenty of non-Western societies (making the adjective idle) or not actually accepted by most Western ones (making the noun a kind of aspirational fiction). Politically real, semantically thin.

What I want:

1. A numbered section of claims both of them could actually sign — and I mean non-vacuous ones, not 'reasonable people disagree'. Things like what the phrase does in an argument irrespective of whether it denotes anything, what would have to be true for it to denote something, and what neither side is claiming.

2. A disagreements table. One row per disagreement, with the realist's formulation and the coalition theorist's formulation, each phrased so that side would recognise it as their own view rather than a version propped up to be knocked down. If you can't find a fair wording for one side on some row, say that and explain why.

3. Three cruxes: sub-questions where a settled answer would move at least one of them. I've got candidates but use your own if they're better. (a) The swap test: take arguments that lean on 'Western values' and swap in a paraphrase — 'liberal-democratic values', 'Enlightenment values', 'modern societies' values', 'NATO values' — and ask what's lost. Does one substitution always work, do different ones fit different arguments, or does none? (b) The defector test: who is allowed to reject an item off the list and still claim the label, and who ticks every box and still gets refused it — and whether that pattern tells us the extension is set by doctrine or by geography, ancestry or alliance. (c) The lag problem: the list doubles as an accusation against the societies it's supposed to describe, so is it descriptive, normative, or permanently both. For each crux give one concrete case or piece of evidence both would accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance.

4. Flag which parts of their dispute are merely verbal, and why.

Please commit: say which side you think is closer to right on each crux and give the reason, and tell me where you're least confident — I'd rather have a definite call with a shaky reason than a survey that ends in 'both have a point'. And no citations, bibliographies, or suggested reading; I know you can't look anything up, so argue it out instead. Keep it in a US/European frame, and don't feel obliged to be even-handed for its own sake — just be even-handed about what each side actually holds.
