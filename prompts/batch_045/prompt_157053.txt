I've been reading a trade-press survey of contemporary moral philosophy — the kind written for general readers by a philosopher who is clearly competent but is compressing a lot — and I want to know how much of its chapter on contractualism to trust before I rely on it in something I'm writing. Here is the relevant passage, reconstructed in my own words and condensed, so the phrasing is mine rather than the author's:

"Contractualism is a sophisticated descendant of the social-contract tradition. Picture a hypothetical meeting of rational, mutually disinterested bargainers who must settle on rules for how to treat one another. Because each bargainer holds a veto and will use it to protect their own interests, the rules that emerge will be ones no one has good self-interested reason to refuse. An act is wrong, on this view, just in case it is ruled out by some set of rules that everyone could reasonably accept. Contractualism is therefore the main rival to utilitarianism, and its most-discussed weakness is the aggregation problem. Utilitarianism simply adds benefits and burdens across people, so it has no difficulty saying we should impose a serious loss on one person in order to spare thousands of people a much smaller loss each. Contractualism cannot say this, because it can only compare one person's complaint with another's, one pair at a time. The person who suffers the serious loss has a far stronger complaint than any single beneficiary, so she can veto any principle permitting the trade-off, and the thousands of small complaints are never added together. That is the aggregation problem, and it is widely regarded as fatal: a moral theory that cannot justify sparing thousands of people a small burden at the cost of one person's larger burden is not a serious candidate, so contractualism is refuted."

My instincts are uneven and I'd like them checked. The bargaining picture feels wrong to me: "reasonable" seems to be doing moralized work that "rationally self-interested" cannot do, and I doubt the view is a descendant of the social-contract tradition in the sense that phrase suggests. But I can't tell whether that is a harmless expository device or a real distortion of what the test requires — and I have no idea whether the move from "no one could reasonably reject" to "everyone could reasonably accept" in the summary is a mere stylistic variation or something that changes the verdicts. On the aggregation part I am genuinely lost. I keep encountering the flat claim that contractualism is committed to imposing small burdens on thousands of people rather than a larger burden on one, which sounds like a position nobody would hold, so I suspect that either the summary or the objection is a caricature — but I can't say which, or where the accurate core is.

So please give me an accuracy audit I can act on. Work through the passage claim by claim and sort it into: what a defender of the view would accept as written; what they would accept only after restating, together with the restatement; and what is simply not the view. Then tell me whether the conclusion at the end survives once the wording is cleaned up, and what the strongest surviving version of the aggregation objection actually looks like — is the aggregation problem a theorem about the view, or a contested pressure point that the view has partial resources against? Finish with a repaired paragraph of your own that a defender could sign off on.

Three things I want handled explicitly:

1. The two-case contrast I constructed while trying to test the aggregation claim myself. Case A: we can either impose a serious, permanent loss on one identified person — she loses the use of a hand — or impose a much smaller but real loss, a week of severe pain, on each of five thousand identified people, and everyone knows in advance exactly who bears what. Case B: the same distribution of outcomes, except that no one knows who will be the one: each of the five thousand and one has an equal chance of ending up with the serious loss, and the decision has to be made before the identities are revealed. Does the difference between A and B change what principles could be reasonably rejected, and if so, is that a principled difference or an artifact?

2. The repair I came up with while thinking about Case B, which I'll call the ex ante clause: when no one knows whether she will be the one to bear the concentrated loss, compare complaints from the standpoint each person occupies at the time of the decision, so the comparison is of prospects rather than of outcomes. Does that clause have principled room inside the framework, or does it smuggle in expected-utility aggregation through the back door? If it does, is that a fatal concession or a defensible one — and in particular, may an agent who does know the identities still reach for the clause by setting aside what she knows, or does that show the clause is a rationalization?

3. Whether the clause solves the aggregation problem or merely relocates it. It looks to me as though the cases that made the problem vivid are precisely the ones where we know who will suffer, so a fix that works only under uncertainty would leave the hardest cases untouched. Tell me if that's right, and what a defender would then have to say about those cases.
