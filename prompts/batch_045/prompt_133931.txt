I have been trying to make the "for want of a nail" style of historical argument precise, using a device I will call replay-robustness. Take an idealized deterministic model of some historical episode — a battle, a close legislative vote — that is chaotic: re-running it from initial conditions perturbed by an arbitrarily small amount produces a wide spread of trajectories over the relevant time scale. For a described outcome H, define its replay-robustness as the proportion of perturbed re-runs in which H still occurs. Note that this is always robustness under a description, and that it falls toward zero as the description gets finer: at the grain of exactly who stood where and who died where, nearly every re-run differs, chaotic system or not.

With that in hand:

(1) In a chaotic system there exist arbitrarily small differences in initial conditions that lead to very different later outcomes.
(2) So for any large historical outcome H, there is some small event E in its causal past such that, had E gone slightly differently, H would not have occurred.
(3) If H would not have occurred had E gone slightly differently, then E is a cause — a difference-maker — of H.
(4) Nothing in (1)–(3) privileges one small event over another: for any event in H's causal past, however trivial, there is some slight variant of it under which H fails. So every such event — the nail, the sneeze, the remark in the corridor — is equally a cause of H.
(5) An adequate causal explanation of H must cite H's causes; citing an arbitrary proper subset of them is not explanation but selection.
(6) Nothing in (1)–(4) distinguishes the events historians actually cite from the ones they ignore, since all alike are difference-makers under some variant.
(7) Therefore no historical explanation of a large outcome is ever adequate: the "for want of a nail" insight, generalized, destroys the practice it was meant to illustrate.
(8) But some historical explanations are plainly better than others, and a few are good.

Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. Of the premises, I would most want to keep (1) and (8). Three things I would like adjudicated in particular. First, whether the grain-relativity of replay-robustness is the load-bearing point here or a distraction. Second, whether the argument ends up proving too much — whether some version of (7) would follow even for a system that is deterministic but not chaotic, simply by describing outcomes finely enough — and if so what that tells us about which step is at fault. Third, whether giving up the step you reject forces us to abandon difference-making accounts of causation altogether, or only to relativize them to a grain; and if the latter, what could possibly entitle us to the grain we choose, and what happens to the nail if someone else's grain is legitimate too.

Finally, say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and name the single claim in your answer that you are least sure of.
