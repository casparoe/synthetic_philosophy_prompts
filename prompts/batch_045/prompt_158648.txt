At a dinner party last weekend two guests got into an argument about Malthus that neither would drop, and because I was the only other person still at the table I got appointed referee without having any view of my own. One of them, Marta, is a systems biologist who spends her time on feedback regulation in ecological models; the other, Deniz, works in economic history. I'll reproduce the exchange as closely as I can.

Marta opened by saying Malthus was right but early. The mechanism, as she put it, is a negative feedback loop: population presses on resources, and whatever gains in output per head occur get eaten up by extra people unless something holds population down; the things that hold it down come in two varieties, ones that raise deaths and ones that lower births. She said this describes the preceding several thousand years tolerably well and looks right for preindustrial England in particular. What broke it, she argued, was a historically unique package: coal, then oil, then industrial fixation of nitrogen, plus a large spare continent feeding the old one. But those are a stock and a pulse, not a permanent change in the structure of the system. On the timescale the theory actually operates on — centuries — the feedback should reassert itself. Score it right but early, and expect the reckoning when the cheap energy and cheap fertiliser are gone.

Deniz replied that a claim that can absorb any finite run of contrary evidence isn't early, it's untestable. And he said the failures aren't only failures of timing. Population growth since 1800 hasn't been geometric; child mortality fell first and then fertility fell, and fertility fell most where incomes rose, which inverts the link Malthus relied on — abundance now produces fewer children, not more. Famine in the last hundred years, he added, tracks war and distribution, not aggregate scarcity. So two load-bearing claims are refuted rather than merely postponed.

Marta conceded the second half of that and reframed the first: what Malthus really got right, she said, is the loop, not the channel. The modern decline in fertility is itself a check — a preventive one, working through deliberate choices rather than through hunger and disease. The homeostat is intact; only the variable that does the adjusting has changed. So the fair verdict is right about the loop, wrong about the channel, and "right but early" is just shorthand for saying that a real regulator was temporarily overpowered by an external input.

Deniz said that's a rescue by redefinition. If a check is anything that lowers fertility for any reason, then no observation can tell against the theory: whatever happens you point at some adjustment and call it a check. And he said the quantitative part — population compounding while food supply only adds — was the engine of the whole argument; once you drop it, what's left is the banality that a finite planet can't support exponential growth forever, which nobody denies and which isn't Malthus's thesis.

Marta then distinguished a hard core from its auxiliaries. The core, she said, is that population responds to resource pressure and that gains per head are self-limiting unless technical change keeps arriving; the auxiliaries are the specific ratios and the specific channels. Judged that way, the core has retrodictive successes in the pre-1800 data on wages, prices and population, and the escape after 1800 was driven by something the core treats as exogenous — the rate of technical change. So the honest verdict on the core is neither confirmed nor falsified but unfinished: the programme hasn't run out, and the interesting question is what happens when the fertiliser and fossil pulse decays or runs into its ecological ceiling.

Deniz's reply had two parts. First, hasn't-run-out-yet is untestable in a very specific way: the test is always still ahead of us. He asked Marta to name a date. Second, he said people conflate two claims: that positive checks operated before industrialisation, which is true, widely accepted, and something plenty of writers had noticed before Malthus; and the claim that income per head is pinned near subsistence over the long run, which is the thing that would make Malthus right in any interesting sense and which two centuries of evidence weigh against. He also said right-but-early requires having predicted the mechanism's return, whereas Malthus predicted it wouldn't go away.

I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning, and I'd like you to. Before you score anything, please separate Malthus's thesis into its independently assessable parts — at minimum the existence-and-shape of the feedback loop, the quantitative claim about relative growth rates, and the timing or horizon claim — and tell me whether Marta's right-but-early verdict even covers all three, or whether it is silently trading on one while the argument is about another. Then rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification each, and say who is more right overall.

Two things I'd particularly like you to take a position on rather than broker. First: Deniz demands an expiry date. Say what expiry date, if any, a long-run Malthusian claim could be given, what evidence would actually discriminate at that date, and whether a claim whose only test lies beyond any specifiable window belongs to science, to prophecy, or to some third category. I'd also like you to consider a verdict taxonomy Marta never offered: for the horizon claim specifically, is it false, not yet tested, or untestable in principle, and how would we tell those apart? Second: there is a rival causal arrow Marta didn't address, on which population density and scarcity induce the innovation that raises the ceiling rather than the ceiling pressing population down. Is that a genuine rival to the loop, or a special case of it that a Malthusian can absorb, and does absorbing it make the theory stronger or emptier?

Finally, be explicit about confidence. Tell me which of the empirical claims these two are leaning on — the pre-1800 dynamics, the causes of the post-1800 escape, the behaviour of fertility as incomes rise — are settled among economic historians and demographers and which are live controversies, and don't present a contested reading as what specialists think. And flag anywhere my own framing, or theirs, has smuggled in a contested assumption, and anywhere you are extrapolating beyond what the evidence supports. Please don't give me a reading list; I want the adjudication itself.
