I am a professor of philosophy who teaches, among other things, the theory of rules and legal systems, and for the past three years I have also served — unpaid, and increasingly against my better judgment — as parliamentarian to a learned society of about 1,100 members whose constitution is now amended at nearly every annual general meeting, and whose members have lately discovered the pleasures of amending the amendment rule itself. Our governing document is short but has accreted: Article 9.1 says the constitution may be amended at an annual general meeting by two thirds of the votes cast; 9.2, inserted later and never reconciled with 9.1, says Articles 1–8 may be amended by a simple majority at any general meeting; 9.3 says Article 9 may itself be amended only by a three-quarters vote with written notice given at the preceding annual general meeting; and 9.4 says no amendment may abolish or restrict the right of any member to vote at a general meeting. An amendment carried in 2019 added a supremacy sentence to 9.1 providing that no later amendment inconsistent with 9.4 shall be valid. My job is to rule, on the spot and in front of the room, on whether a proposed amendment is in order and what threshold it needs; I have about fifteen minutes in the meeting itself, a one-page written ruling afterwards, a tellers' committee of lay volunteers who must be able to follow the reasoning, and a council that will ratify whatever I say unless a member stands up and makes it look arbitrary. I have been working with a rule of thumb — an amendment must satisfy whichever amending provision was in force when it was moved — and it has now broken down twice, so I want something better.

What I would like from you is a decision procedure I can actually run, not a survey of jurisprudence: the questions in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a hard no, and where it stops and hands the question to a person because it is a question of values rather than of rules. Please build in at least the distinctions I keep having to improvise: between the route an amendment takes (the procedure used to adopt it) and its target (what it changes), and hence between amendments that are level-preserving and those that change the level of the rule they travel by; between an amendment that is properly adopted but reckless and one that is simply not valid; between the question I decide at the microphone (is this in order, what threshold applies) and the question that survives the meeting (was it validly made), since I cannot pronounce on the second with any finality; and — the case that cost me the most sleep — between an amendment that changes a protected provision directly and one that leaves its words alone while achieving its defeat by indirection. Also tell me what to do about sequencing: when two amendments are carried at the same meeting, does the second have to satisfy the rule as it stood when the meeting opened or as the first one left it; may a single resolution amend the threshold and immediately rely on the threshold it creates; if part of a resolution is valid and part not, do I sever or strike the whole; and how do I handle a resolution that conditions its own amendment rule on the assent of a body the same resolution creates. Then run the procedure on four cases: (A) a resolution carried 68–32, with no prior notice, to delete the notice requirement in 9.3; (B) a resolution carried 55–45 to lower 9.1's threshold to a simple majority; (C) a resolution carried 81–19 to amend Article 3 so as to create a class of dues-paying affiliate members with no vote at general meetings, leaving 9.4 textually untouched; (D) a properly noticed resolution carried with 78 per cent to lower 9.1 to a simple majority, followed twenty minutes later by an amendment to Article 6 carried 51–49. I want at least one case where the procedure yields an answer I will not like — I would rather have that than a procedure that quietly lets me reach the conclusion I prefer — and I want an explicit statement of what the procedure cannot settle at all.

One more thing, since I will be handing this to colleagues who know the literature as well as I do. For each step, please mark whether it follows from something reasonably settled about how systems of rules work, or whether it quietly embeds a contestable theoretical view — for instance about whether a power that the rules constitute can alter the conditions of its own exercise, whether entrenchment is genuinely possible in a system with no higher law above it, or whether a provision can bind the validity of amendments that come after it. Where a step rests on a contestable view, say so, name the rival view, and tell me what ruling it would produce instead on the same case. Do not smooth a live dispute into settled doctrine, and do not give me citations or reading suggestions: I have no library in the room where I need this, and a bibliography would be of no use to the tellers.
