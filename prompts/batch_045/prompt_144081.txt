I work in comparative constitutional law and institutional design — I draft and review the machinery by which public power is conferred, subdivided, and reviewed: delegation and subdelegation, ultra vires, entrenchment and amendment rules, constituent versus constituted power, competence-competence, and the ways a legal order stops an infinite regress of authorization (basic norms, rules of recognition, the fact that a successful seizure of power eventually becomes law). I am fluent in that vocabulary and use it daily. My mathematics is that of a well-read non-specialist: I know what a formal proof system is, I know the outline of Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, and I know the word "ordinal" without being able to do anything with it.

I have recently been told, at second hand, about the so-called Löbian obstacle for self-modifying or self-trusting artificial agents, and I want to know whether it is a real result about agency or an artifact of demanding the wrong kind of credential. My understanding of it is roughly this: an agent that reasons by proof cannot, by Löb's theorem, certify the soundness of its own reasoning; so if it proposes to build or authorize a successor that reasons as strongly as it does, it cannot prove that successor will behave well, and the standard way out is to have each successor reason in a strictly weaker system — which yields only a finite tower of generations, each step weaker than the last, before the line runs out. Please begin by stating the obstacle correctly in its own terms, as strongly as it can honestly be stated, and correct me if that summary is already a caricature. Then do the work I actually need.

Translate the obstacle, term by term, into the vocabulary of public law and institutional design. Give me the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of a validity claim, of a delegating authority, of a grant that exceeds the grantor's own powers, of a court's power to determine the scope of its own jurisdiction, of a rule whose validity cannot be shown without circularity and whose existence is instead manifested in practice, of fiduciary duty and ex post review standing in for ex ante assurance. Then give me the residue report, in three buckets: what translates cleanly, what translates only under some distortion that you must name, and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. Do not smuggle my framework's assumptions in and call the result a translation — where you have chosen to render "proof" as "legality" or "soundness" as "legitimacy," flag the choice and say what it buys and what it hides.

Four points in particular I want pressed rather than smoothed over. First: legal orders do permit self-authorization, sometimes cheerfully — a tribunal with competence-competence, a constitution that specifies its own amendment procedure, a Grundnorm that is presupposed rather than proved — whereas, on the telling I was given, a formal system that asserts its own soundness collapses. Is that a difference in subject matter or a difference in how much each of us is demanding? Second: the descending tower. In my world, a chain of delegations must indeed narrow as it descends, but nothing forces it to terminate in a fixed number of steps; we terminate it by fiat, or bottom out in a sovereign or a constituent act. Is the finiteness in the Löbian case a theorem about strength, or a design choice of the same kind wearing a proof-theoretic costume? Third: the obvious escape of replacing proof with track record — the agent trusts itself because trusting itself has worked — which in my vocabulary is legitimacy by efficacy and output rather than by pedigree, the move from legality to fact. Is that a solution, a dissolution, or a change of subject? Fourth: my discipline almost never asks a delegate to certify its own reliability in advance; we impose ex ante limits and ex post review, and we review the quality of the exercise rather than the outcome. Say plainly what in the agents' case prevents that substitution, and whether the obstacle is really about trust at all, or only about the impossibility of issuing a particular kind of certificate — a distinction my field insists on and the source discussion may be conflating.

Finally, the verdict. Is the residue evidence that the vocabulary of authorization, delegation, and review lacks the resources to talk about self-trust, or evidence that the obstacle's proponents are claiming something less crisp than "an agent cannot rationally trust itself" — and, if the latter, what exactly is the defensible claim? One aside: I half-remember hearing that there are logical systems which can prove their own consistency, but only by being too weak to do ordinary arithmetic. If something like that is right, tell me what it would correspond to in my vocabulary — a body that can validly declare its own jurisdiction only by giving up the power to count? If I have garbled it, say so and move on.
