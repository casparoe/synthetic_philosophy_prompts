Write an essay on whether the accuracy-first argument for probabilism survives once you drop the assumption that accuracy has to be measured in fine-grained distance-to-the-truth terms.

Setup: the standard argument picks a scoring rule (Brier-ish), notes that for any non-probabilistic credence function there's a probabilistic one that beats it at every world, and concludes that only probablistic credences are rational. Now compare a "coarse-first" measure: what matters first is your worst error over the partition (minimise the biggest miss, whatever the truth turns out to be), and only if two credence functions tie on that do you look at total squared distance. Notice that with a three-cell partition the function (0.51, 0.51, 0.51) — not a probability function at all — has a worst-case miss of 0.49, while any probability function has to give some cell at most 1/3 and so misses by at least 2/3 somewhere. (A variant counts "blunders", errors above 0.1, instead of taking the max; that one behaves differently.)

The essay should work out what the dominance argument turns into under a coarse-first measure, and then commit: does the framework still deliver probabilism, does it deliver something weaker like imprecise credences or permissivism, or does it break down? Defend whichever you think is right. Also say whether a coarse-first measure is even something an accuracy-first person should want, or whether it smuggles in a practical, good-enough notion of belief. Don't end on "it depends on the scoring rule".
