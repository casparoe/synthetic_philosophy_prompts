I run the onboarding programme for a joint venture whose engineering teams sit half in Rotterdam and half in Tokyo, and I spend an unreasonable amount of my time mediating the same argument: my Dutch colleagues treat a hedged refusal as dishonest and a waste of everyone's afternoon, while my Japanese colleagues treat an unhedged one as a small act of violence, and each side thinks the other is either evasive or brutal. Two of my senior engineers — one from each camp, both completely sincere and both unmoved by a year of lunches — have agreed to try an adversarial collaboration instead, and I have to hand them a draft to react to. Call the positions the assertion-norm position (there is a norm on assertion that no local code can dissolve: you may not get a hearer to believe something you know to be false, whether by a flat falsehood or by exploiting an indirect code to convey one; politeness has to be achieved by other means — silence, topic-shift, selectively true answers, hedges that are epistemically honest — and a white lie is at best a minor vice, usually a real one) and the accommodation position (speech is a cooperative, face-maintaining practice; what counts as saying something false is settled by the code actually in force between these speakers; a hearer who gets what they need, in the form they expect and would prefer, has not been wronged, and some polite falsehoods are not merely permitted but owed). I want the document they could both actually sign.

Please draft it, in British English, as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. I want the agreed claims to be substantive rather than platitudes — both camps, I think, can grant that bluntness used as a weapon is a vice, that a hearer's preference is not by itself the whole of the moral story, and that the familiar murderer-at-the-door sort of case is a limiting case rather than a template. For the table, give each residual disagreement in two formulations, one that each side would accept as a fair statement of its own view, plus a column marking whether you take the dispute to be genuine or merely verbal, with a sentence of justification where you say verbal. What I really want, though, is the cruxes, and I have some candidates I'd like you to work through rather than just list: (i) whether conventionalised indirectness — the sort of hedge that everyone in the community knows means no — is an assertion at all, or is closer to a phatic greeting; (ii) the uptake gap, i.e. hearers who lack the code: children, new arrivals, non-native speakers, literal-minded machine interlocutors such as scheduling assistants that book the tentative yes; does the norm calibrate to the hearer's competence, and if not, is that the hearer's misfortune or the speaker's fault; (iii) the overhearer case, where the addressee sees through the polite form and a third party in the room does not — a lie to whom; (iv) who bears the cost of accommodation in a mixed-code team, and whether an interpretive burden that falls steadily on the minority-code speakers is itself a moral cost the accommodation camp has to count; (v) the aggregate or epistemic-commons question, whether a widespread convention of indirectness degrades the local information pool in a way that is unfair to whoever can least afford a decoding failure; and (vi) the explicit-request case, where a hearer says in advance, please be blunt with me, I will not pick up hints — does either camp then owe bluntness, and does the answer differ if the request comes from a Dutch colleague, a new hire, or someone who is simply bad at reading hints. For each crux I want: the precise sub-question, why settling it would move at least one side, one concrete case or observation both sides would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction about that case written down in advance.

Two constraints. First, I am not asking for a verdict: do not tell me which camp is right, and do not smooth the disagreement into agreement that neither of them would sign. Second, assume the drafter has no access to the empirical literature: no studies, no citations, no statistics, and no claims about what research shows. Where a crux turns on how people actually behave, say what you take to be common knowledge and flag it as such; where you would need a study, specify the design and record what each camp expects it to find instead of reporting a result. Aim for something around 1,200 to 1,800 words, and close with a single sentence both of them could accept going into the onboarding handbook for mixed teams.
