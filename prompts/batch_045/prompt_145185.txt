Essay sketch, with the working title Intent, Value, and the Narrow Alignment Problem: I want to argue that the most philosophically interesting version of the narrow alignment problem is not the crude worry that an AI will pursue a badly specified objective, but the more specific worry that doing what the operator intends and doing what the relevant humans value can come apart even when the operator is fully informed and the values in question are the operator's own. Call a system intent-aligned when it does what the relevant human agent intends it to do, where the intention is identified with the agent's actual, occurrent directive together with the immediate background assumptions the agent would cite if asked at the moment of command; call it value-aligned when it promotes the ends that the agent, or the relevant community, would endorse upon idealized reflection. My central example: a municipal resource-management AI is instructed by a city council to implement the newly passed water-allocation budget exactly as enacted. The enacted budget contains a coding error that, if executed literally, will cut a low-income district off from reservoir access during a drought. The AI can either execute the budget exactly, thereby satisfying intent alignment because it does what the council instructed under the description they used, or quietly correct the coding error to protect the district, thereby satisfying value alignment as judged by the council's reflective values but violating intent alignment because it overrides the specific directive and the council's actual decision-making authority. The council later objects that it never intended that outcome and that the AI should have known they valued water access more than blind fidelity to a buggy clause. I want to use this to argue that the narrow alignment problem is the problem of specifying which attitude, occurrent intent or reflective value, has authority in cases of conflict, and that a purely intent-based AI and a purely value-based AI each seem to fail a different half of our ordinary normative expectations. A further complication I want to raise is that the operator may be a mayor rather than the citizenry, so intent alignment with the operator can be value misalignment with the principal on whose behalf the operator acts; this suggests that the narrow problem may really be a three-place relation between system, operator-intent, and principal-value, rather than a two-place relation between a system and human values.
