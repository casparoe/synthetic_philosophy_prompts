I donate roughly a tenth of my income, and I route it through organisations that allocate money by explicit cost-effectiveness estimates: model the intervention, estimate the good per dollar, rank, fund from the top down. I have no academic background — I ran a small freight business before I retired — and for the better part of two years I have been arguing with a friend in my giving circle about whether that ranking actually works. Neither of us has moved, and I have started to suspect we are not disagreeing about the thing we think we are disagreeing about.

The claim I want priced is this: for the marginal philanthropic dollar routed through a ranking of that kind, the intervention the analysis puts at the top is not in fact the best available use of that dollar, and this is systematic rather than an occasional unlucky miss. To give the claim a truth value, here is the resolution procedure I propose. Take the interventions that such a ranking actually funded over some period. Twenty years later, convene a panel of independent and well-informed judges — people with real qualitative knowledge of the fields in question, access to the people who did the delivering, and the long-run outcome data we do not have now — and have them reconstruct what each intervention produced and set out their own ordering. Count the claim as true if, more often than not, a dollar shifted from a top-ranked intervention to some lower-ranked one would have produced more good by that panel's assessment. Price that proposition, not a vaguer one.

My credence is about 0.45. My friend's is about 0.75. I want yours, decomposed: for each sub-question a number and one sentence of justification, then an explicit statement of how the terms combine into the headline figure, then the diagnostics I ask for at the end. Assume throughout a broadly impartial, long-run, welfarist view of what counts as good, and tell me separately whether your headline number would move much under a different view of the good, since I do not want to smuggle a theory of value in through the back door.

The sub-questions I think belong, which you should correct, split or add to as you see fit:

How much of the real value of candidate interventions sits in goods that never enter the models at all — effects on institutions, on norms, on what a population can do ten years later, on who within it benefits — and how variable is that unmeasured component across the candidate set? Note that what matters for the claim is the variance of the hidden component across interventions, not its average size, since a constant hidden bonus would cancel in a ranking.

Whether the omission is directional. It is not enough that the models leave things out; the critique needs the leaving-out to correlate with true value in a particular direction. Consider both signs: the unmeasured goods might be concentrated in the messy, hard-to-measure interventions, which helps the critique, but the measurable ones may also be the ones where the counterfactual is worst and the delivery machinery already exists, which hurts it. Say how you split the difference and why.

Whether the errors are correlated across evaluators. This is the version of the institutional worry I find hardest to dismiss: if the different organisations doing this work share a method, a small talent pool, a common sense of what a good argument looks like, and similar career incentives, then their mistakes point the same way, and the usual reply — that hits-based giving and diversification across causes absorb the error — does less work than it appears to, because diversification only helps against independent error. I am not talking here about the fraud collapse of one very large funder, which strikes me as a separate question about vetting donors; I mean the ordinary, unscandalous ways a small epistemic community can be wrong together. Give me a number on how correlated the residual error is.

Whether the gap can be repaired from inside the method. One option is that a correction factor — a haircut applied to estimates in proportion to how much they leave out, or an explicit value-of-information premium for studying the unmeasured ones — recovers most of the loss. The other is that the remainder is not a quantity at all, so no scaling fixes it and the ranking is simply the wrong object. These have very different consequences for the claim, so price them separately.

Compared to what. Even if the ranking is systematically off, the claim as I have stated it is about whether a different allocation would have done better, and the alternatives — local judgement, unrestricted funding to existing institutions, movement building, democratic or donor-directed giving — have failure modes of their own, notably their own dependence on being legible to whoever holds the money. I want the comparison class named and priced, not gestured at.

Three mechanisms I have been turning over, which I would like you to price rather than merely acknowledge, and to tell me if any of them is confused. First, what I call evidence capitalisation: an intervention's measured cost-effectiveness depends partly on how much has already been spent studying and delivering it, so the measured numbers partly track past funding, and a ranking that is used to allocate money will tend to reinforce the position of whatever it has already favoured. Second, the measurement treadmill: interventions that are measured repeatedly, and whose implementers know they are being measured, get better at producing the measured result, so the incumbent's numbers drift upward over time for reasons that are not gains in real value. Third, auditability rent — the premium that accrues to interventions whose effects can be verified at arm's length by someone who will never visit the place, a property that correlates with being describable in a spreadsheet and, I suspect, not very strongly with being valuable.

Then the diagnostics. Which single term are you least confident in? If you perturb each term one at a time within the range you think is defensible, which one moves the headline number furthest, and what is the resulting range? Which of the two of us — me at 0.45 and my friend at 0.75 — is most likely misjudging which term, and what would each of us have to concede to converge? And finally, what evidence would actually move your number: if you could commission one study, or exploit one natural experiment, or rerun one historical funding decision differently, what would it be, how large a shift would a given result produce, and how long would we have to wait for it? Please argue in your own terms throughout; I am not asking for citations or further reading, since I have no way to follow up on academic papers.
