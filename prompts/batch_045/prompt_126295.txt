Write a short story (around 1,000–1,400 words, literary rather than allegorical) that dramatises Fernand Braudel's three-layered sense of historical time: the longue durée of geography, climate and slowly moving structures; the conjoncture of economic and demographic cycles spanning decades; and the événement, the day-scale surface of decisions, edicts and battles.

Set the whole thing in one invented place, and let the place do the arguing: a delta where the river has abandoned its channel and cut a new one every few centuries, and where each dead channel survives on land as a property boundary, a tax district and a local legend, so that the cadastral map is a stack of fossil rivers and fields are still measured against water that no longer runs. Render the same occurrence in three sections — one night, one human lifetime, one stretch of ten thousand years: a community's decision, during a flood, to cut a new outlet across a neighbour's land, and the litigation, migration and silting that follow it. The man who makes the decision in the first section should be treated with sympathy rather than irony: while he is deciding, his conviction that the outcome really is up to him must feel true to the reader, and no character may deliver the moral in a speech about history — the different scales should be felt through what each section narrates and what it leaves out. Let the story take an actual position on whether the longest scale dissolves the event altogether, or whether the event has a thickness and a contingency that no long-run structure can recover, rather than settling for the easier moral that individuals and their choices do not matter. Close with an afterword of about a hundred words naming which of the three timescales your story in fact makes causally decisive, and what in it that scale fails to explain.
