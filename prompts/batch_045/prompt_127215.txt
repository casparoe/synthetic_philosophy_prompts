I am marking a take-home exam for a graduate seminar on judgment and decision making and would like an independent reading before I finalise the grades. The question was:

**Question (25 marks).** "The contrast between the inside view and the outside view is often presented as giving a general argument for epistemic modesty about one's own plans. Explain the contrast, state that argument, and assess how strong it is. In your answer you must also discuss the following case.

A team is forecasting the cost and schedule of a first-of-a-kind direct air capture plant. Their bottom-up estimate is $400m and four years. They then assemble a reference class of thirty comparable first-of-a-kind energy and chemical demonstration plants. For each, the database records the estimate made at the point of sanction and the final out-turn: the median estimate in the class is $350m and three and a half years, the median out-turn $900m and seven years. The team sets its forecast at the median out-turn, $900m and seven years.

A colleague objects that the class was selected on the basis of, and characterised by, exactly the kind of optimistic inside-view estimates the outside view was supposed to correct for: the sanction estimates came from the same bottom-up process that produced the team's own $400m, and the recorded overruns are measured against them. On this objection the adjusted forecast inherits the very bias it was meant to remove. Assess the objection. Does the outside view still have anything to offer here, and if so what? Does your answer depend on whether the team's own estimate came from the same kind of process as the estimates in the class?"

Below is one student's answer. Please grade it out of 25 and give me the marginal comments I could hand back with it: a mark, a brief breakdown by part of the question, and specific notes on which claims are correct, which are only half right, and which are mistaken — including anything a hasty reader would wave through.

**Student's answer**

"The inside view is what you get by looking closely at the case in front of you: you decompose the job into work packages, price each one, talk to the engineers, and arrive at $400m and four years. The outside view is the perspective of someone who is not inside the project — an external reviewer, a board, a consultant — who has not spent two years living with the design and has not become attached to it. In practice you get it by placing the project in a reference class of similar projects and looking at the distribution of outcomes there. Kahneman's work on the planning fallacy is the standard illustration of why the two come apart: forecasters working from the inside imagine the most likely route to completion and neglect the many ways a project can slip, so their forecasts are systematically optimistic.

The argument for epistemic modesty is straightforward. Modesty about your own judgement means not trusting your own reading of the details when there is a track record to consult; you defer to what generally happens rather than to yourself. Once you have taken the outside view, the details of the particular case carry no evidential weight of their own — they matter only insofar as they put the case into a class with a different distribution. So the team was right to move from $400m to $900m, and arguably should have moved further.

The colleague's objection is, I think, sound, and more damaging than it first appears. Two things go wrong. First, the reference class is not given; choosing it is itself a judgement call. The team chose first-of-a-kind demonstration plants, but it could have chosen all energy projects, or all projects of this size, or all projects its own firm has attempted, and each class would yield a different median. Because selecting the class requires the very case-specific judgement the outside view was meant to set aside, the outside view is contaminated by the inside view from the outset. Second, here the contamination is direct: the figures in the database are overruns measured against sanction estimates produced by the same optimistic bottom-up process that generated the team's own $400m. If the base rates are just other people's inside views, then consulting them is still consulting an inside view — deference to a crowd of forecasters biased in the same direction.

So nothing is gained by the adjustment. I would discount the $900m heavily and move back toward the team's own figure, which at least reflects the specifics of this plant. The outside view is worth taking only where the reference class is fixed in advance by someone independent; here it was not, so the inside view should dominate."
