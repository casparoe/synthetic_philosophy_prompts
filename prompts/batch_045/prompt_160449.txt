You are a professional philosopher who works at the intersection of rational choice theory, collective action, and democratic theory, and who is comfortable doing the relevant arithmetic rather than gesturing at it: pivot probabilities under uncertainty, expected value when probabilities are very small, and the difference between a vote that flips a discrete outcome and a vote that nudges a continuous margin. Please think carefully and do genuine brainstorming before you commit: I would rather see four or five candidate principles sketched, tested, and mostly discarded than one principle defended from the start. Work step by step, and show the arithmetic where the arithmetic is what decides the case.

I help run a small nonpartisan turnout campaign. Every election cycle I have to defend, out loud, a line about when people really ought to vote and when it is genuinely fine not to, and about whether we should keep urging people to the polls when they have not followed the campaign at all. I want something I can actually use in that argument: a principle, not a slogan. I should say up front that I have no library and no web access, so please do not give me citations, names to look up, or reading suggestions. Give me the argument itself, and where a number matters, give me the number and how you got it.

Here are my verdicts, given as honestly as I can. Where I have a verdict and a feeling that come apart, I say so.

1. A national election. I live in a state that has gone the same way by twenty points or more for a decade, and the polls agree. Voting costs me a two-hour round trip on a workday; there is no mail ballot. The stakes are a national government. My verdict: skipping is fine. I do not think I have done anything wrong, and I would not criticize anyone in my position who skipped. I usually do vote anyway, out of habit, but I cannot reconstruct an argument that I must.

2. The same national election, but I live in a state the polls have at within half a point, eight million voters, and the polling place is a twenty-minute walk. My verdict: I should vote, clearly, and someone in my position who skipped for no reason has done something mildly wrong.

3. My condo association, sixty owners, mailed ballot, five minutes to fill in. The question is a one-hundred-and-eighty-thousand-dollar roof assessment, about three thousand dollars each. My verdict: I should vote, obviously, and abstaining out of laziness really is a small moral failing.

4. The national election again, in my safe state, but a small party is polling at four-point-six percent nationally and needs five percent to keep its public funding and its ballot line. My vote cannot affect who wins. My verdict: voting feels like it has a real point here in a way it does not in case 1, and I would encourage people to vote for that reason. I know the chance my one ballot moves a national total across a line is microscopic. I cannot explain why this feels different from case 1, but it does.

5. A city election, forty thousand voters. Candidate A will certainly win, but the city charter sends the race to a runoff if A finishes under forty percent, and A is polling at forty-one. I would like a runoff. My verdict: I would vote and I would drive across town to do it. This feels more like case 2 than like case 1, even though I am not trying to change who wins.

6. My brother has followed nothing this year. He cannot name a single policy difference between the candidates and would pick essentially at random. The election is competitive and he lives ten minutes from a polling place. My verdict: he has no duty to vote, and I think he has some reason to stay home; but if he does vote, I would not blame him, and I would never say so to his face. My campaign's official line is that everyone should vote, and I have started to suspect the official line is false.

7. An election abroad that is decided in advance, where the tally is nevertheless counted and published and is read by outside observers as a measure of how much opposition there is. Voting carries a small risk of being photographed at the polling station. My verdict: I would still vote, and I would accept the risk. The published number seems to be doing real work even though the outcome is fixed.

8. I am living abroad during a close, high-stakes election at home. Getting a ballot notarized and couriered costs about one hundred and fifty dollars and several hours. My verdict: I would do it myself, but I would not criticize anyone who did not, and I do not think the state should have made the choice that expensive.

The pair I keep going back and forth on is 1 and 4. Both are safe-state votes in an election whose outcome my ballot will not change; the probability that my ballot crosses a national five-percent threshold cannot be larger than the probability that it breaks a tie in my own state, and is probably smaller. Yet I treat one as optional and the other as worth encouraging. My tentative principle, which I do not trust, is that I should vote just in case my expected effect on what happens — counting both the chance of flipping a result and the expected effect on the recorded margin or on any threshold the margin crosses, neither of which is ever exactly zero — exceeds the cost to me of voting. I notice that this principle seems to make case 1 mandatory too, which my verdict rejects, so I want it repaired or replaced.

Please do the following.
