Newcomb's problem has now been argued about for more than fifty years, and as far as I can tell the argument sits exactly where it began. A 2020 survey of professional philosophers found a plurality for two-boxing at roughly 39 percent against roughly 31 percent for one-boxing, and the central moves on each side have been on the table since the start. The two-boxers appeal to dominance: whatever is in the opaque box is already there, so taking the transparent box adds a thousand dollars whatever the state of the world turns out to be, and at the moment of choice the million is either present or not and nothing you do now can alter that. The one-boxers appeal to the expected value of the act conditional on its being performed: with a predictor whose track record is, say, ninety-nine percent, the two-boxers in the record walk away with a thousand dollars and the one-boxers with a million, and it is cold comfort to be told that the million was never really available to you. Both sides have replies ready: the two-boxers insist on the difference between an act's causing an outcome and its merely indicating one, while the one-boxers reply that causal independence between act and state is not the same as the act being the right thing to hold fixed, and that the dominance argument quietly helps itself to the very independence the example denies. Meanwhile a third family of proposals, developed mostly by people whose actual question is what procedure to build into an artificial agent and which travels under labels like logical or functional decision theory, recommends evaluating procedures rather than acts: you should ask not what would happen if you took both boxes, but what an agent running your decision procedure gets in situations of this structure, and this, its defenders say, recovers one-boxing without any suggestion that your choice reaches backwards into the prediction.

I have been trying to pry the disagreement apart with two variants, and I want to know whether either of them isolates anything real.

First, the amnesiac series. You play the same game a thousand times against the same predictor, but before each round you are given a drug that erases all memory of earlier rounds; you are re-read the rules, you are never told the outcomes, and you know that each prediction is made independently from a model of your general dispositions rather than from any record of your past play. One-boxing in every round still nets you on the order of a billion dollars and two-boxing on the order of a million. But no round's choice has any causal influence on any other round's prediction, there is no reputation to build, no disposition to train in yourself, nothing to learn and nothing to signal. Whatever the thought "be the kind of person who one-boxes" amounts to, it cannot here amount to a causal story. Does that strip the one-boxing case down to something the two-boxer must answer, or does it merely restate the original problem a thousand times?

Second, the sealed simulation. Replace the predictor with a mechanism you are shown in full. A high-fidelity snapshot of you was taken a week ago; a simulation of you was run on this very decision problem; the opaque box was filled according to what the simulation chose; and you are told that this simulation method has been validated to agree with its subjects across a large battery of other decisions. There is now nothing occult about the correlation between your choice and the contents of the box, and no free-will puzzle anywhere in the neighborhood — only the fact that the process that filled the box and the process now making your choice are, in the relevant sense, the same process running twice. Does either side's argument change once the predictor is demystified in this way? And if neither argument changes, what does that tell us about what the two sides were ever disagreeing about?

My own hypothesis is that the dispute is not about decision theory at all, but about what a decision theory is for. Read as a normative theory addressed to someone standing in front of the boxes asking what it would be rational to do now, the dominance argument looks unanswerable. Read as a specification for what to build — which procedure to instantiate in a system that will repeatedly face situations with this structure — the case for one-boxing looks equally unanswerable. The two questions have different correct answers, each camp is tacitly answering its own, and each then finds the other camp's verdict not merely wrong but incomprehensible. But I cannot tell whether that is the whole of it. The alternatives I keep circling are: (a) a hidden question in the semantics of counterfactuals — whether, in a near-deterministic world containing a near-perfect predictor, a counterfactual such as "if I had taken both boxes, the opaque box would have been empty" is even well-defined and has a determinate probability, and whether the machinery causal decision theory uses to evaluate subjunctive conditionals can be applied here without circularity or regress; (b) the more deflationary suggestion that the original problem description is simply underdetermined, and that the two sides are each filling in a different decision problem and then correctly solving the one they filled in; and (c) the view that this is bedrock intuition all the way down, that no argument on either side has ever moved anyone, and that nothing should be expected to.

What I would like from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and a diagnosis that engages the positions as I have described them rather than surveying the literature. Which of these is the disagreement, or what mix of them — verbal or substantive, a clash of questions, a clash of standards for what counts as a reason, a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work, or intuition with no argumentative upthread? Where exactly does each of my two variants bite, and where does it fail to bite? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal result, a distinction, a reformulation of the question, an empirical fact about how simulable agents are, or nothing at all — together with an honest account of why we should expect no resolution if that is where you land. I would rather have a diagnosis I can argue with than a balanced overview.
