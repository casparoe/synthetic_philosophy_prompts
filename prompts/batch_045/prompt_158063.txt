My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about psychiatric advance directives, and we ended in a stalemate that I would like to turn into a poem rather than another long email.

The case we got stuck on — call it the rebound clause — is this. A woman with a cycling condition signs, during a low phase, a directive whose operative clause says that if she later refuses treatment while in a high phase, that refusal is the illness speaking and is to be overridden. Months later, in a high phase, she attaches an addendum saying the mirror image: if she later asks for treatment while in a low phase, that request is the illness speaking and is to be refused. Neither clause is rash or ill-informed; each was written calmly, with counsel, and each accurately reports how the other phase feels from inside it. Years later she is in a low phase and asking for the treatment. Everyone involved knows that the treatment works reliably and quickly, and that once it works she will revoke the addendum and reaffirm the original directive — which is to say that honoring either document determines which of her shows up next to authenticate it. My friend's position is that this just shows directives cannot bind; they can only inform, and the only real question is what the person in front of you now wants. Mine is that what she wants now is not a clean datum here, because the content of her present wanting is supplied by the very phase whose testimony one of the documents instructs us to discount, and that there is no phase-neutral standpoint from which to rank the phases, since each phase has left a paper declaring the other one the illness. We agreed that whichever way you go, you are relying on a ranking you could not justify from outside both states, and then we ran out of things to say.

What I want is a villanelle in which the two refrains are the two documents' operative clauses. Please write it as a strict nineteen-line villanelle — five ABA tercets and a closing ABAA quatrain, with the first refrain at lines 1, 6, 12 and 18 and the second at lines 3, 9, 15 and 19 — in iambic pentameter throughout, with both refrains repeated verbatim: no swapped words, no variation at the close. Let the end-words of the refrains set the two rhyme sounds the rest of the poem works in.

One refrain should be in the voice of the woman in the low phase, phrased so that it reads equally as the signed clause of the first directive and as her present request at the bedside; the other in the voice of the high phase, the addendum's clause, which she will say again as soon as the treatment takes hold. Both should speak as instruments speak — imperative, addressed to the clinicians who will have to act — and each should be identifiable as belonging to its phase without ever being labeled, so that anyone who has followed the argument can tell which line is which and why it belongs to that phase and not the other.

The six non-refrain lines should carry the story and the philosophical weight: the signing, the cycling, the chart being opened, the treatment taking effect, the revocation that follows — and, somewhere in the last tercet, the point that whichever phase you designate as disordered is ipso facto the one whose testimony you stop crediting, and that no standpoint outside both phases is available to settle which designation is right.

The closing quatrain should put the two clauses side by side for the first time and leave them there: neither capitulating, neither answered, the mutual cancellation itself being the argument. I do not want it resolved into honor the document or treat the patient in front of you, and I do not want a moral drawn. It should also be even-handed enough that my friend, who thinks directives should never bind, will not think I have stacked it — no glamorizing the high phase, no pity for the low one, no sneer at either.

Keep the diction plain and contemporary American English, nothing archaic or ornate, and keep it short enough to read aloud in about two minutes. Ideally it should work as a poem for someone who has never heard the term advance directive, while still being exact enough that someone who works on personal identity and the authority of the earlier self would not wince at the philosophy. If some line has to be sacrificed to keep the form honest, sacrifice narrative rather than accuracy.
