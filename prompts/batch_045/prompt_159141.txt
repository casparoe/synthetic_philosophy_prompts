Hume says that the convention by which property and justice are established is not itself a promise; on his view, promises later acquire their binding force from convention, so convention cannot be explained as a kind of promise. Instead, the convention is a shared sense of common interest. He illustrates the point with the first-person claim that it is in my interest to leave another person in possession of the goods of that person, provided that person will act in the same way toward me, and that the other person is similarly aware of a like interest. When this common sense of interest is mutually expressed and recognized, it produces corresponding resolutions and behavior. He is willing to call this a convention or agreement even without any promise, because the action of each person refers to the action of the other and is performed on the supposition that the other will perform the part of the other. His example is two men rowing a boat: they coordinate the strokes of the oars by convention although they have exchanged no promises. He then anticipates an objection: the rule of the stability of possession might seem not to derive from convention because it arises gradually and acquires force by slow progression and by repeated experience of the inconveniences of transgressing it. He replies that this gradual development does not make the rule any less a product of human convention. On the contrary, repeated experience of inconvenience assures people even more firmly that the sense of interest has become common to all members of society, and it gives them confidence in the future regularity of the conduct of others. It is only on the expectation of that future regularity, he concludes, that moderation and restraint are founded.
