I start a funded PhD in metaphysics in October — four years, and my supervisor wants me to have committed to a topic by roughly next summer. I want to spend it somewhere in the counterpart theory vs. transworld identity cluster, but everytime I try to pick off a piece of it I end up feeling like I'd just be doing Lewis scholarship with extra steps, so I'd like an outside view on where the live questions actually are.

Constraints, so the prioritisation has something to be relative to: it's me and one supervisor, four years, no empirical work. I'm much stronger formally (modal logic, model theory, a bit of philosophical logic) than I am on the history. Ideally whatever I pick ends up mattering to people who don't already care about Lewis — philosophers of physics, or people working on persistence and personal identity, or formal semanticists. And I'd like to publish along the way rather than produce one big thing in year four.

So: what are, say, four lines of work in this cluster that you'd actually prioritise, in order, and what makes them worth four years of one person? I want the reasoning more than the list. For each one: what else it's upstream of, whether it's tractable for someone with my background in the time I have, whether it's neglected or already crowded, and what I'd have to find out in the first six months that would make me move it up or down. Where importance and tractability pull apart, tell me which way you'd go in my position, and why.

Also please say which questions this subfield seems to spend time on are overrated — ones you could settle and nothing downstream would move. I'd rather hear that now than in year three.

Three hunches I keep circling, in case they're worth anything. Tell me where they'd rank, or if they're just confused:

1. Actual duplicates. Physics seems to hand us cases where there are many things exactly alike, differing only in which one they are — identical particles, or, if the universe is big enough, exact repeats of local arrangements. But then: either we already need something counterpart-theoretic to handle duplicates inside a single world, in which case the counterpart theorist isn't adding anything distinctive when she goes across worlds, or duplicates within a world are handled by plain identity and the real question is why cross-world cases should be treated differently. I can't tell which way it cuts, and I suspect the answer determines how much is genuinely at stake in the whole dispute.

2. Same dispute, other joints. The choice between a 'counterpart'-style relation and plain identity across cases seems to reappear for persistence through time, for fictional characters across stories, and for groups and institutions — what is it for the university to have done otherwise? If it's really one stance showing up in four places, maybe the high-value work is the general question: what it costs to go counterpart-theoretic as a policy, and whether the costs are the same each time. Or maybe the cases are disanalogous in ways that make the general question empty, and I'd be better off just doing the modal one properly.

3. Whether de re modal claims have determinate truth conditions at all. The counterpart theorist gets the reply that whether I could have been a pianist depends on which similarity relation is salient, and that sounds like a bug. But maybe it's a feature, and the actual programme is to work out what the space of admissible counterpart relations is and what constraints a decent one has to satisfy — in which case the debate was never identity versus counterparts, but about how much structure the counterpart relation needs. Is that a real line of work or just changing the subject?

If you think one of my three deserves the top slot, say so and defend it. If you think all three are weak, say what I should pick instead.
