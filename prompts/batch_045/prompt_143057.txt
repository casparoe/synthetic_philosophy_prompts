I'm putting together a term paper on multiple realizability for a philosophy of mind reading group, and I've reached the point where I no longer trust the framing we have been using. Everyone in the group is happy to say that the same mental state can be realized by different physical states, and one member offered the usual hardware-and-software picture: the mental state is like a program, the neural state is like the machine it runs on, and of course the program could run on something else. But when I tried to write down what the thesis is supposed to do for us, I found two jobs that may not be the same job. It has to make the argument against type-identity theory bite, and it has to let us decide, in some non-arbitrary way, whether an unfamiliar system counts as a realizer of a given state: a trained neural network whose internal organization we can characterize only statistically, an octopus, a silicon emulation of a human brain, a slowly self-rewiring brain in a single individual over decades, an insect colony treated as one cognizer. So before I read any further into the literature, I want the specification. What would an account of what it is for a physical state or system to realize a mental state have to deliver in order to be adequate for those two jobs, and what is the case or consideration behind each requirement?

Here is what I have already gathered, which I would like you to organize, correct, and extend rather than simply endorse. First, realization looks three-place rather than two-place: a state realizes a mental state only relative to some specification of the causal role, and since indefinitely many true causal-role descriptions are satisfied by any given physical state, the account owes us a principled restriction on which specifications are admissible; otherwise multiple realizability comes for free and refutes type identity trivially, and by the same stroke refutes the possibility of any exceptionless psychophysical correlation whatsoever. Call this the cheap-realizability problem. Second, the account must say whether realization is all-or-nothing or degreed, and handle the near-miss: a system that satisfies the role specification almost but not exactly, or that satisfies it exactly on Tuesday and less exactly after damage or retraining; if it is degreed, we need to know whether the threshold is principled or stipulated. Third, any claim that two systems share a mental state while sharing no physical property appeals to similarity in causally relevant respects, and I cannot see how to state which respects those are without circularity. Fourth, the realized property must retain causal work of its own rather than dissolving into an epiphenomenal shadow, while the account must still fit the causal completeness of physics; I suspect this is where the list breaks, and I want that said explicitly if so. Fifth, the account must steer between two extremes: making realization so demanding that no unfamiliar system could ever be shown to realize anything, and making it so permissive that every system realizes every state under some mapping, with nothing but an unmotivated restriction doing the work. Sixth, it should tell us what evidence could settle the question for a black-box system, and it should let us compare realizers picked out by competing psychological theories and say whether they are realizers of the same state.

Please mark, for each requirement on your list, whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or merely the prejudice of one camp, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories. If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, say so directly and show where the conflict comes from, because that is the result I would most want to write about. I also have a specific suspicion I would like you to test: that the argumentative force of multiple realizability comes from an equivocation between the claim that one and the same mental state can be had by physically heterogeneous systems and the much stronger claim that no physical kind is even nomologically coextensive with any mental kind, with the hardware-and-software picture naturally suggesting the first while the argument against type identity needs the second; tell me whether a good theory of realization should keep these apart, and whether keeping them apart costs the argument anything. Finally, a brief scorecard, a few lines apiece, for how the main contenders do against your list: type-identity theory, a flat causal-role view, a dimensioned view on which realizers contribute their own causal powers rather than merely occupying a slot, a subset view, and the usual Ramsified recipe where the whole psychological theory is quantified over at once.
