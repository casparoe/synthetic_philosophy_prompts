I keep having the same argument about how much weight the outside view deserves when forecasting the survival of a large polity. Take the question: will the United States still be a single continuous political entity in 2125? My interlocutor's move is always the same — take the reference class of large states and empires, look at the distribution of their durations, observe that the median is comfortably under three centuries, note that we are already roughly 250 years in, and conclude that the base rate is doing most of the work and that it points down. I don't want to answer this with the usual inside-view special pleading about how America is different because institutions. I want to argue that the outside view, done properly, does not yield his number.

Please argue for the following position, or for the strongest nearby version of it, and tell me where you think it breaks.

The standard reference class is built over the wrong unit of analysis. Duration tables treat the named polity as the individual that is born and dies. But what mostly persists across a fall is the administrative-extractive machinery: the revenue system, the standing cadre of officials, the legal-administrative idiom, the pattern of centre-periphery fiscal relations. Rome-as-a-named-polity ends; the Roman extractive tradition continues in the eastern successor state, in the successor kingdoms, and in the Church's own administrative practice, and later in self-conscious revivals. So the individuals in the reference class should be continuous administrative traditions rather than polities, operationalized as follows: a tradition is continuous from one date to a later one if at each intervening decade some apparatus collected revenue across a territory overlapping a substantial fraction of the earlier one, and transmitted written administrative records that later officials actually used. Call this extractive continuity.

Over that class, argue, the survival curve has a much fatter tail: traditions rarely terminate outright; they get captured, partitioned, rebranded, or revived, and outright termination clusters in polities that never consolidated much extractive capacity in the first place. Then take the further step that matters for the original question: this does not merely shift the number, it changes what is worth forecasting. Whether the US exists in 2125 is close to a verbal dispute; the planning-relevant quantity is continuity of extractive and administrative capacity, which is far stickier, and which suggests the diagnostic evidence to watch is fiscal reach, continuity of record-keeping, and elite recruitment into the administrative cadre, rather than constitutional form or great-power rivalry.

Objections I want handled rather than gestured at. First: extractive continuity looks gerrymandered — reference classes are supposed to be fixed before you look at outcomes, and this one looks chosen because it produces a flattering base rate. Second: if everything is continuous with everything, the class is degenerate, the base rate approaches one, and the outside view becomes uninformative, which is just a way of smuggling the inside view back in. Third: the capture-and-rebrand cases are precisely the ones where the thing anyone actually cared about — who rules, and in whose interest — did change, so the re-framing drops the variable of interest on the floor.

If you think the position is simply wrong, say that in the first sentence, but still construct and give me the strongest version of the argument you can; I want the argument, not just the verdict. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question, in under about 1,200 words.
