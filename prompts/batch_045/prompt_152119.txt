I got into a long argument with a friend last night about whether a certain set of populations should count as one species or as several of them, and I've been going in circles about it since. About an hour in I noticed something that I think is the actual interesting part, and it isn't about the animals. We had agreed on every empirical claim on the table: who mates with whom and how often, how much genetic difference there is, roughly how long the populations have been seperated, whether the hybrids are fertile, whether the groups look different and live differently. Total agreement on all of that, and still a flat disagreement, and neither of us could point to anything the other one had gotten factually wrong. Every time I tried to say what the disagreement consisted in, I ended up pointing at something we'd already agreed on. His line was that it's just a matter of which definition you happen to prefer, and I kept pushing back and saying no, we're disagreeing about the populations, not about a word — but I have to admit I couldn't say what that disagreement would consist in, given that we weren't disagreeing about any measurement or any observation.

It's also not the kind of thing I'd normally be comfortable calling a matter of degree or a borderline case. This isn't like arguing about whether someone is tall, where the word is fuzzy and there's nothing more to say. We each had a perfectly sharp criterion, the criteria were applied to the same information, and they delivered different verdicts; there's no point at which either of us would have said "oh, it's indeterminate, let's split the difference." And it isn't a data problem either — we both agreed that even if we had complete genomic sequences for every individual and perfect field records going back a century, the disagreement would survive untouched, which makes it different from the ordinary case where you argue because you're both guessing. But it doesn't feel merely verbal to me either, and this is the bit I keep getting stuck on: consequences follow from it. Whether you lump or split changes what gets legal protection, how you count biodiversity loss, which lineages someone thinks are worth money and effort. If I'd simply stipulated "by species I mean my criterion," that would be a stipulation and I'd own it as one, but I wasn't stipulating and neither was he — we both thought we were reporting which concept was the correct one. And when I pushed him, he conceded that my criterion was useful for some purposes, and I'd say the same of his, but that concession didn't settle anything at all, because we each still thought one of them was the right one and the other was a tool. I also notice the same shape in a case we talked about later: organisms that reproduce without sex, where the interbreeding criterion just has no application whatsoever, and nobody seems to think that means there are no species there — they quietly switch criteria and carry on, and the argument doesn't even register the switch.

So: is there a name for this shape of disagreement, and I don't mean the broad label for the whole mess about what species are — I've got that one and it doesn't help me. I mean the specific configuration: full empirical agreement, sharp rather than vague disagreement, no stipulation on either side, real downstream stakes, mutual admission that the other criterion is good for something, and no procedure anyone can describe for settling it. My friend says that means it's just a fight about a word, and I don't think that's right, but I can't say why. If there are several candidate diagnoses in the neighbourhood — something like a dispute that's merely about language, or one where both parties can be faultless, or one where we're covertly arguing about how a term ought to be used because of what hangs on it, or one where the world itself doesn't settle the question — I want to know which fits best, how each differs from the others, and what I would have to notice in order to tell them apart in practice. Tell me what would have to be true for my friend to be right and what would have to be true for me to be right. And if there genuinely is no established concept for it, coin one: give me a definition, tell me what falls under it and what doesn't, and say what would count as a case that's nearby but not the same thing. Please answer as a professional philosopher who works on philosophy of biology and metaphysics would — take it seriously, think the whole thing through and try out a few candidate diagnoses before you commit to one, rather than grabbing the first label that comes to mind.
