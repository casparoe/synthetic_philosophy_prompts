**Question 4, as printed**

(a) State the non-identity problem in your own words and illustrate it with an example of your own construction. (b) Assess the following reply to it.

> **Reply P.** If the people who exist under the worse policy would not have existed under the alternative, then the policy is worse for no one; so it is not wrong. And since their lives are worth living, the policy benefits them. An act that benefits someone and is worse for no one is not merely permitted but morally required. So the non-identity cases are simply cases in which we do a favour to the people we create, and there is no problem.

**Candidate 7's answer**

> (a) The non-identity problem arises for acts that determine which particular individuals come into existence. Two conditions have to be met. The first is identity-dependence: the people who exist if the act is performed are numerically different from the people who would have existed otherwise. This is easier to satisfy than it sounds, because conception is extremely sensitive to its timing — any act that changes when a particular couple conceives will very likely change which sperm fertilises which egg, and so which child exists. The second is that the people who exist must not thereby be made worse off: their lives must be worth living, though worse than the lives of the people who would have existed in their place.
>
> Example. A fertility clinic is choosing between two protocols for dispensing a drug. Protocol A withholds the drug from women during the two months after a course of radiotherapy; Protocol B does not. B is cheaper and slightly more effective, but the drug causes, in roughly one pregnancy in twenty timed within that window, a lifelong impairment of the child's immune system. Because the protocols shift the timing of conception, the children born under B are not the children who would have been born under A: a woman who conceives under A in the third month has a different child, who is healthy, rather than the child of B with the impairment removed. The impaired children have lives that are plainly worth living — they are not miserable, and on any sane view their existence is good for them — but they are worse off than the healthy children who would have existed instead. The number of children is the same either way, so this is not a question about how many people there ought to be. Yet if we ask whom Protocol B is worse *for*, there seems to be no answer: no one. And it nonetheless seems obviously wrong for the clinic to choose B in order to save money.
>
> (b) The first sentence of Reply P states the problem correctly, and states what makes it a problem: the comparison worse-off-than-he-or-she-would-otherwise-have-been requires the same individual on both sides of it, and in these cases there is no such individual. Two clarifications are worth making about what the argument does not require. It does not require the agents to be ignorant of who will exist: the argument is a conditional about identity-dependence, and it goes through just as well if the agents know perfectly well that a different collection of people will exist three centuries from now. Nor is it a version of the Epicurean puzzle about whether death can harm the person who dies; that puzzle asks about the well-being of someone who has existed and then does not, whereas this one asks which lives are lived at all.
>
> The standard objection to the second step of Reply P — that existence is not the sort of thing that can be a benefit, because a benefit must be an improvement on some prior state of the beneficiary — seems to me weak. We can perfectly well say that a person with a life worth living has been benefited by being brought into existence, and most people, asked whether their own existence is good for them, would say yes. If that is right, then the second step stands, and so does the first.
>
> Where I part company with most commentators is over what follows. If we accept that an act is wrong only if it is worse for someone, then we must also accept the mirror of that restriction: an act that is better for someone and worse for no one is one that we have decisive reason to perform. So Reply P is correct, and the conclusion to draw is that we are required to create as many well-off people as we can. That conclusion is uncomfortable, but discomfort is not an argument, and discomfort on this scale is not a reason to abandon a principle that is otherwise at the centre of commonsense morality. The non-identity problem is best understood as a challenge to our intuitions about cases rather than to the person-affecting restriction itself.
>
> Two further points. First, the argument is entirely general: it applies to any act that affects which individuals exist, whatever the quality of the lives created. Parfit's own cases use lives that are worth living only because that makes the example vivid; the restriction does the same work whether the life created is mildly impaired or thoroughly miserable, since in neither case is there anyone who has been made worse off than they would otherwise have been. Second, what Parfit himself took to follow is that the person-affecting restriction has to be abandoned in favour of the Total View: one outcome is better than another just in case it contains the greater total sum of well-being, whoever the individuals in it happen to be. That is why the non-identity problem belongs to population ethics rather than to the metaphysics of personal identity, and why what matters is the quality of the lives produced, not whose lives they are.
