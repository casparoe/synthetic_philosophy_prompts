Everyone works on the positive direction: if I can conceive that P, does that give me any reason to think P is possible? The conceivability-argument people say yes, the physicalist critics say my imagination just isn't up to the job and that I'm mistaking my own limits for the world's. But in actual argumentative life people lean much harder on the negative direction — "I can't even conceive how that could work" gets treated as evidence of impossibility, in philosophy and in science — and nobody has a good account of when that inference is worth anything. My hunch is that's because "I can't conceive it" covers two very different states: sometimes you do represent the thing and it visibly falls apart in your hands (trying to imagine something that is smoothly round all over and square all over), and sometimes you represent nothing at all and just draw a blank (trying, before you know any chemistry, to imagine how a colourless odourless liquid could be water). Call the first clash-inconceivability and the second blank-inconceivability. I suspect only the first ever carries evidential weight, and that the real question is whether you can ever tell from the inside which one you're in — because if you can't, then a lot of arguments that feel very strong are running on a sensation whose source the person having it can't identify.
