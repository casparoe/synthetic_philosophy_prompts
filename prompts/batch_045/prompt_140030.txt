I've been turning this over in my head for a few weeks and keep going in circles, so I figured I'd ask for help. Bit of background: I don't have any academic background in this stuff, I work in logistics and read about philosophy only when I fall down an internet rabbit hole at 1am. The case that got me stuck is my friend Ruth, who runs a small bakery. Last year she hired her cousin as bookkeeper even though two different people warned her the cousin was sloppy with money. She also never got around to setting up online access to the business account, so for about eight months the only numbers she saw were the paper summaries her cousin handed her once a month. When the tax bill came she said, completely sincerely and pretty devastated, "how was I supposed to know?" My brother says that's just self-deception, end of story. I dunno — it feels like she never actually believed the books were a mess, she just arranged her life so she wouldn't find out one way or the other, and I can't tell if that's the same thing. I've started calling it *outsourced self-deception*: you use other people, or just the way your day is set up, to keep the truth at arm's length so you can honestly say you didn't know. But maybe I've just given a fancy name to ordinary avoidance.

What I'd like is a dialogue between two people who disagree about this properly. One of them thinks cases like Ruth's really are self-deception and that there's nothing specially weird or impossible about it — that "deceiving yourself" doesn't have to mean one part of you is sitting there knowing the truth while another part is being lied to. The other thinks the whole notion is incoherent, or at least that we'd be much better off describing what's going on as something else entirely: motivated reasoning, ordinary ignorance, weakness of will, whatever — no genuine self-deception needed. Have them argue about Ruth's case specifically, and also about whether my "outsourced" category is actually useful or whether it just papers over a confusion. I'd love it if they'd also get into whether it matters that she had those warnings up front, and whether there's a real difference between "she'd be crushed if she found out" and "somewhere she already half knows."

Two things I care about: make both of them sharp, I really don't want one of them to be a punching bag who exists only to be demolished, and make sure they actually respond to what the other person just said rather than each delivering a little speech past the other. Set it wherever you like — a long drive, an airport bar, whatever, I'll go with your judgement. And please don't point me at books, papers or author's names; I don't have library access and I'm not going to, I just want to see the argument worked out in plain language.
