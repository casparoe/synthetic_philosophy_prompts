I've been chewing on something off and on for a couple of years, ever since a comment thread on one of the usual rationalist blogs about the 'global workspace' picture of consciousness — the idea, roughly, that for a mental state to be conscious just is for it to be broadcast to a wide set of downstream consumers (memory, speech, deliberate action, planning) rather than stuck inside one specialized module quietly doing its own thing. I like that picture as a piece of mechanism, and I don't think I have a knockdown objection to it. What I have is a recurring puzzle that the picture doesn't seem to say anything about, and I don't know whether the puzzle has a name of its own, or whether it's really just a confusion that has a name for its dissolution.

It shows up for me in three cases that feel like one case. (i) I drive long empty stretches of interstate for work. Several times I've snapped back to attention and found that I could not report a single thing about the preceding twenty minutes — no exits, no cars, no thoughts — and yet my immediate, unprompted reaction is that I was looking at the road the whole time and simply wasn't attending to it. If you asked me whether I was visually conscious of the road during those minutes I'd say yes and mean it, and what I'd be going on is precisely nothing downstream. (ii) A friend and I sometimes play a game where she flashes a word at me very briefly and I have to guess it. On some trials I'm as certain as I am about the blank wall behind my monitor that I saw nothing at all, and my guesses are nonetheless well above chance. She says this is perception without consciousness; when I read around online, other people say the very same evidence shows perception I couldn't get at. Both sides, notice, argue entirely from the downstream side. (iii) The tip-of-the-tongue case: I know I know the word, I can feel it hovering, I reject near-misses, I keep searching, and when it finally arrives it seems retrospectively as though the hovering thing had been the word all along, just not available yet. My verdict on whether the pre-arrival state was conscious appears to be settled by how the story ends.

The general pattern, as I'd put it: every consideration I can produce for or against saying 'that one was conscious' is a fact about what the state did downstream — whether I could describe it, whether it lasted long enough to matter, whether it changed what I did next, whether other parts of me could make use of it. When I try to get at the thing itself, the what-it-was-likeness, the trying is itself one more downstream use. So the distinction I want — between, on the one hand, a state's being there for me in whatever that felt sense consists in, and on the other hand, its being available to the rest of the cognitive machinery — is a distinction I can state in words and then never apply to an actual case from the inside, because the only instrument I have for applying it is built out of the second half of it. To be clear about what I'm not asking: I'm not asking whether consciousness is real, or whether other people have it, or whether a machine could; I'm happy to grant all of that for the sake of the question. And I don't need the labels for the two sides of the dispute I gestured at above — a friend who took philosophy in college told me both sides have names and that the argument has been running for decades, and I can guess roughly what the labels are. She also mentioned that there's a whole literature on how you'd ever test for the leftover part, which sounds adjacent to my puzzle, except that as she described it, it is mostly about designing experiments and about whether subjects can be trusted to report what they saw. My puzzle shows up before anyone runs an experiment, in the privacy of a single case, and it isn't about trusting reports. So: does this predicament have an established name — the situation in which a distinction can be drawn in the abstract but not applied from the first-person side, because every piece of first-person evidence for one side of it arrives in the currency of the other side? If the nearest established concepts only cover part of it, I'd like to know which ones they are, how far each one goes, and exactly where each one stops and mine begins. If there genuinely is nothing established, I'd like a term coined and defined tightly enough that I could decide, for a given case, whether it applies — including for the hard cases: the ones with no reportability at all but very strong retrospective insistence, and the ones where behavior is clearly guided but the subject denies everything. And whichever way that goes, I'd like to know what someone who likes the global workspace picture should say about it: a discovery about the limits of introspection, a confusion the picture dissolves, or just the definition doing exactly the work it was hired to do?
