Here's my opening.

Dana is an astronomer with three names for one planet — Tir, Nef and Sol — picked up on three seperate occasions, in three different ways. She can still coherently wonder whether Tir has a property she has already ascribed to Sol. In year one she discovers that Tir = Nef: a real discovery, she's excited. In year three she discovers that Nef = Sol: also a discovery. In year five someone tells her that Tir = Sol and she isn't surprised at all; she says obviously, that follows from what I already know, and derives it in two steps from her earlier beliefs.

So: the senses of Tir and Sol are as different as ever, and by your own account the thought expressed by Tir = Sol is therefore just as substantial as ever. Yet for Dana it has no cognitive value whatsoever, while for a colleague who missed the middle discovery it's a bombshell. Same senses, same thought, wildly different informativeness. So informativeness can't be a matter of sense-difference — it's just a fact about what the subject already happens to believe. And once you grant that, I don't see why I need senses at all: the whole story is that Dana hadn't yet put it together that Tir and Sol corefer. Your senses look like an idle wheel.

And before you reach for the easy move — I don't think you get to say that cognitive value is an objective property of the thought while surprise is merely psychological. If you do, I'll ask what the puzzle was ever supposed to be: the starting observation is that a=b can extend our knowledge while a=a can't. If that observation is really about somebody's psychology, the motivation for senses evaporates. So pick one: is cognitive value relative to a knower or isn't it?
