I am putting the final session of a graduate seminar on anthropic reasoning together, and I have drifted into a position that a colleague in the philosophy of physics keeps telling me is untenable, so I want to know precisely where it breaks. The short version is that I have started teaching the self-sampling assumption as the sane default and the self-indication assumption as refuted, on the grounds that the presumptuous philosopher case is a straightforward reductio: two cosmological hypotheses with equal priors, one positing a trillion observers and the other a trillion trillion, and the self-indication assumption tells you to be all but certain of the second before anyone has looked through a telescope. I take that to dispose of it. I also take the self-sampling assumption to be the cautious residue, since it only says that you should reason as if you were a random draw from the observers in your reference class, which sounds like modesty rather than metaphysics; when my colleague objects that the self-sampling assumption is hardly cautious given what it does in the doomsday argument, I reply that the doomsday argument needs further substantive premises and is therefore no counter-example to the assumption itself.

Two of the refinements I have added are my own, and I would like them stress-tested. First, to answer the familiar charge that the self-sampling assumption lets you rig any conclusion by choosing the reference class, I propose a Fixed-Point Condition: an anthropic prior is admissible only if, for every hypothesis in the partition, the credence it assigns to that hypothesis equals the expected fraction of observers within that hypothesis who occupy an epistemic situation indistinguishable from yours, where the situation is specified using the reference class the prior itself endorses. I had hoped this was neutral between the two assumptions, since it looks like a bare self-consistency requirement, and that it would block the gerrymandering. Second, I have begun to suspect that the dispute is verbal anyway: once you conditionalise on your total evidence, including that you exist, that you exist now, and that you are the sort of creature that reasons about anthropics at all, the two assumptions seem to me to deliver the same numbers, with the apparent disagreement arising only because people write the likelihoods differently. To make the point examinable I have built a case I call the Audited Incubator: ten observers are created in a white room, a fair coin is tossed, and if it lands tails a further nine hundred and ninety are created later; the wrinkle is that under either outcome exactly ten observers are designated auditors, handed the questionnaire and asked for their credence in heads, while everyone else is a bystander who is never asked anything. Since the number of auditors is fixed across hypotheses and the total population is not, this looks to me like a clean test of whether the two assumptions differ in substance or only in bookkeeping, and I have been telling my students that the self-sampling assumption returns one half here and that this is clearly the right answer.

What I would like, in British English and in a form I could hand out to the seminar, is the strongest case against each of the three claims I have been making: that the presumptuous philosopher disposes of the self-indication assumption; that the Fixed-Point Condition is a neutral constraint rather than the self-sampling assumption in disguise; and that the disagreement dissolves into a verbal one once the total evidence is in. I would also like your verdict on the Audited Incubator, including whether the self-sampling assumption really does return one half there, what the reference class is doing in that calculation, and how the self-indication assumption handles the fact that being an auditor is itself part of the evidence. If you think the residual dispute is genuine rather than verbal, please end with a crisp statement of what each side would have to give up in order to be moved.
