I have no academic background. I left school at eighteen and have spent twenty-two years in operations at a mid-sized freight and warehousing company, the last nine of them as the person who drafts the mission statements, headcount plans, and performance targets for our forty-odd departments, and then has to defend them when a department head appeals. In the evenings I read about artificial intelligence on my own, and there are two claims I keep running into that I want to get straight in terms I can actually test.

The first is the orthogonality thesis: that a system's level of capability and its final goals are independent axes, so that any degree of competence can in principle be combined with any ultimate aim, and there is no tendency for getting smarter to push a system toward better or more sensible aims. The second is instrumental convergence: that whatever a system's final goal turns out to be, certain intermediate aims keep showing up, because they help with almost anything — staying in operation and not being shut off, acquiring more resources, improving its own competence, getting better information, and resisting having its own objectives rewritten.

I would like you to restate both claims as faithfully as you can in my working vocabulary: written charters and missions, targets and thresholds, headcount and budget requests, delegated authority, reorganizations and audits, the annual planning cycle, principal–agent problems, the familiar effect where a measured target stops tracking the thing it was meant to track, and the difference between a unit that exists to serve the company and a unit that behaves as if the company exists to serve it. I am fluent in all of this; you do not need to explain it to me. What I want is the translation, and then — this is the part I actually care about — an honest report on the residue: which parts of each claim carry over cleanly, which parts only survive if you quietly tilt them, and which parts have no expression at all in how I talk about organizations. Please flag it when a translation is doing the latter two things rather than presenting the result as a clean mapping.

Three things from my own experience that I suspect complicate the standard picture, and which I want you to use rather than set aside. First: in my company, whether a unit grabs for more depends less on what its mission says than on the shape of the mission. Units with maximizing missions — grow volume, cut cost per shipment as far as it will go — hoard headcount and budget and fight reorganization. Units with threshold missions — keep recordable safety incidents under a set number, keep audit exceptions below a tolerance — mostly do not; once they are comfortably inside the threshold they will hand resources back. If that is right, instrumental convergence may not be a claim about the content of final goals at all, but about their geometry, about whether they are open-ended and scale-invariant. I would like to know whether that reframing leaves the thesis as a theorem, an empirical generalization, or something close to a restatement, and what it does to orthogonality if the aim shapes that tend to produce the convergent behavior are themselves the aims a highly capable system would be most likely to end up with. Second: the best-run units I have ever dealt with are the ones chartered to achieve a defined result and then dissolve. They do not resist being wound up, because winding up is what success looks like. So I want to know whether there are final goals for which the convergent sub-goals point the other way — a system that genuinely wants to be switched off once a condition is met — and whether the existence of such goals is a counterexample to instrumental convergence or something the thesis was never claiming to rule out. Third: when one of my units resists a rewrite of its charter, the resistance comes from the people inside it, who have their own interests in pay, status, and continued employment, and those interests are not the mission. An artificial system has no internal constituency like that, so I am not sure what it would even mean for it to protect its own objectives, and I suspect my vocabulary imports a machinery of internal politics that has no counterpart here.

Tell me where my framework is the thing that is defective — I assume, for instance, that organizations always have someone outside them who sets the mission, funds it, and can withdraw both, and that the budget is genuinely scarce — and where the residue instead shows that the original claims are less clear or less substantial than they are usually made to look. In particular I want your view on whether orthogonality and instrumental convergence are in tension, as they seem to me to be: the first says capability constrains nothing about aims, the second says the world constrains a great deal about behavior whatever the aims are. If they are in tension, say which one you think is carrying the real weight, and name a case phrased in my vocabulary that would settle it. I have no way to look anything up while you answer, so please work from the ideas themselves rather than from anything you would need to cite.
