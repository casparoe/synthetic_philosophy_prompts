On the night before *Challenger* launched, the people who had the evidence and the people who had the authority reached opposite verdicts about the same O-ring. I want that episode used as a two-way test of two families of view about knowledge: pragmatic encroachment (including interest-relative invariantism), on which what it takes to know shifts with the practical stakes facing the subject, and epistemic contextualism, on which the truth of a knowledge attribution shifts with the standards in play in the attributor's context.

The facts I want you to work from — all I take to be common knowledge, and I do not want the analysis to turn on any finer detail than these:

The shuttle's two solid rocket boosters were sealed at their field joints by rubber O-rings; cold stiffens the rubber and slows its ability to seal. Since the 1970s, NASA and the booster contractor Morton Thiokol had classified O-ring erosion, observed on earlier flights, as an accepted risk rather than a flight-safety prohibition. On 27–28 January 1986 the pad experienced the coldest launch weather in the program's history, near freezing at liftoff after an overnight freeze, and far colder than any previous launch. On the eve of the launch, Thiokol's engineers recommended against launching at that temperature, citing the loss of O-ring resiliency and the fact that no flight data existed at anything like that temperature; NASA officials pressed Thiokol's management, the engineers' recommendation was reversed in a recess during which they were urged to reason as managers rather than as engineers, and the flight was cleared. *Challenger* broke apart 73 seconds after liftoff, killing all seven crew. The subsequent presidential commission attributed the accident to the failure of the seals and blamed NASA's organizational culture and decision-making as much as the hardware; the shuttle program was grounded for more than two years.

What I want is a disciplined mapping in both directions, roughly a thousand words of continuous prose, arguing from the positions themselves rather than reporting who has said what (no bibliography, no citations, and please do not supply or invent case details beyond those above).

First, set up the attributions properly: say what the relevant propositions are (that the seals will hold, that they will fail, that launching is unsafe), who the candidate subjects are — the Thiokol engineers, Thiokol's managers, the NASA managers, the crew — and, for each, what the practical stakes are taken to be. Then give me what each family of view says each of them knew, and what each of them should therefore have done by its own lights, and be explicit about which feature of the case is doing the work in each verdict.

Second, the part I care most about: this is a case of epistemic division of labor. The people holding the evidence are not the people holding the decision, and the people bearing the stakes — the crew — are not parties to the conversation at all. So stake-sensitivity has three possible loci: the stakes of the subject whose knowledge is in question, the stakes of the person attributing knowledge, and the stakes of an affected third party who neither asserts nor decides. Call the third option vicarious encroachment if you like. Tell me which of these loci pragmatic encroachment can coherently accommodate and which it can only gerrymander, what happens when they conflict (the crew's stakes against the schedule and political costs NASA was facing), and whether contextualism, which after all shifts standards with conversational context, has an advantage or merely relocates the problem.

Third, tell me honestly what these views get wrong or miss here. Three things in particular: the evidence base was a handful of eroded seals with no usable base rate for a one-shot decision, so stakes-shifting may be doing all the work with no evidence for it to work on; the engineers' own verdict was reversed by institutional pressure, which suggests that even a correct knowledge claim can fail entirely to guide action in a hierarchy, and so raises a question about the knowledge-action link these views lean on; and hindsight — the accident itself — threatens to make the retrospective attribution come out true for reasons that have nothing to do with what anyone was in a position to know on the night.

Finally: does the case discriminate between pragmatic encroachment and contextualism, or do they deliver the same verdicts here for different reasons? If they do not discriminate, say so and say what kind of case would. And give me your judgment on the bottom line: is this episode a confirmation of stake-sensitivity, an anomaly for it, or better explained by a strict invariantism about knowledge plus a separate norm governing action under uncertainty? Where the theories are silent, say that rather than making them speak.
