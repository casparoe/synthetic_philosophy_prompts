I sit on a small internal committee at an AI evaluation group that has to decide, repeatedly and with money attached, whether to count a system as intelligent, and we have discovered that we cannot: three of us appear to mean three different things by the word, and each of us thinks the other two are quietly changing the subject. Rather than commission another survey of definitions, I want a diagnostic instrument — a decision tree, or a quiz with a scoring key, that sorts a person by what they are willing to say about cases into a named position on what intelligence is. Concretely: nine or ten branch questions, each turning on a concrete system rather than on a slogan, each with two or three answer options, phrased so that an engineer with no philosophy training can answer before having any idea which leaf the answer leads to. So no question should take the form is intelligence general or domain-specific, or does embodiment matter; instead give me the systems and let the answers do the classifying. The cases I want used, since they are the ones that actually broke my committee apart, are these: the Closed-Domain Virtuoso, superhuman inside one sealed game and a lookup table outside it; the Lattice, an enormous pool of narrow specialists behind a learned router, where no component is remotely general but the composite solves problems none of them was built for; the Colony, very simple agents plus local rules that collectively solve a novel problem with no central model anywhere; the Simulator That Never Acts, a perfect generative model of its environment that only ever answers counterfactual questions and never produces an outcome in the world; the Brittle Specialist, excellent within its training distribution and catastrophically wrong one step outside it; the Slow Sage, whose answers are optimal but arrive after ten thousand years; the Archivist, whose entire competence is retrieval and recombination from an enormous corpus with no genuinely novel output; the Self-Rewriting Apprentice, mediocre at every current task but able to rewrite its own learning procedure and improve at an accelerating rate; and a matched pair, one confident and one calibrated, identical in accuracy but different in whether they know when they do not know. Include somewhere near the root a baseline question about whether there is any lower threshold at all — whether a feedback controller or a sediment-sorting riverbed is minimally intelligent or simply not a candidate.

The leaves I need on the tree are: the psychometric or general-factor view; the goal-achievement or rationalist view; the resource-bounded information-processing view, on which intelligence is compression and efficient search under constraints; the sample-efficiency view, on which intelligence is fundamentally a learning rate rather than a level of performance; the embodied, situated or enactive view; the predictive-modeling view that identifies intelligence with having and using a good world model; the distributed or collective view, on which intelligence is a property of systems rather than of individuals; a pluralist or cluster view on which there are several genuine but unrelated things here; and a deflationary or eliminativist view on which the term picks out no natural kind and functions as an interest-relative honorific. Please build the tree from the logical space first rather than from this list: choose the three or four branching dimensions that do the real work, enumerate the combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the actual positions in that space. Where a combination is unoccupied, mark it and say briefly why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unoccupied because nobody has defended it — and where two leaves survive every question you can devise, say so explicitly and tell me what further case would separate them, or concede that the instrument bottoms out in a bedrock intuition neither side can argue for. I also want the scoring key to tell me which of my questions are load-bearing and which are decorative; I would rather cut three than keep a question that every view answers the same way.

Two further constraints. The audience is mixed — mostly engineers, a few philosophers — so the handout has to be usable by someone who has never read the literature, which means no question may presuppose a label and no option may be glossed by naming the position it leads to. And I have a hypothesis about my own committee that I would like you to test rather than flatter: I suspect the real split is between those of us for whom intelligence is a capacity, something a system can possess while never exercising it, and those of us for whom it is a track record, something constituted by actual successes — and that this distinction cuts across the other axes and is what explains why we cannot agree about the Simulator That Never Acts or about the Self-Rewriting Apprentice. If that is right, make it the root node and rebuild the tree below it. If it is wrong — if, say, capacity and track record come apart only in cases I have over-described, or if the distinction collapses into one of the other axes — tell me why, and put the root somewhere that actually discriminates.
