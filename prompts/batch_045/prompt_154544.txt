I spent a little over thirty years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist — scattering, spectroscopy, fitting curves to noisy data — and since retiring I have been working through philosophy of science on my own. I have arrived at formal learning theory and I want to know whether it contains anything I do not already believe for other reasons.

My vocabulary is the quantitative one of a working experimentalist: least-squares and maximum-likelihood fits, chi-squared against a quoted number of degrees of freedom, statistical versus systematic uncertainty, error bars and noise floors, calibration chains and changes of units, regularization and cross-validation, information criteria, and Bayesian model comparison with marginal likelihoods and the Occam factor. Priors in the ordinary sense do not trouble me. I have no training in computability theory: I know roughly what a Turing machine is and nothing beyond that, and I had not encountered Kolmogorov complexity until recently.

Please restate the central apparatus of Solomonoff induction in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: the universal prior that weights candidate data-generating processes by two to the minus their complexity, the additive constant of the invariance theorem, the dominance of the shortest program, the convergence result according to which predictions lock onto any computable source with total expected error bounded in proportion to that source's own complexity, and the claim that together these vindicate Occam's razor. Where a piece has an exact counterpart in model fitting or Bayesian evidence, say so and name the counterpart.

Then give me the residue report, and I would like it organised around four places where I suspect the translation strains.

First, convention versus prior. In my practice a change of units or of parametrisation is pure bookkeeping and cannot alter a single prediction. The choice of reference machine is presented as the same kind of freedom, yet it moves the prior by a factor of two raised to an exponent that is unknown and, I gather, uncomputable. Does that dependence wash out as data accumulate, and if it does, can I ever know that I have taken enough data to be clear of it, given that the exponent cannot be computed? Is this freedom closer to a choice of units or closer to a choice of prior, and has something consequential been admitted under a name that sounds merely conventional?

Second, finite precision. The machinery runs over finite binary strings, while my data are real numbers with quoted uncertainties. When I discretise at the precision my instrument actually supports, what becomes of programs that hide structure in bits below the noise floor? Does the description-length accounting survive a quantisation step, or is the noise floor a place where complexity can be stored for free?

Third, the compressibility of the world. Please make explicit where the framework assumes that the data stream is generated by something of low complexity, and tell me whether that is a theorem, a stipulation, or a hidden empirical premise. Physicists do in fact find that very short equations with a handful of fitted parameters cover enormous ranges of data, but we also believe there are processes whose fine detail is incompressible for all practical purposes — turbulent cascades, decay times, individual measurement outcomes. Is the success of Occam's razor a result about reasoning in general or a discovery about this particular universe; and if I told you the world were incompressible below some scale, what would happen to the razor, and would the framework be able to register its own failure?

Fourth, my notion of simplicity. A good theory for me is a differential equation with few parameters and well-understood limiting cases, not a short program, and I suspect the shortest program reproducing my data would not be recognised by any physicist as a theory at all. How much of the vindication survives if simplicity is measured in my units rather than in bits?

Where the translation fails, tell me whether you take the failure to indict my vocabulary — something an experimentalist cannot say but perhaps should be able to — or to indict the learning-theoretic claim as less precise than it is usually made to sound. I would rather have an honest failure report than a fluent paraphrase, and I am not asking for anything to read.
