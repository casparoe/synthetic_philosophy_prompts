Our research council funds groups rather than projects: each grant runs for a five-year cycle, is renewable, and supports a shared theoretical core along with the auxiliary assumptions the group revises when data clash with it. I sit on the renewal panel. We have roughly a day per group, our reviewers are working scientists with no training in philosophy of science, and we are not permitted to lean on citation counts or journal prestige as proxies for merit.

Please turn Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating problem shifts — including his test for telling a merely ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis from an honest one — into a decision procedure our panel can actually run: the questions in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each from material a group can supply in a renewal report, the point at which the procedure returns a refusal to renew, and the points at which it must hand the decision back to us because what remains to disagree about is a question of values rather than of whether the programme is progressing.

Three difficulties please handle rather than gesture at. First, hard-core laundering: a group can shield a refuted claim by reclassifying it as part of the core it is entitled to protect. Give us a test for when such a reclassification is legitimate and when it is evasive. Second, epistemic versus institutional progress: a programme can stop generating predictions it genuinely risks while growing, placing its students well, and publishing in the field's best venues. Keep those two verdicts separate in the procedure, and say whether, and on what grounds, institutional success should count for anything in a renewal decision. Third, retrospective appraisal against prospective funding: on Lakatos's own terms a programme's verdict is only settled after the fact, and a failing one may still revive. Build in a revival clause stating the conditions under which a degenerating programme deserves another cycle and how many it may have.

Then run the procedure on two cases. Group X is eleven years old. Twice an effect it had predicted failed to appear at the sensitivity it had staked out; each time it revised an auxiliary parameter, and each revision requires a costlier apparatus that does not yet exist. No revision has yet yielded a confirmed novel fact. It has grown from six members to twenty-five, its alumni hold most of the permanent posts in the subfield, and two of its members edit the subfield's main journal; it argues that cutting it would destroy irreplaceable expertise. Group Y is two cycles old and has no confirmed novel predictions. What it has done is publish in advance three sharp quantitative predictions with stated error bars — two testable on existing apparatus within eighteen months, one pending an instrument now being commissioned — and absorb one anomaly by adding an auxiliary that carries an independent, separately checkable prediction.

If we can fund only one, I expect the panel to renew X and hesitate over Y. If your procedure says otherwise, say so plainly. Close by telling me which step of the procedure is most likely to misfire, and what argument or evidence would lead you to withdraw or revise the procedure rather than defend it.
