Below are notes for an essay I am writing on Bernard Williams's integrity objection to impartial consequentialist moralities. I would like your comments on them. The essay’s target is the complaint that, when an agent’s action is guided by an impartial maximizing theory, the agent’s practical reasoning contains an extra thought—one more than his own commitments and relationships call for. Most readers treat this as a claim about the occurrent content of the agent’s deliberation: the agent is said to think, in addition to the thought that this person is his friend, spouse, or child, the further thought that helping this person is impersonally best. I want to argue that this is the wrong diagnosis, and that the objection is better formulated in terms of what I call deliberative vulnerability.

Section 1: The standard picture and its limits.
The integrity objection is often illustrated with cases in which an agent must choose between a personal commitment and the overall good. Suppose an agent, Ron, can save either his wife or a stranger from a wreck; he can save only one. An impartial theory tells him to save the stranger if and only if doing so produces more good. Ron saves his wife because she is his wife. The objection, as usually stated, is that if Ron must also treat the impartial calculation as the determinant of what he ought to do, then his motivation includes a thought over and above his concern for his wife—a thought more than the situation requires. I claim this phenomenological reading is too weak. An agent can act from love without consciously running the impartial calculation, and yet still be in the state Williams objects to.

Section 2: Deliberative vulnerability.
Call an agent deliberatively vulnerable with respect to a commitment when three conditions hold. First, he recognizes some impartial benchmark as having authority over his conduct. Second, he acknowledges that the benchmark might, in principle, require him to act against the commitment. Third, his acknowledgment of the first two conditions is not merely retrospective but is built into the standing policy he would apply if he were to reconsider what to do. The extra thought need never be tokened on the occasion of action; what matters is its eligibility to enter deliberation and, if it enters, to defeat the commitment. Integrity, on this account, is not the absence of an extra occurrent thought but the absence of a certain kind of standing vulnerability.

Section 3: The Off-Duty Rescuer variants.
Consider Lena, an off-duty wilderness guide who happens on a capsized boat. Two people are drowning: her daughter, and a stranger who is a prominent medical researcher. She can save only one. She instantly swims to her daughter. Later, she reflects. In the closed-record variant, Lena’s character and training are such that the question whether saving her daughter was impersonally optimal does not arise as a live input into her deliberative policy; she may later learn that the impartial calculation permitted saving her daughter, and she is glad, but she does not treat that permission as part of what justified her, nor as something that could have overridden her. In the open-record variant, Lena has expressly adopted the policy that she will save a loved one only if the impartial calculation does not forbid it, and she treats that calculation as having veto authority over her partial motives should she ever have time to consult it. In neither variant need Lena have consciously thought the impartial thought at the moment of rescue. My claim is that Williams's objection clearly applies to the open-record variant and not to the closed-record variant, even though the occurrent phenomenology may be identical. Therefore the objection is about standing deliberative structure, not about the tokening of an extra thought.

Section 4: Consequences.
If this is right, then the integrity objection is not merely a relative of the demandingness objection, though it overlaps with it. An agent could satisfy all the demands of impartial morality on a given occasion and still remain deliberatively vulnerable, because the vulnerability consists in keeping one's partial commitments under open-record audit by an impartial benchmark. The objection is instead about the authority structure of the agent's reasons. This suggests a test for whether a moral theory respects integrity: does the theory require agents to keep their deepest commitments in a condition of open-record audit by an impartial benchmark, or does it permit those commitments to operate with closed-record authority?

I would like comments on the following:

1. Accuracy with respect to Williams: Does the distinction between occurrent extra thoughts and deliberative vulnerability capture what is actually at issue in Williams's integrity objection, or does it turn his point into a claim about moral psychology that he would repudiate? I am not asking for citations, only for your assessment of whether my reading distorts the objection.

2. The phenomenon: Is it really true that an agent can be in the state Williams criticizes even when no extra thought occurs to her at the moment of action? Does the open-record Lena really exhibit the vice, if it is a vice, that the closed-record Lena lacks?

3. The relation to demandingness: Williams's integrity objection is sometimes folded into the demandingness objection. Does my standing-vulnerability formulation keep the two distinct, or does it collapse integrity into demandingness?
