Hey — hoping you can help me think through a committee thing before Thursday's meeting.

I'm on the small curatorial committee for a permanent exhibit at a social history museum (keeping it vague on purpose) about generations. One room per cohort, birth years on the wall, artifacts, the whole bit. We've been at it for two years and I can't reverse-engineer our own decisions into a rule.

What we've aproved:

- Baby Boomers (1946–64). Marisol's reason, and she's the member whose reasoning has actually been consistent: sheer size. They competed with each other for school places, jobs and housing, and that competition did the causal work. She said explicitly it wasn't about shared memories.
- Silent Generation (1928–45). Small cohort, Depression childhood, wartime scarcity, distinctive caution.
- Gen X (1965–80). Approved narrowly: the "forgotten middle child" framing tracks something real — bad labor-market entry, latchkey childhood — and the label has been doing work in how institutions treat people for thirty years.
- Millennials (1981–96). Approved: 9/11 and the crash landing at career entry, internet arriving in adolescence.
- Gen Z (1997–2012 — I can never remember wether we said 1997 or 1998 for the cutoff). Approved, though Marisol worried out loud at the time that what defines them is a technology rollout rather than an event.
- The "Wall generation": East Germans who were 14 to 18 in November 1989. Approved when a Berlin partner museum asked us to co-produce an annex. Event-defined, one country, and it overlaps the Silent/Gen X years almost completely.

What we've rejected:

- Xennials (1977–83): "a marketing convenience for people who can't decide." Which is odd, because if anything they have the sharpest developmental signature on the list — analog childhood, digital working life — and they do call themselves that.
- Generation Jones (1954–65): "Boomers' little siblings with a branding problem."
- Gen Alpha (2013 onward): turned down last spring as "a name we needed for whatever comes after Z," even though parents and marketers now use it constantly.
- The "9/11 generation," proposed as everyone old enough to remember watching it live on TV: rejected as "an audience, not a cohort," since members ranged from about four to forty.

Now a junior curator has proposed a room for "the Always-Onlines": everyone who got their first smartphone at or before age 12. From her packet:

- It isn't a birth-year band. Members were born anywhere from about 1994 to 2012 depending on the country they grew up in, family income, and how early their parents adopted. Age at first phone is a continuous variable — there's a bulge around 12 or 13, no cliff.
- No shared public event whatsoever. What they share is a technology diffusion, staggered by region and family.
- But the survey work she attached finds a discontinuity: people who first got a smartphone at 11 or 12 differ noticeably, at the same age, from people who got one at 15, on sleep, loneliness, attention complaints and diagnosed anxiety — controlling for birth year and current age.
- Nobody self-identifies as an Always-Onliner. She coined the term in the proposal.
- It cross-cuts four rooms we've already built. An Always-Onliner can be a Xennial, a Millennial, a Gen Z or a Gen Alpha.

So: what's Marisol going to say, and what should we actually do if we care about being consistent with ourselves? Which precedent is the closest analog — the Gen Z approval, the Xennials rejection, the Wall generation, or the 9/11 rejection — and does picking a different analog change the answer? If our track record genuinely doesn't settle it, I want the principle we'd be committing to spelled out honestly for both options, because I'd rather know what we're signing than just vote.

Two more things. First, name the one further case you'd put in front of us that would really separate the rules we might be running — better to find out now than three meetings from now. Second: our demographer advisor keeps saying "a cohort is a question you ask of the data, a generation is a claim about a person." I can't tell if that's deep or a way of dodging the vote. Tell me which.

Please answer in American English, and write it as prose I can read out loud at the meeting — a short list of the candidate rules we might be running is fine, but don't give me a report with headers.
