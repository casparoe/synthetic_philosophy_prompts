I teach a graduate seminar on historical explanation, and I want a designed case — not an illustration — that forces three positions into conflict over whether a single individual can matter at the scale of the longue durée. The positions, as I define them for the seminar: (i) the structural historian in the longue-durée tradition, for whom the slowly moving constraints — geography, climate, the demographic regime, techniques of cultivation, the reach of markets — fix what is possible, while events and individuals are surface disturbances that structures absorb; (ii) the counterfactual or event-centered historian, who holds that removing a named person at a particular juncture can redirect a trajectory, and characteristically explains this by that person's character, office, or decisions; (iii) the structural-demographic or cliodynamic modeler, who also works with slow aggregate variables but treats them as a nonlinear dynamical system, with thresholds and sensitive dependence near them.

Design the case so that the following verdicts fall out of the positions rather than being written into the scenario:

- The structural historian must conclude that removing the individual changes nothing at the longue-durée scale.
- The counterfactual historian must conclude that it changes a great deal — while being clearly wrong about why: the individual's character, office, talents, and decisions must be obviously irrelevant to the outcome, so that their usual explanatory resources do no work here even though their verdict is right.
- The cliodynamic modeler must be the position whose own resources actually account for the divergence.

Constraints on the scenario. One paragraph. Invent a small, concrete setting — a valley, an upland parish, a riverine market town — rather than reaching for a famous episode; nothing in the case should depend on a fact a reader would need to look up. No science-fictional devices and no exotic machinery: the individual simply dies a few years earlier than they did, of an ordinary cause. The individual is nobody: no office, no wealth, no fame, no unusual talent, no descendants who matter. What diverges downstream must diverge at the level of slow structure — a different land-tenure or crop regime, a different settlement pattern or demographic ceiling, a different craft or commercial trajectory over a century or more — and not merely a different battle, treaty, or succession. The mechanism by which one death propagates must be structural in kind, though triggered by one person: a technique, a pathogen, a seed stock, a demographic or commercial threshold. And the chain from the death to the divergent structure must be traceable in the space you have, with the mechanism named rather than gestured at.

Then show that the design works. Say what each of the three positions says about the case and why its own commitments force that verdict. Identify which single feature of the case is doing the work, and show what happens to the verdicts when that feature is removed or its size changed — the individual duplicated, the population enlarged, the system moved away from the threshold. Give the structural historian's strongest reply: the most forceful way they can grant your causal story and still deny that it counts against their framework. Finally, since the case is for a seminar on whether the dismissal of individuals is a causal claim or a claim about explanation and description, mark explicitly where your scenario would be cheating, and where you think it is honestly extracting a verdict from a theory rather than stipulating one.
