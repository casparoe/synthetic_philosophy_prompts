I'd like an independent reading before I settle on a mark. The question was:

A "tiling" agent is required to approve a rewritten version of itself before installing it. Suppose a parent agent P reasons in a formal system T and will install a child C only if it can prove in T that every action C takes is safe. State precisely what the Löbian obstacle is for this arrangement and derive it from Löb's theorem. Then say whether the obstacle is best understood as a corollary of Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, and whether it is best understood as a version of the Vingean claim that a more capable successor's behaviour cannot be predicted by its designer. Finally, evaluate a proposed fix: give the parent, as an additional axiom, the formal consistency statement for the child's system. Does this restore the parent's ability to certify an improved successor?

The answer I'm grading reads as follows.

Löb's theorem says that for a theory T extending Peano arithmetic and any sentence φ, if T proves □_T φ → φ then T proves φ, where □_T is the standard provability predicate. Taking φ to be a contradiction and noting that □_T ⊥ → ⊥ is equivalent to the consistency statement for T, we recover Gödel's second incompleteness theorem: if T proved its own consistency it would prove ⊥. So Löb's theorem is a strict generalisation of the second incompleteness theorem, and it will do the same work for us here.

In the tiling set-up, the parent installs the child only if it can prove, in T, that whatever the child does is safe. The only general way to get such a proof is to establish a reflection principle for the child's system: for the safety-relevant sentences ψ, □_C ψ → ψ. Once the parent has that, it can read the child's conclusions as its own. But by Löb's theorem the parent can obtain □_C ψ → ψ only in the cases where it can already prove ψ outright. So the parent can never certify a child whose reach exceeds its own: for just those sentences the child might settle and the parent cannot, the needed trust statement is unavailable. Note that this is a limit on the parent's ability to justify installing, not a claim about the child's values. Even a child with exactly the parent's utility function and exactly the parent's source code is uncertifiable, whereas a child whose proof system is a fragment the parent can survey is perfectly certifiable. That asymmetry is the obstacle: the only successors a tiling architecture can vouch for are ones no better than itself, so the pressure in such a design is always toward drift rather than improvement.

Is this just the second incompleteness theorem again? I think so, essentially. The child, being at least as strong as the parent, cannot prove its own consistency; the parent, being no stronger, cannot prove it either; and without a consistency proof for the child the parent has no reason to accept anything the child says. The Vingean reading is also basically right. The whole difficulty is that the successor is cleverer than its designer, so its reasoning is opaque to us, and once you build something whose inferences you cannot follow you have lost the ability to say in advance what it will do. This is why the obstacle gets worse the larger the improvement is.

The proposed fix therefore seems to me to be on the right track and to work. Give the parent the consistency statement Con(T_C) as a new axiom, so that the parent is now strictly stronger than the child and can see that the child never derives a contradiction. Once the parent knows the child is consistent it can trust whatever the child proves, and the construction can be repeated: each generation adds the consistency statement, or the reflection principle, for the next, and iterating this along the constructive ordinals yields an unbounded ladder of self-improvement in which every rung is certified by the one below it.

One last remark, to show that the theorem is doing real work outside this setting. The same phenomenon explains why two mutually-referring proof-based agents in the prisoner's dilemma cannot cooperate: each would need to prove the other sound in order to prove that the other will cooperate, and Löb's theorem says neither can do that, so both defect. The impossibility of self-trust and the impossibility of cooperation between such agents are the same fact.
