I'm a fourth-year PhD student putting together the final session of a graduate seminar on diachronic rationality, and I want to close with a case I haven't been able to build myself. The topic is money-pump arguments against declining impatience — discounting that is steep over short delays and shallow over long ones, so that preferences violate stationarity while remaining complete, transitive, and continuous at every individual time. Every pump I sketch either sneaks in a synchronic cycle (which would make it a boring re-run of the intransitivity argument) or else depends on the agent being myopic or naive. So I'd like you to design one to specification.

Here is what I need. One paragraph of scenario, realistic rather than science-fictional — I'd suggest a hospital, a university payroll, or a workplace scheduling system; ordinary people, no demons, no perfect predictors, no exotic psychology, and no lotteries (every outcome is a known sum on a known date, so nothing turns on attitudes to risk). The victim's preferences at each time are complete and transitive over dated monetary outcomes; her only departure from the standard model is non-stationarity of the declining-impatience kind. No temptation goods, no addiction or visceral states, no false beliefs, and no surprises: she foresees the entire schedule of offers and knows exactly how her own preferences will evolve. Every choice she makes must be, by her own lights at the moment of making it and given correct foresight of what follows, strictly better than the alternative — so the pump cannot work by naiveté, myopia, or ignorance. The exploiter is an ordinary firm that posts its fee schedule in advance (so its offers can be taken as credible), ends up strictly richer, bears no risk, and produces nothing; check explicitly that its gain is not really a fee for a service her initial self would have paid for, and if your exploiter's profit only survives because it is advancing liquidity that her later self genuinely values, say so and either explain why that still counts as a pump or revise the case. Finally, she must end up strictly worse off, by the lights of her own preferences at the start of the sequence, than she would have been had the schedule never existed — and tell me separately whether she is also worse off by her own lights at the end, or content with how things turned out.

The sticking point I keep hitting is this: I post a swap at the early date and a swap back at the late date, and a fully sophisticated agent simply refuses the first swap, having foreseen the second. So I need a structure in which foresight does not let her opt out. I don't mind what device you use — a forced choice imposed by an institution such as a payroll election she has to make or a deadline she cannot extend, an offer that is made to her whatever she happens to hold, anything of that kind — but you have to flag the device explicitly and say why it isn't cheating. What would count as cheating: making her naive, myopic, ignorant, or intransitive at a time; stipulating that she cannot refuse an offer she would in fact refuse; or building the case so that the verdicts are asserted rather than generated by the preference structure. After the scenario, give me the numbers: her discount function, or a table of the dated comparisons that do the work; for each node, which option she prefers and by how much in her own units at that time; and a check that the end state really is dominated by her initial lights. Then give me the verdicts of three accounts — the sophisticated backward-induction account, on which she plans by backward induction over her actual future preferences; the resolute or plan-based account, on which rationality attaches to a plan adopted at the outset even where executing it means acting against what she most prefers at the moment; and the successive-selves or time-slice account, on which each temporal stage is a separate evaluator — saying for each whether she was exploited, whether she was irrational, and what she should have done. Then isolate the feature doing the work: show the pump fails when you remove each of (a) non-stationarity, (b) the impossibility of binding her future self, and (c) whatever makes the later offers unblockable, and tell me which of the three is doing the real work and which is merely enabling. Close with what a defender of declining impatience — someone who denies that steep short-run discounting is any kind of irrationality — would say about your case. I want it for this purpose: to force the question of whether money-pump arguments show that declining impatience is irrational, or only that it is expensive to have such preferences in a world containing institutions able to arbitrage between your own time-slices.
