I've been trying to get my head round how goverment departments here cost out safety regulation — the sort of thing where a rule that prevents a handful of statistical deaths gets waved through or binned depending on whether the monetised benefit beats the cost. As far as I can tell, the number stuck on a prevented death comes out of surveys: you ask people what they'd pay for a very small reduction in their own risk of dying, scale that up, and that's your figure. Three things about the practice puzzle me.

One: the same surveys also ask what people would need to be paid to accept a small increase in risk, and people name a figure much bigger than what they'd pay to avoid the same risk. The appraisals ignore those answers entirely and use the pay-to-avoid figure for both directions. Two: a decent chunk of respondents refuse to name any figure at all — no amount of money, they say — and those answers get stripped out as protest responses rather than treated as data. Three: when the people at risk are kids, what goes in is what their parents say they'd pay, even though it's the children's lives on the line, and nothing gets deducted for the parents' own distress or for the fact that the parents are themselves being counted elsewhere.

What I can't work out is what any of this says about what "utility" is even supposed to mean in these documents. Is the money figure a read-off of a preference ordering, so that utility is just a convenient index for ranking bundles and there's nothing more to say about it? Or is it meant to be a quantity of wellbeing that can be totalled across people and compared between them? And do the three bits above even presuppose the same thing? The first looks like it assumes the pay and accept numbers ought to have come out the same; the second looks like it assumes a refusal to trade isn't a preference at all; the third looks like it's helping itself to a surrogate-preference story and a welfare story at the same time, which I suspect is double counting.

So: reconstruct the theory of utility the practice only really makes sense under. Then tell me which parts it strictly requires and which would sit fine with a rival theory as well — maybe the protest-answers rule is compatible with several views and it's the parent/kid bit doing the real work, or the other way round. If the three bits do pull in different directions, say which one would have to give. And say concretely what an appraisal would look like if you swapped the implicit theory for (a) a view on which utility is a genuine cardinal measure of wellbeing, comparable between people, and (b) a view on which preferences can be incomplete or lexicographic and some goods just aren't commensurable with money. Would the guidance spit out different numbers, or would it stop being able to produce numbers at all?

One more wrinkle I want your take on: suppose someone proposes an amendment — use the accept figure rather than the pay figure whenever the people at risk already hold, in some legal or customary sense, a claim not to be exposed (they live beside a plant that wants to raise emissions), and keep using the pay figure where nobody was ever owed anything (a firm deciding whether to fit safety kit it was never obliged to fit). Does that amendment actually fix anything, or does it just relocate the same commitment about what the money numbers are measuring?
