I am writing a paper on moral compromise as a source of gains from trade, and I need a case for the section in which I argue that the usual division of labour between consequentialism and deontology has a mirror image. Normally the story is told with the deontologist as the rigidity problem and the consequentialist as the flexible problem-solver; I want to show that there are bargains whose gains are available only to agents who are not, at the point of action, maximizing — gains forfeited by exactly the flexibility consequentialism prizes. So I need a case built to specification, not gestured at. It will appear in a journal article for a general philosophical readership, so it must be readable without stage-setting: one paragraph, a realistic institution, nothing exotic.

The specifications. Two standing parties inside a single institution — a hospital ethics committee, a school board, a municipal council, something of that kind — who know each other well and will face each other again. Their values are genuinely different and not reducible to a common scale; neither is correcting a factual mistake, neither is a fanatic, neither can be converted by further argument, and neither is nearer the truth than the other. Hold all of that fixed. There is a package on the table: A gets more of what A weights heavily, B gets more of what B weights heavily, each conceding what the other cares about more, so that both are better off by their own lights and the gain is not just a divisible good split down the middle. Each concession must be a positive act of complicity in something the conceding party registers as a moral cost — not a forbearance, not a payment, not something it was going to do anyway — and the two concessions should be of roughly comparable weight.

The verdicts I want generated, not stipulated: an integrity-based deontological view of the Williams sort, on which an agent's identity-conferring commitments give her reasons that are not to be traded off, can accept the package; straightforward act-consequentialism, taken as a decision procedure, cannot reach it. It must fail for a structural reason — the gains depend on each party being credibly committed in a way a transparently maximizing agent cannot be, in circumstances where the parties' reasons are public and their characters mutually known — and the indirect escape route of cultivating the disposition while keeping the criterion private must be closed by the facts of the case rather than by fiat. No demons, no lie detectors, no perfect predictors: credibility has to come from ordinary social facts, and each party's unwillingness to deal with a known-flexible partner has to be a genuine commitment problem rather than pique.

Then show the work. State which feature of the case generates each verdict, run both views through it explicitly, and show that the consequentialist's failure is not simply a welfare sum that happens to come out negative. Say what happens to the case if you remove the transparency of reasons, and what happens if you remove the incommensurability — if both losses are measurable on one scale, does it collapse into a routine maximizing problem? Finally, give me the strongest objection a consequentialist will make, the indirect and rule-consequentialist replies above all, and tell me honestly whether the case survives it or whether it only shows that consequentialism must be self-effacing.
