I've been arguing with my running club about what our town council should do about the night market in the square: a few thousand people get a small amount of pleasure from it every weekend, and one man who lives above the square says it costs him his sleep and has made him ill. I found that I had very firm views and that I couldn't state the rule I was using. A friend who studied a bit of philosophy mentioned something called contractualism — I believe she said the philosopher was named Scanlon — and as I understood her, the test is whether anyone could reasonably reject a principle permitting the act, and the important consequence is that you are not allowed to add up many people's small complaints to outweigh one person's large one. I liked that consequence, then tried it against other cases and lost confidence in it, so I'd like the rule stated properly, by something that can hold all the cases at once.

My verdicts, going on gut alone. The night market: close it, or make the organisers fix the noise — three thousand flickers of enjoyment don't buy one man's ruined sleep. The case I half-remember from a podcast, about a man stuck in a transmitter room, where getting him out in time means millions of people miss a football match: get him out, obviously. A grid operator can either cut four hundred households' power for fifteen minutes each or one household's for two weeks, and that household needs the power for refrigerated medicine: cut everybody's for fifteen minutes — and here I notice that I am adding up, or at least spreading. A rescue boat can reach six strangers or my one friend, and the stakes are identical either way: save the six, and I can't see how the no-adding-up rule gets me there, since one death cancels another exactly. Same shape but not the same stakes: four hundred people each spared an hour of quite bad pain, or one person made to endure an hour of quite bad pain so that those four hundred are spared it — I go back and forth on this one and genuinely don't know what to say. A factory whose output gives each of a million people a benefit so tiny that not one of them would notice or bother complaining if it disappeared, at the cost of one identifiable person's broken night: wrong, but I can't say whose complaint it is. And one that worries me: if I refuse to add up three thousand people's flickers because they belong to different people, why am I happy to add up three thousand flickers when they all belong to the same person, given that nothing about the flickers themselves has changed?

My own attempt at the rule is this: you may add up costs when you are spreading them out, but you may not add up benefits when you are using them to buy somebody's suffering. Please test that against the cases, repair it or replace it, and give me at most two or three candidate principles stated precisely enough that I could apply them myself, with the case that motivates each clause, and their verdicts on cases I haven't listed — especially the same-person version, and a version where the benefit to each of the many is just as large as the burden on the one. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot save and which one you would overturn. Please also say how confident you are in what you've given me, and single out the part you are least sure of. Keep the whole thing under 700 words.
