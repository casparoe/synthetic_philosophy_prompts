Bit of an odd ask. I'm a philosophy PhD student (mind/lang, externalism-adjacent) and I've picked up paid work helping a mid-sized hospital system fold its departmental vocabularies into one clinical data dictionary. So: every week I get a dozen or so pairs of terms — "sepsis" as the ICU uses it vs "sepsis" as billing uses it, "malnutrition" in the paeds notes vs in the dietitians' notes, that kind of thing — and I get about twenty minutes a case, no clinician to ask, and whatever I decide has to be signed off by a governance committee of two physicians and an IT guy with zero patience for theory.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run: a short ordered set of tests that takes two terms as used by two communities and gives me merge / keep separate / escalate-to-a-human-because-this-is-a-purpose-question-not-a-semantic-one. Grounded in the real thing though, not common sense with jargon sprinkled on. I know Putnam's Twin Earth set-up, I know the slogan that meanings aren't in the head, I know the move that reference is fixed by what the stuff actually is and not by the criteria or stereotype users associate with the term. What I can't see is what any of that tells me to *do* with two spreadsheets on a Tuesday afternoon.

Three things I need. (1) Which bits of the theory have to survive the translation and which I can drop — a governance committee cannot handle possible worlds, and I suspect some of the distinctions matter more than others, but tell me which and why. (2) The hard cases: especially where both groups use the same criteria but the stuff turns out to be two different kinds (the jade-ish case — one word, two minerals), and where there's no underlying kind at all ("failure to thrive", "wellness"), where I think the theory definately goes quiet and I should say so instead of faking it. (3) Run it on three pairs, including one I have a stake in: I've already told the committee that ICU "sepsis" and billing "sepsis" should stay separate, and if the procedure says merge, I want to hear that. Also say plainly what the procedure can't do.
