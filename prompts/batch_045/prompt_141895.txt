I bet small amounts on sports — a few dozen bets a year, nothing serious — and I also sit on the committee of a very small charity that makes a handful of grants each year. I came to the Kelly rule through the betting, working on it on my own, and I have been trying to work out how far the argument for it actually reaches. I have ended up with a chain of reasoning whose conclusion I cannot accept, and I would like to be told which link to cut and what cutting it costs me.

(1) Where a decision of the same kind recurs, and where each round's result becomes the next round's starting point, strategies should be compared by their long-run behaviour. If strategy A finishes ahead of strategy B with probability approaching one, and ahead by a factor that grows without bound as the rounds pile up, then choosing B is a mistake that no one who understood the situation would make.

(2) The strategy that wins in the sense of (1) is the one that maximises the expected logarithm of the multiplier at each round. In my own case: stake the fraction of the bankroll that maximises the expected log of your wealth after the bet, decline any bet that is not in your favour, and never stake everything on anything.

(3) I want to keep both (1) and (2); as far as I can tell they are the whole case for the rule, and the simulation results I have run for myself bear them out.

(4) But nothing in (1) or (2) mentions money, or gambling, or even risk. What the reasoning seems to use is only that the results of successive rounds multiply together. So the general principle should be: for any decision whatever, maximise the expected logarithm of whatever quantity the decision multiplies.

(5) A friend of mine puts (1) in a way that does not mention repetition at all, and his version worries me more than mine. He says you should average over time — over the single process you are actually living through — rather than over an imaginary population of copies of yourself that branch apart at every chance event. On that way of putting it, ordinary expected-value reasoning is averaging over things that do not exist, and the log rule is simply what you get when you average in the only way that is genuinely available to you. His version seems to apply to a decision you make exactly once.

(6) Now apply (4) to the grants. Suppose next year's budget is roughly proportional to how much good this year's grants are seen to do, because donors give again when they can point to a success; the quantity we are multiplying does compound. (4) then says to choose grants so as to maximise the expected log of the good done. In particular it says to prefer a grant that will certainly save 10 lives over one that has a fifty-fifty chance of saving 1 life and a fifty-fifty chance of saving 80: the second has a geometric mean of just under 9, which is less than 10. It also says we should be indifferent between the certain 10 lives and a coin flip between 2 and 50 lives, and that the risky grant only becomes preferable somewhere around a coin flip between 2 and 200.

What I cannot accept is (6). We are not risking extinction here: the worst case is that one life gets saved instead of ten, the committee keeps meeting, and there is no realistic path to zero. If we took the coin flip in (6) year after year we would save around forty lives a year instead of ten, and after ten years the arithmetic is not close. Nothing about our situation seems to make the timidity rational, and yet the derivation looked like it had nothing to do with timidity.

So something among (1), (2), (4) and (5) has to go, and I cannot tell which. If I have to choose, I would rather give up the application to grant-making than give up (1) or (2), since without them I do not understand why the betting rule is supposed to be right at all — but I would need to be told what principle separates the two cases, because on the face of it both involve a quantity that compounds.

Three further things I would like addressed. First, a suspicion I have: maybe the mistake is that our *money* compounds but the good we care about does not, since we want the total number of lives and not the growth rate of anything — in which case (4) should have said to apply the log rule to the compounding quantity rather than to the valued one. But that seems only to move the problem: if we maximise the growth rate of the budget we appear to be treating next year's donors as more important than this year's patients, and if we instead maximise expected lives we are back with the ordinary expected value the argument was supposed to refute. Second, does anything turn on the fact that we cannot be wiped out, or that we could simply stop granting for a year after a bad outcome — or is the log rule insensitive to that? Third, a friend says the whole thing collapses because "lives saved" has a conventional zero, and that if you write the outcomes as the number of people alive afterwards, including the huge background of people who were never at risk, then the logarithm becomes almost flat and the rule quietly turns back into plain expected-value maximisation. I do not know whether that is a refutation of (4), a reason to think the log rule was never about value in the first place, or a confusion of mine.

Please tell me which step you reject, why that step looked sound, and what each of the alternatives would cost someone who wants to keep the betting rule.
