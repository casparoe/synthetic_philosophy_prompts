I'm writing a short paper on the inefficacy objection — the argument that individuals have no reason to refrain from contributing to collective harms like emissions, industrially produced meat, or a doomed ballot measure, because no single contribution makes a difference to whether the harm occurs. I keep having the same exchange with my advisor. She says that in the cases people actually argue about, once you imagine an agent who knows *with certainty* that she is not pivotal, every interesting reply to the objection turns out to be one of four moves: the expected-difference move (she might be wrong about where the threshold lies, and in expectation her act matters); the complicity move (she would be joining a joint action or cooperative scheme that harms); the expressive or signaling move (her act says something, or registers as demand); and the character move (acting that way makes her the sort of person who acts that way). Her contention is that these four are doing all the work in the literature and that nothing is left over — that "causal contribution" without difference-making is not an independent moral notion but a way of gesturing at one of the four. I want to test that with a case in which all four are switched off and she still seems to have a reason to refrain.

So here is what I need you to build: a single-paragraph, realistic, present-day scenario — no trolleys, no science fiction, no thought-experiment furniture — in which an agent's individual contribution is part of what produces a serious collective harm, and in which:

- the harm is threshold-shaped rather than a sum of individually perceptible increments, and it is *certain* — publicly checkable within the story, known to her, not merely probable — that the threshold will be crossed whether or not she contributes, so the expected-difference reply has nothing to work with;
- it is equally clear that the collective does make a difference: had far fewer people contributed, no harm would have occurred, so the puzzle is entirely about how a reason transmits from the collective fact to the individual act, not about whether the collective matters;
- her contribution is genuinely *in* the quantity that does the harming — the load, the tally, the concentration, whatever the mechanism aggregates — rather than merely correlated with it or preempted by it, so that she is a real causal contributor while being no difference-maker;
- the contributors are symmetric and simultaneous: no shared plan, no coordination, no institution assigning roles, no communication, and no fact of the matter as to who carried the aggregate over the line, so complicity and last-straw replies are unavailable;
- nobody ever observes the act, it leaves no trace that feeds into anyone's later decision, and it has no effect on her own motives, dispositions, or subsequent conduct, so expressive, signaling, and character replies are unavailable;
- her personal stake is trivial and ordinary — convenience, a small pleasure, a saved errand — and the case is not a rescue or an emergency.

The collective may be small if that is what certainty requires; what matters is that non-pivotality is known, not that the numbers are enormous. But if you can keep the numbers large and the certainty intact, so much the better, since that is closer to the cases the literature cares about.

After the scenario, do the check: state the harm, the operative quantity, the agent's act, and the relevant counterfactual, and show why (a) a difference-making or expected-consequences view says she has no reason to refrain, (b) a complicity view that requires joint action or a cooperative scheme also says she has no reason to refrain, and (c) a causal-contribution view on which contribution is not counterfactual difference-making says she does. Then give me your honest verdict on whether (c) survives once the four moves are off the table, or whether the case quietly smuggles one of them back in. Finally, isolate the feature doing the work: describe the minimal change to the scenario that converts her from a causal contributor into someone whose act is causally inert with respect to the harm, so that even (c) falls silent, and say how a difference-making theorist would argue that this change is morally irrelevant — that contribution without difference-making was never the sort of relation that could ground a reason in the first place. If you think no case satisfies all of these constraints at once, say so and explain what that failure would show about my advisor's claim.
