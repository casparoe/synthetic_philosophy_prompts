Im a journalist covering science and ideas, and I've finally been assigned the "is the world actually getting less violent" piece that every editor hands out eventually. I've read enough of the pop version to be suspicious of it: the charts seem to quietly switch what they're counting mid-argument (homicide rates, then battlefield deaths, then violent crime, then suddenly a graph about executions or school corporal punishment), and the denominator gets swapped whenever it's convenient. So before I type any sentence like "violence has fallen by X percent," I want the spec. Write me the desiderata: what a serious account of how violent a period is would have to get right — the phenomena it must cover, the cases any proposed metric has to handle, the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse, the findings from archaeology, criminology and demography it can't contradict — each with the case or consideration behind it, and with a flag on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp dressed up as rigor.

Here is the pile of things I keep tripping over, as raw material — but please don't just convert my list into yours; tell me which of these are genuine desiderata and which are politics smuggled in as methodology. (1) Per-year risk versus lifetime risk: as lifespans lengthen, the annual chance of being a victim can fall while the chance of ever being victimized rises, so "per capita" doesn't settle which quantity is the level of violence. (2) What I've started calling interaction-density scaling: people in dense, mobile societies encounter vastly more strangers per year than their ancestors did, so a constant violence-per-stranger rate shows up as rising violence-per-person-year — which denominator is principled, and is there any non-arbitrary one? (3) The displacement problem: violence that gets moved off the books rather than reduced — into prisons, into supply chains, into poorer countries, into the future as slow environmental harm. (4) Latent versus realized violence: a heavily policed peace may be one where enforcement is doing the work, and I can't tell whether that's less violence or the same violence differently arranged. (5) The tail: any metric built from realized deaths is nearly blind to the arrival of industrial-scale capacity for killing, but an expected-deaths metric can't be checked against evidence at all. (6) The definitional floor: is violence one thing or a cluster, does it include neglect, preventable death, violence against animals, and does it matter that people in earlier eras didn't recognize as violence much of what we now would. (7) Whether a hundred deaths in one afternoon and a hundred deaths scattered across a decade should be treated as the same number. (8) Whether "the world" is even the right unit, given that improvement in one region can be compositional — a decline here produced by moving something there.

The part I care most about: if some subset of these requirements can't be jointly satisfied, say so and show me which — because if the standard of evaluation turns out to be impossible, that is the story rather than a footnote to it. Also tell me which single requirement does the most damage to the measures journalists actually cite, and which of my eight worries above is really just one camp's prejudice. You can give the two or three standard approaches a line or two each at the end if you like, but the list is what I'm paying for, not the scorecard.
