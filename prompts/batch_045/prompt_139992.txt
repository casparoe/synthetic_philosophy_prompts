I want your help stress-testing a position I have ended up holding about moral dilemmas, and I would rather you argue against me than map the dialectic. I am not looking for a survey of who has defended what, or for the standard objections as they appear in the literature. I want the structure built from cases and principles, so that someone with no library could check every move.

The position has two parts.

First: genuine moral dilemmas are possible. There are situations in which an agent ought to do each of two things, cannot do both, and therefore does something she ought not to do whatever she does — with no option available of doing what she overall ought to do. I mean something stronger than that hard choices leave regret, or that agents reasonably feel torn, or that someone else would be warranted in blaming her. Regret without wrongdoing is not enough for me.

Second, and this is the part I actually care about: the familiar set-piece cases are poor arguments for that claim. Where two people need a resource that saves only one, or where a promise to a dependent conflicts with a larger public duty, the case can be resisted by massaging the description — by arguing that one duty is in fact overridden, or that the conflict was rigged by stipulation, or that the agent's real obligation was to the outcome and the appearance of two duties was an artifact. So I have moved to an accretion route. Take a large number of obligations, each individually weak and each individually, uncontroversially overridable by a slightly greater competing good: small promises, small debts of assistance, small returns of things borrowed, small obligations to be somewhere. No one of them generates a dilemma. But suppose enough of them land at once that the agent cannot discharge them all, and suppose no single one of them is such that it is the one that should be sacrificed. The accretion thesis says that genuine dilemmas can be generated from below, out of materials none of which is itself dilemma-generating — and that if dilemmas there are, they are far more likely to look like this than like the tragedies that get staged in ethics.

Tell me what would count against that package, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and in each case say whether the problem is one a sympathetic defender of the accretion route should concede or one she can reasonably dismiss. I want at least these five lines of attack addressed:

1. The compensation objection. If each obligation is individually overridable by a small competing good, why is the whole bundle not overridable by the sum of those goods — making the case an ordinary one in which many small reasons lose to one larger reason, rather than a dilemma? Is there any route from the overridability of each to the overridability of all that does not simply assume reasons aggregate additively? And if the accretion thesis depends on a non-additive or non-transitive account of outweighing, what exactly would show that account wrong?

2. Holism about reasons. If the weight or even the existence of a reason depends on which other reasons are present, then the accretion case may not contain the obligations its own description attributes to it. The hundred small duties may not survive into the situation in which they conflict, in which case there was never anything to accrete — only one situation with one overall requirement. What would settle whether duties are fixed holistically by the whole situation rather than summed out of pairwise comparisons? Is there any case that discriminates between the two, or is the choice between them itself underdetermined by everything we can get into view?

3. The gap between ought and may. Even granting that the agent cannot discharge everything and that no duty is singled out for sacrifice, why is the correct verdict that she ought to do each thing rather than that she may discharge any sufficient subset? What would settle this? I take it this is where the argument is thinnest, and I want to know whether any consideration short of stipulation favours the ought verdict — and, if not, whether the accretion route has really delivered a dilemma or only a permission.

4. The independence of residue. Suppose I am wrong and the right account of these cases is that the agent acts permissibly but reasonably feels residual guilt. What would show that? I would find it most persuasive if someone could separate the phenomenology — the guilt, the sense of owing something, the reluctance to be consoled — from the claim that a duty went unmet, without leaning on the very intuition the dilemma theorist is appealing to. Say whether that separation is achievable in principle or whether it is the kind of thing only a stipulation could deliver.

5. Scale. If the route works, then agents with many small standing obligations — anyone with dependents, colleagues, and a calendar — are plausibly in dilemmas much of the time, doing what they ought not to do most days. Is that a reductio, a bullet to be bitten, or evidence that my account of obligation, not my account of dilemmas, is what has gone wrong? And if I bite it, does the notion of a dilemma lose its force by becoming routine?

Two closing requests. First, an honest assessment of exposure: is this position insulated by its own qualifications, and if so is that a defect in a view of this kind, or an acceptable feature of a claim in ethics that is not making empirical predictions? Second, tell me which of the defeaters you have identified are live — plausible enough that you would expect some of them to be true — as opposed to merely logically available. I care much more about that distinction than about the length of the list.
