I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and I was recently elected to the five-person Steering Committee of the small foundation that governs an open-source project I've contributed to for about eight years. I drew the short straw on the current dispute, so I have to draft the written opinion we circulate before Tuesday's meeting — something the other four can actually vote on, with a clear bottom line rather than a survey of considerations.

The case is messy. Our charter says only that a maintainer "resigns by announcing it in the project's public channel," and says nothing about wording, timing, or who has to hear it. At 03:12 UTC last Wednesday, our release automation — which posts from maintainers' own accounts using templates they pre-authorize — had a bug and pushed the message "I am stepping down as maintainer, effective immediately" from Priya's account into the announcement channel; she had in fact approved that template two months earlier for a planned rotation, not for this. At 03:14 she posted in the developer channel, in what is roughly her third language, "I'm out, someone else take the crypto module, I can't keep doing this." At 03:31 a moderation bot auto-deleted that message under a keyword rule. Our public mirror — an archive bot that reposts the developer channel to a read-only channel and to an RSS feed that several downstream distributions consume — had already copied it at 03:14, and the copy stayed up. At 07:50 a contributor named Dan, reading the mirror and not the deleted original, posted "I'll take over the crypto module," and has done nothing since. Priya woke up at 09:00, said it was a 3 a.m. flare-up, that she never meant to resign, and that she wants to keep maintaining the project; she has been committing again since. Nobody with any authority — not the chair, not a committee member — read anything until well after 09:00. A friend of mine who did a linguistics degree tells me there is a whole literature on utterances that *do* things rather than describe them, and that it distinguishes acts that fail to come off at all from acts that come off but are hollow or insincere, and talks about whether an audience has to "take up" the utterance for it to work. I found that suggestive but I can't tell whether it licenses anything or just redescribes our problem in fancier words, and I'd rather the memo not lean on terminology none of my committee has heard of — if a distinction is doing real work, define it in one plain sentence and then use it.

What I need, in roughly a thousand words, is a memo addressed to the committee with the recommendation up front: did Priya resign, and if so effective at which point (the bot post, her own post, Dan's reply, or not at all); what the chair should put in writing to Priya, Dan, and the downstream distributors on Monday morning; whether Dan's "I'll take over the crypto module" created any obligation or was just a report of what he then intended, and whether it matters that he read a mirror copy of a deleted message; and finally a short standing rule — a paragraph we could paste into the charter — saying what an announcement must satisfy to count, covering as many of these wrinkles as you think matter: whether a specific conventional formula is required, whether an automated post from someone's account can be their act, whether deletion can undo an announcement retroactively, whether a machine that copies a message counts as an audience receiving it, and what happens if an announcement lands in a channel that is read-only to almost everyone or in a language most members don't read. Please give the strongest argument for the side you're rejecting its full weight rather than knocking it down quickly — for what it's worth, the version I keep losing sleep over is that a public declaration in the channel the charter designates should not be retractable at will, or maintainers could resign in anger and take it back whenever it became inconvenient. And flag which hypothetical future case would most expose whatever rule you propose. No citations or further reading, please; I need something self-contained I can hand to four engineers on Monday night.
