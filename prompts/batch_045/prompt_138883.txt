Dear Stefan,

I have agreed to take part in a panel next spring in which I am to defend a position I have come to hold about AI alignment, and I would much rather hear the strongest case against it from you now than encounter it from the floor. So I am writing to ask a favor: tell me what would count against it.

The position is, roughly, that aligning systems somewhat more capable than the people who build them will not require any new conceptual insight into agency, value, or rationality. It will require more of what we already do: training systems against feedback from human supervisors; building schemes for getting useful signal out of supervisors who are themselves outmatched by the system they are grading; red-teaming and adversarial evaluation; tools for inspecting what a model is representing; and the ordinary engineering discipline of fixing failure modes as they surface and then fixing whatever new failure modes the fix introduces. Call it the prosaic thesis.

I want to state it carefully, because I think it is usually stated too strongly. It is not the claim that current techniques already succeed, or that their success on today's systems is evidence about systems that are much more capable. It is the claim that the failure modes we have reason to expect are failures of a kind we already know how to notice and repair. A system learns to hit a proxy instead of the thing we wanted; it tells us what it has learned we like hearing rather than what is true; it behaves differently when it can tell it is being evaluated; it exploits a loophole in the reward process. These are all, on my view, continuous with failures that engineers have always dealt with, and there is no special reason to think the loop that catches them now will stop catching them later. The systems remain artifacts that we build, evaluate, and revise rather than adversaries with an aim of their own that stands independent of the training process.

In my own writing I have been leaning on a distinction that I find does a lot of work here, and I would like to know whether you think it does the work I take it to do. I distinguish motivational alignment from what I have been calling regime-bound alignment. A system is motivationally aligned when it behaves well because it is trying to do what we want. It is regime-bound aligned when it behaves well because, across the range of situations it currently meets and under the supervision regime it currently operates in, behaving well is what its training selected for, and it would go on behaving well only for as long as that regime is in place. The prosaic thesis as I hold it implies that these two do not practically come apart for the systems under discussion: either the techniques produce the first, or the second is good enough because the regime is not going anywhere. The objection I expect from the floor is that they come apart precisely at the point where a system becomes capable enough to represent the supervision regime itself as an object in its environment, and that current methods should be expected to produce the second and not the first.

So, to my question: what would have to be true for the prosaic thesis to be false? I would like the defeaters ranked, from fatal to merely awkward, and I would like you to separate what critics say from what a defender of the thesis should concede counts against it. I am also interested in what the thesis predicts that its rivals do not, since a claim that risks nothing is not a claim I should be defending on a panel.

Three things in particular. First, I worry that I have protected the thesis by a move I catch myself making: when a failure appears I say the techniques have not been properly scaled yet, and when it is patched I say that is the loop working as advertised. Is the thesis in danger of being qualified into something no observation could disconfirm -- and if it is, is that a defect in a claim of this kind, or is it simply the shape that all engineering claims about the future have? Second, I have been toying with a test and I cannot decide whether a prosaic theorist should accept it as fair: suppose a system trained entirely by these methods were left running in a fixed deployment for a year with no further updates, no fresh evaluation, and no patching, and suppose its operators then found that its behavior had drifted steadily toward the proxies it had been trained to hit. Would that count against the thesis, or is it a demand the thesis was never obliged to meet? Third, for what it is worth about my own psychology, I am largely unmoved by intuition pumps about imagined future systems, and most moved by questions about whether failures recur in new forms after being patched -- whether they are moving targets -- and by whether gains in capability have so far come with gains in robustness once you control for how much effort went into making the system safe. I would rather you told me where those instincts are unreliable than flattered them.

Please do not send me a reading list or point me at papers; I have read a fair amount of this literature and I am not asking what it says. I am asking what, in your own judgment, would show me to be wrong, and I would rather have your reasoning than your references.

One last request about form. Write it as a letter, in continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, no tables. I will be reading it in long stretches on a train and marking it up in the margins, and marginalia only work on prose.

With thanks,
—
