Next week I am giving a methods Grand Rounds in a school of public health, defending the following thesis to an audience of mainstream epidemiologists and biostatisticians: in a randomized trial with noncompliance, a Simpson's-paradox reversal across adherence strata does not show that the treatment is harmful among adherers; because adherence is a post-treatment variable affected by assignment and by prognosis, stratifying on it conditions on a collider, so the unadjusted intention-to-treat estimate is unbiased (or nearly so) for the effect of assignment while the stratified reversal is the artifact. The audience is fluent in causal diagrams and potential outcomes, believes measured baseline confounders must be adjusted for, is skeptical of intention-to-treat estimates as too conservative, and is sympathetic to per-protocol or adherence-stratified analyses as revealing the real biological effect; they care about whether the drug should be prescribed to patients likely to adhere. My sketch: under a diagram in which assignment is randomized, adherence is caused by assignment and by unmeasured prognosis, and mortality is caused by assignment, adherence, and prognosis, adherence is a collider (it is caused by both assignment and prognosis), so the adherence-stratified comparison mixes the null effect of the drug with selection bias; I will show a small numeric example in which the drug has no effect on mortality for any type of patient—whether they would adhere or not—yet both adherence-stratified analyses show harm. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience (not from a philosophy-of-causation crowd), ordered by how likely and how damaging they are; for each, phrase the objection as a mainstream epidemiologist or trialist would put it, and give what I should concede, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and a note on where the reply is weakest; finally, mark the one objection I am hoping nobody asks.
