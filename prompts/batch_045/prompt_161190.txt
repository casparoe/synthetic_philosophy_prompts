Two people in the ethics reading group I sit in on — Mara, a postdoc, and Dev, who's finishing his PhD — got into a long argument after we went over Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism and his one-thought-too-many point about the man who saves his wife rather than a stranger. I was supposed to referee and totally failed. Rough reconstructoin, eight moves:

(1) Mara: the integrity objection isn't a distinct objection at all, it's the demandingness objection in nicer clothes. Williams is really saying that impartial maximizing costs the agent too much — it asks her to abandon the projects she's built her life around. But consequentialists have a standing reply to that: scale the demand down, or go indirect. So all the talk of integrity does is dramatize a cost that any plausible consequentialism already counts.

(2) Dev: that misses the shape of it. Demandingness says the requirement is too costly; integrity says the requirement is the wrong kind of thing. Even a modest impartial demand — take the efficient job instead of the one you actually want, give away a tenth of your income — still makes the agent treat her deepest commitments as one input among others, revisable whenever the ledger says so. The complaint isn't about the size of the sacrifice, it's that the theory leaves her no way to be for anything in particular. Proof that they're separate objections: satisficing consequentialism answers demandingness completely and does nothing whatsoever for integrity.

(3) Mara: then make the dilemma explicit. Either the loss of integrity is a bad consequence for the agent, in which case consequentialism can weigh it and the objection just is demandingness again; or it isn't a matter of consequences at all, in which case Williams has helped himself to a non-consequentialist value and the argument is circular against utilitarianism. Either way his objection isn't the independent thing Dev says it is. Also: if integrity is a cost, the theory that counts it is the theory that respects it, and Williams can't complain that being counted is itself the insult.

(4) Dev: the dilemma only looks forceful if you assume everything that matters shows up as a cost to be traded off. Integrity is also a claim about what an agent is permitted to be moved by, and about what she can acknowledge as her reason. That's what the one-thought-too-many bit is doing: the man who saves his wife isn't being asked to do something exhausting, and he might not be sacrificing anything. Williams's point is that if the impartial consideration is what moves him, or what he thinks he has to appeal to, something has gone wrong in his relation to his own action. And note the objection doesn't scale with cost — it bites on easy acts too. So it can't be demandingness.

(5) Mara: that just relocates it. What the one-thought-too-many case shows is at most something about deliberation, not about rightness. There's a perfectly standard distinction between the criterion of rightness and the decision procedure: the right act is the optimific one, and good agents very often shouldn't calculate — they should act from love, loyalty, habit. The wife-rescuer isn't defective for not running the numbers, he's just not using the procedure. But Williams needs his objection to be about what's right. If it's only about which thoughts should run through your head, he's giving psychological hygiene tips and hasn't touched utilitarianism as a theory of what to do.

(6) Dev: if the gap between the criterion and the agent's actual reason is that wide, the theory has stopped being a theory of what to do in the only sense that matters. Ask the rescuer why he did it and he says: she's my wife. Now suppose he also believes that answer is really just shorthand for some impersonal fact about aggregate value. Then on his own theory the reason he'd cite isn't the reason he has — his first-personal reason has been demoted to a useful illusion. That's not advice about mental tidiness, that's a claim about which reasons he has, and it's incompatible with his reasons being what he takes them to be. Integrity is just the demand that your moral theory let your real reasons be your real reasons.

So far so good — then they each produced a case, and this is where I got properly lost.

(7) Mara's case: a committed aid worker pulls her own sister out of a burning car, and while doing it has a split-second thought along the lines of would this be defensible from an impartial standpoint? But — and this is her point — the thought isn't what moves her, she wouldn't cite it if asked, and if the answer had come back no she'd have done it anyway. It's more like a pilot running a checklist: the thought occurs in a monitoring register rather than an operative one. Is that one thought too many? If not, Williams's objection is about the role a thought plays rather than about its content or even its occurence, and then the consequentialist can just reply: fine, we'll keep the impartial consideration in the monitoring role only, and the objection has no bite against the criterion.

(8) Dev's case: take the mirror agent — someone whose one deep ground project *is* impartial benevolence, whose identity is basically I maximize welfare, who has no competing personal attachments and would be wretched doing anything else. Utilitarianism asks nothing of him that conflicts with his commitments: no cost, no alienation, no dirty hands. If integrity means don't turn me into a channel for impersonal value, the mirror agent already is that channel and is delighted about it. So either Williams has no complaint here at all — which shows the objection is contingent on people happening to have partial projects, i.e. it's a bit of moral psychology rather than a constraint on moral theory — or Williams does have a complaint, which means integrity was never about matching the agent's existing commitments, it's a substantive ideal about which commitments are permissible ones to have. And that version is much harder to argue for.

They went round about four more times without moving: Mara said the mirror case exposes Williams as a covert perfectionist, Dev said the checklist case exposes consequentialists as compartmentalizers who want the impartial view to be the real reason in the seminar room and no reason at all in the fire. I couldn't tell who'd won and I still can't.

Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what — not just who wins? I'd especially like: a verdict on each of the eight moves, whether the dilemma in (3) actually holds, whether Dev is right in (6) that the criterion-versus-procedure reply doesn't save consequentialism, and whether the two cases at the end really test what each of them claimed they test, or whether one (or both) of them is a dud that changes nothing. And: what's the thing they're both missing?
