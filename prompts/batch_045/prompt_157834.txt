I am writing a seminar paper on Sen's liberal paradox and I have built an argument whose conclusion I am certain is false, without being able to say where it breaks. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

Let me fix the vocabulary first. Unrestricted domain: the aggregation rule must return a social ranking for every logically possible profile of complete and transitive individual orderings. Weak Pareto: if everyone strictly prefers x to y, then x is socially preferred to y. Minimal liberalism: there are at least two individuals, and each of them is decisive, in both directions, over at least one pair of alternatives that differ only in that person's own conduct within their own personal sphere. Sen's 1970 result is that no rule meeting all three of these yields a social ordering.

Here is the argument, with the steps numbered so that you can point at one.

(1) In Sen's illustration there are three alternatives: the prude reads the novel, the lewd fellow reads it, or nobody does. The lewd fellow prefers that the prude read it to reading it himself, and prefers reading it himself to its going unread. The prude prefers that nobody read it to reading it himself, and prefers reading it himself to the lewd fellow's reading it. Decisiveness then ranks lewd-reads above nobody-reads and nobody-reads above prude-reads; Pareto ranks prude-reads above lewd-reads, since both prefer that outcome; and the social ranking cycles.

(2) What drives the cycle is that on the one pair over which Pareto does any work — prude-reads against lewd-reads — both people's rankings are preferences about how the *other* person exercises their own right. Call such rankings nosy. The lewd fellow gives up his own reading for the sake of the prude's discomfort; the prude takes up a book he finds indecent in order to deny the lewd fellow an enjoyed reading.

(3) The obvious repair is to exclude nosy preferences from the domain. But I do not see how to state that exclusion without circularity. A nosy preference just is a preference about another person's conduct *within a sphere protected by a right*. So the domain restriction presupposes an answer to the question the paper is asking — which spheres are protected — and cannot be stated before the theory of rights is in place.

(4) The alternative repair is to keep the domain unrestricted and restrict Pareto instead, so that unanimity counts only when it is not nosy. I also cannot make this work. Nosy preferences are not preferences that fail to affect the holder: the lewd fellow's amusement is as much an effect on his own welfare as my sleeplessness is an effect of my neighbour's tuba practice at three in the morning. So "affects the holder" does not separate the two cases. What is supposed to separate them is that in one case the other person's conduct lies outside their protected sphere — which returns me to the circularity of (3).

(5) So the paradox is not an artefact of Sen's choice of example, and neither of the two repairs that would confine it is available.

(6) Nothing in the proof depends on the content of liberalism either. Take any two rights held by different people over matters that combine independently — my right to paint my own front door green or blue, my neighbour's right to keep his dog indoors or out. There are four combined states. Let him be decisive over the dog's location holding my door green, and me over my door's colour holding the dog indoors. Suppose I prefer blue, given that the dog is indoors; suppose he prefers the dog indoors, given that my door is green; and suppose each of us nosily ranks a state in which the other's right is exercised in a way we dislike above a state in which our own right is exercised as we like. Then decisiveness and one Pareto move close a cycle over three of the four states exactly as before. So what the theorem really uses is: two or more right-holders, combinatorially independent spheres, unrestricted domain, and Pareto.

(7) Therefore any regime of rights held by two or more people over independent matters is incompatible with Pareto, and since (3) and (4) fail, the only remaining options are to abandon unrestricted domain wholesale, to abandon the Pareto principle, or to conclude that no society of two or more people can hold rights at all.

(8) The last of these I cannot accept: rights are held and honoured every day, and a paper that concluded otherwise would have proved far too much.

(9) One further thing troubles me. My construction in (6) requires the two spheres to be combinable, so it says nothing about two people who each hold a right over the *same* indivisible object — say, two siblings each with a recognised say over whether their father's single violin is lent to a museum. Yet contested rights over shared objects are the cases where conflict is most expected, and independent spheres are the cases where it is least expected. If my argument is sound, the ordering is inverted. I do not know whether to treat that inversion as a reductio of the argument or as a genuine discovery about independence.

I am attached to (1), to (8), and — I confess largely because it would be the most interesting thing in the paper — to (6). I would sooner give up unrestricted domain or an unrestricted Pareto principle than any of those, but I do not trust my own ranking of the costs. Please tell me which step you reject, why that step looked sound enough to pass me by, and what rejecting it costs: what I lose about the paper's central claim, and what each of the other escapes — a domain restriction, a conditional Pareto principle, giving up the requirement that the social ranking be an ordering, or reconceiving rights as something other than decisiveness over a pair — would cost me instead.

Please reply in continuous prose, as you would write in a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
