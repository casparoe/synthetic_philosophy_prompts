I have no academic training in philosophy or economics. What I do have is a small unpaid role on a committee that hands out a modest emergency fund, which means that several times a year I sit with a stack of short letters from people who all need money and who all describe, in their own words, how badly things are going for them. We have to rank them. In doing this we seem to be doing something that I'm told is called interpersonal comparison of utility, and I've been turning it over in my own head ever since, because the more I think about it the more I think the difficulty isn't that people lie or that we can't read minds. The difficulty is that even if everyone reported their own inner life perfectly honestly, we still wouldn't have a shared scale. My worst day and your worst day are both, for me and for you, the bottom of the scale, and the distance from my bottom to my ordinary Tuesday may be a completely different size than the distance from your bottom to your ordinary Tuesday. You can slide everyone's zero up and down and stretch everyone's unit by any factor you like, and nothing in anybody's experience tells you which sliding and stretching is the right one.

The scheme I found myself sketching on a notepad, as a way of making this concrete, works like this. Once a year every adult files two private reference points: their floor, meaning the worst day they can actually remember living through, and their span, meaning the gap between that floor and a day they'd call merely fine. A public agency then places everyone on one common scale by treating each person's floor as zero and each person's span as one. I noticed, after I'd written it down, that the scheme has a second problem stacked on top of the first: the agency still has to decide whether it is comparing how badly off people *are*, or how much people would *gain or lose* from a particular decision, and for two particular applicants those two questions can point in opposite directions.

Please write a joke set in a country that has actually implemented something very much like this scheme, which I'll call the Calibration. I'd like it to be a short, dry, matter-of-fact joke — a setup and a punchline, under 200 words, in which some ordinary scene (a clerk's letter, a hearing, a family argument at a kitchen table) reveals the Calibration handing down a verdict that common sense rejects. The laugh should come from the listener being able to see exactly *why* the machinery produced that verdict: because the choice of someone's zero and someone's unit already contains the whole decision, or because level and gain came apart. It should not rest on economists being heartless, or on utilitarianism in general, or on a pun on "utility" or "utils," and it should not name any philosopher, theorem, or paper — it needs to land for an intelligent reader who has never heard of the academic debate, while still being recognizably about the real problem to someone who has. Please don't add a footnote or a character who explains the philosophy afterward; if the joke has to be explained, it hasn't worked.
