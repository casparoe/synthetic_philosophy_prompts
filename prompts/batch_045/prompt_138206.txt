I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas, mostly — and I'm putting together a short podcast episode on invisibility as a philosophical device, which has meant going back to Glaucon's challenge early in book 2 of Plato's Republic, the story of the shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible and promptly does everything he was previously too afraid to do. I want to spend most of the episode reading Tolkien's One Ring against that story, but every time I sit down to write I get stuck on whether the two are even the same kind of thing. Glaucon's setup looks like a controlled experiment: the shepherd gains impunity and nothing else about him changes — same desires, same character, same capacity for shame — so if he still behaves badly, we're supposed to conclude that everybody is just only for the sake of reputation. The One Ring isn't like that at all. It has something like a will of its own, it gets heavier the longer you carry it, and it seems to corrupt people by way of the good they want to do rather than by dangling grubby rewards in front of them. Gandalf and Galadriel both refuse it, and the reason each of them gives has to do with what they would do with it if they had it.

So my friend, who did some philosophy at university and is enjoying being insufferable about it, says Tolkien is simply changing the subject: if the ring rewrites the agent, then the fact that the agent then behaves badly tells us nothing whatever about human nature, and Glaucon's question just goes unanswered. I think there's something in that, but I also half suspect it's Glaucon's version that's incoherent, and that Tolkien is quietly making the point that you can't strip away the social scaffolding — being seen, being answerable, having your plans blocked by other people — and still have the same person left over to run the test on.

Anyway. What I'd like is a reading that takes both the story and the argument seriously and doesn't use one merely to illustrate the other; I won't be quoting passages on air, I just need the shape of the argument and a few scenes to point listeners at. Concretely: is Glaucon's shepherd and Tolkien's bearer really the same experiment, or is the One Ring a different device — it doesn't only grant impunity, it makes you a ruler, since everyone else stays visible and stuck under the usual reputational regime, so maybe the real question on the table is the corruptingness of power rather than the rational defensibility of justice. What do I do with the spread of outcomes: Gollum carrying it for centuries, Boromir falling to it without ever touching it, Denethor and Saruman failing much the same test with no ring at all, Faramir and Sam shrugging it off quickly, Frodo holding out further than anyone and then failing at the very end, and Gollum's role in the Ring's destruction — is Tolkien saying something there about whether good will is any defence, or about whether the thing can be used well even by the people who mean well? And does Tom Bombadil, on whom the Ring seems to have no effect whatever, break the whole reading? Where the book genuinely resists the philosophical frame, please say so — I'd rather tell listeners that a bit doesn't fit than force it. And does Tolkien end up adding anything to the why-be-moral debate that the arguments on their own don't give you?
