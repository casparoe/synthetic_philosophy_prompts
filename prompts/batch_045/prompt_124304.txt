I am helping to write the standing decision rule for a small shared house — five adults, one kitchen, one common room, and a rotating supply of disputes — and I have found that my confidence about particular cases runs well ahead of my ability to say what rule I am applying. I know the shape of the impossibility result associated with Sen, the one showing that unanimity and a minimal assignment of protected choices to individuals cannot both hold for a social ordering that is transitive and defined over every pair, so I have stopped expecting a rule that satisfies every condition at once. What I want instead is the best rule that fits the verdicts I actually have, stated precisely enough to be written into the house rules, together with an honest account of what it cannot fit. Here are the verdicts.

First, the one I am most confident about. There are three options: Rowan reads a certain novel, Priya reads it, or nobody reads it. Rowan would prefer that nobody read it, and would rather read it himself than have Priya read it. Priya would most enjoy Rowan reading it, and would rather read it herself than have nobody read it. My verdict is that whether Priya reads is hers to settle and whether Rowan reads is his; so the rule ranks Priya-reads above nobody-reads above Rowan-reads, and therefore ranks Priya-reads above Rowan-reads, even though both of them prefer that Rowan be the one to read it. I am willing to give up unanimity to keep this, and I do not find the resulting conflict with unanimity a reason to change my mind about the case.

Second, the case I keep pairing with the first and cannot reconcile with it. Priya practises the drums in her room for two hours a night. Rowan has a neurological condition; sustained noise causes him pain and destroys his sleep. As far as I can tell the formal shape is the same: something Priya does with her own body in her own room, objected to by Rowan. But my verdict flips. Here I think Rowan's claim is genuine, that the rule should not let Priya's preference settle it, and that no-drumming should be ranked above Priya-drumming. If the difference is supposed to be that in one case Rowan is bothered in his person and in the other only in his mind, I want to know why that distinction bears the weight it is being asked to bear, given that in both cases what upsets Rowan is Priya's use of her own person and in both cases the upset is real.

Third, overlapping claims. The common-room window: Maya is cold, Jonas is overheated, and each seems to me to have as good a claim as the other to have open-or-shut settled by their own comfort. My verdict is that neither has the kind of claim that settles it, that the rule should return no ranking here at all, and that we should toss a coin or split the day by the hour. I am entirely comfortable with the rule falling silent, and I would rather it fell silent than that it ranked one of them above the other.

Fourth, the upstream case. The house subscribes to a magazine in which the novel is being serialised. Whether Priya reads a copy she already has is, by the first case, hers alone. But I also think the house may vote not to subscribe, and that in that vote Rowan's dislike of Priya's reading is allowed to count in a way it is not allowed to count downstream. I feel no contradiction, which probably means I do not understand my own rule. Is there a coherent distinction between deciding what someone may do and deciding what is available for them to do, and if there is, does it survive being iterated — the house declining the subscription, the library card, the bookshop?

Fifth, deferral and unformed preference. Wei is a new housemate who has not read the novel and has no view about whether to; asked, she says the house should just decide. My verdict is that the house may decide, and that this does not show the protected choice was never hers. But if she later forms a view, I think it reverts to her and the earlier decision stops counting, which suggests to me that the assignment of protected pairs cannot simply be read off whoever holds which preferences at the moment of decision.

Sixth, bad reasons. Rowan's actual objection to the novel turns out to be envy: he wants to be the only person in the house who has read it. My verdict is that this makes no difference and that Priya's reading is still hers to settle. I include the case because I suspect it causes trouble for the most natural way of drawing the line in the second case.

The best rule I have been able to formulate myself is this: exclude from the unanimity condition any preference whose content concerns what another member does with their own person or their own room; give each member control over their own protected pair, where control means their preference conditional on whatever the others end up doing; and where two members' protected pairs overlap, return no ranking and fall back on a coin toss or a time-share. Please test that against the six cases, tell me where it gives the wrong answer, and either repair it or replace it with something better. I would also like to know what the repaired rule says about three cases I have not listed: the neighbour who hears the drums but has no standing in the house rule; a decision such as the colour of the shared hallway, where nothing seems to be anyone's personal sphere in the first place; and a member who has a preference over their own protected pair but disowns it on reflection, saying they wish they did not want what they want.

Finally, tell me plainly which of my verdicts the rule you favour cannot accommodate, and, if I have to surrender one to get a clean rule, which is the cheapest to lose and what I lose by losing it. Please write the whole thing as one continuous piece of prose, the way you would write a considered email to a friend — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no numbered or lettered lists; refer to the cases by describing them in the sentence rather than by label. Say what needs saying and then stop.
