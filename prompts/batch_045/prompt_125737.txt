you're a professional philosopher of language who, for some reason, has also ended up writing and delivering a LOT of wedding toasts -- you're the person people ring when they're stuck. take your time with this one: start by brainstorming four or five ways a toast in this situation could go wrong (socially as well as philosophically), then make a short list of things the speech absolutely must not do, and then write it. about four minutes spoken, plain english, no jargon, no explaining the theory to the room, no asides to the philosophers.

the setup: the groom is my oldest friend. I did a philosophy BA ten years ago and now write ad copy, so I know just enough to embarrass myself. the bride is a philosopher of language, and her old supervisor -- a fairly big name in the field, and the sort of person who will listen to a toast as a professional -- is at table four. the other half of the room is the groom's family: his mum's side, from a village where the first language is romanian, and several of them speak almost no english. sixtyish people, band starts afterwards.

two constraints that are doing real work:

1. the couple put a line on the invitation asking people not to make any claims about them -- not you two are so well matched, not you're going to be so happy, nothing that could turn out true or false about who they are or what's coming. they want the toasts to do something rather than say something. (the bride's own work is behind this; she's got a paper arguing that the ordinary wedding speech is a category error, describing where it should be enacting.)

2. the groom's mother does want to do a toast and cannot face standing up in front of sixty people in english. so she wrote something and asked me to read it out. the trouble is that the bride's paper is specifically about cases like this -- she calls it the loaned voice, and her claim is that when you read out someone else's words, whatever gets done is done by you, not by them; you can report their toast but you can't perform it on their behalf. the supervisor disagrees (his line is roughly that a spokesperson reading out a head of state's statement isn't making it their own statement -- with the right framing and the right uptake it's still the head of state's). so half the room will be running that argument in their heads while I'm talking, and I can't exactly pick a side in four minutes.

two more things you should know. her text as written is full of assertions about the couple -- how happy she is for them, how she always wanted a daughter, that kind of thing -- so I can't read it as it stands without breaking rule 1. and she hasn't been entirely at ease with this wedding (no church, no priest, she's been a bit cool about it since the engagement), so I can't honestly stand up and tell everyone she's over the moon -- but equally I'm not going to say that at a wedding.

what I want the speech to actually pull off: set things up so that his mum performs one small act herself, in her own language, in the kind of set formula of welcome she grew up with -- not a description of anything -- at a moment where the whole room can take it up even though most of them won't understand a syllable of it. without me pretending it was my act, without me reading it like a newsreader, and without asserting anything about the couple on the way there. if there's a line where the room has to do something together for it to count, tell me what it is and where it goes.

give me the speech, and then a short note afterwards on which bits are load-bearing and why. and please, no one should leave feeling they sat through a lecture -- his family should just think it was a nice toast.
