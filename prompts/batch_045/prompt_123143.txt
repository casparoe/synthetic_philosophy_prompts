Two colleagues of mine got into an argument about Marta's plan to give 10% of her salary to whichever charity an evidence-focused evaluator rates as most cost-effective, and I ended up refereeing without having any idea what to say.

(1) Marta: cluelessness about the far future is just ordinary uncertainty, and ordinary uncertainty is precisely what expected value handles. The unpredictable downstream effects are as likely to help as to harm, so they cancel, and she should act on the evidence she actually has.
(2) Dev: the cancellation premise is the entire question. He distinguished sharp variance from silence: if the spread of possible long-run effects is orders of magnitude wider than the near-term effects she is measuring, then ranking charities by measured effect is ranking by the last decimal place — the term that dominates the sum has *no* expected value, not merely a large one.
(3) Marta: grant, for the sake of argument, that the long-run term is indeterminate. You still cannot get "do nothing" out of "I don't know" — the indeterminacy cuts both ways, so the only usable signal left is the near-term evidence, which still favours giving. Cluelessness gives nobody a reason to stop acting.
(4) Dev: agreed it doesn't licence paralysis, but said it does change the *shape* of the conclusion: it kills the strong claims, the ones about this charity being many times better than the alternatives or about giving being something like a duty. Under cluelessness the sensible move is to choose actions that look decent across a wide range of long-run scenarios — strengthening institutions, preserving optionality, or waiting for better information — not to maximise a measured near-term ratio.
(5) Marta: "robust across scenarios" and "wait for information" are themselves bets about how long-run scenarios are distributed, so the distribution Dev said we lack has been smuggled back in; and waiting is an action with its own unknowable effects, so it can't be the humble default.
