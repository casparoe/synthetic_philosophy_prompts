I write literary fiction under my own name and crime fiction under a pseudonym, and for eleven years I have kept submission records in a spreadsheet: two imprints of the same house, every submission logged with format, word-count band, whether it came through an agent, and the month it went out. When I finally totalled the columns, the acceptance rate for the pseudonym was lower. Then I sliced the same numbers, and in every slice it was higher — hardcover and paperback, short and long, agented and unagented, spring and autumn. I had walked into Simpson's paradox in my own data, and a friend, who is an economist and enjoyed this considerably more than I did, built the following argument on top of it.

(1) An aggregate association can be reversed in every cell of a partition at once; reversal in all the subgroups simultaneously is arithmetically possible, so "look at the subgroups rather than the total" is not a way out of the paradox.

(2) Nothing in the joint distribution itself tells you whether the aggregated table or the disaggregated one carries the causally meaningful comparison. The same numbers are compatible with the imprint being hostile to crime fiction and with crime submissions clustering disproportionately in the harder cell — the harder format, or the longer band — which does the work instead.

(3) So which reading is correct depends on a causal story about what influences what, supplied from outside the data.

(4) But the causal story is precisely what the disparity was meant to be evidence for. If I must already know it in order to read the table, the disparity is doing no evidential work; it merely illustrates a conclusion arrived at elsewhere.

(5) And the problem is general. For any observed disparity there is some partition that reverses it, and with enough covariates, one that reverses it in every cell; the data never select among partitions. So no statistical disparity is ever evidence for a causal hypothesis — not for bias in editorial selection or in hiring, not for a drug's efficacy, not for the connection between smoking and lung cancer, including in the much-cited graduate-admissions case. Every such inference must have smuggled its conclusion in beforehand as a causal assumption.

(6) That is absurd. Some disparities are superb evidence, and my friend, who takes statins on the strength of exactly this kind of inference, does not believe it either.

I would like to keep (1), which I checked by hand, and (6), because I want to be able to say that the eleven-year pattern in my own records is at least some evidence about how the house treats the pseudonym — or, if it is not, I want to be told plainly why not. I am willing to surrender anything between them, but I cannot locate the fault, and I would like to know what each surrender costs.

There is one further thing I cannot fit into the argument, and I suspect it matters. My own question is not really "is there bias?" but "where should I send the next book?" — and for that question I usually know which cell the book will fall into. If the pseudonym does better in every cell and I know which cell I am in, the disaggregated table seems to settle my decision whatever the truth about bias, which makes me wonder whether the sure-thing principle is doing quite different work from the inference in (2)–(5), and whether it still applies when the cell is not independent of the name on the manuscript — if, say, the imprint routes crime submissions into a different format because of what they are. If the decision question and the inference question come apart, I would like to know which one the argument is actually threatening, and whether the cost lands in the same place for both.
