hey — I'm on a four-person team that maintains the eligibility rules engine for a state benefits program (a SNAP-like food program plus two smaller cash programs). Rules come from statute, regs, internal policy memos, and since last year from a couple of court orders that flatly contradict some of the regs. The base is now ~4,000 forward-chaining rules over a few hundred predicates, and it regularly derives that the same household both does and doesn't qualify for the same benefit in the same month. Right now every one of those becomes a ticket, legal argues about it for weeks, and the case sits in a manual queue. We're drowning and I want a triage procedure instead.

Constraints, so you don't design me something we can't run: we are stuck with a SQL-backed forward chainer, no re-platforming, no new reasoner, no new headcount. Two analysts with no logic background have to run this, and they get about half a day per contradiction. Legal counsel signs off on anything that changes a rule, and they can realistically absorb maybe five repairs a month. Wrong denial means someone loses food assistance; wrong payment means an audit finding and a clawback from a person who can't pay it back.

What I want is a decision procedure, tests in order, that takes one derived contradiction and sorts it. Here's roughly what I think the steps have to cover, but reorganize and correct me:

1. Is it actually a contradiction? I suspect a lot of ours are artifacts: one side is negation-as-failure (we just have no record) rather than an explicit assertion; or the two rules have different effective dates or different scopes, so it's an indexing problem; or the "conflict" is really a borderline case at a vague threshold with rounding. Each of those should get a different cheap fix and a closed ticket, and none of them should be handed to paraconsistency as if they were deep.

2. Blast radius. This is the bit I keep failing to explain to my team: switching to a paraconsistent reading stops A and not-A from entailing everything, but it does not stop the contradiction from spreading — anything that follows from A still gets both values, and then so does what follows from that. So tolerating a contradiction is not the same as containing it. Give us a cheap, approximate way to measure how far one propagates with the SQL and python we already have — reachable predicates, depth, whether any output is a payment or denial, whether the disputed predicate is a hub used by dozens of other rules — and be explicit about what the approximation misses.

3. Is it containable rather than repairable? When can we cut the propagation path or gate the rules so the contradiction stays local and the case just gets flagged for a human, and when is that unsafe because the disputed predicate is load-bearing all over the system?

4. When is the right answer genuinely both — an actual dialetheia, not a bug? My guess is the test is something like: two norm sources of equal authority, no applicable lex posterior or lex specialis, both deliberately left standing, and the system really does require both to be true of the same person at the same time. In that case there is no repair to make, and the honest output is a written tie-break or discretion policy plus a human decision at case level. Please say clearly that containing contradictions does not commit us to believing true contradictions — paraconsistent and dialetheist are different commitments, and I need the procedure to only ever make the first one.

5. Otherwise repair, routed to legal, with priority set by blast radius and by who eats the error.

For each step: what counts as a good enough answer, where the procedure stops with a no and we close the ticket, where it stops with escalate, and where it hands off to a person because the question is one of values (which error we'd rather make) rather than facts. Mark those hand-off points as un-eliminable judgment; I don't want fake precision.

Then run it on three from our queue:

(a) Household income lands two dollars over the gross limit because one rule counts a chunk of overtime and another excludes it, and both rules trace back to the same statutory section.

(b) A court order from last March says a class of people must be treated as eligible for program A; the reg still says ineligible; the reg hasn't been updated and legal says the order is binding until further notice.

(c) The one I expect to dislike: a rule saying an applicant with an outstanding felony warrant is ineligible versus a rule saying eligibility turns only on financial criteria. Everyone here has treated this as a harmless legacy artifact that's been in the base for years, but I checked and the disputed predicate feeds something like forty rules including recertification and the overpayment-recovery module. If your procedure tells me this is the urgent one and the stuff we've been firefighting is mostly noise, say so and show the reasoning.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do. I assume it can't tell us which substantive repair is correct, can't adjudicate the value conflict between speed and accuracy and not denying people who qualify, and that step 2 needs tooling we don't have — so tell me what to substitute and what that substitute gets wrong.

No reading list or citations please, I have no library access and I'm not writing anything up — I need something I can definately hand to two analysts on Monday morning.
