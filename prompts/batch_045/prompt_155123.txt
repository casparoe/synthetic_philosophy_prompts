I'm on a small committee of a professional society that has to decide, roughly twice a year, whether to withdraw an invited lecture, issue a condemnatory statement, or do nothing, and I've found that every framework we reach for is either a free-speech absolutism that treats all informal sanction as mob coercion or a social-justice framing that treats it as legitimate counter-speech — neither of which tells us anything about the cases we actually face. So before I read another general essay on whether "cancel culture" exists, I want the specification: the desiderata that a theory of informal social punishment has to satisfy to be good enough for an institution like mine to act on (not for a legislature, and not for a moral philosopher scoring positions from the outside). For each requirement give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are only preferences of a camp — I suspect several things people demand here, such as that a sanction be imposed by someone with demonstrable authority over the sanctioned person, or that it be accompanied by publicly articulable reasons, are really just the institutional mindset generalised beyond its jurisdiction.

The conceptual work is where I want the effort, and here are the phenomena I insist a specification handle, several of which I've had to name myself because the literature I've seen doesn't distinguish them. (1) Sanctions with no sanctioner: hundreds of individually trivial acts — an un-invitation here, a withdrawn invitation to co-author there — aggregate into an outcome worse than any participant intended or could have prevented; tell me whether a theory must be able to evaluate an aggregate that no agent chose. (2) The legibility asymmetry: within a community, declining to join a sanction is itself read as a position, so a theory has to say whether non-participation is sanction-like conduct, neutral, or something the theory simply has no verdict on. (3) Anticipated sanctions that never arrive: much of what people complain about is self-censorship in the shadow of expected costs, and if a theory counts those as punishments then nearly all contested speech inside any community is already being punished, while if it doesn't it seems to miss the central complaint. (4) The deniability asymmetry between collective and diffuse agents: my society can be asked to justify itself; a scattered crowd cannot, and any requirement of answerability appears to bind the actors who do least harm and exempt the ones who do most. (5) Threshold-insensitivity: because one person's contribution to a cascade is usually imperceptible, expected-consequence reasoning is unavailable to the individual deciding whether to sign the letter — so a theory that aims to guide individuals must either be non-consequentialist about the aggregate or tell us which surrogate reasoning ("what if everyone relevantly situated did this?") is licensed, and what it does to proportionality when the total harm is a function of how many happen to act. Please also keep the distinctions that I think must not be collapsed: withdrawing association oneself versus getting third parties to withdraw it, answering a speaker versus reducing their audience, condemning versus imposing costs, the wrongness of punishing without standing versus the wrongness of punishing excessively, and punishing an individual versus shifting what is sayable at all (if the point is forward-looking norm change of the kind that makes a practice come to seem dishonorable rather than refuted, say whether that makes the individual's desert irrelevant and whether it uses them merely as a means).

Where I most want you to go is the structure of the list itself: tell me which two or three requirements you take to be in genuine tension and why — my own candidates are action-guidingness for a single agent who doesn't know how many others will act, against proportionality to the harm actually produced; and severity that depends on other people's unpredictable uptake, against any requirement that a sanctioner be able to gauge the sanction's weight in advance. Then tell me which single requirement does the most exclusionary work, and which of these requirements are contingent on empirical claims that could turn out either way (whether audience costs really do cascade, whether sanctions harden rather than soften the sanctioned person's views) as opposed to holding as a matter of the concept of punishment. Only after that, and briefly, score the usual families against the list — the more-speech/counter-speech view, deterrence and norm-enforcement views, retributive or desert-based views, and views on which some speech is itself a rights-violation that sanction merely answers — a line or two each, and skip any you think I've misdescribed.
