Not sure this is the right place but here goes. I help run a volunteer network of cheap air-quality sensors — about 60 of them around a mid-sized city, same model, same firmware, no reference-grade station anywhere near us, and no budget for one. We argue about calibration constantly and there's a pattern I keep seeing that I don't have a word for. It shows up in our group chat and also in papers I read, and I can never quite point at what's wrong (or right) about it.

(i) Someone says: my sensor has read the same value every morning at 7am for three weeks and it tracks the same daily curve as my neighbour's, so it's reliable. The obvious reply is that a sensor stuck on one wrong number, or drifting slowly, does exactly that too. Agreement with itself shows the thing is consistent, not that it's pointed at the truth.

(ii) But then someone says: okay, but two sensors agreeing is not nothing. If they were just random they'd disagree. And that feels true in a way (i) doesn't. Say A and B agree with each other 90% of the time — people in the group immediately start talking as if each one is therefore at least 90% right, and I'm pretty sure that inference is broken, but every time I try to explain why I end up just repeating myself. It seems like agreement buys you *something* real even when it doesn't buy you that.

(iii) And it spreads. A gets calibrated against B, B against C, C against A. Each pairwise calibration gets written up on its own, seperately, and each one looks completely fine on its own, and at the end the whole network gets described as "calibrated". No single step was circular. There's just no step anywhere that touches the world.

(iv) The one that actually bugs me: the argument in (ii) depends on the sensors failing *independently*. But the only way we could ever tell whether they fail independently is by looking at what the sensors say. So it's the same loop again, one level up, and I don't know whether that makes it worse or whether it's just the same thing wearing a hat.

A friend who did a philosophy minor said something vaguely about "bootstrapping" in epistemology, but everything I've found under that name is about a single source checking itself in a single loop, and it's always presented as an obviously bad argument that gives you knowledge too cheaply. Mine isn't one loop, it's smeared across a bunch of individually fine-looking steps, and in (ii) I can't shake the feeling that something genuine is being gained. So either I've found the right neighbourhood and I'm misdescribing it, or it's a different thing.

Things I'd want carved rather than named: what exactly the loop does and doesn't buy you (I think it rules some stuff out and not other stuff, and I want to know which is which). Whether a partial anchor breaks the circle or just moves it — say we know some physical constraint, like that two of the sensors sit in identical sealed enclosures and so *should* read the same, or that a set of readings has to add up to a fixed total. Whether one genuinely trustworthy sensor somewhere in the network calibrates the whole thing, and how far that spreads before it runs out. Whether any of this changes if the question is about changes over time rather than absolute levels — "pollution went up 20% this year" feels much less demanding than "the level is 34". And whether it matters if the sensors are deliberately different models instead of sixty copies of one model, which is the fix everyone proposes and which I suspect just relocates the problem.

So: does this have a name, in epistemology or in statistics or measurement theory, and if the nearest concepts only partly fit, tell me which ones, how my case differs from each, and which fits best. If nothing fits, say so and coin something, with a definition and the boundary cases where it stops applying. I don't want a label, I want the carving. And please flag where your own answer is shakiest — which bit of the distinction you'd abandon first under pressure, and what would convince you that (ii) is either completely fine or completely empty.
