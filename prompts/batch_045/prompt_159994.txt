Here's the draft, warts and all:

---

Benacerraf's dilemma is standardly presented as a clash between two demands on a theory of mathematical language. First, a semantic demand: our account of mathematical sentences should be of a piece with our account of the rest of language — Tarskian truth conditions, singular terms referring, quantifiers ranging over a domain. Second, an epistemic demand: our account should explain how mathematical beliefs could be reliably produced, in roughly the way our best accounts explain reliable belief about medium-sized dry goods. Platonism satisfies the first and wrecks the second, because reference to acausal, non-spatiotemporal objects can't do explanatory work in a story about how we end up believing truly. The anti-realist reconstructions — truth as provability, truth as holding in all models, truth as following from our stipulations, and so on — do the reverse. The usual lesson is that we must give something up, and the familiar menu of positions (fictionalism, plenitudinous platonism, various structuralisms, quietism about reference) is really a menu of different decisions about which demand to soften.

I want to argue that this framing smuggles in an assumption: it treats both demands as applying to mathematics as a single block. My claim is that the epistemic demand is regional while the semantic demand is not, and that once you see the asymmetry the dilemma stops being a dilemma about mathematical truth and becomes a problem about the unity of mathematics.

The epistemic side is regional because our mathematical beliefs aren't all held the same way. In one region — elementary arithmetic, finite combinatorics, anything where there is a feasible proof or a computation you can actually run — we appear to have a check on the belief that is insensitive to what the objects are: you re-run the proof, you check the computation, and it doesn't seem to matter for the reliability of that procedure whether numbers are sets or sui generis or nothing at all. Call this the checkable region. In another region — whether there are measurable cardinals, whether the continuum hypothesis has a determinate truth value, whether some strong axiom of infinity is even consistent — the only game in town is what set theorists themselves call extrinsic justification: fruitfulness, fit, elegance, explanatory payoff downstream. Here the reliability question has completely different shape, and it is only really here that Benacerraf's challenge has teeth.

The semantic side, by contrast, looks stubbornly all-or-nothing. If the Tarskian apparatus is the right story for "2 + 2 = 4," it's hard to see how it stops being the right story for "there is a measurable cardinal." Reference and satisfaction aren't the sort of thing that switch off partway up; you don't get a domain for arithmetic and no domain for set theory while running one uniform semantics. Structuralists and fictionalists can look like they're doing exactly that, but as far as I can tell what they actually offer is a uniform paraphrase rather than a regional semantics.

So the dilemma yields different verdicts in different places, and I want to defend a staggered realism: as much realism as you like in the checkable region, non-factualism or an error theory where the epistemic demand bites. I take this to be better than plenitudinous platonism, which on my reading buys uniformity by making truth cheap enough that it stops explaining anything about reliability, and better than blanket fictionalism, which throws away the regions where we do seem to have a reliability story. Two loose ends I can't close. (1) The regional boundaries look both arbitrary and technology-sensitive — I don't want the metaphysics of arithmetic to depend on processor speed — and my tentative fix is to cut on the kind of justification available in principle rather than on feasibility, but I'm not sure that's stable. (2) I'd like to say the dilemma doesn't generalize cleanly to the moral case, because there the properties are partly constituted by our responses in a way mathematical properties aren't, which would break the analogy — but I can't make this precise and it's the paragraph I'm most tempted to delete.
