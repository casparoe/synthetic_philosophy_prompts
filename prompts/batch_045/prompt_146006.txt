I am defending a thesis chapter in a departmental work-in-progress session next week: twenty-five minutes of talk, then an hour of questions. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a survey of the literature and not a sympathetic restatement of my view. Try to break it.

The thesis. Call it partition-relative causation. The sketch I will be defending runs: (1) every causal claim is evaluated against a partition of the space of possible states of the system concerned, that is, against a grain at which the cause and the effect are typed; (2) the exclusion principle invoked by the standard argument against mental causation — no one effect has two distinct sufficient causes at the same time, short of symmetric overdetermination — holds only when the competing candidates are typed by one and the same partition; (3) in a realization pair, the psychological typing is coarse, since it collects physically heterogeneous realizers under one type, while the physical typing is fine, so the mental candidate and the physical candidate never compete for the same effect; (4) hence the argument cannot even formulate its competition premise without first selecting a partition, and nothing in causation itself selects one — the selection is fixed by which counterfactual structure we are trying to track; (5) the price, which I pay willingly: there is no partition-free answer to the question whether mental states cause behaviour, and the mental adds no causal influence over and above the physical, only counterfactual stability across a range of realizers.

The audience. Two people who accept the exclusion argument, one cheerfully (mental properties simply are not causal-property-shaped) and one as a route to a ruthless identity view; both know the closure and supervenience premises by heart and will want to know what a coarsely typed cause is supposed to be made of. Three non-reductive physicalists: one who favours proportionality-style replies, one who favours the program-explanation line, one who holds a realizer-friendly inheritance view. They are my natural allies, but each will want to know what my apparatus adds to theirs, and each will be quietly annoyed if I don't say so. One philosopher of science of interventionist sympathies, for whom causes are variables in a model and who will ask what my admissible variable sets are and whether the coarse variable is invariant, modular and independent. One metaphysician who holds that causation is a relation in nature rather than a relation to our questions, and who will think any view on which causal facts shift with explanatory interest has changed the subject. Several graduate students and a visiting aesthetician who suspect the whole debate is verbal. The chair, a Humean about laws, will let anything run long.

What I want from you. Eight or nine objections, in the order they will actually arrive in the room rather than the order they appear in the literature. For each: who raises it; how that person would phrase it, using their own vocabulary (screening off, overdetermination, proportionality, intervention, invariance under intervention, realization, closure, distinctness of properties, same cause under a different description, nothing left for the mental to do); whether it really bites against the thesis as stated above or only against a weaker version; a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds; and one sentence on where that reply is soft. Then: the two objections I should concede in my first two minutes rather than wait to be asked, and how to concede them without surrendering the thesis; the single reply that has to land if the chapter is to survive, and your honest view on whether it does; and the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Lead with a single sentence stating your bottom line — the objection most likely to sink the session and whether my answer to it works. Then give the prep sheet. No preamble, no restatement of what I have asked for, and no general background on the exclusion argument before the first objection. Keep the whole response under about fourteen hundred words.
