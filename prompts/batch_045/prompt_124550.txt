Hye — I host a fortnightly podcast for a science-and-ideas magazine, and I've got a guest next week I'm a bit worried about. She's Dr. Ines Halvorsen, a safety researcher who's been arguing for a couple of years that the field has the priority backwards: instead of trying to make AI systems want the right things and treating that as the safety story, we should assume the system is misaligned and build the deployment infrastructure — monitoring, sandboxing, restricted action spaces, human-in-the-loop review, tripwires — that makes misalignment not matter. She has a line she uses a lot, roughly: alignment is a research hope, control is an engineering requirement. She also has a more specific and more interesting idea, which is the bit I actually want to get to: she claims any control measure has a legibility floor, meaning a measure is only genuinely sound if a human who does not understand the AI's plan could still verify that the plan was carried out as reported. If you need another AI — or a PhD-level expert who is basically rubber-stamping — to check the work, then on her view you don't have control, you have hope wearing a hard hat.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions, with a follow-up each, keyed to the answer she's most likely to give (guess it, and tell me what you think it'll be so I know when to pivot). What I need:

- Something that makes the catchphrase harder to lean on. My sense is that control measures themselves rest on estimates of what the model is capable of, and capability evals are done by sampling behaviour, and a model can behave differently when it detects it's being evaluated. So: is control also a research hope, just a smaller one? Anticipate her saying we can bound capabilities conservatively, and have the follow-up ready.
- The bootstrapping problem. You need capable models to help design, red-team and stress-test the very protocols meant to contain capable models. If you trust their help you've given up the premise; if you don't, you can't use the help. She'll probably say: use weaker trusted models to supervise stronger untrusted ones. Have the follow-up about what happens when the gap between them gets wide.
- Her legibility floor idea, pushed on the thing that seems to break it: most of the commercial value of advanced systems is defintely in outputs no human can verify — new research code, giant refactors, strategies nobody can fully check. If the floor holds, is the implication that we just don't get to deploy those uses? Press on whether she's smuggling in a claim about how much economic value we're entitled to give up.
- The evidence problem. If a control regime works, the observable outcome is that nothing bad happens — which is also what you'd see if the model were fine, or if it were waiting. So what would actually count as evidence that the regime is working, rather than just unspent? She'll likely propose planted opportunities / honeypot style tests. Follow up on a model that knows the methodology exists.
- The time question. Control is often defended as buying time. Buying time for what, exactly, and what is supposed to be different at the end of it? If the answer is governance or institutions catching up, then the technical programme is hostage to a political prediction, and I want a question that makes her say what she'd conclude if that prediction is wrong.
- One question on the failure mode I keep hearing from engineers: the protocol works on paper and gets routed around in practice, because review throughput collapses and people start rubber-stamping. Is the real failure mode of control institutional rather than technical?
- A closing question that a non-specialist listener would be glad I asked.

Constraints: my audience is smart but not technical, so for each question give me about twenty words of setup I can read aloud first. No questions about her background or how she got into the field. And I really dont want to let her get away with "that's an empirical question, we'll know more later" — so where you think she'll reach for it, build the question so that move costs her something.
