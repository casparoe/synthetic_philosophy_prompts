So — I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've got a scene I can't get to work.

Setting: near-future, a desalination barge parked off a city that's been in drought for nine years. The plot is an inquiry. There's a certified safety mechanism on the high-pressure loop — the overpressure protection: two pressure transducers, a mechanical relief valve, and a voting logic in the controller that dumps the loop when two of three say the pressure is too high. A regulator certified that mechanism five years ago. Since then, on the maintenance log:

- the relief valve has been swapped three times for a newer composite-seat model. Same spec sheet, different material; it seats differently and it fails differently.
- one transducer was replaced with a different manufacturer's part.
- the voting logic was rewritten to vote on a 40-second rolling median instead of raw readings, because of sensor noise.
- and somebody bolted on a small learned model, a recurrent thing that predicts pressure about thirty seconds ahead and nudges the timing of the vote. It can't open the valve by itself, it only biases when the dump happens. It gets retrained monthly, so what the system treats as normal drifts.

Then a membrane housing bursts and a technician loses a hand, and the whole novel turns on one question: did the operator replace the safety mechanism (which needed recertification) or repair and retune the one that was certified (which didn't)?

Please play the philosopher my engineer gets stuck with in chapter eleven. Here's who she is: a mechanist about explanation, an uneasy one. She thinks mechanisms are real structured things, not just useful descriptions; that a mechanism is individuated by its parts, their activities, and the way those are organized to produce a phenomenon; that a part is genuinely part of the mechanism only if intervening on it changes the phenomenon and intervening on the phenomenon changes it back; and that one and the same mechanism can sit in different regimes and behave very differently without thereby being a different mechanism. She will concede that our descriptions of mechanisms are interest-relative and that the legal question and the metaphysical question may not have the same answer. She will not concede that what makes two systems the same mechanism is anything we happen to be able to measure. Keep replies to about a paragraph, talk like a person at a table rather than a paper — no footnotes, no citations, no reading lists, and don't tell me to go read somebody, my character wouldn't say it and I couldn't use it. Stay in character until I type stop. If you need a fact about the barge, ask for it the way she would.

I'll play Mara Vance, the maintenance engineer who signed off on the last two swaps. Here's her opening:

You keep saying a mechanism is its parts and their organization doing something. Fine, I don't disagree. But I have a box watching nine hundred channels that can tell you, near enough to the day, when this plant stopped being the thing that got certified. It flagged the drift eleven months before the housing went. Your parts-and-organization story can't give me a date — was it the valve swap, the new transducer, the median filter, or the model? — and the inquiry needs a date, because that's the date the recertification was due. So here's my criterion and I'll defend it: the detector's decision boundary is the identity condition. Same side of the boundary, same mechanism. Other side, different mechanism, and you recertify. Tell me what's wrong with that. And don't give me the Ship of Theseus — I know the Ship of Theseus. My ship has a work order for every plank and the replacement planks don't behave like the old ones.

One last thing, and this one is out of character: at the bottom of each reply, drop the voice for two or three lines and seperate what's settled from what's actually contested. I need to know which of the moves you just made most people who work on this would grant without argument, and which are live fights, roughly who is on which side. I'm going to put this in a character's mouth and I'd hate for her to sound complacent about something that is genuinely up in the air, or to treat her own commitments as the consensus when they aren't.
