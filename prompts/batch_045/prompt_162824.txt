I'm a backend engineer and my team ships an LLM-based coding assistant, and we keep having the same argument in standup: does it actually understand code, or is it just very good at pattern-matching? Marketing wants a yes/no. I think the argument goes nowhere because we are all using "understand" differently and nobody will say which position they actually hold. So I want to build a sorting quiz to force people to commit.

What I want from you: a taxonomy / decision tree of the positions on this question — would passing an open-ended, unconstrained conversational test (the imitation-game style setup: a judge chats with the thing for as long as they like and has to say whether it's a person) settle that it has a mind / understands things / is conscious? Please build it from the logical space first: the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the real named views in that space, and call out the combinations nobody occupies with a one-line guess about why (incoherent? unattractive? just unexplored?).

Format: about eight questions, each one a concrete case rather than a statement to agree with, two or three answer options each, plus a key that maps answer patterns onto leaves. Audience is my teammates — smart, zero philosophy, alergic to jargon — so every question has to be answerable by someone who doesn't know any of the labels. No question that just asks "do you think machines can think?"

The distinctions I want doing the branching:
- black box vs mechanism — is the verdict allowed to depend on how the thing is built, or only on what it says? Case: something that's a giant lookup table mapping every possible conversation history to a plausible reply, with no other internal state whatsoever. If that thing keeps a judge fooled for a lifetime, is there a mind there? (I half-remember a named objection sort of like this, something about a "blockhead"? Don't rely on me having the name right, and honestly I don't care who said what — the labels just need to be good enough to put in a doc.)
- definition vs evidence — does passing the test *constitute* having a mind, or is it merely evidence for a further fact? If something could have a mind and still fail the test, is that a problem with the thing or with the test?
- competence symmetry — a being vastly smarter than us answers correctly, but its answers are so far beyond the judge that the judge can't recognize them as right and votes "machine." Test failure or test artifact?
- provenance of the answers — our models are trained on basically everything humans have written. If a system passes partly because it has absorbed the actual recorded conversations of millions of people, does that change what passing shows, compared to a system that got there some other way?
- whether being made of the right stuff (biology, or whatever the relevant "stuff" is) matters at all, and whether a neuron-by-neuron simulation of a real person's brain that never talks to anyone is a problem case for the test.
- binary vs graded — maybe the right output is "how much of a mind" rather than a yes/no.
- whether the real question is about a social status (do we owe it anything, do we treat it as a person) rather than a hidden inner fact.
- whether the judge being told the system's architecture should be allowed to change the verdict.

Leaves I definitely want: the Turing-ish behaviorist/functionalist one; the "right behavior produced the wrong way doesn't count" one (Chinese Room flavored); a biological / right-substrate one; a "it's just about which predictive stance is useful, patterns all the way down" one; and a skeptical "there is no fact of the matter / this is the same problem as other minds" one. Plus at least one leaf you have to mark unnamed because nobody defends it.

Two more things. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate — I'd rather get a note saying "these collapse, and here's the case that would pry them apart" than a fake branch that just restates the same view twice. And at the end, tell me which of my eight distinctions is doing no real diagnostic work so I can cut it, and give me one question I didn't think of that does separate two leaves I lumped together.

I'm going to run it as an anonymous poll and then put the taxonomy in a team doc, so keep the wording something I can paste without editing much.
