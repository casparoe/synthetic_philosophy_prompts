I convene a small evening group — fourteen or so regulars, ages from their twenties to their seventies, most of them comfortable with fractions and percentages, and most of them visibly relieved when no algebra appears — that meets for ninety minutes once a month in a room at the public library to work through practical reasoning. We have index cards, dice, a bag of coloured beads, a bag of dried beans, a lamp with a dimmer switch, and a prize at the end worth no more than a bar of chocolate or a raffle ticket. I have been reading about probability and belief revision on my own for about a year, with no formal training, and I have become convinced mainly of one negative thing: the short rule that gets repeated whenever this subject comes up — that a reasonable person just conditions on what she learned — covers only the case where experience hands you a proposition outright.

Most of actual life is not like that. You catch half of a remark, you see something under a bad light, you get a reading from an instrument you only half trust. Nothing is settled. There is no new fact in your possession; you have simply come away leaning further in one direction than you were leaning before. I want my group to feel the difference between those two situations, and to feel it before anyone names it.

So I am asking for a runnable activity, and I have some opinions about its shape.

It should open in the comfortable case: something is simply revealed, everyone updates, and the point of that phase is how unremarkable it is — nobody needs a rule, the arithmetic is forced, and if two such revelations arrive in the other order nobody ends up anywhere different. Then it should move to the difficult case, where participants do not learn anything at all but are nudged: their confidence over a small set of exclusive possibilities is moved by an experience rather than by a fact. I would like them to invent the obvious repair themselves — spread the shift across the cases in proportion, leaving the relative odds inside each case exactly as they were — and I would like them to commit to specific numbers, physically, before anyone discusses method. Ten beans pushed around on an index card will do; I do not want numerals floating in the air.

Then I want two things to happen to them that they cannot talk their way out of.

First, the repair should fail somewhere. There should be a case where the nudge does not just move their confidence about which case they are in, but also tells them something about the inside of a case, so that holding those internal ratios fixed produces something they can see is wrong. I would rather they discover this than be told it, and I would rather the discovery be about a case they have already committed numbers to.

Second, the order should matter. If two nudges arrive and they apply the repair to each in turn, they should be able to end up in detectably different places depending on which came first — and nothing in the setup should tell them which order is the right one. I would like the scoring arranged so that someone who notices this can exploit it, or at least so that the discrepancy shows up in the record rather than in a disagreement they can shrug off.

Constraints, some of them negotiable if you say why: no lecture and no formula on any surface until after the last bean has been moved; no medical-test scenario, no politics, nothing requiring specialised background; a payoff scheme that makes honest reporting of a number the best policy and that I can run from a printed card without doing arithmetic in my head; one facilitator plus at most one helper; robust to a participant who is clever and contrary; and nothing that requires anyone to be singled out or embarrassed. Please give me the actual numbers to load into the bag and the actual sizes of the nudges, chosen so the effects are large enough to survive rounding and argument, and a minute-by-minute timeline with a note of where I can cut.

I also want a debrief that does more than summarise. It should map specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the underlying idea, and it should tell me what will predictably go wrong: the person who insists the nudge was really just a signal they could have conditioned on; the person who refuses to commit to a number because they do not want to be wrong; the group splitting into two camps and each camp assuming the other has misunderstood the rules; the participant who works out that the whole thing has been arranged and says so, correctly. And I want you to be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will simply have to say myself — I assume at least this: that real experience does not arrive already sorted into a labelled set of exclusive cases, and that the cases here are discrete and few while actual confidence moves continuously.

One request that matters to me more than the rest. If the order-dependence I am expecting is not real for the situation as I have described it — if a properly specified update of the kind I am groping toward would give the same answer either way, and the difference my group thinks it has found is an artefact of sloppy set-up — tell me so plainly, and tell me what feature I would have to add to the set-up to make the order genuinely matter, and what that would cost me in realism. I would much rather hand fourteen people a smaller truth than a manufactured one. By the same token, if the thing I have been calling a nudge has an established name and an established formula, tell me the name and write the formula down for my own reading — with the notation explained, since I will be reading it alone — and tell me whether soft evidence and conditioning on a weighted mixture are the same proposal under two descriptions or two different proposals. I have seen both characterisations online, I suspect at least one of them is a distortion, and I do not want to repeat to fourteen people a slogan I only half understand. None of that vocabulary should enter the room on the night.

What I want them to be able to say on the way out, in their own words, is why the familiar advice is not advice you can follow when nothing has been learned, and why the natural way of following it anyway is a decision you make rather than a conclusion that follows.
