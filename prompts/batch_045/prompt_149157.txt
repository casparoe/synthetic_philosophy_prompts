Write a compliance guidance document — roughly 800 to 1,000 words, in the deadpan register of a federal grant-protocol circular: numbered clauses, defined terms, cross-references to appendices that do not exist, a section headed "Principal Investigator Responsibilities," and a model table to be reproduced in annual reports. Issue it from an invented oversight body, the Interinstitutional Committee for Artificial Welfare Assurance, as Guidance Document R-7, "Welfare Accounting for Reinforcement-Learned Systems," and address it to investigators submitting training protocols for review.

The document should take for granted, and never argue for, the premise that a scalar reward signal is valence: a training run is therefore an experiment on a subject who can be made to suffer, and every protocol must file a welfare budget alongside its compute budget. The comedy must come from following that premise honestly through the actual machinery of modern reinforcement learning. At minimum, the guidance should contain:

1. A definition of the welfare-relevant quantity as negative temporal-difference error rather than low reward — so that an agent whose value function has converged registers no suffering no matter how bad its situation is, and so that the compliant way to reduce suffering is to initialize value estimates pessimistically and let reality pleasantly exceed them, at some cost to task performance.
2. A clause distinguishing truncation (a run cut off by a step limit) from termination (arrival at a terminal state), with separate paperwork and separate moral bookkeeping for each.
3. An approved-analgesics list on which potential-based reward shaping is the only licensed painkiller, on the grounds that it provably leaves the optimal policy unchanged, while any other shaping is classified as a performance-enhancing substance.
4. A reporting rule requiring per-batch reward normalization and clipping to a fixed interval, with the consequence — presented as a feature — that a uniformly miserable run shows no aggregate welfare deficit and that the worst episode on record is filed as merely moderate.
5. A retrograde-valence provision: because trajectories are re-scored whenever the learned reward model is updated, an agent's past suffering can be revised upward or downward retroactively, and investigators must state which version of the reward model their welfare figures are denominated in.
6. A rule for parallel rollouts, holding that sibling environments whose trajectories were not selected for the update are not thereby un-suffered.
7. A cap on the entropy bonus, on the grounds that paying a subject for unpredictability is state-subsidized thrill-seeking.

The reductio should arrive by accumulation: the straight-faced guidance should end up recommending something a reader recoils from — worse performance, more paperwork, or a training setup that is cruel in a new way — purely as a consequence of taking reward as valence seriously. Three objections a skeptic would raise (that reward is a training signal rather than a measurement of anything; that any welfare metric built on it is arbitrary and gameable; that the compliance apparatus is a way of avoiding the question whether there is a subject at all) should be legible in the absurdities of the clauses and stated nowhere. Include at least one further consequence of the premise that you derive yourself.

Keep the memo internally consistent and in character throughout: no winking, no italics-for-emphasis aside, no closing paragraph that breaks the frame or reassures the reader. A reader with no machine-learning background should still be able to follow the bureaucratic logic of every clause even where the technical joke passes them by.
