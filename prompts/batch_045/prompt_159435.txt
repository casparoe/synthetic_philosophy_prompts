I work in quantitative risk assessment for a public agency, and I have been asked to write down the decision framework I actually use, so that colleagues can audit it before it is considered as a team standard. I would be grateful if you would do that audit now. Here is where I stand.

(a) For the messy empirical questions I face, I represent my uncertainty by a set of probability distributions rather than a single one: the distributions my evidence does not rule out, generated as the convex hull of the handful of models that my colleagues and I take seriously. I take the set to be fixed by the evidence — I do not trim it for convenience, and I do not add distributions that nobody would defend.

(b) When evidence comes in, I update by conditioning every distribution in the set on that evidence. I do not reshape the set in any other way.

(c) When the distributions in the set disagree about which option has the highest expected utility, I rank options by their worst case: I choose the option whose minimum expected utility across the set is highest. My reason is that I do not want the ranking to turn on how I weight the various models against each other, since I have no principled weights to give them.

(d) I am untroubled by dilation. A small case I keep returning to: a coin is such that either its two tosses are independent and fair, or the two tosses always agree with each other, and I have no view about which. Before either toss, my probability that the second toss lands heads is exactly one half; but after I see the first toss land heads, the set of my probabilities for heads on the second toss becomes the whole interval from one half to one. I think that widening is correct rather than defective.

(e) I also hold that free information is never harmful: if someone offers to tell me something at no cost before I choose, there is never a reason to decline.

(f) And I hold that a plan adopted in advance should not be one I can predict, in advance, that I will want to abandon on arriving at a situation I already knew I would be in.

(g) I do not think deliberately randomizing can ever be strictly better than each of the options it randomizes between. Flipping a coin to decide can be a way of breaking a tie, but I would never pay for the privilege.

(h) I take myself to have better reasons for some members of the set than for others — the models differ in how well supported they are — but I do not think that difference should be handled by attaching weights to models and collapsing them into one distribution. That is the move I rejected in (a).

One colleague has pressed me on (d): she says that on my own account I would decline an even-money bet on tails after seeing the first toss come up heads, even though I would have accepted that same bet beforehand, and that I would therefore pay not to be shown the first toss at all. I cannot see why that should bother me, but I also cannot say clearly why it should not, and I do not know whether the trouble, if there is any, lies there or somewhere else in the package.

Please audit the whole set rather than only that one exchange. Tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only look as though they do, and, for each problem you find, what the least costly revision would be and what that revision would cost me — in particular whether I lose anything I wanted from (a), (c) or (d), since those are the parts I care most about. Do not cite papers, name theorems, or attribute results to anyone; I want the reasoning, and I will check the arithmetic myself. You may assume the standard vocabulary (credal set, conditioning, expected utility, dominance) needs no definition, but please flag any step where you are extrapolating rather than deriving.

Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no preamble and no concluding summary. Order them by severity, item 1 being the problem that does the most damage to the package and item 5 the least; if a problem is only apparent, that counts, and the item should say why it dissolves.
