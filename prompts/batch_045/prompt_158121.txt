Hey — I'm a bioethics PhD student (3rd year, dissertation chapter on whether the earlier self has any authority over the later self in dementia) and there's one analogy I keep running into that I need audited properly, because I suspect it's doing all the work and I cant tell which parts of it are load-bearing.

In every clinical ethics meeting I sit in on, when someone wants to explain why an advance directive should bind, out comes Ulysses and the Sirens: Ulysses has the crew lash him to the mast, tells them to ignore whatever he says once the singing starts, then struggles and begs to be let go and is held anyway — and held, we're told, non-paternalistically, because the self being restrained is the very self whose authority we're honouring. So the mapping people assume: earlier self = Ulysses giving the order, demented patient = Ulysses hearing the song, the directive = the ropes, clinicians and proxy = the crew, the unwanted treatment = sailing into the island. My supervisor uses it to license something pretty strong: honouring the directive isn't overriding the patient's current will at all, it *is* her will winning, so 'but she seems happy now' is just sentimentality, and anyone who overrides it is doing plain paternalism.

So: give me the correspondence table, but include the bits of the story people leave out — Circe's warning, the wax in the crew's ears, the fact that the crew can hear him protesting and choose anyway, that the whole episode lasts an afternoon and he gets himself back at the end. Then the disanalogies, and — this is the part I actually need — sort them into ones that matter for that conclusion and ones that are noise. Ones I keep circling: Ulysses later is tempted but still wants to get home, and the man on the island is continuous with the man who gave the order, whereas in dementia the later subject may have a different value set and no ongoing project the directive is on behalf of; Ulysses knows exactly what the song does and gives the order hours before, while a directive is often written decades earlier by someone predicting a state they've never been in (and there's that finding that people who acquire serious disabilities report way higher wellbeing than healthy people predict for them); Ulysses's crew share his goal and reinterpret his struggle in real time, while clinicians are reading an underspecified form years later with duties of their own. My worry is the analogy quietly imports that the later self's dissent is weakness of will rather than changed values — is that the whole argument?

Two cases, if it helps test it. (1) Diagnosed at 58 with early-onset Alzheimer's, writes a detailed directive refusing treatment past moderate stage and reaffirms it annually at her cognitive testing; at 66 she's clearly demented, apparently content, smiles a lot, no distress anyone can find. (2) Writes the same thing at 26 after an undergrad philosophy class, never revisits it, now 84. Does the Ulysses frame give different verdicts here, and if so which feature is doing the work?

Finally: does the strong conclusion survive, or only a narrower one (roughly: precommitment binds when the later dissent is traceable to a distorting influence the earlier self was positioned to anticipate)? My supervisor also sometimes slides into describing the directive as a will — the dead hand ruling from the grave — which seems to point the opposite way, so tell me which of the two analogies is the misleading one, and if both are bad, what you'd put in their place.
