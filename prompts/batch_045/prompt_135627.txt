I am a journalist who covers science and ideas. Last year I wrote a piece on how expert panels and committees end up taking decisions that no member of the panel actually endorses, and as a result I have been asked to give the forty-minute framing talk at the first working session of a seven-member Algorithmic Triage Oversight Board at a large hospital system, and then to take questions. I would like you to prepare me for the question period.

What I am going to propose. The board must decide whether to deploy a machine-learning triage tool in the emergency department. Its draft charter says that deployment is to be supported by findings on three considerations — call them A, the tool's triage accuracy is non-inferior to the residents' in this hospital's own patient population; B, its error rates do not differ materially across the hospital's three largest patient groups; C, the notice and consent arrangements meet the hospital's existing standards — and that all three must hold. My recommendation is that the board stop voting up-or-down on the question "shall we deploy," and instead vote separately on A, B and C; state in advance which combinations of findings are sufficient for which action; record in the minutes which combination carried; and treat the deployment decision as following from that record rather than as a separate vote. Call it the reasons-first proposal.

Two features of the draft charter create problems that I have not seen handled in the usual presentations of the discursive dilemma, and I expect to be pressed hardest on these.

Thresholds. Findings are adopted by simple majority. Full deployment requires five of seven; a two-year monitored pilot with an independent evaluator requires only four of seven. The charter does not say whether the five-of-seven requirement attaches to each finding or to the package. In the first case I plan to put on the table, three members accept all of A, B and C; two accept A and B but not C; two accept C only. So A, B and C each pass five to two — each clears even the supermajority bar — while only three of seven members think the tool should go in at all. I want to argue that the supermajority requirement should be read as attaching to the findings, on the ground that requiring five members to endorse the entire conjunction would in practice impose near-unanimity on any multi-part test. I am not confident this is right, and I expect it to be the first thing that gets contested.

The written-rationale requirement. The hospital's counsel added a clause requiring every affirmative decision to be accompanied by a written statement of the considerations the board affirmed, so that the decision can be defended if it is ever challenged. The board may also authorise deployment by a second route: D, comparable tools are already in routine use at peer institutions with no documented pattern of harm; and E, the deployment is structured as a monitored pilot with a funded sunset. In the second case I plan to present, two members accept A, B and C, two accept D and E, and three accept none of them. The pilot passes four to three, but every single one of A, B, C, D and E is rejected five to two. Deployment carried, and there is no consideration the board can honestly record itself as having affirmed. I want to recommend a rationale gate: if no sufficient route has majority support on each of its elements, the proposal fails whatever the headcount on the up-or-down question. I need to know whether that gate is principled or whether it is an ad hoc way of making the problem go away by making deployment harder, since it bites hardest in exactly the case where the board most wants to act.

The table. A chair who is a senior emergency physician, thinks in terms of clinical judgment, is impatient with procedure, and would rather talk it through and then take one up-or-down vote. A second clinician, strongly evidence-oriented, who has read the accuracy literature closely and will treat A and B as matters she has views about. A bioethicist sympathetic to the spirit of reasons-first, who will nevertheless ask whether a committee's recorded reasons are anyone's reasons and whether the board is a group agent or merely a tally. A health-services researcher and statistician who will say that A and B are factual questions with right answers, so that votes are noisy signals and the sensible response is to pool the evidence and weight by competence rather than to count heads across connected propositions. The hospital's counsel, who cares about the record and about judicial review, and who will notice that voting on findings gives members more places to vote strategically in order to steer the outcome. A patient representative who wants to know who is accountable, and whether reasons-first lets members hide behind findings they privately rejected. And a health economist who thinks the whole apparatus overheats a decision that is cheap to reverse and will be revisited every two years, and who believes small bodies that deliberate before voting do not land in these configurations anyway.

What I want from you: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this table, in roughly the order they will arrive, each phrased the way that person would phrase it rather than the way a philosopher would, each with a two-minute reply I can give out loud and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes, because I would rather concede something real than be seen defending a formalism; tell me which single reply has to land if the proposal is to survive the session; and tell me which question I should be hoping nobody asks. Where an objection turns on a distinction — between a truth-tracking and a legitimacy rationale for a procedure, between a board that is discovering a fact and a board that is taking responsibility for a decision, between deliberation before a vote and the aggregation of the votes themselves — mark the distinction explicitly instead of smoothing it over. I do not need citations, a reading list, or a survey of the literature; I can source that myself. I need the arguments, and I need them aimed at this room.
