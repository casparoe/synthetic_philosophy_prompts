I'm writing a novel about a restorer who spends a single night making infinitely many tiny changes to one painting, and I need the ending to be philosophically sound, not just pretty.

So build me the scene. Thomson's lamp gets toggled at 1/2, 3/4, 7/8 of a minute and there's famously no fact about whether it's on at the two-minute mark: no last toggle, and the states just go on, off, on, off without ever settling. I want a case of that same shape — infinitely many discrete operations, all inside a stretch of time a person could sit through, no last operation, no relativistic cheats or infinite-speed gadgets, nothing a bright teenager couldn't follow — where the state at the end *is* determinate, and where my character can know what it will be before dawn.

Constraints, cos I can see the obvious dodges: the steps have to keep reversing each other (so no easy fix where every move pushes the same way), the finished state has to be one that no finite number of steps would have reached, and the determinacy has to fall out of the machinery of the case — a quantity that's conserved, steps that shrink, whatever — and not from me as narrator just decreeing how the painting turns out. There's a wager riding on it, so the character needs grounds, not hope.

Give me the scene in a paragraph; then show me it meets each constraint; then name the one feature doing the difference from the lamp, and give me a seprate twin version differing in exactly that respect that flips back to there being no fact of the matter. Last: what would somebody who holds that supertasks like this just can't be completed, or just have no determinate outcome, say to wave my case away — and is that reply any good? British English please, and feel free to be definately blunt about where I'm kidding myself.
