I'm in the upper sixth at a comprehensive sixth form, and along with two other students I help run our school's Philosophy and Society Society; each spring we run an eight-week, student-taught discussion course for Year 12 pupils and finish it with a fifty-minute closed-book essay worth twenty-five marks, and this year I've been asked to write one of the three questions. I'd like mine to be on the gender-equality paradox — the much-reported finding that in countries which score well on indices of gender equality, women make up a rather smaller share of science and engineering graduates than they do in less egalitarian countries. I got into an argument about it at lunch: one friend, who hopes to read engineering, said it obviously shows the differences are innate and that we should stop trying to engineer them away; another, from the debating society, said it shows nothing of the kind and that the whole line of research is a distraction; I found I couldn't say precisely where either of them went wrong, and that is part of why I want to write the question. What I don't want is something pupils can answer by reciting a summary of the debate, or by pointing out that correlation is not causation and then stopping.

So that nobody can have read the answer in advance, I've invented the follow-up study that will be printed in a data box above the question; pupils may use only what is in the box and cannot look anything up. The invented study, which I'll call the Nordhavn follow-up, reports three things: first, that the original pattern replicates across several dozen countries and holds within the same country across cohorts, so it is not an artefact of comparing very different places at a single moment; second, that the pattern appears only in countries with unusually generous social insurance — long paid parental leave, strong income protection for people who cannot find work, and student debt that is written off rather than pursued — while in countries scoring equally high on the equality index but with thin safety nets, women's share of science and engineering degrees is the same as or higher than in the least egalitarian countries; and third, that when parents rather than pupils are asked to choose a degree for a fictional seventeen-year-old described as equally good at mathematics and at essay subjects, the parents' choices are more gender-stereotyped in the high-equality countries than in the low-equality ones. The question I want pupils to confront is roughly whether these findings are evidence that the career differences are natural rather than social, and whether they have any bearing at all on whether anything ought to be done about them.

Please give me three things. First, the question exactly as it should appear on the paper, worded for able seventeen-year-olds with no statistics and no philosophy beyond a couple of sessions on fact and value. Second, a model answer of around eight hundred words, written to the standard of a genuinely strong pupil in that setting rather than to the standard of a journal article, which at minimum separates the claim that the pattern is real from the claim that it is innate; says what the safety-net finding and the parental-vignette finding each do to the naive reading, and how those two cuts differ from one another; and reaches the crux, that the account on which fair conditions simply allow innate interests to express themselves and the account on which economic security cushions risk so that people default to what they find intrinsically interesting or socially legible predict the same data, so this study cannot tell them apart even in principle. The answer should then say what, if anything, follows for justice — including the awkward case I'd like it to face rather than dodge, that on these findings a policy which makes people more economically secure may also make their choices more gender-typical, so that we have to decide whether that is a cost worth paying. Third, a marking scheme out of twenty-five built around five must-hit moves, with the marks for each, a note on what earns the top band (say twenty and above) as against a middling fifteen to nineteen, and one line per move on how pupils typically get it wrong — for instance by treating innate as automatically meaning unobjectionable, or by assuming that a more egalitarian country is simply a less sexist one in every respect that matters here.
