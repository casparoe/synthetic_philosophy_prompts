ok so here's a position I keep coming back to and can't quite knock loose. I think there's a real, non-consequentialist moral difference between lying and merely misleading, and that lying is the worse of the two by default. Not because lies do more damage, or are more likely to work, or are harder to catch — I want to hold all of that fixed and still say there's a difference. The feature I think is doing the work is something like: when you lie, you put your epistemic authority behind a piece of content — *you* are the one saying it, on the record — whereas when you mislead you get the hearer to walk themselves to the false belief, and you retain the ability to say "I never said that." The deniability gap is the whole ballgame for me. The liar is on the hook for the content; the misleader isn't.

Tell me what would count against this. I don't mean the easy stuff — yes, I know misleading is often *nastier* in practice, more manipulative, more cowardly, and that real liars sometimes get more respect than real misleaders. I'm asking the narrower question: what would show that the gap I'm pointing at isn't doing the moral work I think it is, or isn't really there?

Four cases I've chewed on, with my own answers; tell me where I'm cheating.

1. The code case. Speaker and hearer share a convention tight enough that a literally true utterance transmits a false proposition as reliably as any assertion — a corrupt broker's "the position is comfortable," said to a client who's been trained to read it as *get out today*. Here the misleader is fully in control of what the hearer ends up believing. My answer so far: reliability was never the point, what matters is whether she *said* it. But that worries me, because now the whole morality of the thing seems to hang on a technical notion of what was asserted, and I can't say why a technical notion should carry that weight.

2. The self-blinder. Someone who deliberately arranges not to know what the hearer will infer, precisely so she can mislead while keeping clean hands — refuses to look at the numbers, sets up filters, tells herself she never checked. Intuitively this is *worse*, closer to lying, maybe worse than lying. But on my account she's a paradigm misleader, maybe the purest one there is. Does that break the view or just make it uncomfortable?

3. The tuned misleader. She's studied you, knows your inference habits, and picks the one true sentence that she is as certain as she is of anything will land you on the false belief — arguably more intimate control over your mind than a liar has, since a liar at least leaves you the option of disbelieving her.

4. The bald-faced case. Someone says something obviously false with no hope at all of being believed and no intention of deceiving. No deception, no deniability gained. If my account still classifies this as *the* bad one, that's evidence something has come loose: the wrong-making feature looks like it's floated free of anything happening between the people.

The thing that would move me most: a case where a misleader is more on the hook for the content than a liar is. Because if being "on the hook" can come apart from having asserted it, my story is in trouble in a way no amount of refinement fixes.

Also say honestly whether the view has a gradient hiding inside it — how much authority is behind the content, how much the hearer is enlisted as an instrument — and if it does, whether that counts as falsification or as clarification. Rank the defeaters fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which ones a defender of my position would herself concede as counting against it versus which she'd dismiss as changing the subject. If you conclude the position is basically insulated against all comers, say so and tell me whether that's a vice for a claim of this kind.
