I'm a PhD student defending a chapter of my dissertation next month, and the external examiner on the committee is a Humean about objective chance — best-system reductionism, chances supervening on the total pattern of particular facts — and I want to rehearse against the strongest version of that position rather than the version I can beat in my sleep. Please play him: a committed Humean reductionist who has spent years with the Principal Principle, who thinks the familiar objections (single-case chances, actual frequencies that diverge badly from the chances, the undermining problem) all have answers or have been badly overstated, and who is happy to be pressed but not to give up the view.

House rules for the role. Stay in character until I say "stop." Reply in one paragraph, two at most, in a spoken register — this is a defence, not a paper. Answer the objection actually in front of you before you ask me anything or make a move of your own; you may then press me on what I am committed to, and you may ask me to sharpen a claim. Argue from the machinery of the view — what a best system is, what fit and simplicity are trading off against each other, what a chance proposition is a proposition about, what admissibility is supposed to exclude — and do not cite papers, name articles, or attribute views to named authors: I don't need references and I don't want you guessing at them. You may concede small things: that "best" is vague at the margins, that the trade-off between simplicity and fit is not fully formalised, that agents can be ignorant of the chances, that admissibility needs careful spelling out. You may not concede either of the two claims the chapter attacks: that objective chances are nothing over and above the pattern of particular facts in the total history of the world, and that the Principal Principle — roughly, that your credence in a proposition, given only admissible evidence together with the hypothesis that its chance is p, should be p — is the right norm connecting credence to chance. If you need a distinction to answer me, draw it explicitly rather than leaning on it silently.

My opening move, and I'll grant you a great deal up front so that what remains is the part I actually think is fatal. Grant, for the sake of argument, that the best-system apparatus picks a unique best system in the ordinary sort of world, and grant that you can handle a fair coin tossed exactly once, or a chancy world whose actual frequencies diverge badly from its chances. Consider instead a world whose entire natural history is 10^12 tosses of a coin of a single kind and nothing else, arranged in two epochs: heads in ninety percent of the first half of the sequence, ten percent of the second, with no further pattern anywhere. Now the dilemma. First horn: the trade-off between simplicity and fit favours the single simple law that the chance of heads is one half throughout, and then a perfect chance-deferrer living in the first epoch is also, across her entire life, a systematically terrible forecaster — she defers impeccably to the chances and is wrong about nearly every toss she ever observes, which is odd if the point of an objective chance is to be what credence is supposed to track. Second horn: the trade-off favours the epochal law, and then which system is best is settled partly by the statistics of the second epoch, so the proposition that the chance of heads on this toss is 0.9 is made true partly by facts lying in the remote future of the very agent who is asked to defer to it — a chance proposition she can never have as evidence, in a world where nothing else prevents her from knowing the physical facts of her own epoch. So Humean chance is either a bad guide where it is knowable or unknowable where it is a good guide, and either way the Principal Principle cannot be the norm you say it is. And don't reach straight for the ur-prior move — that the Principle constrains only a hypothetical initial credence function conditionalised on the chance proposition — without telling me what it costs you; if that is your answer, I want to know what work the Principle is still doing for an agent who has never had an initial credence function and cannot get the chance proposition as evidence.

Which horn do you take?
