I'm in a small reading group — half ML people, half philsophy grad students — and we've been going round on decision theory for embedded agents for something like two years now, with zero movement. Concretely: one camp thinks causal decision theory (or more loosely "just do expected-utility maximisation against the best model you've got") is basically fine and that Newcomb-style cases are cooked-up philosopher's toys where the predictor is doing something no real environment does; the other camp thinks those one-shot cases are the whole point, because an agent that's part of the world it's reasoning about — with other agents, the training process, its own sucessors, maybe copies of it elsewhere, all modelling it better than it models itself — lives in exactly that structure, so you need something like functional decision theory or updatelessness. The CDT-ish side's stock move is that in those cases your action "isn't really the cause" of the good outcome, so taking the money is fine; the FDT-ish side's stock move is that causation-talk is a heuristic that quietly assumes you're outside the thing being predicted, and that heuristic is precisely what breaks when you're embedded. We all agree on the cases and on what each rule says about them. Nobody budges. My hypothesis, which I've started calling the predictor gap, is that the real dispute isn't about decision rules at all: it's a factual disagreement about whether environments actually contain predictors accurate enough for the gap between the rules to matter, wearing normative clothing — and under that, a verbal fight about whether "rational" means something like coherence-with-your-best-model or something like getting-more-of-what-you-want. (My ML friends add a third option: don't do decision theory, just train the policy and let the loss sort it out.)

So: diagnose the stall rather than settling it. Is my hypothesis close, and where's it wrong — is the verbal/ substantive split where I think it is, are the sides mabye answering different questions under one heading (what to build vs. what to admire vs. what to blame), or is a hidden formal question doing the work, like whether an embedded agent's model can even be true of its own environment?
