I'm giving a 25-minut talk next week at a decision-making meetup I help run, and I need Q&A prep. My thesis is that satisficing isn't a dumbed-down version of maximizing that you fall back on when you're tired or the calcuation is too expensive — it's what rationality looks like once you notice that the set of options you're choosing between was never handed to you. It's produced by the search: searching creates options you didn't have (you find the apartment) and destroys ones you did (the offer expires, another buyer moves), and it also changes you, because looking at options is mostly how you find out what you wanted. So there's no fixed menu sitting there with a best item on it waiting to be discovered, and the instruction "pick the best one" never gets an argument. Second thing I'm arguing: the aspiration level is secretly doing two jobs at once — it's the acceptability threshold and it's the stopping rule — and collapsing them is a feature, because splitting them (accept whatever clears a low bar, then keep looking forever) gives you something worse than either satisficing or maximizing.

The room is roughly: two ML/optimization engineers who think anything short of argmax is sloppiness with a fancy name; three behavioral-econ grad students who actually know Simon and Gigerenzer and will say satisficing is either trivial or just expected utility again in disguise; a few startup founders who use "good enough" as a lifestyle slogan and will agree with me for bad reasons; and one decision-analysis guy from an insurance company who'll want to know what sets my threshold and why that isn't just the same optimization problem pushed up a level.

Give me the objections in the order they'll actually come, phrased the way each of them would put it, ranked by how much damage they'd do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede before anyone asks, which single reply has to land for the talk to survive, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
