I'm a science journalist — I cover neuroscience, psychology and machine learning for a magazine and a small newsletter — and I got talked into this at a dinner after a panel on neural correlates of consciousness, where I said, perhaps too confidently, that most coverage of that literature misses a single unresolved dispute that decides how the results should be read. The consequence is that in June I'm running one ninety-minute hands-on session at a week-long writing retreat for about fourteen early-career science communicators: mostly freelance writers and university press officers, two or three with a cognitive-science degree and the rest with none. The room has a projector, index cards, pens, a phone for timers and unreliable wifi; there's no budget, no real money for stakes, and every participant is expected to publish a short piece afterwards based on the session, so whatever happens has to be something they can write honestly about rather than a stunt.

The dispute I want them to run into is the one about whether a mental state's being conscious consists in the subject's being, in some way, aware of being in that state — the family of higher-order views, in the version where the second-order state is something like a thought or a tacit judgment that one is in it. I want them to feel both halves of it: the pull of the idea (that a sensation you are in no way aware of seems not to be like anything for you) and the force of the complaint that it gets things wrong, that people plainly have conscious experiences while absorbed and unaware that they are in them, and that the view seems to over-intellectualize consciousness by making it require a further act of self-monitoring that infants, animals and distracted adults might never perform. I've roughed out three mechanics and I want you to turn them into something runnable, or to replace them if you can do better. First: a beeped experience-sampling procedure, in which participants do a demanding continuous task and, at unpredictable intervals, write down what was present in experience at the instant of the beep — I want them to notice the gap between the state they were in and the report they produce a moment later, and in particular to confront the strange implication that the beep, by making them aware of the state, seems in some sense to make it conscious, which is the moment the view starts to feel both powerful and slightly absurd. Second: paired observers who log fidgets, scratching and signs of discomfort that the subject later denies having noticed, plus at least one case where the subject demonstrates having discriminated something they insist they never experienced. Third, and most important for this audience: I hand them a made-up but realistic abstract of a brain-imaging study in which a region associated with monitoring and metacognitive judgment is active exactly when subjects report awareness of a stimulus, they draft headlines for it in small groups, and then they discover for themselves that the design cannot distinguish the higher-order state's being constitutive of conscious experience from its merely being what makes a report possible — which is the confound that makes nearly every empirical result in this area ambiguous, and the reason I said what I said at the dinner.

What I need from you is the full session design: the rules and materials for each phase, a minute-by-minute timeline for ninety minutes including setup and any handouts, concrete tasks hard enough to produce genuine absorption and mild failure, and the wording you would actually use for instructions, given that participants must not hear the theory's name, the phrase higher-order, or any lecture before the debrief. Then the debrief: map each moment participants will have lived through onto the specific claim it is evidence for or against; say which of the standard objections the activity can make vivid and which it cannot touch at all — I assume the regress worry, the question of what makes the higher-order state itself conscious, and the retreat to a thin or merely dispositional second-order state are things I will simply have to assert — and be candid about where the analogy between the activity and the real cases breaks down. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong: participants who treat I didn't notice it as it wasn't conscious and conclude the view is trivially true; participants who conclude it is trivially false because they cannot accept that their absorbed aches and itches were not conscious; the two people with cognitive-science training who name the view in the first ten minutes and deflate everything; and the real risk that fourteen journalists leave able to write only the piece saying that consciousness is just self-monitoring, or that neuroscience has settled a question it has not. For each failure, how I steer it without turning the last twenty minutes into a lecture.
