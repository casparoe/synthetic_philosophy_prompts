I'm writing a short "where I stand on the replication mess" doc for my lab — partly so incoming grad students know what they're signing up for, partly because I keep saying slightly different things in different meetings and I want to find out whether that's because I'm inconsistent or because the disagreements are only apparent. Here's the set, roughly as I'd write it down:

(a) The replication problem is real and the causes are mostly structural: publication bias, chronically low power, and researcher degrees of freedom. Outright fraud is a rounding error, and I don't think "most published findings are false" is the right headline either.
(b) A failed replication is genuinely good evidence about the literature — specifically, it licenses the inference that the original published effect size was inflated, because of the usual winner's-curse / significance-filter story.
(c) That said, I think almost every effect my lab works on is real in some form, and when a specific replication fails I think the default explanation is a hidden moderator — different population, different wording, different decade — rather than the effect being zero. To be concrete and slightly embarrassing: we ran a preregistered, well-powered multi-site replication of one of our own flagship effects last year, got a point estimate essentially at zero with a confidence interval that excluded the original estimate, and I still describe that as "we hit a moderator," not "we failed to replicate." I don't think I'm being irrational there but I can't fully say why.
(d) The fixes I actually believe in are preregistration, much larger samples, and meta-analysis; I think the point estimate from a good meta-analysis is the number we should be quoting. But I also think exploratory work is where the interesting hypotheses come from and I don't want it strangled by having to declare everything in advance.
(e) I think p-values are basically a fine tool as long as you don't treat 0.05 as a magic line, and I'm not persuaded we all need to become Bayesians. Default-prior Bayes factors look to me like significance testing with extra machinery bolted on, and I don't think a field-wide switch to Bayes would have prevented any of this.
(f) The most underrated problem, worse than power in my view, is measurement: our operationalizations are noisy proxies for the constructs in the theory, reliability is often mediocre, and I suspect that's doing more damage than anything on the analysis side.
(g) A well-powered null is informative — it's evidence of absence, or close enough — and it should be publishable and should count for something in a lit review.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Go through this and tell me which pairs actually fight each other and which only look like they do — I'm fairly sure at least a couple of the apparent fights are fake (the "Bayes would have saved us" thing and the preregistration-vs-exploration thing feel seperate to me, but maybe I'm rationalizing). For the real ones: rank them by how bad they are rather than listing them as equally serious, and for each say what specifically would have to give and what that would cost me downstream. Then tell me which single revision cleans up the most. Please also flag any place where I'm using one commitment to insulate another from evidence — I'd rather hear that than a tidy summary. Use American English, keep it concrete and tied to the actual clauses above, and please don't resolve everything by telling me to adopt a framework I've already said I'm skeptical of without saying why that's the only way out.
