I write a very small newsletter about stratigraphy (don't laugh) and I've been sitting in on the open sessions of the working group that has to decide whether there's an Anthropocene at all and, if so, where its base goes. I need to file a piece predicting what they'll do with the submission that came in last month, so I'm less interested in the right answer than in what these particular people will do given what they've already done. Take my summary of their track record as given — I'm paraphrasing from my own notes, I'm not asking you to check anything, and assume no internet.

Their rulings so far:

- voted down the 1610 CO2-minimum horizon with the American ragweed pollen turning up in European lake cores; their stated reason was that the signal arrives at different dates in different cores, so it isn't a horizon
- voted down first-coal-soot in English peat, 1700s: the same signal shows up roughly a thousand years earlier in Asian cores
- voted down a nitrogen-isotope spike: comparable spikes occur naturally further down the column, so it isn't unique
- voted down the "Great Acceleration" curves — population, GDP, fertiliser: not in the rock
- in an earlier boundary case, voted down a first-appearance of a fossil taxon, on the grounds that the oldest known occurrence keeps getting older the more sections people sample
- voted FOR the 1952-ish radionuclide horizon, but only as a point in one designated annually laminated lake section, with the dating coming from the laminations and the marker being the chemistry in the sediment rather than the layering itself
- declined, in another case, to put a marker in an ice core: ice is a correlation tool, not a place a boundary can live
- refused to let each region set its own base
- ruled that a climate-science body's earlier vote for "1950" doesn't bind them
- accepted technical submissions from an atmospheric chemist and a soil scientist, but rejected a petition signed by a few thousand ecologists because it wasn't a stratigraphic argument

The new submission: a group wants the base at a lead-and-mercury signal from 16th-century colonial silver refining, dated to a single decade in the 1540s from annually banded corals and confirmed in a Greenland ice core, with isotope ratios that fingerprint the ore source. It's co-signed by a commission of historians and Indigenous scholars who explicitly ask the group either to accept it or to say out loud that questions about who caused what are outside its remit.

What will they do? Which rule are they actually running, what other rules fit their verdicts just as well, and which of those rules come apart on this one? Give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and tell me what a future submission would have to look like to force them to show which rule they're using. Also: does their track record give them any way to duck this by saying it isn't theirs to decide, and if they did duck it that way would they be contradicting themselves?
