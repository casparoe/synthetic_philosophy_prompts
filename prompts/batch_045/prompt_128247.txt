I'm retired now — thirty-odd years in protein crystallography, most of it spent watching crystals refuse to diffract — and a couple of years ago I joined a philosophy reading group at the public library. My question comes out of something I kept seeing at work rather than out of the books.

Twice in my career I watched two labs converge on the same result within weeks of each other. Same molecule, same conformation, same ugly detail everyone had been arguing about. One group got the paper out first and got their name attached to it; the other group had it too, had done work that looked just as good to me, and essentially got nothing — a footnote, a citation, a shrug. And the odd thing is that everyone involved, me included, said at the time that the result was ready to fall, that if not these people then somebody else within a year or so, the field was right up against it. And then we all went right back to behaving as if the difference of a few weeks was enormously important.

That combination isn't just lab gossip, it's built into institutions, and that's what I want to ask about. Patents: twenty years from filing, to whoever files first (as far as I can gather that's now the rule almost everywhere rather than first to invent), on condition that you disclose enough that a competent worker in the field could reproduce it, and on condition that it wasn't obvious given what was already known. Then there's naming things after people, and the big prizes, which as I recall will put at most three names on a discovery and won't consider anyone who has died. And underneath all of it, the apparatus for settling who got there first — filing dates, sealed submissions, dated notebooks — together with the habit of treating the independent second discoverer as having confirmed rather than discovered.

So: what does that bundle of practices presuppose? What view of discovery does it only make sense under? Does it strictly presuppose that the individual made a real difference to when or whether the thing got found, or is it compatible with the view that the discovery was going to happen anyway and the rules are just doing something else — supplying incentives, or drawing a bright line so we can stop arguing, or rewarding speed because speed is socially useful, or giving the rest of us a cheap way to keep score? I'd like you to seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and to tell me where it's underdetermined: which rival views of what credit is for would alllicense the same set of rules, so that the practice can't tell us which one is right. And there's an internal tension I can't quite state properly: the patent conditions seem to require both that the step was too big for the average competent worker to have taken and that the same average competent worker can follow your recipe once you hand it over, which suggests the law has a picture of the field as a moving front with the inventor standing just barely past it. Does that picture commit it to anything about inevitability?

Here's the part I made up, so you can push back on it as much as you like. At the reading group I floated a rule we ended up calling delay credit: don't reward being first, reward the counterfactual gap. Some panel estimates, after the fact, how many years the discovery would have been delayed if that person or that lab had never existed, and the reward is scaled to that number. If the estimate is near zero because a rival was three months behind, you get a short patent term and an honorific mention, not twenty years and your name on the thing. If you were thirty years ahead of anyone else, you get a long one. Independent co-discoverers split according to their measured delays. I like it because it seems to track what I actually care about, and I distrust it for three reasons I can name: it pays people for working on problems nobody else is near, which is often where the science is least ripe; it rewards anyone who quietly slows everybody else down, since slowing the field raises your own measured delay; and applied to something like Newton and Leibniz, or Darwin and Wallace, it seems to hand out almost no credit to anybody, which is definately not how we in fact feel about who did what.

So the second half of what I'm asking: what would the delay rule presuppose, if it were adopted in place of the priority rule — what view of credit, or of desert, or of what a discovery is? Is it coherent, or does it just relocate the problem, say from the race between two people to the clock of a field that nobody can observe directly? And if you think both rules are defensible for different purposes, tell me what that disagreement bottoms out in, because I suspect it's the bit I'm really asking about.
