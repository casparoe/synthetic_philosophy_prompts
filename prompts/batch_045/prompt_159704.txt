Say Mill, having just finished On Liberty in 1859, got shown Sen's 1970 impossibility result. You know the one: there's a single copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover, a prude who'd rather read it himself than let the lewd guy read it, and a lewd guy who'd actually get more pleasure out of the prude having to read it than out of reading it himself; grant each of them decisiveness over the one pair that just concerns whether *he* reads it, add Pareto, and you get a cycle. I don't want "Mill was against censorship, so he'd reject the setup" — everyone says something like that. I want to know exactly which condition he gives up, and why. Here's what I keep getting stuck on: Sen's condition is stated over preferences over whole social outcomes, while Mill's harm principle sorts *conduct* into self-regarding and other-regarding, and Mill is perfectly willing to say that a taste you have is not one you get to act on. So: is the prude's nosiness enough for Mill to say the preference isn't inside his protected sphere at all — and if he says that, hasn't he hollowed out the liberal condition until it barely says anything? Or does he bite the bullet somewhere else, dropping Pareto, or admitting that "your own sphere" can't be picked out without asking *why* you want what you want, which social choice theory can't do with preferences-as-black-boxes? One more thing: does whatever answer you give travel to a case I'm actually designing — a platform that promises each user control over their own feed, plus a guarantee that it never settles on an outcome everyone likes less than some alternative? Would Mill's way out survive there, or does that setting make nosiness unaviodable?
