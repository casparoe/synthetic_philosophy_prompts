Our ethics review board at a large technology company has started using Arendt's banality thesis as a working framework for difficult cases, and one case has split us. A mid-level engineer implemented a ranking change she privately thought would amplify inflammatory content in a country where such content had already been linked to mob violence; she raised the concern once in a meeting, was overridden, and shipped the change. I have been arguing that Arendt's point is that perpetrators like Eichmann are ordinary and interchangeable: people of no particular hatred or conviction, occupying a slot that thousands of others would have filled just as readily. On that reading, evil of this kind is banal in the sense of being statistically normal for its setting, so the individual contribution is nearly replaceable and blame properly attaches to the system and to whoever designed it. Carried to its conclusion, the same reasoning would make Eichmann among the least culpable of the perpetrators, since the machinery would have run perfectly well without him, and I am not sure I see how to avoid that implication. A colleague says I have the thesis backwards, that on Arendt's own terms it licenses a harsher judgment of our engineer rather than a gentler one, and that it does so precisely because of her ordinariness rather than despite it. Which of us is reading Arendt correctly, and what does her account actually let a review board like ours conclude about someone in this engineer's position?
