I write about science and ideas, and every time I explain the Church–Turing thesis to readers I get the same two furious emails. One says it isn't even a factual claim: "computable" just means computable by a Turing machine, the thesis settled the definition decades ago, done. The other says no, it's an empirical claim about what physical systems can do, it could be false, and people have sketched exotic setups that would refute it. A third correspondent, newer, says the real live question is what we should count as computation in systems like brains or neural nets at all. These people have been at it forever and nobody seems to budge anybody.

My own guess, tell me if it's wrong, is that they're answering different questions under one name: one about pinning down an informal mathematical concept, one about the laws of physics. But I want the fuller diagnosis. Is the rest of the standoff verbal, or is there a substantive disagreement about standards of evidence — about what could possibly count as evidence for a claim nobody thinks is provable — plus bedrock intuitions neither side argues for?

And concretely, because I get pitched things like this more than you'd think: suppose a group sends me a preprint claiming a buildable device that settles, after finite time, whether any Turing machine with fewer than, say, a million states halts — the physical evolution runs what looks like an unbounded amount of computation and then relaxes into a stable state you can just read off. Assume it works. Does that refute anything? My worry is that the definition side will say a bounded gadget tells us nothing about the concept of computability, while the physics side will say it kills the physical thesis as stated, and both will feel vindicated — which would itself be evidence about what kind of fight this is. So: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a sharper distinction between the theses, a formal result, a different question — or should I just tell readers to expect no resolution, and why?
