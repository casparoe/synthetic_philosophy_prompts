Hey — I'm in a small reading group on moral psychology (six of us, mostly grad students, plus one retired guy who I'm pretty sure shows up mostly for the wine), and this week we did Williams on internal and external reasons. One member is writing on Williams and gets visibly annoyed whenever anyone calls him a Humean, which is part of why I'm suspicious of the summary I found in a free online encyclopedia of philosophy I won't name. I've copied the relevant paragraph out below:

"According to Bernard Williams, all reasons for action are internal: an agent has a reason to do something only if she already has some desire that doing it would serve, or could acquire such a desire by correct reasoning from the desires she already has. This is the internal reasons theory. It follows that there are no external reasons — no facts that give an agent a reason to act independently of what she happens to care about — and hence that moral requirements give no reason to anyone who lacks an independent desire to be moral. Williams's argument is essentially Humean: since motivation always requires a pre-existing desire, a consideration that left the agent entirely cold could never explain why anyone acted, and so could not be a reason at all. Critics object that this makes reasons embarrassingly subjective: on this view a callous person has no reason not to be cruel. Defenders reply that the reasoning clause can do a lot of work, since the agent might be brought to see the connection by rational reflection. But this only relocates the difficulty, because whether reflection helps depends on psychological facts about the particular agent. So there is no fact of the matter about what anyone has reason to do, and moral disagreement turns out to be merely a clash of attitudes."

What I'd like is an accuracy audit rather than an essay: go through it more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on this literature would accept, what's a simplification they'd wince at but would let pass in a popular entry, and what's straightforwardly wrong. I especially want to know about two things. First, whether the slide from "there are no external reasons" to "there is no fact of the matter about what you have reason to do, so moral disagreement is just a clash of attitudes" is something the internalist is really committed to, or whether it's importing a seperate thesis. Second, whether the "pre-existing desire" framing is fair to Williams or whether my friend is right to bristle.

The thing I actually care about, though, is a case the entry waves away in half a clause. Suppose someone's motivational set contains, along with a genuine commitment to some project, a standing higher-order policy: she will not treat any consideration as a reason if she only came to accept it because somebody argued her into it (she thinks being talked into things is a kind of weakness). The entry's line, as I read it, is that this is a non-problem — the policy is just one more element of the motivational set, so there is either a sound deliberative route from the set taken as a whole or there isn't, and the theory is untouched. That feels too quick to me: it means whether she has a reason depends not just on what's in her motivational set and on the non-motivational facts, but on the path by which the deliberation reached her — same agent, same set, same conclusions of sound deliberation, but the reason exists if she somehow arrived at it on her own and doesn't if it was argued. Is that a genuine problem for the view, or something the internalist can absorb, and if it is a problem does it hand anything to the externalist or is it a complication both sides share?
