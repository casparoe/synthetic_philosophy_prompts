I work on the internal resource-allocation team of a large public hospital network — roughly forty sites under one corporate budget. Four to six times a month, a committee of four of us (a finance director, two clinicians on rotation, and me) has ninety minutes to decide how some scarce thing will be rationed for the coming quarter: MRI scanner slots, subspecialty radiology reporting time, intensive-care beds during the winter surge, a one-off allocation of a drug in short national supply, cloud GPU hours for the research arm, nurses redeployed between units. Historically the answer has been whichever of administrative allocation or internal chargeback the most senior person in the room preferred. I want to replace that with a procedure.

The material I want it built from is the socialist calculation debate. Mises's argument that without genuine markets in the factors of production there is no way to compute what something really costs; Hayek's recasting of it, that the knowledge relevant to allocation is dispersed, local to time and place, frequently tacit, and that a price is a device for transmitting that knowledge to people who will never possess it; Lange's reply that a planning board can post prices, watch for surpluses and shortages, and revise them, imitating a market without the property relations. I do not want a verdict on who won. I want those arguments turned into questions my committee can ask in order, in ninety minutes, with no economist present, and get the same kind of answer from as the last time.

So please write the procedure itself. I need:

- the sequence of questions, each phrased so that a non-specialist can answer it from records the organization actually keeps, with an explicit statement of what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as no answer;
- a default rule for when a question cannot be answered at all, and a defence of that default, since most of our no-answers will be permanent rather than temporary;
- the points at which the procedure returns a hard stop — do not allocate this by internal price — and the finding that triggers it;
- the points at which it stops and refers the decision elsewhere, because what remains is a question about what the good is for or about what the mode of allocation expresses, and not a question about information or efficiency;
- a note on which distinctions from the debate you preserved in translation, which you dropped as unusable at this scale and under this time limit, and why each dropped one was droppable.

I should say what I have already been doing informally, so you can keep what is sound and tell me what is confused. I have been asking four things. The refusal test: a number functions as a price only if some party inside the system can actually decline the trade at that number; if every unit is obliged to accept, the number is an index and using it in an optimization is arithmetic theatre. The channel-degradation audit: take the last three decisions of this kind that were made centrally, and ask whether the information that would have changed them existed locally and whether any part of it survived being reported upward; if it existed but never got written down, that is Hayek's case and it recurs. The latency ratio: compare how long it takes a shortage to show up in whatever signal we are using against how long the scarcity itself lasts; my instinct is that when the signal is slower than the thing it tracks, Lange's trial-and-error never converges and the choice is between a queue and luck. Soft-budget absorption: does anyone's discretionary budget actually shrink when their unit consumes more, and if not, is the price a memo. I also suspect there is a fifth question I am missing about inputs with no outside buyer or seller — a single-source legacy system, one named surgeon's time — where an internal transfer price seems to be anchored to nothing, but I cannot turn that suspicion into a question.

Then run the finished procedure on three decisions we face this quarter. One: cloud GPU hours for research groups, which are homogeneous, divisible and trivially metered, and for which we can look up what commercial cloud actually charges. Two: intensive-care beds in a winter surge, where I expect the committee is hoping an internal price will end the bedside argument about who gets the last bed and I suspect the procedure will refuse to give them that. Three: nursing shifts across units, where the knowledge of which shift is needed where is local, constantly changing, and largely held by the ward managers, and where the finance director will strongly resist the answer if it is let them trade. Give me the verdict and the decisive question in each case, in the form of a single page the director could sign, and say which step was a judgment call dressed up as a procedure.

Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do. I can think of two candidates I want you to adjudicate: one-off allocations that will never be repeated and so admit no learning and no Langean adjustment, and goods whose value is realized only by a set of units used together, where a per-unit price misdescribes what is being allocated. Add whatever failure modes you think I have not seen.
