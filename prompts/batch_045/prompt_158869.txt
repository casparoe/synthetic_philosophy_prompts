So I got into an argument after a department talk last week — someone said Lewis-style modal realism was just ontological largesse with no upside, I found myself defending it, and now I've agreed to present on it at our metaphysics reading group next month, which means I need to know where it's actually weak rather than just rehearsing the sales pitch. The version I'm defending is a bit more commital than the textbook one, and I want the commitments tested, not the caricature. Call it hard plenitude: there really are ever so many concrete worlds, each spatiotemporally and causally isolated from all the others; 'actual' is indexical, so calling this world actual adds nothing to it; de re modal claims about you or me go via counterpart relations rather than transworld identity; and what fixes which worlds there are is recombination over the fundamental entities, properties and relations that this world actually instantiates. On top of that I've bolted on three riders, and it's the riders I'm least sure about: (1) recombination only ever runs over the actual fundamental properties, so there are no alien natural properties and no possibilities that require them; (2) possibility is perfectly determinate, i.e. every modal claim has a sharp truth value fixed by what's out there in the plenitude, and apparent vagueness in modals (could this table have been one molecule thinner and still been the same table? could this conversation have gone one word shorter?) is vagueness in our concepts or plain ignorance, never vagueness in reality; (3) there is no empty world, so it's false that there might have been nothing at all. I'm also leaning on the view for more than bare modality — I use Humean best-system laws plus the plenitude to get counterfactuals and objective chance without primitive similarity orderings or primitive nomic necessity, and I've been toying with using it to underwrite the counterfactuals in structural causal models, where you'd say an intervention just selects one world from among the many, though I genuinely can't tell whether that last bit does any real work or is decoration.

What I want from you is an exposure map for this package. Not a list of stock objections I could find anywhere — I want them sorted by how much damage they'd actually do to the view as I've stated it, and I want you to be clear about which ones target the core and which only target my riders, since dropping a rider is much cheaper for me than dropping the view. Concretely: which cases can the theory literally not represent, and how bad is that (the nothing-at-all case, alien-property possibilities, possibilities involving vague or borderline objects)? Does the plenitude turn out not even to be set-sized, and if so is that a technical nuisance or does it break the quantification the view is stated in? Is the isolation principle doing definitional work or empirical work — because if it's empirical, then multiverse cosmology or some future detection of causal influence from outside would be evidence, and if it's definitional I worry I've insulated myself, especially since whenever I bring up other universes in cosmology I find myself saying they'd just be disconnected parts of our world, which feels like a dodge. What about things that have arguably already happened: the success of interventionist, model-relative counterfactual semantics in causal inference, which never quantifies over worlds and is perfectly happy with underdetermination — does that count as a defeater for my claim that the plenitude is what makes counterfactuals determinate, or does it just show the scientists aren't doing semantics? And which results in other areas does the view stand or fall with: counterpart theory for de re modality, Humeanism about laws, whether conceivability is any guide to possibility, whether mereological composition is unrestricted? I also want to know whether the view has a collapse risk — that if I relax the riders enough (allow alien properties, allow a bare empty world, let similarity do some primitive work) hard plenitude quietly becomes a notational variant of a rich ersatzism, so the concrete ontology was never earning its keep.

What would move me personally, so you can weight things: I'm most moved by a case the theory cannot describe at all, by an argument that the plenitude is idle because a sparser actualist or ersatzist story delivers the same explanatory goods, and by genuine internal tension. I'm much less moved by appeals to weirdness or to what plain folk supposedly think, and not at all moved by 'that's too many things', since cheapness per modal fact is the entire point of the view. Also, no citations, reading lists or bibliographies please — I'm writing this on a train with no library access and I'd only be guessing at what's in them, so argue it out rather than pointing me at papers.
