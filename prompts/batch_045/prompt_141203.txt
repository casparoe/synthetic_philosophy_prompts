I chair the scientific panel for a five-year research programme on how we get from objects to claims about the past, and I have to defend a portfolio to a funding board in six weeks. I would like you to act as a sceptical outside reviewer and give me a defended prioritization of lines of work, not a survey of the field. Please argue from the structure of the inference problem itself; I do not want a reading list, a literature map, or citations, and I would rather you were wrong in an interesting way than safe.

The programme's constraints, which are fixed and non-negotiable:

1. No new excavation or fieldwork. We can fund re-analysis of existing museum and repository collections and of legacy excavation archives, including collections whose provenance documentation is incomplete, inconsistent, or partly reconstructed decades after the fact.
2. A modest budget for new measurement on those collections — imaging, residue and use-wear analysis, radiometric dating, geochemistry — at a unit cost that rules out large-n work. Think hundreds of objects carefully measured, not tens of thousands cheaply classified.
3. No site- or period-specific monographs. We fund method, calibration, and the machinery of inference.
4. The required output is reliability guidance. Someone who must make or assess a consequential public claim about what people in the past believed, or about how they were governed — a curator writing a permanent display, a heritage official, an expert witness in a repatriation or land-use proceeding — should be able to tell an inference the record actually supports from one that is merely a coherent story, and should have a vocabulary for saying the difference out loud. The board will ask what changes in practice if this programme succeeds, and that question needs a real answer.

I have been working with five stipulative labels for what I take the difficulties to be. They are my coinages, not established terms, and I am as interested in whether they carve the problem correctly as in anything else:

- signature collapse — the suspicion that the map from institutional arrangements to durable material residues is many-to-one, so that quite different forms of authority, or quite different beliefs, are statistically indistinguishable in what survives. The live question, as I see it, is whether this collapse is a property of the record itself or of the coarseness of our descriptive vocabulary — whether finer measurement would break the tie, or whether no measurement could.
- survival asymmetry — the thought that the filter between past practice and present assemblage is not neutral with respect to what we want to detect: practices whose operation is verbal, bodily, ephemeral, or perishable leave systematically less, so that our sample is biased against certain kinds of institution in a way that is not merely small but directional.
- phase averaging — the mismatch between the temporal grain of deposits and the temporal grain of our claims: a phase in a sequence may span generations, while an institution or a belief is a claim about what people at some time took to be binding. A long-run pattern of apparent stability might be produced either by one durable institution or by repeated short-lived ones founded, collapsing, and re-founded.
- the answer-key problem — the record never arrives with its own interpretation attached, so an inference procedure can apparently only be tested by running it forward: build or borrow a case where the past is independently known, generate or document the residue it would leave, and see whether the procedure recovers it. The question is how much confidence forward-only testing can purchase, and what its ceiling is.
- confidence laundering — the observed drift of a claim from hedged in a specialist report to flat in a display text, a heritage designation, or testimony, with the hedge lost at each hand-off.

To keep the prioritization from floating, here is a fixed reference case. Call it Case U: a regional mortuary sequence in which grave assemblages converge over roughly two centuries on a narrow, highly standardized set of object types and depositional treatments, and then diverge again; the earliest usable documentary evidence postdates the sequence by about five centuries and says nothing about the period. Right now the honest reading of Case U is a disjunction: a central authority imposed a norm; a decentralized prestige system converged on one without any authority at all; or the appearance of standardization is an artifact of which dead people were selected for burial in the first place. Judge each candidate line of work partly by whether, if it succeeded, we could say more about Case U than that disjunction, and by whether it could tell us that we cannot say more.

Two items are already on the table because they are fashionable and visually legible to the board: large-scale automated classification of artefact images, and a much bigger comparative database of grave assemblages for analogue-matching. I suspect both may be ways of buying more data of the kind that got us into this situation. Tell me whether either deserves a slot, and if so under what condition and at what size.
