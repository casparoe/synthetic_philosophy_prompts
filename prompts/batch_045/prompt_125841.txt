I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm finishing a piece about a mid-sized transit authority (I'll keep it unnamed) that has started ranking its bus routes for service cuts using something it calls a "revealed welfare score," built entirely from fare-card data: routes that keep their riders after a fare increase or a headway cut are scored as highly valued, and the authority has used those scores to discount testimony from public hearings, on the grounds that what riders do is harder evidence than what they say at a microphone. Before I characterize the intellectual background, I want to be sure I'm describing it fairly.

To frame the piece I read a recent popular-economics book on how economists think about choice. I no longer have the book to hand, so what follows is my own condensation, reconstructed from notes, of its account of revealed preference theory; I'd like it audited as if it were the actual passage:

"Economics used to explain choices by appealing to an inner feeling of satisfaction, utility, but since nobody can observe feelings, the field gave up on them and simply redefined utility as behaviour. On this view, saying that someone prefers the bus to the train means nothing more than that they take the bus when both were available; utility is just a numerical filing system for recording choices, so the theory makes no claim about the mind and cannot be falsified by anything going on inside a person's head. What keeps it from being a tautology is that it still predicts: once I have seen you pick A over B in circumstances where you could have had either, you must pick A again every time the two are on offer, and because that consistency is required for every pair, a person's whole pattern of choices has to line up in a single ranking. There is also a policy moral, which is that what people do is better evidence of what is good for them than what they say: if residents keep riding a route the authority plans to cut, or fail to enrol in a benefit they are entitled to, that reveals that the route or the benefit is worth little to them, and overriding those choices on the strength of what people say at a public meeting is just paternalism."

My instinct is that the behaviourist first half is essentially right and that the policy moral is where the book overreaches. But a philosopher I interviewed for the piece said the reverse: that the "utility is just bookkeeping" framing is the dated part, and that reading observable choice as evidence about welfare is closer to what the theory was originally for. I can't tell which of them is wrong, or whether both are.

Could you go through my condensed passage sentence by sentence and tell me, for each, whether it is accurate, a defensible simplification, or subtly wrong — and, where it is wrong, what a careful defender of the theory would say instead? I've gathered that there are weaker and stronger versions of the consistency condition and that they are not equivalent, but I don't trust my notes on the difference. Then, in one further paragraph: given whatever survives your corrections, does the transit authority's inference actually follow? The wrinkle I keep getting stuck on is that the fare-card data is not drawn from a single fixed menu of options — fares, headways and available alternatives differ by neighbourhood, and the menus themselves changed after an earlier round of cuts — so the authority is comparing choices made under different conditions and treating continued ridership as a measure of how much a route is worth.

Please don't give me citations, further reading, or named sources; I can't verify them and won't use them. A numbered list, one entry per sentence of the passage, plus that closing paragraph: 600 words maximum for the whole answer.
