I am in my final year of secondary school and have been working through anthropic reasoning on my own for an independent project. I have landed on the self-indication assumption, and I want to know what would have to be true for me to be wrong — not a survey of the debate, but a map of the view's exposure, with the defeaters ranked.

The position, as I hold it: the fact that I find myself being someone is itself evidence, and it favours a hypothesis by a factor of N when that hypothesis says the world contains N times as many observers. So I accept what the presumptuous philosopher is supposed to establish. If two cosmological theories are exactly tied on every piece of evidence telescopes can gather, and one says the universe contains a trillion times more observers than the other, then the bigger-world theory is a trillion times more likely, and no further astronomical data could overturn that. I do not think this is a reductio. I think the verdict looks absurd only because we are used to treating the bare fact of our own existence as carrying no information at all.

Here is what I want. Rank what would count against this, from fatal to merely awkward, and keep separate what a defender of the view would themselves concede counts against it from what only its critics allege. Say what the view actually risks: does it predict, or recommend betting on, anything its rival does not? It disagrees with the self-sampling assumption about how the subject in the Sleeping Beauty setup should set her odds — a third rather than a half, as I understand it — and I take that to show the dispute is not merely verbal. But is there anything outside thought experiments, anything I could in principle observe or wager on, where the two come apart? If there is not, tell me plainly whether that is a defect in the view or simply what a constraint on prior probabilities looks like, and whether the view has been insulated to the point where nothing could dislodge it.

Three things I want addressed head on.

First, my own candidate for the decisive objection, which I want stress-tested rather than agreed with. Consider two hypotheses about human lives that every non-anthropic consideration treats as equally likely. On the first, a human life is one continuous subject of experience from birth to death. On the second, lives contain exactly the same experiences in exactly the same order, but are divided into ten successive segments separated by complete amnesia, so that each human life contains ten distinct subjects of experience and no one can tell from the inside which arrangement obtains. As far as I can see, my view should make me ten times more confident in the second hypothesis, since on it there are ten times as many subjects for me to turn out to be. That strikes me as worse than the presumptuous philosopher, because nothing has been added to the world: the same experiences have merely been parcelled out among more owners. If a defender instead says the view counts observer-moments rather than subjects, then the second hypothesis gets no boost whatsoever. So I want to know what principled reason selects one counting unit over the other, whether the presumptuous philosopher conclusion survives that choice, and whether the counting unit is doing undefended work that the view cannot discharge.

Second, the view needs a prior over how many observers exist before the existence-update is applied, and if a serious cosmological hypothesis says the number is infinite, the update looks either undefined or infinitely decisive. Decisive against the view, or a technicality a defender can absorb?

Third, I gather the view was originally introduced partly to defuse the doomsday argument, and I have been treating that as a point in its favour. Is it genuinely support, or is avoiding the doomsday argument something the rival can also manage?

What would move me most: an argument showing that the verdicts depend on a choice the view cannot make for me — a reference class, a counting unit, a prior — because then it is not one view and my confidence is misplaced. I will not be moved by the observation that the conclusion is counterintuitive; I know that already.

Please commit rather than catalogue. Name the single strongest objection, say whether it is fatal or merely costly, tell me whether I should keep the view and what I should replace it with if not, and mark the places where you yourself are least confident in what you have said.
