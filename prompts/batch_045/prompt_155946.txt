At a seminar in our biology department last week, two colleagues spent the better part of an hour arguing about a case that one of them had constructed for the occasion, and they asked me afterwards to write up who had the better of it. I could not decide, so I am passing it on. I should say up front that I have no stake in the outcome: I work on something else entirely, and I found myself agreeing with whichever of them had just finished speaking.

The case. Imagine a ring of thirteen populations of a salamander-like vertebrate encircling an uninhabitable basin — call it the Kestrel ring. Adjacent populations meet and interbreed freely at every contact zone; the two terminal populations, which overlap at the northern end of the ring, do not interbreed in the field and produce sterile hybrids when crossed in the laboratory. Three further wrinkles. First, mate recognition in this animal depends on a cuticular compound that is not produced by the host genome at all but by an obligate bacterial symbiont in the gut; the symbiont is reacquired from the environment by each generation and also moves horizontally between host populations wherever they come into contact, and if you clear an animal of its own symbiont and recolonise it with the symbiont of the terminal population, it will now court and mate with the terminal population. Second, population 7 came into existence about nine thousand years ago as an allopolyploid — a hybrid between two of the ring populations that doubled its chromosome set — and is therefore instantaneously and completely isolated from both parents, while looking nearly identical to one of them and occupying the same niche. Third, population 12 was extirpated by a flood two centuries ago and the same locality was recolonised shortly afterwards by dispersers from population 11, so something continuous in space and ecology is discontinuous in descent.

The exchange, as close as I can reproduce it.

(1) Marta: What makes a group of organisms a species rather than a mere assemblage is gene flow; that is the whole point of the notion, and every other proposed criterion — morphology, diagnosability, ecological role — is at best evidence for it. Applied here, the thirteen populations form a single species, because genes travel around the ring; the failure of the terminal pair to interbreed is a local breakdown within one species, not a boundary between two. Population 7 is a separate species the moment it arises. So the answer is two.

(2) Devin: That cannot be right, because the relation you have just defined is not transitive. Adjacent populations are the same species as one another all the way round, yet the terminal pair are not the same species as each other. A relation with that shape is not an equivalence relation, and a category whose membership relation is not an equivalence relation does not carve the world into things at all — it gives you a predicate that holds and fails without dividing anything. Your count of two is therefore not a discovery about the ring but an artefact of imposing a partition on something that resists one.

(3) Marta: You are treating a four-dimensional object as if it were a slice. Species are historical entities extended in time, and a synchronic snapshot of a lineage that is in the very process of dividing can perfectly well be indeterminate at the margin; the ring is one lineage caught in the act of becoming two, and the right description of it is exactly the one I gave. The non-transitivity is a property of the moment, not a defect in the concept.

(4) Devin: Then take the general-lineage idea seriously instead of using gene flow as a proxy for it: a species is a segment of a population lineage, and what we are really tracking is ancestry and descent, with reproductive isolation as one causal mechanism among several for keeping segments separate. That handles the ring — lineages are historical individuals, and their edges can be fuzzy without the notion failing — and it handles population 7 beautifully, since a new lineage can begin in a single generation.

(5) Marta: But which lineage? The host nuclear gene tree gives you one genealogy, the symbiont's gene tree gives you a different one because the symbiont moves sideways, and the allopolyploid carries two parental genomes at once, so depending on which marker you choose you get different segments, different boundaries and different counts, and nothing in the lineage idea tells you which marker is privileged. Worse, the lineage metaphor presumes branching descent; here the thing that actually reproduces — host plus symbiont — has a partly web-like ancestry, so there may be no single tree for the segments to be segments of.

(6) Devin: That last point overreaches. We do not say that a human being fails to be an individual because she is full of bacteria; the host lineage is the taxon and the symbiont is part of its environment, ecologically intimate but genealogically external. Anyway, the moral of the whole discussion is pluralism: there is no single privileged question here, there are several well-posed questions — how much gene flow, which segments of which genealogy, how many diagnosable clusters — and the conservation agency that has to list protection units should simply fix on the concept suited to its purpose and say so.

(7) Marta: If the count swings between two and thirteen depending on which question you pick, and the law protects whatever the count is a count of, then pluralism is not a reconciling metaphysics but a licence for a bureaucratic decision to masquerade as a biological one. Either species are real, separate things and one of our concepts is getting at them, or the law should be rewritten to protect whatever it actually wants to protect.

Please go through the exchange move by move: for each of the seven, say whether it is clearly right, right but incomplete, half right, or clearly mistaken, and give the reason in a sentence or two. I am especially interested in the moves that turn on a technical claim rather than a rhetorical one — whether the failure of transitivity really is fatal to Marta's concept, whether a species can have a founding generation of one individual, and whether Devin's way of demoting the symbiont to the host's environment costs him anything he needs elsewhere in the argument. Then step back and give an overall verdict: who is more right, on what, and what — if anything — both of them are missing. If you think the whole dispute turns on a distinction neither of them drew, say what it is and where it would have bitten.

Please write the answer in British English, in prose rather than bullet-point fragments, and keep it under about nine hundred words.
