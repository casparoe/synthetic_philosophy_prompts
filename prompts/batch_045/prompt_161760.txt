I'm seventeen, in my final year of school, and for an independent project I've been trying to work out what exactly goes wrong when someone grants the premises of an argument and still refuses the conclusion. I started from Lewis Carroll's dialogue in which Achilles gets the Tortoise to accept A and to accept that if A then Z, and the Tortoise still will not accept Z; it agrees to have the rule itself written down as a further premise, then demands that the new rule be written down as well, and so on. I have since collected a set of cases where my reaction is fairly settled, plus one question I cannot settle at all, and I would like to know whether there is a single principle behind the reactions.

1. The Tortoise. I think it is at fault. I also think two further things: writing the rule down as an extra premise does nothing for it, since it grants each new conditional instantly and is no closer to Z; and there is no further true proposition that it is missing, because I can imagine a Tortoise who accepts every conditional anyone might offer and still refuses Z, and I would still blame it.

2. Yara, who is nine and has never been taught anything about one thing's following from another. She will happily agree that everything with four equal sides and four right angles is a rectangle, and that the shape on the page is a square, and then shrug when asked whether it is a rectangle. I do not want to call her irrational; she is not refusing anything, she simply does not yet have the idea. But if she is not at fault, then whatever the Tortoise is guilty of cannot just be failing to believe a consequence of what it believes.

3. The program that runs our school timetable. It stores a list of facts and a list of rules, and it has no part that ever applies the rules to the facts, so it never outputs anything that follows. Nothing here is irrational and nothing here is at fault. So the fault in case 1 is not merely a matter of what someone ends up doing or saying.

4. Idris, who accepts the axioms of a theory and has verified a random sample of the steps in a proof of some claim C that runs to a trillion lines, nearly all of them generated by a machine. He withholds C. I think he is being reasonable and I would withhold as well. Yet he has granted the premises and granted that each step is licensed, and I cannot see what distinguishes him from the Tortoise except that his inference is long.

5. Priya, who is highly confident of P and highly confident that if P then Q. She then learns from a source she has good reason to trust that people with her training get inferences of exactly this shape wrong about three times in ten, and that nobody can tell in advance which ones those are. She can find nothing wrong with this particular inference; she just has this statistical evidence about her own performance. My reaction is that it is fine for her to end up considerably less confident in Q than her confidence in the premises would suggest, and I would not call her irrational for it. This is the verdict I feel least sure about, and it seems to sit badly with case 1: if the Tortoise is at fault for not moving from premises to conclusion, why is Priya not at fault for failing to move all the way?

6. Theo, who has decent evidence for each of P, that if P then Q, and that not-Q, and who has noticed the clash. I think he must give something up, but I do not think anything tells him which, and I do not think he is irrational for continuing to believe all three this week while he tries to work out which is weakest. So the requirement, whatever it is, seems to concern how he eventually settles rather than requiring him to have settled already.

7. Sami, who has been persuaded by an argument he cannot rebut that a deceiving demon has scrambled his judgments of validity, so that he can no longer tell which arguments are valid. I want to say two things that pull against each other: that he should still go by the arguments that really are valid, and that he would be reasoning badly if he drew conclusions merely because an argument struck him as valid. Since the scrambled appearances are all he has to go on, I am not sure what I am asking of him, and I am much less confident here than in case 1.

The question I cannot settle: when I try to explain to a classmate why it is a good rule to accept Q whenever you accept P and accept that if P then Q, every argument I can produce for the rule uses the rule. My instinct is that this is not worthless, that there is something to being shown the rule is already at work in everything you do, but that it is plainly not a proof, and that if someone remained unmoved by it I would not call them irrational. I cannot tell whether this is the same shape of problem as the Tortoise's or a different one.

My own stab at a principle: a thinker is rationally required to accept a conclusion just when she believes the premises, grasps that they entail it, and has no reason to doubt her own grasp in that particular case. I think that handles cases 1 to 4, 6 and 7, but I cannot tell whether the third clause is what rescues case 5 or whether it quietly empties the principle of content, since almost any doubt can be redescribed as doubt about one's own grasp.

So: is there a principle that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them? Please state it precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not thought of, and say which of my cases motivates each clause. Tell me what it says about the argument that uses the rule to defend the rule, and what it says about a case I have not listed, such as someone who accepts the premises of an argument and is told by a mathematician she trusts that it is valid although she cannot follow it herself. And tell me plainly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which one you would overturn.

I would rather have a definite answer than a survey: commit to the principle you think is best, give your reasons, and do not leave the choice to me. Where you are unsure, say which part you are unsure about and what would settle it.
