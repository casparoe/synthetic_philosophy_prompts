**The question (25 marks)**

A service has two independent latency monitors, alpha and beta, running on separate nodes. Each is configured to send a failover request to the orchestrator if p99 latency exceeds 200 ms for 10 s. The orchestrator fails over on the first failover request it receives and ignores all further requests for the next 30 s. At 09:14:00, p99 latency spikes to 900 ms.

*Case 1 (near-simultaneous).* Both monitors detect the spike. Alpha's request arrives at 09:14:01.000 and beta's at 09:14:01.003; the orchestrator fails over on alpha's. Had alpha's request never arrived, the orchestrator would have received beta's and failed over at 09:14:01.003. Had beta's never arrived, it would have failed over at 09:14:01.000 on alpha's.

*Case 2 (late preemption).* Same setup, except that beta has a 50 ms backoff: it waits until 09:14:01.050 and then sends its request only if no failover is already in progress. Alpha fires at 09:14:01.000, the failover begins, beta sees it and stays silent. Had alpha not fired, beta would have fired at 09:14:01.050 and the orchestrator would have failed over then.

*Case 3 (omission).* A kill-switch flag, DISABLE_AUTOFAILOVER, is OFF. Had it been ON, the orchestrator would have ignored both requests and no failover would have occurred.

(a) [7 marks] State the simple counterfactual theory of causation (the Lewis-style "but-for" account, including how it extends to indirect cases). Apply it to Case 1 at the coarse grain — take the effect to be simply *the orchestrator failed over* — and say what verdict it gives about alpha's request and about beta's request. Then state the intuitive verdict and say, in a sentence, why the gap matters for the theory.

(b) [9 marks] Sketch a Woodward-style interventionist account: what is an intervention, and what does it take for X to be a cause of Y? Then (i) say whether the interventionist account gets a better verdict than the counterfactual one on Case 1, and if so how; and (ii) apply it to Case 2 and explain how it yields the intuitive verdict that alpha's request caused the failover and beta's silence did not. Identify what feature of the account does the work.

(c) [6 marks] A standard objection to interventionism is that "intervention" is itself a causal notion, so the account is circular and cannot be a reductive analysis of causation. Write the strongest reply you can on behalf of the interventionist and say what it concedes. In the course of your answer: must an intervention be a manipulation a human agent could actually perform? Must it be randomized? Does it matter?

(d) [3 marks] On either account, is the flag's being OFF (Case 3) a cause of the failover? What is the main difficulty?

**The answer (verbatim, service names swapped for alpha/beta)**

(a) The counterfactual theory says that c is a cause of e just in case, if c had not occurred, e would not have occurred. Lewis extends it to indirect causation by allowing a chain of counterfactual dependence: if c hadn't occurred, d wouldn't have, and if d hadn't, e wouldn't have. Applied to Case 1 at the coarse grain it gives the wrong verdict twice over. If alpha's request had not arrived, the failover would still have happened, because beta's would have arrived 3 ms later; so alpha's request is not a cause. If beta's had not arrived, the failover would have happened anyway on alpha's; so beta's is not a cause either. The intuitive verdict is that at least one of them caused the failover — most engineers I asked said alpha's, since that is the request the orchestrator actually acted on. The gap matters because the theory makes the verdict depend on whether a backup happened to be available rather than on what actually produced the outcome, so it withdraws causes in exactly the kind of redundant system we spend our time building.

(b) The interventionist account says: X is a cause of Y, relative to a set of variables and a model of the causal structure, iff there is a possible intervention on X — a surgical change that sets X's value, affects Y only through X, and leaves the rest of the structure intact — under which the value (or the probability distribution) of Y changes. Two things are worth flagging. First, causal claims are model-relative: whether "beta's silence" or "the latency spike" or "the flag being off" is the cause depends partly on which variables we have put in the model, and this is why the account handles the context-sensitivity of causal talk better than its rivals. Second, the point of cashing causation out in terms of interventions is that it connects directly to experimental practice: randomising assignment in an A/B test is one way of making a manipulation satisfy the independence requirements the account imposes, i.e. of ensuring the intervention is not correlated with other causes of the outcome.

(i) This is the case interventionism is supposed to win. Because an intervention is surgical, it changes one candidate at a time while holding everything else fixed at the value it actually had. So to test alpha we hold beta fixed: we consider the situation in which beta stays silent and alpha's request does the work by itself, and there alpha's firing plainly makes a difference to whether the failover occurs. So alpha is a cause; and the same argument with the roles swapped shows that beta is a cause too. This is how interventionism escapes the overdetermination problem that sinks the counterfactual theory — it can isolate each candidate cause, where the counterfactual theory can only ask about a world in which the other candidate also happens to disappear.

(ii) In Case 2 the same manoeuvre gives the intuitive verdict. Beta's actual value is *silent*, so we hold beta fixed at silence and vary alpha: the failover tracks alpha, so alpha's request is a cause. If instead we hold alpha fixed at its actual value (fired) and vary beta, nothing changes, so beta's silence is not a cause. The simple counterfactual theory gets this wrong, because it lets beta's merely possible firing fill in the gap: had alpha not fired, beta would have and the failover would still have happened, so on that theory alpha's request is not a cause either. The feature doing the work is the holding-fixed: interventionism evaluates a candidate against the other variables at their actual values, whereas the counterfactual theory lets those variables take whatever value they would have had.

(c) The circularity objection is well known and, I think, correctly answered by giving up the reductive ambition. "Intervention" is a causal notion, so an interventionist account cannot be a reduction of causation to non-causal facts; Woodward's own response is to treat it as a characterisation of the inferential and explanatory role of causal claims — what they license us to predict under manipulation, and how they support explanations — rather than as a definition in non-causal terms. The cost of this is that the account is tied to what agents can do. If interventions are manipulations, the account has no grip on causes no one could manipulate, such as the sun's gravitational field or the mass of a distant galaxy. Woodward more or less accepts this, and it marks a real difference from Lewis, whose counterfactual account carries no such restriction.

(d) Yes: on either account the flag's being OFF comes out as a cause, since the failover counterfactually depends on the flag's value, and an intervention setting it to ON would prevent the failover. The difficulty is metaphysical rather than formal. Both Lewis and Woodward are uneasy about treating absences or omissions as causes, because an absence doesn't seem to have what it takes to bring anything about, and in ordinary engineering talk we would rather say the latency spike caused the failover than that a missing kill-switch did. If you are willing to let absences into your ontology, both theories deliver the verdict you'd expect; I don't think either has a principled story about when to allow it.
