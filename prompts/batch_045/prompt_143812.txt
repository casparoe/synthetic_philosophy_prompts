Hi — I write about science and ideas for a living, and I've been banging my head against a piece about the 'man of his time' defence for about a week, mainly because my own gut reactions don't add up to anything I can say out loud. So here are my verdicts, and I'd like the principle behind them, if there is one. A Roman senator who owns slaves: I barely blame him at all. A Virginia planter in the 1790s who owns slaves: I blame him plenty, even though it's the same institution — the difference for me seems to be that by then the language of universal rights was in the air and there were actual abolitionists you could have listened to. Jefferson: no defence from me, and I actually score him slightly worse than a neighbour who did the same things, because he wrote the words and by most accounts had private doubts; and yet I don't want his statue down, I want the plaque rewritten, which sits oddly with how little I care about the Roman. Columbus: no defence whatsoever — he was a governor whom contemporaries accused of serious brutality, the Crown sent someone out who arrested him and shipped him home in 1500, and I'm completely fine watching his statues come down, even though the scale of what Jefferson did is arguably far worse, so my reactions there are backwards on the numbers. Churchill: I shrug at a lot of the race-and-empire material, but I don't shrug at the wartime Bengal famine, and I count it against him more that he kept arguing for empire after the wind had clearly turned — someone who merely goes along with a settled norm and someone who turns into that norm's loud defender once it's contested do roughly the same acts, and I score them very differently. A hypothetical: a nutrition researcher in the late '60s quietly takes money from a sugar lobby to argue that the evidence linking sugar to heart disease is shaky; his peers did it, professional norms more or less permitted it, and he probably half believed it. I blame him outright and give him none of the discount I gave the Roman, and I can't tell you why those two come apart. And looking forward: I get genuinely defensive when people say future generations will judge us over factory farming the way we judge the past, and I want to say that would be unfair — but I refuse the same generosity backwards, and that asymmetry bugs me.

My own draft, which I'm pretty sure is broken: you get a discount when (a) nobody in your information environment was saying the thing was wrong in a way you could actually have heard, (b) you didn't double down once people started saying it, and (c) the bad thing isn't what your celebrated achievement actually consists in. Please give me a principle, or two or three rival candidates, stated tightly, with the case that forces each clause; then tell me what it says about cases I haven't listed — a revered mid-century scientist whose famous result can only be extracted from data from an experiment that was already considered unethical at the time, someone who was *ahead* of their time (do they get credit symmetric to the blame I hand out?), a living eighty-five-year-old who said awful things in 1974 and never walked them back, and a statue that turns out to have been erected in the 1890s versus an identical one erected generations later as deliberate pushback, where I suspect I'm judging the people who put it up and not the person at all. Then: which of my verdicts your principle can't save, and where your own reasoning is weakest — what argument, or what kind of case, would make you switch. No reading lists or citations please, I want the argument itself. Oh, and for what it's worth, I notice I'm definately not neutral here: I want to come out still being allowed to blame people.
