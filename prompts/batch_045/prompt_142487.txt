hey — I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background, and I got nerd-sniped by Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge a few weeks ago and have been reading about it on my phone at the gym. The part I think I have straight: knowledge is supposed to be a counterfactual relation between your belief and the world — you know that p only if you believe p, p is true, and the method you actually used wouldn't have handed you "p" in the nearby situations where p is false (plus the mirror-image condition where p is true). And the thing everyone brings up as the fatal cost is that this breaks closure under known implication: I can know I'm sitting at my desk, and know that sitting at my desk means I'm not a brain in a vat, and still not know that I'm not a brain in a vat, because in the closest worlds where I am a vat-brain my method cheerfully keeps delivering "you're at your desk". I can follow the mechanics of that, but I have no feel for the *shape* of the failure, and I want a gut-level picture, not a proof.

So I invented a thing to help me think about it, and I want to know if its stupid. Call a belief sensitive@k if, in the k nearest worlds where p is false, my actual belief-forming method still doesn't output p. sensitive@1 is basically the textbook condition; large k is a much harsher requirement. On this picture closure isn't a switch that flips off, it's a budget that drains: I might be sensitive@10 on "the service is healthy" but only sensitive@3 on "the deploy didn't half-fail", because the nearest worlds where the second claim is false sit further out and my dashboard behaves differently over there. Every step where I competently deduce q from p then eats some counterfactual slack, and long enough chains of implication leave you with nothing left.

What I actually want from you:

1. Is that a coherent object, or does it fall apart on contact? My worry is that sensitivity@k needs a metric — or at least an ordering where "the next nearest world" is a well-defined thing — and that once you've got distances you've smuggled in more structure than the original theory wanted. Does it collapse into something philosophers already have a name for? I've seen the word "safety" floating around and it sounded adjacent, but not the same thing.
2. How does the budget behave when you iterate implications? Is there any clean claim available — degradation is monotone, it hits zero after finitely many steps, something like that — or does it depend so much on the particular implication that "budget" is a misleading metaphor and I should drop it?
3. Does the graded version escape the standard complaint that denying closure is just irrational (if you know p and you know p implies q, how can you refuse to say you know q), or does it simply re-run that same complaint once per value of k?
4. Where does my monitoring analogy mislead me? I keep reasoning as if "a check that still reports UP in nearby failure worlds" were the whole phenomenon, and I suspect that's a special case that flatters the intuition.

One more thing: I have a vague sense Nozick had his own move here, something about re-applying the tracking conditions to the combined method "believe p, then deduce q", but I can't reconstruct it and I don't want to guess. If it's relevant to any of the above, say so — and please flag when you're unsure. I'd much rather get "I think, but not certain" than a confident invention.
