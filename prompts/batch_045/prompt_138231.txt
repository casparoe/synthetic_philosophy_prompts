So I'm on this tiny committee at my university that hands out a hardship fund — a few thousand a term, way more applicants than money — and we keep trying to be principled about whose need is greater, which has made me realise I have no settled view at all on how you compare how well off two different people are, or how much some fixed thing would help each of them. Here are my gut verdicts. Two applicants both want $400 toward a laptop: one is broke and it's the difference between finishing her thesis or not, the other is comfortable and just wants a nicer machine — obviously the first, no contest. Petra has had chronic migraines for fifteen years and rates this one 5/10 and works through it; Sam has his first ever migraine, rates it 9/10 and is wrecked — I want to say Petra's episode is costing her at least as much as Sam's is costing him, because she's recalibrated her scale downward, and I don't think we should let that count against her. Miriam has had a severe mobility impairment since childhood, reports 8/10 life satisfaction, and is genuinely the least bitter person I know — and here I want to take that at face value and give her the cost of the equipment and not a penny of extra priority on top. Kostas needs a €200 bottle to get the pleasure I get out of a €12 one, and Bea is content with almost nothing, not depressed, just small appetites — I resent the idea of topping Kostas up, but I'm perfectly happy to give Bea her share and I even feel she's cheap to make happy so we should do it, which I notice is the same phenomenon viewed from the other side. And one person tortured for an afternoon strikes me as worse than a thousand people with a bad headache, but worse-than reversed if it's a million, so I seem to have some kind of threshold and not a smooth exchange rate. Also: I count a dog's distress at something, less than a human's, and I cannot say what the fraction is.

The thing I've been toying with as a principle is a kind of common currency that isn't money: ask each person how many days of their own life, at their current level of wellbeing, they'd give up to avoid the bad thing or get the good thing, and compare those numbers across people. It feels like it gets Petra (she'd give a lot, her days are hard), it gets the broke student, and it gets Sam vs Petra right without trusting either of their 1–10 numbers. But it breaks in ways I can see coming: someone who is already very badly off has cheap days and so looks cheap to help, someone who'd rather die than endure something trades away their whole remaining life and blows up the scale, and Bea's days are so pleasant that she'd trade a lot of them, which perversely inflates her claim in the opposite direction from where my gut went. The fallback I keep hearing about is the Harsanyi-style move where you don't compare anyone, you just ask what you'd choose if you had an equal chance of being each applicant, and let your own intrapersonal preferences under uncertainty do all the work. I can't tell whether that dissolves the problem or just relocates it: do I imagine being Miriam with Miriam's adapted psychology, in which case I just get Miriam's own verdict back, or with my own, in which case I get an answer that has nothing to do with her? And does it secretly assume everyone is equally good at converting resources into wellbeing, which is exactly what Kostas and Bea are supposed to be a problem for?

So: is there a principle that captures that whole spread, or close to it, and if not, which of my verdicts is the one I have to give up? I'd like it stated precisely enough that I could actually read it out at a meeting, with the case that motivates each clause, and I especially want to know what it says about the three cases I haven't put on the list — comparing a person now against that same person in twenty years with completely different tastes and a different baseline, comparing people who don't exist yet against people who do, and an applicant who can't answer the life-days question at all because she's in the middle of a psychotic episode. I'd also like to know whether the honest answer is sometimes that two applicants are genuinely incomparable rather than just hard to compare, because we currently resolve those by awkwardly splitting the money and everyone hates it. And don't tell me to go and read up on social choice theory, I have a dissertation to finish and no library access this term.
