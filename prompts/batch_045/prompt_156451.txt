I'm drafting a short essay on Kafka's parable *Before the Law* — the one the prison chaplain tells in *The Trial*, in which a man from the country spends his entire life sitting outside a gate that stands open, deferred by a doorkeeper who accepts everything he offers and speaks of still more terrible doorkeepers further in, and who at the end shouts that the gate was made for this man alone and is now being shut. I want to read it against the fragments on justice and force in Pascal's *Pensées*: the argument there is roughly that justice unarmed is impotent and force unjustified tyrannical, so that one must make the just strong or the strong just; that since men cannot get justice to be seen as just they have simply declared force legitimate, which is why custom rather than equity is what is actually obeyed, and why a killing can be murder on one bank of a river and lawful on the other; and that the title by which law holds must never be examined, since any inquiry behind the veil finds only usurpation and would dissolve the state.

Three things I want the reading to settle rather than gesture at. First, the man never asks by what title the doorkeeper forbids him: is that Pascalian obedience to enacted authority, or something the parable treats as pathology, and does the difference matter to Pascal's case? Second, the doorkeeper is himself a subordinate to doorkeepers he has never seen, so delegated force has no terminus, whereas Pascal's project of making the strong just seems to need a point at which force stops being mere usurpation. Third, the last line makes the law's address radically singular — the gate is his alone — while Pascal's argument runs on law's being everyone's law, obeyed because it is received; say whether that breaks the parallel or can be absorbed.

One further thought of my own to test: for Pascal the law is visible and only its title is hidden, while in the parable the prohibition and its enforcer are entirely visible and the Law behind the gate is never seen at all. Is that inversion the real thrust of the parable? Where the Pascalian reading distorts the parable, or the parable simply fails to illuminate Pascal, say so — I would rather have the limits stated than have the fit forced. End by saying what, if anything, the parable shows about the relation of force and justice that Pascal's argument cannot show on its own.
