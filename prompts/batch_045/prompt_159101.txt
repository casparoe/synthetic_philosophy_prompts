ok so I built this for my reading group (we're doing social choice / group agency stuff) and I think I've accidentally proven that groups don't exist. The conclusion is obviously false, so something in the chain is broken, but every step looks fine to me and I can't name the leak.

Setup: a three-member panel (A, B, C) has to rule on whether to grant a permit; let X = "the permit should be granted". Two procedures both look perfectly legitimate: ask them to vote on X directly (conclusion-based), or have them vote on the reasons and let the ruling follow from whatever the majority of reasons supports (premise-based).

(1) What a group judges is whatever its aggregation procedure delivers; there's no procedure-independent fact of the matter. So if two procedures that are each individually fair — anonymous, neutral, taking each member's full sincere judgment set — deliver different verdicts, there is no fact of the matter about what the group judged.

(2) You don't need a case with a specially structured set of reasons for the two procedures to come apart; you can manufacture the structure. For any proposition X and any Y at all, X is equivalent to (X ∨ Y) ∧ (X ∨ ¬Y). So you can always put X∨Y and X∨¬Y on the agenda as X's "premises", and premise-based voting then applies to every question whatsoever.

(3) And here's a profile where it bites. Y can be anything — say, "the cafeteria is out of oat milk".

             X     Y     X∨Y    X∨¬Y
      A      yes   yes   yes    yes
      B      no    yes   yes    no
      C      no    no    no     yes

Each member is fully consistent (each accepts X iff they accept both disjunctions, as the equivalence requires). A majority rejects X. But a majority accepts X∨Y (A and B) and a majority accepts X∨¬Y (A and C). Conclusion-based: permit denied. Premise-based: the conjunction of what the majority accepted is equivalent to X, so permit granted.

(4) Since Y was arbitrary and the profile is jointly consistent, the divergence isn't a quirk of multi-premise legal reasoning or of weird agendas. It's available for every proposition the group could consider — on some agenda, for some profile of individual judgments.

(5) So, by (1), for any X there's no fact of the matter about whether the group judged X.

(6) So no group ever judges anything. The court never decided the case, the hiring committee never voted to hire, the state never declared war. Collective belief, collective intention and corporate responsibility are all error-theoretic.

(7) That's absurd. And it gets worse: run (1)–(6) with a single person's time-slices (or her sub-personal systems, or whatever) playing the role of the group and you get that no individual ever believes anything either, and that's even more absurd.

I'd most like to keep (2), (3) and (4): I checked the truth table and the equivalence by hand, and (4) feels like the bit with real bite, since it's what turns a cute puzzle about courts into a problem about everything. I keep going back and forth between (1) and the step from (4) to (5). My worry about (4)→(5) is that (4) is a "there is some agenda, there is some profile" claim while (5) is about this group with its actual agenda and its actual profile, so maybe the quantifiers are doing something sneaky — but if that's the leak, I don't know what plugging it costs me. Does the paradox then just collapse into practical advice about how to design agendas and constitutions, or is something radical left over?
