I'm a junior at a fairly large public high school, and I've been asked to design and run one ninety-minute session of our school's new "Great Questions" seminar — a mixed group of about eighteen sophomores and seniors, in an ordinary classroom with a projector, a whiteboard, index cards and dice but nothing else, and no real money changing hands. My session is on the argument that the shift from foraging to farming, beginning roughly ten thousand years ago, was a bad bargain: longer hours of harder work for a narrower and less reliable diet, shorter stature and worse teeth in the skeletal record, crowding and disease, grain that can be stored and therefore counted, seized and taxed, and with it the first durable inequalities between people — set against the fact that farming feeds vastly more people, that almost nobody today wants to return to foraging, and that the seminar itself (literacy, free time, eighteen people in a room fed by other people's fields) only exists downstream of the transition. I tried a cut-down version last semester in which I simply laid out the case for and against and then took a vote, and it collapsed within five minutes into "farming is obviously progress" versus "hunter-gatherers were peaceful and free," with nobody moving an inch; I want to rebuild it so that students run into the problem themselves before anyone names it, and I'd rather they didn't hear the phrase "original affluent society" or the famous claim that agriculture was the worst mistake in human history until the debrief.

Two things I want built into the machinery rather than said out loud. First, my history teacher made a suggestion I keep turning over: that "was it a mistake?" may be a badly formed question, because it assumes a decision-maker standing at a decision-point, when what actually happened looks more like a slow local ratchet — small groups edging into cultivation over centuries, each step sensible on its own — and because once a settlement's population has grown to what stored grain can feed, a return to foraging would starve most of the people then alive, so the option is gone not by anyone's choice but by arithmetic. If that's right, then the questions worth separating are whether the transition was good for the people who lived through it, whether it was avoidable given what was visible to them at the time, and whether it is good that it happened given everything that came after, including us; those can come apart, and I'd like the game's structure to make them come apart for the players. Second, I want the honest tension between ledgers to be something they feel: the visible scoreboard during play should reward total calories and group size, so that the teams who "win" are visibly worse off by the measures they themselves care about (free time, food variety, health, being bossed around), and a second ledger is only revealed at the end. There's also a version of the point I can't quite formulate — that everyone sitting in the room is descended from the people for whom farming worked, so our very ability to ask is a product of the outcome, and any verdict we reach is delivered from inside the result — which I hope the debrief can handle without sliding into "so nothing can be said."

What I'd like from you is a runnable design: the setup and materials; roles or team structure; a round-by-round timeline for ninety minutes; explicit mechanics for labor, storage, raiding or defense, population growth and the irreversibility of the shift once population has adjusted; the scoring, including how the second ledger is computed and revealed; and a debrief that maps specific moments in play onto specific parts of the argument, including the three-way split above and the "asking from inside the outcome" worry. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm especially worried about the noble-savage reading, about students deciding the whole thing is just a matter of taste, and about one team farming early and snowballing so hard that the simulation stops being informative — and be frank about which feature of the argument the game cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply in the debrief myself, since I suspect the real answer depends on evidence about population density, land abundance and generations of time that a classroom can't stage.
