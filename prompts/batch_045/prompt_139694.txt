You are a philosopher of language who works on reference, modality, and the boundary between semantics and metasemantics. Before you answer, reconstruct each position below in its strongest form, setting out which premises the other side actually grants and which it denies; only then say what you think. I would rather have a slow, careful reconstruction than a fast verdict, and if you think I have misdescribed one of the three positions, correct me rather than answering the question I asked.

Here is the stall. A small working group I belong to has spent about two years on the cluster of issues around rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori, and nobody has moved since the fourth session. Three positions are on the table, and each has a reply to the others that the others regard as question-begging.

(1) The direct-reference side runs the modal argument, the epistemic argument, and the argument from modal innocence: names keep their bearers across counterfactual situations in which the associated descriptions fail; a competent speaker can assent to one identity statement involving two names of the same thing while dissenting from the other, so the names cannot contribute the same descriptive content; and if names meant descriptions, ordinary contingent predications would come out trivially necessary.

(2) The descriptivist side concedes rigidity outright and buys it back with a rigidifying device: a description of the form the thing that actually satisfies F is rigid, so rigidity was never the issue. What the modal argument really needs, they say, is the claim that no description gives the semantic content of a name, and that claim is what the argument asserts rather than establishes.

(3) The two-dimensionalist side says both are half right: assign names a primary intension that does the epistemic work and a secondary intension that does the modal work, and the necessary a posteriori becomes the predictable special case of a contingent primary intension paired with a necessary secondary one. The Kripkean reply is that the primary intension models what speakers would say under a counterfactual supposition, not what their words mean, and that a semantic theory is not in the business of modelling ignorance. The descriptivist reply is that the two-dimensionalist has quietly taken the descriptivist's explanatory burden on board and relabelled it. Meanwhile the descriptivists keep producing the case of a name introduced by an explicit stipulation that it shall denote whoever did such-and-such, whoever that was, and claim this shows that rigidity and descriptivism are not even in tension.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the term rigid designator is quietly doing two different jobs and the arguments need both while each side only ever wins one. Job one is semantic: the expression's semantic value is invariant under counterfactual evaluation. Job two is metasemantic: the reference of the expression is not mediated by anything's satisfying an associated descriptive condition. The modal argument appears to establish job one, but the conclusion people draw from it is job two, and the descriptivist's actually device is a way of granting job one while refusing job two. On this reading the argument has never been valid as stated, and the two-dimensionalist is not a third option at all but a proposal to split the explanatory difference. My suspicion is that the only shared datum in the whole area is the necessary a posteriori itself, and that everything else is theory choice about which burdens a semantic theory has to discharge: counterfactual truth conditions, cognitive significance, compositionality, learnability, or the epistemology of reference.

Is that right? More generally: what kind of impasse is this? A verbal dispute about content or about modality; a disagreement about which cases count as data and which are merely intuition-mongering; a hidden empirical question about what speakers would judge under counterfactual supposition, which somebody could in principle go and test; two or more different questions being pursued under one heading; a disagreement about the explanatory role a semantic theory is supposed to play; or bedrock intuitions about reference that neither side can argue for at all?

Please give me the diagnosis supported by what each side actually claims, and tell me which single move in the exchange above is doing real work and which are distractions.
