I’m a software engineer with no formal philosophy background; I’m taking a part-time practitioner course on AI safety, and I got into an argument with a coworker over lunch about whether a well-known industry failure is actually a case study in a theoretical distinction we covered or just a mundane data-quality mistake. I’d like you to analyze the episode through the lens of process-based versus outcome-based supervision of AI systems. The distinction, as I understand it from class, is between supervising only the final outputs a system produces—the labels, rewards, or results—and also making the system’s intermediate reasoning or decision procedure explicit and checking that each step is the right kind of step. The episode is the widely reported Amazon résumé-screening case. The salient facts, as I understand them from public reporting, are these: Amazon built an experimental machine-learning tool to rate software-engineering applicants; it was trained on about a decade of résumés together with the ratings or assessments that past candidates had received, a corpus skewed heavily toward men because the technical workforce had been predominantly male; the model learned to downgrade CVs that carried markers of women’s affiliation, such as women’s societies or women’s colleges; engineers tried to make the model neutral to the most explicit gendered terms, but the debiasing did not hold; and Amazon ultimately abandoned the project without, as far as the public account goes, putting the tool into production as a live hiring filter. I already know the headline lesson that biased training data produces biased models, and I don’t want that regurgitated. What I want first is a careful mapping: in outcome-based terms, what exactly was the outcome being predicted or optimized, what were the training labels and the feedback signal, and what would proper outcome supervision have required the team to notice or do differently; in process-based terms, what decision procedure or intermediate feature reliance would have had to be exposed and supervised, what would a process-supervised version of the tool have looked like, and what kinds of reasoning or features would the overseers have been checking.

Second, say which parts of the episode each style of supervision explains, which parts it misses or misdescribes, and what the case in turn shows about the process-versus-outcome distinction: does it confirm the standard worry that outcome-based supervision is dangerous when the outcomes it learns from are themselves shaped by historical injustice, does it reveal a cost or blindness in process-based supervision that the alignment discussion underrates, or is it an episode the distinction was never designed to handle. Be disciplined about who the supervisor is, what counts as an outcome, what counts as a step, and where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Finally, please be explicit about calibration: state how confident you are in the verdict, name the single argument or consideration that would most change your mind, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail or where you are filling gaps in the public account with plausible assumptions rather than established fact.
