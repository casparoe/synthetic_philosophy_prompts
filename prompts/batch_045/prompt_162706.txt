hey — I need one essay question for the take-home in my upper-level ancient philosophy seminar. Third-years, mix of philosophers and classicists, they've read Nicomachean Ethics book V and Politics book I; 90 minutes; answer should run to about 700 words. Topic is Aristotle on money and exchange, and I want something they can't pass by reciting "need is the real standard, money exists by convention not by nature, usury is unnatural because money is barren." Ideally the question makes them run the text against a case instead of summarising a doctrine.

So here's a case I've been playing with. It's invented, so don't worry about whether it's historically plausible — worry about whether it's a fair test.

The city of Sykaminon, after a bad harvest, decrees two things. First: every contract, fine, tax and posted price-list is to be reckoned in a unit called the chreia-unit, fixed by statute to a basket of necessaries (so much grain, oil, wine, firewood, a cloak). The basket is fixed by decree for twenty years and no coin denominated in chreia-units is ever struck — you cannot pay with one. Payment is made in silver staters, at a rate against the chreia-unit posted daily in the agora. Second: a merchant, Kleinias, lends 100 chreia-units, repayable as 110 chreia-units in a year, settled in silver at whatever the posted rate is on the day of repayment. Over that year the posted rate of the unit in silver happens to rise by exactly 10%.

The passages they get on the handout are these (standard English translation, Bekker 1133a–1133b):

(A) "Hence all commodities exchanged must be able to be compared in some way. It is to meet this requirement that men have introduced money; money constitutes in a manner a middle term, for it is a measure of all things, and so of their superior or inferior value, that is to say, how many shoes are equivalent to a house or to a given quantity of food. [...] It is therefore necessary that all commodities shall be measured by some one standard, as was said before. And this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together, since if men cease to have wants or if their wants alter, exchange will go on no longer, or will be on different lines. But demand has come to be conventionally represented by money; this is why money is called nomisma (customary currency), because it does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will."

(B) "Now money serves us as a guarantee of exchange in the future: supposing we need nothing at the moment, it ensures that exchange shall be possible when a need arises, for it meets the requirement of something we can produce in payment so as to obtain the thing we need. Money, it is true, is liable to the same fluctuation of demand as other commodities, for its purchasing power varies at different times; but it tends to be comparatively constant. Hence the proper thing is for all commodities to have their prices fixed; this will ensure that exchange, and consequently association, shall always be possible. Money then serves as a measure which makes things commensurable and so reduces them to equality. If there were no exchange there would be no association, and there can be no exchange without equality, and no equality without commensurability. Though therefore it is impossible for things so different to become commensurable in the strict sense, our demand furnishes a sufficiently accurate common measure for practical purposes. [...] There must therefore be some one standard, and this accepted by agreement (which is why it is called nomisma, customary currency); for such a standard makes all things commensurable, since all things can be measured by money."

What I want the question to make them do, in three moves:

(a) Say which of the jobs money does in these passages survives being detached from the thing that actually circulates — measuring, so that proportionate requital by diagonal conjunction can be stated (builder to shoemaker as shoes to house), versus being something you can hold and "produce in payment" later. If only one survives the split, say whether Aristotle's account of reciprocal justice can do without the other one, and why.

(b) Decide whether Sykaminon's chreia-unit is a better or a worse representative of demand than silver is, in Aristotle's own terms. He says the real standard is demand and money merely represents it by agreement; the basket tries to fix by law what he says is fixed by what people actually want. But wants alter, and he says exchange then goes "on no longer, or on different lines" — and the basket cannot alter for twenty years. Also worth their noticing: he admits commensurability here is only "sufficiently accurate for practical purposes," and he floats the thought that prices ought to be fixed. Does the reform give him what he asks for, or does it make reciprocal justice more conventional than he can allow?

(c) Work out whether Kleinias's loan is tokos by the standards of the Politics argument against interest (money meant for exchange, breeding money from money, gain coming from the money itself rather than from what it procures). They have to separate the increment in chreia-units from the increment in silver: if the 10% in units just tracks the 10% move in the posted rate, Kleinias gets back no more silver than he lent, and then it's not obvious which of Aristotle's complaints still bites. Which complaint tracks the denomination and which tracks what the contract actually does?
