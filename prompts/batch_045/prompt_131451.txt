Hi — let me set the scene, because I think the constraints are what make this hard.

I retired four years ago after a career in field biology (fisheries ecology, mostly Pacific salmon, with an earlier stint in ornithology), and since then I've been sitting in on a philosophy of biology reading group at the university in town. That's how I ended up re-reading the animal signalling literature I thought I already understood, and getting annoyed with it. One of the people in the group claims "costly signal" is unfalsifiable as it's used in most papers, and I've been defending the Zahavi-flavoured version without being able to say exactly what would settle it.

Here's the concrete thing I need. I volunteer as the "expert advisor" on a citizen-science archive: camera-trap video, trail-cam audio, and phone clips sent in by volunteers. We get about two thousand clips a season. Three undergraduates do a first-pass coding over the summer with maybe twenty minutes of training each, I sign off on anything they can't resolve, and the annotated set goes on a public repository that a couple of other groups have already started pulling from. So the codebook matters more than I expected when I said yes.

What I want from you is the coding procedure: a decision tree in words that an undergraduate can run in two or three minutes per clip, on observational footage only — we can't do experiments, we can't manipulate anything, and in maybe half the clips there's no receiver in frame at all. The tags I currently have are: not-a-signal (a cue, or something no one responds to), signal-reliability-unsecured (a convention anybody could fake cheaply), index (reliable because physics won't allow otherwise), costly-or-handicap, and uncodeable. Rework the tag set if it needs reworking.

The distinctions I want the procedure to honour, because I think they're where the arguments actually live:

- What counts as a signal at all versus a cue that happens to be informative — given that we often can't see whether any receiver responded.
- Cost has to be marginal and differential (the next unit of signal has to cost a low-quality individual more than a high-quality one), not just "expensive in absolute terms." A lot of what I read seems to forget this.
- A distinction I've started making on my own and want stress-tested: some handicaps are paid once, when the ornament or the body part grows, so that by the time you see the display the cost is already sunk and can't be dialled up or down in the moment; others are paid each time the signal is given, so the signaller can vary it right now. It seems to me only the second kind can do the work the formal models need it to do, and I'd like the procedure to keep these apart if that's right.
- Cost that exists only because of what receivers do back — a display is costly because rivals punish it or predators notice it — which means if we can't establish who was watching, we can't establish the cost.
- Cost borne by somebody other than the signaller: a juvenile whose condition was set by how much its parents provisioned it, say, where the expenditure was somebody else's.
- Index versus handicap: reliability that comes from physical constraint versus reliability that comes from price. I want these to come out as different tags, and I want to know what to do when a signal is both.
- And the bit I suspect no procedure can fix: cost and benefit measured in different currencies, and how you'd convert between them.

For each step I want the question, what counts as a sufficient yes given only the footage we have, where the tree returns a hard no, where it returns uncodeable-and-here's-the-experiment-you'd-need (I would much rather hand back an honest blank than a confident wrong tag), and where it hands the decision to me because it's a call I have to make once for the codebook rather than a fact about the clip.

Then run it on three clips from the archive:

1. A gazelle stotting in front of a camera, no predator anywhere in frame.
2. Nestling birds begging, nest-cam audio plus video — the case everyone treats as the model of honest signalling. I have a feeling the procedure is going to give me an answer here that I won't like, and I'd rather you tell me straight than soften it.
3. A male frog's advertisement call, where the pitch tracks his body size because a small animal physically can't produce a low one — but the calling is also energetically expensive and attracts predators, so I can't tell whether I'm looking at an index or a handicap or a bit of both.

Finally: which of the distinctions above are load-bearing for coders at this training level and which I should drop as definately too fine-grained, and where the procedure will misfire in a way I should expect. I'd rather it misfire predictably than quietly. And don't give me citations or a reading list — I don't need them, and I've no library access anymore anyway.
