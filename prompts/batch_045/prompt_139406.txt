I am writing a novel — set in the present, with a long flashback strand — whose protagonist is a sculptor blind since infancy who has her sight restored by surgery in her late thirties, and in which a philosopher and a neuroscientist spend a good part of the book arguing about her. I have been reading my way into Molyneux's question so that the argument is not a strawman, and what I find is a dispute that seems not to have moved in three centuries: the question as originally put — the blind man made to see, the cube and the sphere, can he tell which is which by sight alone, without touching them — was answered in the negative early, and the surgical cases that have been reported on and off ever since seem mostly to go the same way, and still both sides argue at something close to full strength, and I cannot tell whether anyone takes anything to have been settled. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I should say at the outset that I am writing in a cottage with no library: please give me the argument itself, not a reading list, not citations, not a survey of who said what in which decade.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: three questions are running under one heading. First, whether the blind person already possesses the relevant spatial or geometrical concepts — whether cube-as-touched and cube-as-seen are one concept with two routes of application, or two concepts that happen to share a name. Second, what recognition requires: correct sorting and naming, or an experienced sameness, the small shock of familiarity, the oh-it-is-that-again, which correct sorting might survive without. Third, whether sight and touch deliver their information in a shared, amodal spatial format or in formats different enough that any transfer has to be learned from scratch. My suspicion is that these are answered separately and that people cite an answer to one as though it settled the others — but three cases I have invented for the book make me doubt my own story. In one, my sculptor names cube and sphere correctly the first time she is asked, yet says the sight is entirely unfamiliar, that she cannot say how she knew, and that what she sees resembles nothing she remembers touching; she can name, but she cannot match a seen cube to a felt one among distractors. In another, she fails on the first day and succeeds on the fourth: if the question is about the first moment of sight, and any visual experience at all disqualifies the answer, then the word immediate is doing work no one defines. In a third, she has used a camera-driven tactile display for years before surgery, so that she has in some sense seen shapes with her skin — and whether that counts as sight, as touch, or as a third thing seems to decide the answer without any experiment being run, which looks to me like the mark of a verbal dispute masquerading as an empirical one.

So: tell me what this stalemate actually is, supporting the diagnosis with what each side really says rather than by listing positions, and be explicit about which it is — a verbal disagreement about the terms; a hidden empirical question we have the means to settle and have simply failed to settle cleanly, with the choice of task or of what counts as success doing the real work; a substantive conceptual disagreement about innateness or about what a concept is; several questions under one name, as I have guessed; or an ill-posed question that ought to be split and abandoned. Where is my three-question hypothesis wrong, and what is the strongest case for a rival diagnosis — in particular, is the fact that different tests give different verdicts a sign of a real empirical question or a sign that success has been defined differently at each step? Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a differently designed test, a prior agreement about what would count as recognition, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Finally, since I have to stage it: if two people who are each right about something were to disagree here, what exactly would they be disagreeing about? Close with a single last line of the form \answer{...}, containing one of the labels verbal, hidden empirical question, substantive conceptual, different questions under one heading, ill-posed — or a short phrase of your own inside the same brackets if none of them fits.
