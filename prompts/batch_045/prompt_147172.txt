Posting this after yet another dinner-table argument. A friend defends Jefferson (and Columbus, and Churchill) with the same move every time: he was a man of his time, you can't judge him by today's standards — and then uses that to close the question of whether the guy should be blamed, or whether the school named after him should keep its name. I want to know what that sentence could actually be claiming, because I suspect one reading is doing all the work and it isn't the one he'd say out loud.

The distinction I keep comming back to, tell me if it's the right one: "of his time" could mean statistically typical — most educated men around him thought and acted more or less like this — or it could mean something modal: no better option was really available to him, given what he could have known and done. Those come apart badly, since there were contemporaries who said the opposite and acted on it, so a view can be typical without being the only thing on offer. But I'm not sure typicality was ever meant to excuse by itself, so maybe the real readings are about who the reference class is (his peers, or the dissenters), what "can't" means (couldn't have known better? couldn't have acted otherwise? couldn't have without giving up something that mattered to him), which stretch of a sixty-year life we're even talking about, and whether the sentence is about the agent at all or about us — about what blaming and commemmorating are actually for.

Can you lay the readings out as a menu, from trivial to substantive, and for each say whether it's plausible and whether it actually gets him from "man of his time" to "so don't blame him, and keep the name"? I'd also like you to mark which readings defenders retreat to when pushed, as opposed to the easy ones critics attack. Be honest about how confident you are in each verdict, and if my typical/available distinction is muddled or the slogan is doing no real work here, just say that plainly instead of politely giving me six readings.
