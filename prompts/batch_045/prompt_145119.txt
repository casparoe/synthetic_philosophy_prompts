I'm teaching a graduate seminar on grounding and fundamentality this term, and the final session keeps running aground on a confusion I'd rather fix with a poem than with another handout of definitions. My students persistently run together two things I've taken to calling *bedrock* and *hub*: a fact's being ungrounded — nothing explains it, the explanatory buck stops there — and its being load-bearing, the rung through which every chain in the neighbourhood passes even though it is itself grounded by something else. The two come apart constantly in the toy structures we draw on the whiteboard, and I want the poem to hold them apart as well.

So: a sestina — six sestets with the standard retrograde rotation of end-words (6-1-5-2-4-3), then the three-line envoi — in which the six end-words are six rungs of a single grounding chain, each grounding the next. I'd suggest something like choice, nerve, cell, bond, atom, field, but choose your own if those don't sing, provided they really form a chain and aren't just a list of successively smaller items. Two features of the form should be doing philosophical work. First, the rotation scrambles the order of the rungs from stanza to stanza while the dependence structure stays exactly the same, and I want that scrambling to feel like a discovery rather than a gimmick: grounding is not causation and not temporal priority, so re-ordering the chain costs nothing, and no rung should become more or less derivative for landing first or last in a given stanza. Watch accordingly that the dependence never gets described as production or as history — no sparking, no giving rise, no coming before. Second, because every end-word appears in every stanza, each of the six is a hub of the poem's texture, ubiquitous and structurally indispensable; mere ubiquity therefore cannot be the mark of fundamentality, and the poem will have to find some other way of marking which rung, if any, is bedrock. That is what the envoi is for: I'd like the two words in its final line to be the pairing that shows where explanation stops. Somewhere in the body of the poem I also want one line that gets the nothing-over-and-above relation right — the derivative rung is nothing over and above its grounds and yet not identical to them.

Commit to a definite answer about whether the chain terminates at all: if it does, say in what; if it doesn't, make the failure to close into the substance of the poem rather than an elegant shrug. Don't let it resolve into a vision of the all being one, and don't reach for the turtles. If you'd rather put the cosmos at the bottom than the smallest thing, that option is open to you. Then add a short prose note of 150–200 words saying which rung the poem treats as bedrock — or that it treats none as bedrock — and flagging where the poem leans on a contested reading: transitivity, the coherence of infinite descent, the claim that a whole is prior to its parts. If you think my bedrock/hub distinction collapses under pressure, say so in the note and build the collapse into the poem; but keep the verse itself committed rather than hedged.
