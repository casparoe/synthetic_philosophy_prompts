I'm writing a paper for my political philosophy seminar on Nozick's entitlement theory, and I want to build the essay around the dispute over the hoard in *The Hobbit* (the novel, not the films), since it gives me four competing claims in a single scene. As I remember the sequence: the treasure was made and held by the dwarves, Smaug took it by force and held it for generations, Bard kills the dragon and claims a share for Lake-town both as compensation for the destruction Smaug caused and as heir of Girion, Thranduil's elves want a share on no very clear ground, Thorin claims the whole thing by inheritance and refuses to negotiate, and Bilbo — who has only the fourteenth share promised him in his contract — hands the Arkenstone, which he found and which Thorin values above everything else, to Bard and Thranduil to force a settlement and avert a battle.

What I want is a reading of that sequence in Nozick's terms: which of the three principles each party is actually invoking, and where their invocations are confused or run together.

Two things I've been chewing on and would like taken seriously. First, the Wilt Chamberlain argument gets much of its force from the picture of thousands of small, willing transfers, each individually unimpeachable, adding up to something no patterned principle can permit. The Hobbit is structurally the opposite case: one enormous holding acquired and reacquired by conquest, with almost no consensual transfers except Bilbo's contract and his gift. Does Nozick's argument in fact depend on the many-small-steps structure, or only on each step being voluntary? If it's the latter, is the novel a fairer test of his view than Chamberlain is, or an unhelpfully easy one?

Second, I've been toying with a continuously applied proviso: instead of checking Locke's condition that appropriators leave enough and as good for others only at the moment of acquisition, check it at every moment the holding is held, so that a holding can become unjust through nobody's wrongdoing — a dragon, a ruined town, a change in what others can do — and generate a rectification claim with no prior violation behind it. Read that way Bard's claim isn't compensation for a theft no living person committed, but a proviso-based claim about the present condition of Lake-town. Can entitlement theory make any sense of that, or does it collapse into the need-based patterned claim Nozick rejects? If it collapses, is that a defect in the theory or in my proviso?

Finally, where does the book resist the reading? Tolkien's treatment of the dragon-sickness — Thorin's deterioration once he holds the hoard, the suggestion that the gold is in some way bad for whoever holds it, not merely badly acquired — looks like a complaint about holdings that has no slot in a theory of holdings in justice. Is there any way to reconstruct it as a claim about justice, or should my paper end by conceding that the part of the novel it most wants to take seriously is precisely the part the theory cannot see? I'd rather have a reading that is honest in both directions than one that turns the scenes into illustrations.
