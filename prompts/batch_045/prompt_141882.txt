I'm a PhD student in cognitive science finishing a dissertation on categorical perception, and I'm leading the final session of a small reading group on Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project — about twelve people, ninety minutes, mostly philosophy and linguistics graduate students. The session is built around the idea the project was meant to test: that a program able to design an entire alphabet in one coherent style would have to treat a letter not as a fixed template but as a category with a centre and a fuzzy perimeter, whose parts fill roles defined partly by each other and partly by contrast with the rest of the alphabet, so that an 'a' is what it is by not being an 'o', a 'd', a 'g', or a 'q', and by surviving the stretching, thickening, and cropping of its stem, bowl, and terminal. I want to close the session by reading a poem rather than summarizing, and I'd like you to write it.

I've tried drafting it myself twice and both versions failed in opposite directions: one turned into a riddle whose answer is the word identity, the other into a versified abstract of the theory with a letterform standing in for a thesis. What I want instead is a short poem I can read aloud in under two minutes: exactly five numbered stanzas of four lines each, twenty lines, nothing before the first stanza and nothing after the last, no title, no notes, no stage directions. Each stanza should show the same letter in a different material setting — a child's first pencilled attempt, something painted or engraved, something typed, something scrawled at speed — and each should close with the identical one-line refrain, the same words in the same typography every time, which should mean something slightly different in each mouth it comes out of.

The constraints that matter: the fifth stanza should be the case that breaks the spell — the same shape somewhere it is not an 'a' at all, a Cyrillic 'а' or an IPA 'ɑ' or the single-storey form a reader can't decide is a typeface choice or a different letter — so that the unchanged refrain suddenly has to do work it hasn't had to do yet. A specialist should be able to tell that the poem is committed to the role-and-contrast picture and not to a stored template, and that it knows the difference between the shape, the letter, and the token; a non-specialist should just hear a poem about a letter. Avoid the project's own vocabulary (no essence, no platonic, no grid), don't explain anything, and don't let the last stanza resolve into a moral.
