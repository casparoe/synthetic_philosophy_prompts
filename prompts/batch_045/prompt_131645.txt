I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and next term I'm co-teaching a short upper-level seminar that pairs Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein on rule-following with a few films; we're doing Nolan's Memento in the second week, and I want to get my own reading straight before I stand up in front of twenty students. My co-instructor thinks the film is basically an illustration of the private language argument — man without a community cannot follow a rule, therefore he cannot mean anything — and I think that's both too quick and too tidy. The distinction I keep pushing is between two failures Leonard suffers: a mnemonic failure, in which he cannot retrieve which rule or which intention he adopted, and a constitutive failure, in which there is no fact of the matter about which rule he adopted in the first place. Kripke's skeptic is claiming the second, and I want to know how much of the film's drama actually depends on the second rather than the first, and how much of its horror comes from our not being able to tell them apart from the inside. I'd also like you to take seriously a point one of my undergraduates made last year: that Leonard's Polaroids, notes and tattoos are an external memory store, so the symbols really are public and stable, and the regress of interpretation Kripke's skeptic relies on shouldn't get any grip. I don't think that's right, but I need a better answer to it than "the symbols still have to be interpreted."

Here's what I'd like you to work with, roughly: the annotations he scrawls on the Polaroids, where the label fact is written over something that is plainly an inference, guess or self-instruction; the Sammy Jenkis story and its revelation as Leonard's own, given that Leonard's former job was literally to adjudicate whether a claimant's condition was genuine, i.e. to act as the community's check on someone else's capacities; Teddy as the only available community, and a corrupt and manipulative one, which seems to me to cut against Kripke's skeptical solution rather than for it, since that solution needs a functioning practice of agreement and correction and Leonard's only interlocutor is someone with an interest in his not being corrected; the closing decision to have information tattooed that will send him after a new target, together with the closing voice-over about believing in a world outside his own mind and lying to himself to be happy; and the film's own reverse-chronological form, which puts us, the viewers, in the position of inferring the rule governing the sequence and then being forced to revise it. One thing I want handled carefully: the indeterminacy of the name John G., under which several people fit and which Teddy and Leonard use differently, is not obviously the same phenomenon as what the skeptic alleges for the invented rival function Kripke calls quus, and I suspect the film quietly runs the two together.

So: give me a reading of Memento through the rule-following paradox — what the film dramatizes, what it complicates, and where it resists the reading and an honest critic has to say so. Please don't summarize the plot beyond what you need, and don't give me citations or a reading list; I have the secondary literature covered. What I'm short on is a view about whether the film is better used as an illustration of Kripke's Wittgenstein, as an objection to the skeptical solution, or as something that tells against both by showing what happens when the only available community is a liar — and, at the end, whether you think the film adds anything to the debate that the arguments alone don't. Under about 1,200 words, if you can.
