I am a novelist at work on a literary science-fiction manuscript, and I want the speculative cognitive science in it to be robust enough that a reader with some philosophy-of-mind background could not dismiss it as confused hand-waving. The story turns on an alien lineage that appears to have reached something like the fourth of the four kinds of mind often distinguished in philosophy and cognitive science: Darwinian creatures, shaped mainly by genetic evolution; Skinnerian creatures, whose behavior can be reshaped within a lifetime by reinforcement and conditioning; Popperian creatures, which can internally preselect among candidate actions or models so that unsuccessful hypotheses can be discarded before they are acted out; and Gregorian creatures, which offload part of this generate-and-test process into cultural tools, especially public symbols and language, thereby importing cognitive labor accumulated by earlier generations. My imagined species look Gregorian: they build and inhabit a dense public symbolic environment, use external markers to stabilize norms, and transmit elaborated cognitive tools across generations. Yet their individual organisms seem capable of little more than a crude Skinnerian capacity for trial-and-error learning, with scant evidence of the inner, Popperian simulation that in the standard story seems to come first. To make matters harder, the reinforcement may operate at the level of the colony or lineage rather than the individual, because the units that survive and reproduce are not the same as the units that do the learning.

What I need is not another summary of the taxonomy but a specification. Please write the desiderata that any adequate theory of the relations among these kinds of mind—and of the possible transitions, bypasses, or degenerate cases between them—must meet to be good enough for serious speculative worldbuilding. Give me the requirements list: the phenomena such a theory must explain, the distinctions it must respect, and the results or constraints it cannot contradict, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement. I have in mind phenomena such as how design can accumulate without foresight; how a merely conditioned organism could come to have, or appear to have, internal models; how external symbols could scaffold, precede, or substitute for individual preselection; and how the units of selection, the units of learning, and the units of cultural transmission can come apart. The distinctions I do not want collapsed include individual versus colony-level learning, merely having a tool versus being a Gregorian creature, internal simulation versus external scaffolding, selection by consequences versus representation of consequences, and genetic, neural, and cultural inheritance. The constraints should rule out any theory that silently smuggles in intentionality, foresight, or teleology, or that treats symbols as non-physical. For each desideratum, mark whether it is non-negotiable for conceptual adequacy or merely a preference of one approach. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single requirement that would do the most to exclude the tempting simplistic stories, such as the claim that each kind is just more conditioning plus a larger brain, or the claim that each new kind is a miraculously added module.

Then apply the list to my case, because my real question is whether the aliens can be made coherent at all. I am not asking for a reading list, citations, or scholarly apparatus; I want the conceptual specification and its implications. Briefly score two stylized positions I have been considering: the Uniformity Thesis, which holds that the four kinds are fully continuous and that my aliens are simply Skinnerian conditioning scaled up within a shared cultural environment; and the Saltation Thesis, which holds that Popperian inner selection and Gregorian symbolism require distinct representational capacities that cannot be built from conditioning alone, so the aliens must either secretly be Popperian individuals or have a symbolism that is really a collective innate structure. More important than the scores, though, is what the desiderata imply. If they force the conclusion that a Gregorian creature must rest on prior Popperian individuals, tell me which requirement does so and whether that requirement is negotiable. If they instead leave room for cultural tools to bootstrap Popperian-style internal selection from a merely Skinnerian, possibly colony-level base, say which desideratum would have to be relaxed and what the cost would be. The deliverable I want is the criteria and what they entail for the case, not a survey of existing views.
