I build eval infrastructure at a small AI lab, and for the last year I've been the person who actually runs the debate experiments — the setup from the 2018 paper where two agents take turns making short statements for and against an answer and a judge picks the winner. The argument about whether this thing works has completely stalled in my corner of the world, and I can't tell anymore whether it's a real disagreement or people talking past each other.

Here's how it goes. One camp says the whole point is the asymmetry: it is much cheaper to point at a specific broken step in someone else's argument than to build a lie with no broken steps, so in equilibrium the honest debater wins, and you get a human overseeing systems smarter than herself. The other camp says the judge is the bottleneck, not the debaters — the game is zero-sum, so both sides are optimizing for looking right to a specific fallible judge, the honest side can be buried in obfuscated or just long-winded arguments, there are equilibria where everyone grandstands, and what the judge is actually measuring is fluency. Both camps have now been saying this to each other for years.

My own runs with current models are what got me stuck. Debate reliably helps on questions where the truth bottoms out in something short and checkable — a unit conversion, a lookup, a step of arithmetic where you can point at the exact line. It does nothing or actively hurts on questions where the right answer depends on weighting considerations, which is exactly the regime the scalability argument is supposed to cover. And if I hold the content fixed and just swap in a more fluent model on one side, the judge flips. That smells like the mechanism is persuasion, not verification, but I definately don't trust my own setup enough to conclude that.

So here's the modification I keep pushing in design reviews, and I want to know if it's even aimed at the right target. Instead of a binary win/loss, make each debater decompose their case into atomic claims, adjudicate each claim separately, and charge a debater a penalty for every claim the opponent successfully refutes — so the cost of lying scales with how much of your case is exposed to refutation, rather than being all-or-nothing on a holistic impression. Call it ClaimLedger. My worry is that it sneaks in the premise under dispute: a judge who can already adjudicate atomic claims is most of the way to not needing the debaters.

What I actually want is a diagnosis. Is this a verbal dispute — people meaning different things by works, oversight, or scalable? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, something about equilibria of debate games with bounded, model-based judges? Are the two camps answering different questions under one name, one about an inference-time safety guarantee and one about a cheap accuracy boost or a training signal? Or is it bedrock disagreement about whether optimization pressure toward winning arguments generalizes? My hypothesis is the different-questions one, but I hold it loosely and I'd rather be told I'm wrong. Then, given whatever you think it is: what would actually count as progress — an experiment design that separates persuasion from accuracy, a formal result about which equilibria exist once the judge is not ideal, a reframing of the question — or is this a case where no progress should be expected, and why. I don't need citations or a reading list, I've read enough of the discourse to know it loops; I need someone to tell me what kind of loop it is.
