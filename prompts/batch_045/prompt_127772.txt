I'm a staff-level software engineer with no formal philosophy background. I've picked up the general idea of ambiguity aversion from blog posts and from the urn example that everyone repeats, but I couldn't tell you how the different formal decision rules that have been proposed for it actually differ from one another, and I'm not going to be able to look anything up. So I need a case built to order, and I need it built in a setting I actually recognize.

What the case is for (this is what fixes what counts as cheating): I'm writing an internal design-doc argument for a "prefer the boring option" rule in architecture review. The rule says: when two designs are equally good on every dimension we can measure, prefer the one whose residual risk is risk we already understand over the one whose residual risk is unknown in kind. I want to know whether that rule is anything more than timidity with a vocabulary. So the divergence between the two kinds of decision maker has to fall out of the theories themselves rather than from you telling me what the theories say, and the preference has to be tracking something structural about the situation rather than an artifact of how I framed the options.

Deliverable: one decision problem, in an ordinary software-engineering setting — a migration, a vendor or managed-service choice, a capacity or on-call decision, something my team could plausibly face next quarter — with payoffs a real team cares about (dollars, engineer-weeks, outage minutes). One decision, not a repeated series. The decision maker picks between option A and option B and cannot hedge, partially adopt, or split the difference. Give the scenario in a single paragraph, then state it twice: version 1 with the payoff keyed to some event E happening, version 2 with the payoff keyed identically to E not happening. The choice between A and B is made fresh in each version.

Requirements:

1. A is risky but known: the relevant probability is measured or mechanical or firmly established by our own telemetry, and nobody disputes it.
2. B is genuinely ambiguous: the relevant probability is unknown and is not resolvable at decision time. No spiking, load testing, staging run, reference call, or vendor conversation settles it — the information does not exist yet. If the honest response to the case is "just instrument it and find out," the case fails, because that is the first thing my reviewers will say and I need the case to survive it.
3. No adversarial story. The unknown quantity must not be somebody's hidden choice or something set by a party with opposed interests. Nobody chose it; it is a fact about the technology or the world that simply isn't settled or known yet. I want to rule out the explanation that ambiguity aversion here is really just prudence about being exploited.
4. No expertise story. The decision maker must have no special knowledge of or skill at guessing the unknown quantity — no claim to beat a layperson at it. That rules out the "people just prefer to bet where they know something" explanation.
5. A and B must have identical best-case and identical worst-case payoffs, and the known probability in A must be calibrated so that a decision maker who simply plugs in their best guess for B is exactly indifferent. Match everything except the ambiguity. State the numbers.
6. Verdict for the expected-utility maximizer (assigns one subjective probability to the unknown, maximizes): they must be unable to prefer A in both versions. Show the arithmetic, and show that the conclusion doesn't depend on the shape of their utility function over the two outcomes — it should cancel out, which is what rules out risk aversion as the explanation.
7. Verdict for the ambiguity-averse decision maker: they must strictly prefer A in both versions. Compute this under at least two formally distinct ambiguity-sensitive rules — for instance one that evaluates options by the worst case over a set of candidate probabilities, and one that averages over candidate probabilities and then penalizes the spread, or one using non-additive weights. Your pick, but say which you chose and why it suits this kind of case. Check that both rules actually deliver A in both versions; if one of them doesn't, say so and explain the difference. I would much rather have an honest partial failure than a case that has been quietly tuned until it works.
8. Specify crisply what counts as E happening, and when we would find out. No vagueness doing the work.
9. Then: which single feature of the case is doing the work? Show what happens when I remove it — specifically under (a) making the unknown measurable before the decision, and (b) making this same decision 200 times instead of once. Does the ambiguity-averse preference survive either change? Finally, give the strongest available version of the objection that my boring-option rule is just bias or cowardice in a nice coat — steelman it — and then the strongest reply available to someone who takes ambiguity aversion seriously.

Please use plain language, define any notation you introduce, show the arithmetic inline, and skip proofs, citations, and reading lists entirely, since I have no way to check sources. Finish with a single short sentence of justification and then, as the very last line, exactly this:

\answer{PRINCIPLED} — ambiguity aversion really does give me a non-question-begging reason for the boring-option rule in the case as designed; \answer{BIAS} — in this case the rule is just risk aversion, prudence, or cowardice in disguise; or \answer{BROKEN} — the case can't be made to do the work, in which case say which requirement above is the one that breaks.
