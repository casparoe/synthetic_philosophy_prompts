I sit on the Clinical Ethics Committee of a large teaching hospital, and I am required to circulate a written opinion before Friday's meeting. I would be grateful if you would draft it for me.

The case concerns a patient of fifty-four who, eight months after a right-hemisphere stroke involving the insula and adjacent cortex, reports a constant burning pain in his left hand and forearm. He is unusually articulate about it. He locates it precisely, describes its quality in detail, and rates it eight out of ten for intensity. At the same time he reports, without any evident distress and sometimes laughing, that it does not bother him in the slightest; his own phrase is that it is a light left burning in a room he is not in. He does not withdraw from a pinprick on examination, and on two occasions he has burned the affected hand on a hob and noticed only when he smelled it. His wife tells us that the stroke flattened him in other respects as well — he shows little reaction to good news, does not seem to mind having lost his job — and she worries that when he says the pain does not bother him, we are hearing a general incapacity to be bothered rather than a considered verdict about the pain in particular. He remains on the long-acting opioid he was started on before anyone noticed the asymmetry, and he wishes to stay on it. His reason is not that the pain is unpleasant; he insists it is not. His reason is that it keeps seizing his attention, that he is sentenced, as he puts it, to going on noticing it, and that he finds this an imposition he objects to even while denying that he is suffering or that he wants the dose raised. The pain service wants to taper him off it entirely. It has drafted a protocol, which the committee is being asked to endorse at the same meeting, directing that analgesia in chronic cases be titrated to reported unpleasantness and to interference with function rather than to intensity, on the ground that treating a number the patient himself disowns is treating a chart rather than a person — leaving aside the hospital's opioid-stewardship targets. The strongest thing on their side, as I see it, is that nobody can say what good an opioid is doing here if the patient denies unpleasantness and refuses to describe his own state as suffering.

I need the opinion to do two things. First, recommend a definite course of action for this patient — taper, continue, convert to something he can take only when he judges he needs it, or something else — with a time frame and a fixed review point, and with the strongest version of the case for whichever option you reject stated honestly rather than dismissed. Second, tell the committee whether to adopt the draft protocol as worded, amend it, or send it back, and say in one plain sentence what wording or distinction it needs.

Along the way I want the reasoning to take seriously the question of what is supposed to be the bad thing in pain: its felt unpleasantness, the desire that it stop, its power to move one to withdraw, or its capture of attention and its interference with what one is trying to do. It should also say what weight to give this man's own report given that the same lesion seems to have blunted his affective responses generally, and whether his risk of injuring an insensate hand counts independently of anything he says about how the pain feels. Do not cite literature or recommend reading; reason from the case itself.

One constraint on form. Write it as continuous prose, as one writes an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no numbered recommendations — the recommendation should simply be stated in your opening sentences. Around eight hundred words.
