You are a philosopher of science who has worked seriously on truthlikeness—Popper's problem of what it would take for one false theory to be closer to the truth than another. Before you write, spend some time thinking the problem through on your own and let your first pass be messy; I would rather have a considered list than a tidy one, and if part of your considered view is that the list I am asking for cannot be produced as stated, say that too.

I want the requirements, not a theory. Adequacy is relative to a job, so here is mine. I am not trying to underwrite scientific realism or to show that later theories are closer to the truth than earlier ones. I am trying to rank competing imperfect accounts of a single episode: two eyewitnesses, or two historical summaries, or two encyclopedia entries about the same event, where you have good reason to think each of them is somewhere literally wrong. I want a defensible way of saying that one is closer to the truth than another, and I want to know in advance what I should be demanding of a theory that is supposed to deliver that verdict.

Here are the cases I want any such theory to have something to say about, with the demand I take each one to carry. Set the scene: 43 people were in the hall, and the argument broke out after the vote, not before. (1) One account says forty-one people were present and the argument broke out before the vote; another says between twenty and eighty people were present and there was some kind of argument at some point around the vote. The cautious, uninformative account must not win by default, and the bold one must not win by default either—if a theory can only tell me that hedging pays, or only that boldness pays, it has no interest for me. (2) Take the first account and conjoin to it the statement that Vienna is the capital of Austria. Nothing may be allowed to improve its score merely by adding truths that have nothing to do with the episode; that is the complaint about an overly generous notion of content, and I want to know whether it is a real desideratum or a technical annoyance. (3) This is the case I care most about. Account A says the decision was made because the chair lost patience with the debate; that is true as far as it goes, but it leaves out the budget shortfall, which was the larger cause. Account B mentions both the impatience and the shortfall but misdates the discovery of the shortfall by a day. Does a theory of closeness to the truth have to penalize what is left out, or is silence none of its business? Argue this one; I have gone back and forth. (4) Re-describe the same account in different units or a coextensive vocabulary, and its score had better not shift—or so it seems to me, and I want to know whether that is a genuine requirement or a prejudice of the logical camp. (5) One account gets the order of events right and the numbers wrong; another gets the numbers right and the order wrong. Must the theory rank them, or may it return a verdict of incomparability, and is requiring a total ordering a real desideratum or just tidiness? (6) Whether the notion must apply to things that are not sentences—a timeline, a map, a table of figures. (7) Whether it must be possible for us to have evidence that one account is closer to the truth than another. Someone who thinks the aim of inquiry is solving problems rather than reaching the truth will deny this is a requirement at all; someone who wants the notion to underwrite claims of progress will insist on it. Mark camp-relative requirements as such, and say which camp each belongs to. Finally, the baseline: it must be possible for one false account to be closer to the truth than another. Popper's own definition, in terms of truth content minus falsity content, was shown in the mid-1970s not to deliver this—on it, no false theory comes out closer to the truth than any other, and he is on record as accepting the criticism almost immediately. So the baseline is not trivial, and I want it stated as a requirement with the consideration behind it.

Two more things, and the first is what I most want from you. Tell me which subset of the requirements on your list cannot be jointly satisfied, and what the proof or the case is; if the list itself turns out to be a result, that is worth more to me than any scoring. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories, with your reason for picking that one. My own guess is that it is the anti-hedging requirement from case (1), but I suspect the omission case is where the real damage is done.

Then, briefly, score three candidate approaches against the list and no more. First: add up the true consequences and subtract the false ones, possibly with weights, the way the post-Popperian content and consequence approaches do. Second: take likeness literally, and measure closeness as overall similarity between the way the account says the world is and the way the world is, with possible worlds ordered by their similarity to the actual one. Third: count only those consequences that bear on a fixed set of questions and discard the rest as irrelevant, with relevance fixed by our interests in the episode. Keep the scoring short; the criteria are where I want the thinking.

Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write an email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, no tables. I do not need citations or a bibliography, and I cannot check them, so work from the cases and the arguments rather than from what anyone has printed.
