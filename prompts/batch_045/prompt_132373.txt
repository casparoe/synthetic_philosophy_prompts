The question (20 marks)

A "Janus cosmology" is a model of a universe with a bounce: a spacelike hypersurface of minimum volume at cosmic time t = 0, a contracting branch for t < 0 and an expanding branch for t > 0. Suppose that in such a model the entropy S(t) takes its minimum at t = 0 and increases monotonically with |t| on both sides. Call the direction in which entropy increases, for any observer, that observer's thermodynamic future. Answer all four parts.

(a) In which temporal direction does the thermodynamic future lie for observers situated on the contracting branch? What would such observers count as their past? Would they and we agree about which events are past?

(b) Does the mere coherence of a Janus cosmology show that the direction of time is not an objective feature of the world but only a matter of which branch one occupies — or, as it is sometimes put, a matter of perspective? In answering, distinguish carefully between the claim that the entropy gradient constitutes the arrow of time and the claim that it merely indicates it.

(c) Advocates of such models sometimes claim that they dispense with the need for any special boundary condition: once there is an entropy minimum somewhere, they say, the second law of thermodynamics does the rest, and nothing has to be posited about a beginning. Assess this claim. You should say what explanatory work a low-entropy boundary condition is supposed to do, and then say whether a minimum located in the middle of the history does that work, avoids having to do it, or does something else entirely.

(d) Two positions were discussed in the course. Gradient Reductionism holds that temporal asymmetry consists in the entropy gradient, and that the psychological arrow (memory, records, anticipation) and the arrow of causation are to be explained in terms of it. Causal-Primitive accounts hold that the asymmetry of causation is primitive, and that an entropy gradient is what one should expect given it, rather than the other way round. Can the psychological or the causal arrow come apart from the thermodynamic arrow in a Janus cosmology? What would each of the two positions say about that case, and which of them does the case favour, if either?

Background the students could rely on: the near time-reversal invariance of the microdynamics; the second law treated as statistical rather than dynamical, so that anti-thermodynamic behaviour is improbable rather than impossible; the classical reversibility and recurrence objections to the statistical account and the role of boundary conditions in answering them; the label "Past Hypothesis" for the posit that the universe's early state was a very low-entropy macrostate; and the point that the hot, dense, nearly uniform early universe counts as low-entropy once gravitational degrees of freedom are included in the count, since clumped configurations occupy almost all of the accessible phase space.

The answer

The second law is not a dynamical law in the ordinary sense. Given the near time-reversal invariance of the underlying microdynamics, nothing in the dynamics alone entails that entropy should be higher at one temporal end of a history than at the other; anti-thermodynamic behaviour is not impossible, only overwhelmingly improbable. That is why the classical reversibility and recurrence objections were answered not by altering the dynamics but by treating the second law statistically and adding an assumption about boundary conditions. On our branch, that assumption is the Past Hypothesis: the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy macrostate. It is easy to miss how special this state is. The early universe was hot, dense and very nearly in thermal equilibrium, which sounds like a high-entropy condition; but once gravitational degrees of freedom are counted, a smooth and nearly uniform distribution is exceptionally special, since almost all of the accessible phase space corresponds to clumped configurations. So the smooth early universe has very low entropy, and clumping raises it. The Past Hypothesis is therefore not the near-tautology it is sometimes taken to be.

(a) On the contracting branch entropy increases towards t → −∞, so that direction is the thermodynamic future of observers there, and the bounce lies in their past. We would consequently not disagree with them about which events are past: the bounce is in the past for both of us, and the two arrows point away from it in opposite temporal directions. The two branches are not, however, time-reverses of one another in any sense that would identify them; they are distinct regions of a single history.

(b) It follows that the direction of time in a Janus cosmology is not an objective feature of the world, but only a branch-relative or perspectival one. On each branch the future simply is the direction of increasing entropy, and there is no branch-independent fact about which way time goes. This is what the gradient reductionist should say, and I think it is right: once we see that the arrow is nothing over and above the gradient, the residual question "but which direction is really the future?" stands revealed as empty.

(c) The real appeal of a Janus cosmology is that it appears to remove the need for a special initial condition. If the second law is statistical, then given any hypersurface on which entropy is close to minimal, entropy will increase away from it in both temporal directions; the second law itself then guarantees the gradient. And the bounce is not an initial condition requiring separate explanation, since it lies in the middle of the history rather than at an unexplained beginning. The model has this further virtue: a single low-entropy hypersurface does for both branches, so nothing needs to be said about some remote boundary on the contracting side. The Past Hypothesis, on this way of looking at it, is best understood not as an additional posit but simply as what the second law asserts when it is applied to the universe as a whole. There is no separate boundary condition to be explained; there is only the law.

(d) The psychological arrow cannot come apart from the thermodynamic arrow, because forming a memory or making a record just is an entropy-increasing process: a memory trace is a correlation between a present state and an earlier one, and establishing it requires dissipation. So a Janus observer's memories point back towards the bounce exactly as ours do, and they, like us, remember the low-entropy end of their own branch. The causal arrow cannot come apart either. On the causal-primitive account, the entropy gradient is precisely what explains why causes precede effects — that is the whole point of taking the gradient as basic in the dispute with the reductionists.

In sum: a Janus cosmology is coherent, and it shows that the direction of time is a wholly local, gradient-relative affair. It therefore undercuts the idea that a cosmic boundary condition is needed to explain why time has a direction at all.
