I TA for an upper-level seminar on formal epistemology and decision theory, and I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere for long enough that "don't get Dutch-booked" mostly functions for me as a slogan rather than an argument. So this term I wrote a question designed to get past the slogan: it asks what exactly the bookie is allowed to do, what the converse result really says and how it is related to the main one, and what happens when credences are not sharp. It's a small class, the final is graded by me with a second reader, and I have one answer I'd like an independent read on before I finalize marks. I don't want to bias you either way, so I haven't said what I think of it; I'll only note that it is unusually fluently written for this cohort, and I'm slightly worried that the fluency is doing work the arguments aren't.

The question was:

Let Ω = {ω₁, ..., ωₙ} be a finite set of states and F the algebra of all subsets of Ω. An agent has a credence function cr: F → [0,1]. A bet on E ∈ F at stake s costs cr(E)·s and pays s if E obtains and 0 otherwise, where s may be negative (a negative stake is a bet sold rather than bought). Assume throughout that the agent's utility is linear in money over the stakes in question, that she will take either side of a bet at her own fair price, and that she accepts any finite package of bets each member of which she individually regards as fair or favorable; the bookie may offer her any finite package at those prices. Call a package a sure loss if its total payoff is strictly negative in every state.

(a) [7 points] State the Dutch book theorem as precisely as you can, being explicit about what the bookie may do and about what the agent is assumed to accept. Then say exactly what the theorem is supposed to establish about the rationality of probabilistically incoherent credences, and whether any further, non-mathematical premise is needed to get from the mathematics to that conclusion. Illustrate with the case cr(A) = cr(B) = 0.4 for disjoint A, B, and cr(A ∪ B) = 0.9.

(b) [5 points] State the converse Dutch book theorem, and say exactly how it is related to the result in (a): is it a restatement, a contrapositive, or an independent result that needs its own proof? Prove whatever needs proving. Does it show that probabilistic coherence is sufficient for the rationality of a credence function?

(c) [8 points] Now suppose the agent's doxastic state is imprecise: instead of a single credence function she has a nonempty set S of probability functions on F. For a gamble X: Ω → ℝ, say X is acceptable-permissive iff E_Q[X] ≥ 0 for some Q ∈ S, and acceptable-unanimous iff E_Q[X] ≥ 0 for every Q ∈ S. A sure loss is now a finite set of acceptable gambles whose sum is strictly negative in every state. (i) Under the permissive rule, does the agent avoid sure loss? Give an argument or a counterexample. (ii) Someone claims the permissive rule is fine provided S is convex, and that non-convexity is the source of the trouble. Assess this claim. (iii) Under the unanimous rule, is sure loss avoided, and does convexity of S matter there?

The answer I want you to grade reads as follows:

(a) The theorem says: if cr violates any of the probability axioms — non-negativity, cr(Ω) = 1, or finite additivity — then there is a finite package of bets, each offered at the agent's own fair prices and each available with either sign of stake, whose total payoff is strictly negative in every state. For the example: have her buy a unit-stake bet on A ∪ B at 0.9 and sell unit-stake bets on A and on B at 0.4 each. In a state in A the payoff is 0.1 − 0.6 + 0.4 = −0.1; in a state in B it is 0.1 + 0.4 − 0.6 = −0.1; outside A ∪ B it is −0.9 + 0.4 + 0.4 = −0.1. So she loses 0.1 whatever happens.

Three assumptions are doing the work: that the agent takes either side of any bet at her fair price and at any stake; that she accepts a package if she accepts each of its members; and that utility is linear in money over the relevant stakes, so that her fair price really does track her credence. What the theorem delivers on its own is exploitability, not irrationality: there is a package she will judge acceptable and which cannot but lose. To conclude that incoherent credences are irrational we need a further, non-mathematical premise — that accepting a guaranteed loss, one you can see is guaranteed, is a defect in your reasoning rather than just bad luck about which offers came your way. This is also why the prudential or long-run version of the argument, on which the incoherent agent who keeps betting at her own prices gets drained, is not the same argument: it needs extra assumptions about which bets the bookie gets to offer and about the agent's continuing to accept them, whereas the strict version concerns a single package at a single time.

(b) The converse theorem follows straight from (a) by contraposition: (a) tells us that incoherence implies bookability, hence that not being bookable implies coherence, i.e. that a coherent cr admits no Dutch book. One can also see it directly: if cr is a probability function then every bet at the agent's fair prices has expectation zero by her own lights, so any finite package has expectation zero, and nothing with expectation zero can be strictly negative in every state. So coherence is necessary and sufficient for immunity from sure loss. It does not follow that coherence is sufficient for rationality, since coherence is only a structural constraint on the credence function taken as a whole: a coherent agent can be certain of something absurd, or can simply ignore her evidence. The Dutch book argument can only ever criticise the structure of a system of credences, never its relation to the evidence.

(c) (i) Under the permissive rule the agent can be booked as soon as S contains two distinct functions. Take Ω = {ω₁, ω₂}, Q₁ = (0.9, 0.1), Q₂ = (0.1, 0.9), S = {Q₁, Q₂}. The gamble X = (1, −7) has E_{Q₁}[X] = 0.9 − 0.7 = 0.2 ≥ 0, so it is acceptable; the gamble Y = (−7, 1) has E_{Q₂}[Y] = 0.2 ≥ 0, so it too is acceptable; but X + Y = (−6, −6), a sure loss. So the permissive rule is not safe.

(ii) The convexity diagnosis is right: convexity is exactly what is needed. Suppose S is convex and every member is a probability function. Take any finite set X₁, …, Xₙ of acceptable gambles and, for each i, a witness Qᵢ ∈ S with E_{Qᵢ}[Xᵢ] ≥ 0. By convexity the average Q̄ = (1/n)Σᵢ Qᵢ belongs to S, and by linearity of expectation E_{Q̄}[Σᵢ Xᵢ] = (1/n) Σᵢ E_{Qᵢ}[Xᵢ] ≥ 0, so the sum cannot be negative in every state. Non-convexity is precisely what breaks the argument, since the average of the witnesses may then lie outside S; and convexity is necessary as well, because a non-convex S is incoherent in just the way a non-additive single credence function is — it cannot be recovered as the set of expectations of any one probability measure. So the permissive rule is safe exactly when S is convex.

(iii) The same diagnosis goes through for the unanimous rule. Sure loss is avoided only if S is convex, by the same averaging argument: a gamble acceptable under every member has non-negative expectation under the mixture, and the mixture is in S only if S is convex. Given convexity, no finite package of unanimously acceptable gambles can sum to something everywhere negative. Convexity is thus doing the real work in both cases — it is the analogue, for an imprecise credence state, of additivity for a sharp one.
