The background. I have a friend, an old university friend who now works in machine learning, and we have had the same argument four or five times over about six years, most recently for three hours after a dinner. It always ends in the same place: he repeats his move, I repeat mine, and we agree to disagree. That reproducibility is what I want you to explain.

The dispute, stated concretely. The no-free-lunch theorems, as he states them and as I am happy to grant them for the sake of the argument — please do not spend your answer correcting the formalism. For any two search or optimisation algorithms, their expected performance is identical when averaged uniformly over the set of all possible objective functions: any advantage one has on one class of problems is exactly cancelled by a corresponding disadvantage elsewhere, so no algorithm is universally better than random search. And there is an analogous result for supervised learning: averaged uniformly over all possible input–output distributions, no predictor beats any other on points outside its training sample. His reading is that this is Hume's problem of induction turned into a theorem. On his view: no rule of inference has any in-principle advantage over its rivals; the only thing that breaks the tie is a prior assumption about what the world is like; and that assumption is precisely the thing Hume argued could not be justified without circularity. Induction is not refuted, it is exhibited as groundless, and now we have a proof of the groundlessness rather than a philosophical intuition about it.

My reading is that the theorems are about a uniform average over all possible worlds, and that the overwhelmingly large majority of the things being averaged over are pure noise — patternless, algorithmically incompressible, with nothing to find. Averaging uniformly over those tells you nothing about our situation that you did not put into the choice of measure. Treating _no assumption_ as _uniform weight over every possibility_ is itself a very strong assumption, and on my view the interesting question is untouched by the theorem: is there some assumption weak enough that it does not simply restate the conclusion, but strong enough to get induction off the ground? He replies that I am changing the subject by demanding a non-uniform measure. I reply that he is sliding between _no algorithm is best everywhere_ and _nothing justifies induction_.

Late in the last argument we arrived at a third position that neither of us could place. Call it the learnable-worlds restriction: give up on all possible worlds, restrict attention to worlds in which some agent could in principle learn — worlds that are not adversarial, not algorithmically random — and ask whether there is a free lunch _within that class_. He says yes, and that this shows the optimism of the sciences can be given a formal footing. I say the restriction is the uniformity principle smuggled back in, since assuming that the world is learnable is exactly what was supposed to be shown. He says it is not an assumption at all but the precondition of there being a question to ask. We went round on that for half an hour.

Two further things we disagree about, which may matter more than the above. First, what would count as settling it. He says a formal result would: exhibit a privileged measure over possible worlds — he suggested one that weights worlds by their simplicity, so structured worlds dominate the average — and show that some algorithms dominate under it, and that is a free lunch. I said that would settle nothing Humean, partly because a simplicity-weighted measure just is an inductive bias relocated one level up, and partly because _it does better on average in worlds weighted this way_ is a claim about reliability, not a justification. He said that if I demand justification rather than reliability I have changed the subject; I said that if he accepts only reliability he has abandoned the problem rather than solved it. Second, we disagree about which facts are even data: he treats the predictive success of the sciences and of modern ML as irrelevant, on the grounds that citing it is induction vouching for induction; I treat it as the one datum any account has to accommodate, and he treats that as the datum being question-beggingly described.

My hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that this is several disagreements stacked under one name: a verbal one about whether _induction_ names a rule of inference or a social-epistemic practice; a substantive one about whether epistemology should want justification or reliability; and a hidden formal question about whether there is any privileged measure over possible worlds, or whether the choice of measure is itself an inductive assumption and the regress is real. I may well be wrong: it may be a single bedrock disagreement about what _no assumption_ could possibly mean, or it may be close to a pseudo-dispute, if both of us already concede that some bias is unavoidable and are only arguing about whether to call the result _justified_.
