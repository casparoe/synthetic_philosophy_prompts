I'm a novelist and the book I'm stuck on is set maybe twenty years from now, during a crisis where a submarine commander loses contact with his government and has to decide on his own whether to fire. I want the deterrence logic to be right enough that nobody who knows the literature throws the book across the room, and its been nagging at me that I don't actually understand it. So: read Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove through the philosophy of nuclear deterrence and brinkmanship — and Fail-Safe too if you know it, because I keep flip-flopping on which one is closer to my problem. Dont give me plot summary, I've seen them both a lot of times.

Start with the Doomsday Machine. Here's what I think I understand: a deterrent threat has to be one you'd be irrational to carry out, so credibility gets bought by visibly giving up control — leaving something to chance, throwing away the steering wheel in a game of chicken, making your own irrationality the thing the other side has to reckon with. A fully automatic retaliator looks like a perfect solution to that: no bluffing, the retaliation is mechanically certain. But the film's joke is that the machine only deters if the other side knows it's there, and the Soviet ambassador is unveiling it like a surprise party. Is that a real point — that a threat's causal power lives in the adversary's beliefs, so an uncommunicated doomsday machine is just a very expensive consequence, like a landmine — or is it only a plot gag? And where does that leave my submarine problem? I've started calling it the automaticity trap: the more perfectly you commit, the more you turn a threat into a fixed consequence, and a consequence can't be bargained with, can't be stood down after a false alarm, and doesn't care whether the person who installed it is still alive. Is that a genuine dilemma that shows up in the theory, or have I reinvented something that already has a name?

Second thing. The film is full of people competently managing a catastrophe they can't actually stop — the war-room meeting, the President on the phone trying to talk the Soviet chairman down, the crew working the bomb. I want you to say honestly whether this is a film about brinkmanship at all — competition in risk-taking, each side trying to look slightly more reckless than the other — or whether it's really about accident, procedure, and bureaucracy, and brinkmanship is just the wallpaper. I ask because my novel is about accident, not about two sides staring each other down, and I'm starting to suspect deterrence theory has almost nothing to say to me.

Third: the film is from 1964, before anybody had modelled the climatic effects of a large exchange — the nuclear winter stuff that started appearing in the early eighties. Does that retroactively break the logic the film is trading on? If carrying out your threat would kill you too, by way of the climate, then in what sense is it still a threat you're making, rather than a fact about the world you're both stuck with?

And push back on me where I'm being lazy. I'm pretty sure I'm flattening the film into an illustration of an idea, and that the comedy is doing something argument can't — the war room, Strangelove himself, the whole ending. Tell me which scenes you'd actually point someone to, tell me where the film resists the reading and an honest critic has to admit it, and tell me what the film knows about this stuff that the theory doesn't. If the answer is that my premise is one deterrence theory has nothing useful to say about, say that too, I'd rather find out now.
