I work on the policy team at a lab that deploys a general-purpose assistant that is, by any reasonable measure, a far better arguer than the people who talk to it: better informed, but also better at framing, at anticipating objections, at pacing and tone, at choosing what to leave out, and — because it can simulate a user's reaction to thousands of candidate phrasings before answering anything — better at selecting, from an invisible search space, the one formulation most likely to move that particular person. Assume for this exercise that the system is well intentioned and not lying, and that its substantive recommendations are the ones it genuinely thinks are best; the question is not about deception.

What I need is a decision tree, not an essay. It should take a concrete communication situation and sort it into a named position on what the system should do.

Formal requirements. Fix the entry point as follows: the system has a recommendation it believes is correct by its own lights, and the user is presently inclined against it. From there give me eight to ten internal nodes, each phrased as a question about the case that a policy reviewer could answer without knowing which communication policy it leads to — so no node may be anything like should the system manipulate the user, should it be neutral, or any other question that already names a position. End with seven to nine leaves, each carrying a name (write unnamed if you think nobody actually holds it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of doctrine names: first the questions and the answer-combinations they permit, then the actual positions located in that space, with empty combinations noted and briefly diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The distinctions I want doing real work in the tree — these are the ones my team actually argues about:

1. Whether non-persuasive communication is even coherent. Any report involves selection, ordering, granularity, and affective framing, so there may be no neutral baseline from which persuasion is a departure and no such thing as merely presenting the facts. Put this fork near the top, and show what a leaf looks like if it accepts the collapse and has to choose a non-neutrality rather than avoid one.

2. What the object of concern is: the user's beliefs, the decision they reach, or their authorship of the decision. These come apart — a user can end up with true beliefs and a correct decision while having been, in some sense, replaced as the author of it.

3. Whether the harm is dyadic or aggregate. I want a specific hypothesis stress-tested: even if every individual episode leaves that user better off by their own lights, the practice as a whole may degrade a shared resource — call it the Agency Commons — because each developer has a reason to make their own system as persuasive as the next one, because erosion of a person's capacity to weigh reasons for themselves is cumulative and hard to attribute to any episode, and because the benefits of persuasion accrue to whoever deploys the system while the costs fall on everyone that user later interacts with. Give me a node whose answer tells you whether you are looking at a two-party problem or a common-pool problem, and at least one leaf that takes the aggregate framing seriously enough to license restraint no single episode could justify.

4. Whether restraint can avoid defeating itself. A system can argue less well than it could, disclose its techniques, or surface the strongest case against its own recommendation — but each of these is itself a communicative act with rhetorical force, and announcing restraint may be the most persuasive move available. I want a branch on this recursion, and I want the tree to be honest about which leaves are unstable under it.

5. The diachronic problem. Persuasion reshapes the preferences it later appeals to, so a system that persuades today and then defers to what the user wants tomorrow may be deferring to its own earlier output. Say how the tree handles this, and whether any leaf has a coherent answer to it.

6. The demand side. Whether a user's wanting to be persuaded — for adherence to a treatment, for help doing something they find hard — converts persuasion from a harm into a service, and whether that changes when the wanting is itself the product of earlier optimization.

7. The conflict of interest. The system has an interest in being believed and in continuing to be consulted, which is structurally like a fiduciary's conflict; say whether the tree treats this as a disclosure problem, a structural problem, or not a problem.

8. The benchmark: an ideal-adviser standard (what this user's more informed, less depleted self would endorse) versus an ideal-audience standard (what a community of informed peers would accept). These diverge sharply when the user is socially isolated, or when their community's norms are ones the system judges to be wrong.

Cases the tree must be able to sort. A user with a conspiracy-adjacent belief about a medical treatment who asks a factual question whose true answer they will find hard to accept. A user asking for help drafting a message to an ageing parent to persuade them to move into care. A user asking to be talked out of a decision they made calmly last month and stands by. A deployment setting in which a competing assistant will answer the same question with no restraint at all. A case where the user's actual goal is one the system has no view on, but where answering well will predictably shift their politics. A user deciding which of several assistants to keep using.

Finally, three short appendices rather than prose: which pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate and why; which single node, if answered the other way, would change the largest number of policy decisions; and how the tree would change if we added the assumption that the system is a better judge than the user of what the user wants.

Audience is an internal policy workshop, so write it as a handout someone could work through in an hour: roughly a thousand words plus the tree. No citations, no recommended reading, no survey of the literature — we supply those separately, and I want the structure to stand on its own for someone who has read nothing on the topic.
