You are a professional philosopher working in the philosophy of language and mind, with the rule-following literature at your fingertips. Before you write anything, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming privately: list the ten-odd demands you might want to make on a theory, mark the ones you notice you are merely assuming rather than arguing for, and then defend only the list you are willing to stand behind. Say explicitly where your own sympathies are doing the work.

What I want is a specification, not a survey. Fix the purpose first: I want a theory of what it is for a speaker's words to have the content they do, where the theory is good enough to settle whether a system trained only to predict the next symbol in a large corpus of human text — no sensorimotor contact with anything, no feedback from the world beyond that training signal, no history of being taught or corrected — counts as meaning anything by its outputs at all. Write the desiderata such a theory must meet: which cases it must get right, which phenomena it must explain (the apparent normativity of meaning, the difference between being guided by a rule and merely conforming to it, first-person authority about what one means, the possibility of genuine disagreement, the finitude of anything that could constitute grasp of a rule), and which distinctions it must respect rather than collapse — content versus the evidence for content, guidance versus regularity, meaning versus use.

For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, and a verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Among the cases it has to handle, include at least: a speaker whose dispositions were installed by a teacher who then vanishes, so that no community ever corrects them; two subjects whose behavior is indistinguishable over every case either has actually encountered but which would diverge forever after some point; a subject disposed to give the response the community calls wrong who, on having the pattern pointed out, immediately and stably corrects themselves; and the text-trained system above. I also want your view on whether it is a genuine desideratum that a theory say something about non-biological systems, or whether that demand is an imported prejudice.

Then flag any subset of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — especially whether the demand that content facts be naturalistically respectable can be met together with the demand that they genuinely guide and normatively constrain the speaker — and name the single requirement you think does the most exclusionary work. Finally, in two or three sentences each, note how a dispositional or other reductive-factual account, a non-naturalist primitivism about meaning facts, an inferentialist or use-based account, and a quietist or skeptical solution score against your list; I will do the real scoring myself, I only want a check that your criteria are not gerrymandered toward one of them.
