I'm a retired crystallographer — thirty-odd years of fitting models to noisy powder patterns, so I have a professional weakness for theories that die because somebody finally counted properly. Since packing it in I've been reading my way into history and philosophy of science, and I've got stuck on Kepler and astrology, which strikes me as far more interesting than the usual line that he was a great astronomer who unfortunately also cast horoscopes. I'm trying to turn this into a short piece for our local astronomy club's newsletter (audience: mostly retirees, they'll read anything under a thousand words), and right now it's more of an idea with notes then an essay. Here's where I've got to.

The claim: Kepler's real move wasn't to keep a harmless residue of astrology while doing serious astronomy on the side; it was to rebuild astrology into a theory with a closed hypothesis space, and that is exactly what made it mortal. Notes on how I get there: (1) In the big book on harmonics he ties the aspects that do any work to the regular polygons and star polygons inscribable in the circle, which keeps the old sextile, square and trine but adds newcomers like the quintile at 72 degrees, the biquintile at 144 and the sesquiquadrate at 135 — and, crucially, it predicts in advance that a separation of, say, 100 degrees does nothing at all. A finite menu fixed by geometry rather than by tradition. (2) Traditional astrology could not lose: signs, houses, dignities, lots and receptions always left a way to explain a miss. Kepler, as far as I can make out, threw out most of that apparatus and complained about the astrologers the way I'd complain about a colleague with a badly calibrated diffractometer — wrong measurement model, arbitrary tolerances, confounds not controlled. His error theory is methodological, not a metaphysical retreat. (3) The causal story runs through something like a faculty of the soul that answers to the same ratios that make musical consonance, so for him this is celestial acoustics, not occult effluvia, which is why he can think he's being a good physical scientist. (4) The twist I actually want to hang the piece on: if closure is what makes a theory testable, then the reformed astrology was the one that had to die, and the unreformed version survived precisely because nothing could touch it. Astrology wasn't refuted so much as outcompeted by the shreds that couldn't be refuted. In my old field that's the difference between a model with four fitted parameters and one with forty — better data kills the first and just makes the second wobble. (5) Weak spots I already know about: I think he kept weather records and that he never fully stopped doing horoscopes (there's at least one done for a general late in his career), and I'm not sure whether he ever really put the aspect theory to a systematic test or whether he just narrowed his claims. Also one of the men at the club says I'm praising as an epistemic virtue what was really a defensive rhetorical narrowing — say less, defend less — and I can't tell if that objection kills my reading or just adds a paragraph to it.

So: what I want is comments, not a rewrite. Which of the historical claims above are wrong or shaky — I'd rather find out now than after the thing goes out. Is the twist an actual point, or is it just the banality that falsifiable theories die sooner dressed up in period costume? If you were defending Kepler's astrology on its best day, which of my premises would you attack, and can you make that defense stronger than I've made the attack? Where is the argument weakest (my own suspicion: the step from a closed hypothesis space to must-eventually-lose seems to need the extra premise that the effects are weak and the data noisy, and I don't know whether that's in Kepler or only in me)? And does his joke about astronomy's foolish daughter having to feed her wise mother support my reading or undercut it? Please skip citations, footnotes and reading lists — I can't get to a university library and my readers won't chase them — though if the thing I'm calling closure of the hypothesis space already has a standard name in philosophy of science, tell me the name so I know what I'm reinventing. One more thing: as you go, say how confident you are in each of your main claims (sure / fairly confident / guessing), and single out the one point you're least sure of; if my notes rest on a detail you can't check from memory, say so rather than papering over it.
