so I've been trying to get straight in my own head what "establishment" actually is, because every time I read about it the word seems to mean something slightly different. The decomposition I ended up with is that a church–state arrangement is really four separate connections that can be plugged in or unplugged more or less independently:

- the money wire: does the state collect money on a religion's behalf, or hand it money — church tax, clergy salaries, building upkeep, subsidies
- the personnel wire: does the state have any say in who runs the religion or in what gets taught or practised
- the honor wire: does the state symbolically identify with a religion — oaths, ceremonies, reserved seats, public prayers
- the discipline wire: does the state tell individual people what they may wear or display, i.e. is the duty on citizens and not only on officials

and my working rule has been pretty crude: count the live wires, and the country with more of them is the more "established" one. (I know that's probably too simple, which is the point of this.)

Take it to the 2004 French law banning conspicuous religious symbols in state schools. As I understand the setup: it's law 2004-228, signed in March 2004 after the Stasi commission report the previous winter and twenty-odd years of headscarf rows going back to the late 80s; it bans pupils from wearing signs or clothing that ostensibly manifest religious affiliation in public primary and secondary schools; it doesn't name any religion; discreet symbols are fine; universities aren't covered. Meanwhile, in the same country in roughly the same period: private schools under contract with the state, overwhelmingly Catholic, have their teachers paid by the state; chaplains in the army, hospitals and prisons are on the state payroll; and Alsace-Moselle never went through the 1905 separation at all, so clergy there are state employees, bishops are nominated by the President after a proposal from the Pope, and there's religion teaching in state schools unless parents opt out. Whereas England still has an established church, bishops in the Lords and a monarch who has to be in communion with Canterbury, but no church tax and nothing resembling the 2004 ban — and Germany has no established church, but the state collects a church tax for the registered churches and religion is an ordinary subject in state schools.

What I want is a careful mapping, not a vibe. Which of my wires is actually live in the 2004 case, and which am I only managing to stretch to fit? Who even is "the state" here — the ministry, the school, the teacher, the pupil? A schoolgirl in a headscarf isn't a church and isn't an official, and I keep going back and forth on whether that means the discipline wire is the only one that's genuinely live, or whether the whole case is one my decomposition can't even describe.

Then the two directions. First, does the law make sense on my account — is it roughly what you'd predict from a country with the other wires in the state I described, or is it a bolt-on that has nothing to do with them? My hunch is that the school is doing something special here (minors, captive audience, citizen-formation) that none of my four wires captures, but I can't tell whether that's a real gap or me wanting the frame to be more interesting than it is. Second: a friend said my wires thing obviously has to be broken, because it scores France as more churched than Britain, and Britain is the one with the actual established church. I don't know if that's a reductio of the frame, a consequence I should just accept, or evidence that I've built a measure of church–state entanglement and then quietly started calling it establishment.

So: where does the frame illuminate the case, where does it misdescribe or stay silent, and if it needs a fifth wire or some kind of scope variable — who benefits, ministers versus adults, state agents versus service users, national versus regional law — what does adding it cost, and does the thing stop being able to rank countries at all once you do? If any of my facts are off, say so in passing, but please don't let correcting me become the answer.
