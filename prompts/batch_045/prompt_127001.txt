I keep watching arguments about transformative AI turn on whether it is like nuclear weapons, the printing press, or the Industrial Revolution, and I want to stop asking which comparison is correct and ask instead what a comparison would have to be good for. Please write the specification.

The purpose it has to be adequate to: guiding a decision that must be made before the evidence is in. A regulator, a funder, or a lab deciding now whether some concrete inference goes through — about how fast a capability diffuses, whether unilateral restraint is stable, whether a pause would be enforceable, whether the first movers capture durable advantages. Not forecasting, not mood-setting, not winning an argument. So the unit of adequacy is the inference an analogy licenses, not the analogy taken as a whole; an analogy can be good for one inference and worthless or actively misleading for another.

Here are the requirements I keep half-formulating. Sharpen them, discard the ones that are not really desiderata, and add what I have missed. For each, give the case or consideration that motivates it and say what a candidate looks like when it fails it.

1. Named inference. The analogy must state which specific inference it is carrying, and that inference must have a direction and a subject, not just a topic.
2. The name-stripping test. Restate the lesson with every proper noun removed, as a claim about feature-values. If the inference collapses when the names go, the episode's prestige was doing the work.
3. Incrementality. Delete the analogy; if the conclusion is unchanged, the analogy was decoration.
4. Ex ante availability. Was the lesson recognizable to people living through the earlier transition, or only reconstructable once the outcome was known? Say what we should do with analogies whose lessons are purely retrospective.
5. Declared axes. The analogy must say which dimensions it is mapping — tempo, whether the artifact is a tool or something with its own initiative, how concentrated control over it is, whether the change is reversible, whether diffusion feeds back into further improvement, whether contemporaries could tell a transition was underway — and which of those axes actually carry the inference, and which it is leaving dark.
6. Defeat conditions. What would have to be true of AI for this analogy to misfire, and how would we know?
7. The boomerang condition. Analogies get picked up by people arguing the opposite policy. What does this one license for them?

Then: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a camp — I suspect that demanding an analogy involve existential stakes, or that it be drawn from an episode that ended tolerably well, are both smuggled conclusions rather than criteria. Flag any subset of the surviving desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied; if the list is itself a result, say so plainly. And commit to a view on which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates, with your reason.

Finally, a scorecard against the list — nuclear weapons, the printing press, the Industrial Revolution — one line each, no more than 150 words total. Keep the weight on the criteria; the scorecard is an appendix.

Take a definite position throughout and defend it. Do not survey the options and leave me to choose. Under 800 words, in American English.
