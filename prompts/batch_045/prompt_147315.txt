Hey — I run a perception reading group, upper-level undergrads plus a few grad students, 14 people, 75 minutes. All I've got is one projector, a stack of index cards, pens, and some chocolate; no lab gear, no money for prizes (chocolate and "you pick next week's reading" are the stakes), and I cant get to the library so please don't send me to papers or reading lists — everything has to work with what's in the room.

Topic is cognitive penetration: whether what you know, believe or want can change what a thing literally looks like to you, as opposed to just changing what you conclude about it or what you're willing to say out loud.

I've got a rough idea and I want you to build it into something runnable. Ive been calling it the mislabel-then-match thing. Show people a degraded or ambiguous stimulus that's genuinely hard to identify (those black-and-white two-tone blobs that snap into a face once you've seen the original, or something heavily blurred). Before the reveal, give some of them a true label, some of them a deliberately false label, some of them nothing. The point of the false label is that most demos use a true prior, so "your experience got penetrated" and "you just inferred smarter" predict the same result and the demo proves nothing. Then, instead of asking what the object was, make them do a forced-choice perceptual match — which of four comparison figures or patches matches what they actually saw — and only after that ask for the identification and a confidence number. Half the room reports privately on a sealed index card, half out loud. And on a couple of trials, pay them for agreeing with the rest of the group instead of for matching the stimulus, so "what I saw" and "what I said" visibly come apart.

What I want:

- Exact rules, roles, materials, a timeline that's roughly minute-by-minute, and the payoff scheme in points.
- The debrief: map each moment of the activity onto the distinction between experience actually being penetrated, judgment-only effects, and reporting/response bias — and add the fourth option people always forget, that repeated exposure just retrained their perceptual system slowly rather than anything happening online in the moment.
- Commit to one ordering of the conditions and defend it; don't give me three options and leave me to pick.
- A short, definite prediction: what will the numbers actually look like, which of those hypotheses does this discriminate cleanly, and what result would make you scrap the design.
- What will go wrong the first time I run it (I'm especially worried about people seeing the solution and thereby destroying the stimulus for later trials, and about the two or three students who've already read about this stuff).
- Be frank about what the activity can't show. I suspect the whole "did your experience change, or did you just become disposed to say so?" question is unreachable by this method, and I want you to tell me what I have to say out loud myself at the end.

Ideally they walk out genuinely unsure whether their own introspection can tell seeing differently from judging differently, and annoyed about it in a productive way. Take a definite line on the design choices and give reasons — no menu of options. American English please.
