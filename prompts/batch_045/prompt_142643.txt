I work on a platform team, and a lot of my job over the past few years has been onboarding new engineers onto our deployment and debugging procedures. Three things keep happening, and I have come to think they are all one thing, though I cannot make the shape of it out.

The first is a runbook. Our deployment runbook instructs new hires to drain active connections, then flip the feature flag, then verify. For about three years now, flipping the flag has drained the connections atomically; the first step is a no-op. We have kept it anyway, because removing it makes training measurably worse. I have compared cohorts informally: engineers trained on the current, accurate description make more first-month mistakes than engineers trained on the superseded description, even though both cohorts are told the true facts about the system whenever they ask. The false step appears to be doing real work — giving a beginner somewhere to put their attention, or a place to stand while the rest of the procedure is still unfamiliar. There is a further wrinkle: engineers who are told, after six months or so, that the step does nothing at all sometimes start making the beginner mistakes again.

The second is the reverse. When I ask our strongest engineers to write down how they go about diagnosing a flaky test, they produce clean, ordered procedures. Juniors who follow those procedures literally do considerably worse than juniors who simply shadow the senior for a week. And on the few occasions we have recorded the screen, the seniors do not do what they described — not out of dishonesty, but because what they wrote is a retrospective summary that reads like a method. The written version is not worthless, incidentally: it works fine as a reminder for someone who can already do the thing.

The third is smaller and personal. I can tell within a second or two whether a code review comment is a genuine objection or a style nitpick dressed up as one. I have tried many times to state the rule, including as a lint rule we prototyped and abandoned; every formulation I produce catches both categories or neither. I am not guessing, though — my hit rate is high and I would bet on individual calls.

What strikes me is that the two directions do not line up. A description can be false and still reliably produce the ability; a description can be accurate and still be inert with respect to producing the ability. So the thing I am noticing is not just that some knowledge lives in the hands and resists being said. In the runbook case it is said perfectly well, and what is said is wrong, and the wrongness looks load-bearing. In the flaky-test case what is said is not wrong — or at least not straightforwardly wrong — and the failure is one of transmission.

A friend suggested tacit knowledge, which seems to me to miss the first case: that procedure is fully articulable, it is just false. I have also seen the phrase knowing-how versus knowing-that somewhere, but if that only distinguishes two kinds of knowledge, it does not explain why a false propositional description should outperform a true one. Someone else suggested muscle memory or the procedural/declarative distinction, which seems to be a claim about where things are stored in the brain rather than about this mismatch between a description's correctness and its power to produce competence.

So: is there an established concept for the mismatch itself — the two-sided failure of correspondence between how true a description is and how well it generates the ability it describes? Is it one phenomenon or two, and if the useful-false-description and the inert-true-description are normally treated separately, how are they related to each other? I am told there is a long-running disagreement about whether knowing-how is really just a special case of knowing-that; does the runbook-style case have any bearing on that, and if so on which side? If nothing established fits, I would want a term coined for it, with a definition and boundary cases, since I want to know what does not count: a mnemonic, a simplification told to a child, a placebo, a motivating lie. The label matters less to me than the carving — I want to know what this is, what it is not, and where its edges are. I should add that I have no way to chase down papers or citations, so names of authors and works will not do me much good; the distinction itself is what I am after.
