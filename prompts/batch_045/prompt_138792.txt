I have been reading about T. M. Scanlon's contractualism, on which a principle is permissible only if no individual could reasonably reject it, with rejectability assessed by comparing that individual's situation under the principle against the alternatives. The familiar difficulty concerns principles that would spare a large number of people a very small harm each while imposing a severe harm on one person: because each of the many has only a trivial personal complaint and the one person has a grave complaint, the principle looks rejectable, even though the many trivial harms seem to add up to more than the single severe harm. I keep encountering a variant that does not fit that illustration. In it, a single person would suffer a huge number of individually trivial burdens over a lifetime, such as a small daily irritation for decades, while the alternative is one severe burden at a single time; the trivial burdens together seem to outweigh the severe one, but the framework compares each person's complaint, and it is unclear whether the trivial burdens form one aggregated lifetime complaint, many negligible complaints, or something to be compared one by one with the severe burden. A second instance: the same puzzle arises for a small, tightly bound group that treats the burdens as one collective complaint rather than as many separate personal complaints. Is there an established name for this phenomenon, meaning the treatment of many trivial complaints within a single life or within a tightly unified group, as opposed to across many discrete individuals, or, if not, can you coin and define a term? In your answer, identify the nearest established concepts, explain precisely how the phenomenon differs from each, including the standard case of trivial harms to many different people and any doctrine about the separateness of persons, and say which fits best or why none does; I want the carving and its boundary cases, not a bare label, and I am not asking for citations or a reading list.
