I'm a journalist — I write and present a long-form radio series on science and ideas — and I'm scripting the final episode of a three-part run on what we actually mean by "collapse". The series covers the end of the Bronze Age in the eastern Mediterranean, the so-called Maya collapse, and Rapa Nui, and the thread I can't get away from is whether any of these are failures in the sense we instinctively mean, or whether the word is an artefact of what happens to the *evidence* rather than to the people. As I've been reporting it, a crude version of the sceptical case goes like this: when palaces stop paying scribes, the record doesn't get emptier, it gets *coarser* — fewer dated inscriptions, fewer distinguishable building phases, no more fine ware from the palace workshops, just plainer pots made at home — and a population that is still roughly where it was, still farming the same terraces, simply becomes harder to see. I've started calling this the resolution fallacy in my notes: we mistake a drop in the resolution of the record for a drop in the number of people, and then tell a moral story about the people to explain a fact about the record. The symmetrical mistake, which I also want to avoid, is the consoling one: that because the descendants are still there and theLanguage is still spoken, nothing of value was lost, and "collapse" is only ever a judgement by people who liked palaces. I don't want the episode — or the poem I'd like to close it with — to settle into either register.

What I'd like is a sestina: unrhymed, thirty-nine lines, six sestets and a three-line envoi, in a loose iambic line of about ten syllables, readable aloud in a minute and a half without the listener needing to know any archaeology. The formal reason for a sestina is the whole point of the episode: the six end-words never change, they only change *order*, so the same vocabulary of a society keeps returning in a new arrangement — nothing lost, only permuted — and I want that rotation to carry the argument rather than the argument being stated on top of it. So: choose six plain concrete nouns drawn from the material record (things like a pot, a script, a terrace, a wall, a seed, a hearth), no abstractions such as "decline", "resilience" or "sustainability", and let their successive rearrangements dramatise the disagreement — the catastrophist reading in some stanzas, the continuity reading in others, ideally without either ever being announced as the right one. One of the six should be "letters", and I'd like you to exploit both senses at different turns, since in the Bronze Age case what disappears is at once a script and a set of messages, a technology and a set of relationships. I'd also like the last line of the sixth stanza to end on whichever of your six words carries the sense of failure, and the envoi to bring all six back together in a way that refuses that word without denying it — a landing, not a verdict.

Please write it in British English spelling and usage throughout, and keep the diction ordinary enough that it survives being read over music; it will be credited at the end of the episode and may be reprinted in the accompanying online piece, so nothing in it should depend on inside knowledge of the debates.
