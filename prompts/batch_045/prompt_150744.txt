I am putting together a short anthology piece for my seminar on classical Chinese moral psychology, and I would like you to compose one of its dialogues. Please write it in American English.

The dialogue is between two scholars: a defender of Mencius's claim that human nature is good, and a defender of Xunzi's claim that human nature is bad — or, on the reading she prefers, that our native dispositions are raw, unruly, and self-preferring, such that order and goodness arise only through deliberate effort and training. Both should be first-rate: each knows the texts well, grants what is genuinely strong in the other's position, and attacks the best version of that position rather than a caricature of it.

Here is the case they are asked to interpret. Suppose a hypothetical longitudinal study followed several hundred children reared from infancy by caretakers who met their physical needs adequately and used language constantly for practical purposes, but who never praised, blamed, instructed, or in any way modelled moral behaviour. Three stable patterns emerge. First, the children reliably show spontaneous distress at signs of another's physical pain and sometimes act to end it, though almost only when doing so costs them little. Second, they show no spontaneous tendency to share scarce goods on anything like an impartial basis, and they protest vigorously when their own share is reduced. Third, when a caretaker eventually introduces an explicit rule about taking turns or not striking others, most children comply within weeks with little coercion, and some come to be distressed by their own violations of it; how quickly and how deeply, however, varies enormously with temperament and with how the rule is introduced. Set aside whether such a study could or should ever be run, and take the description at face value.

Each scholar argues that this evidence confirms her own account of human nature and diagnoses where the other's reading goes wrong. Along the way, have them work through at least these: whether dispositions that require teaching to reach their full expression can count as part of our nature at all; whether Xunzi's thesis concerns badness or mere rawness; whether the variability in the third finding is noise or is precisely the phenomenon at issue; what each position would have predicted before the study was run; and whether, if the results had come out differently — spontaneous impartial sharing, or no spontaneous distress at all — either position would have been in genuine trouble.

Do not let them collapse into a bland synthesis in which both turn out to have been right all along. I want each to leave the exchange still convinced, but having been forced to answer something she would rather have avoided. Around 900 to 1,200 words.
