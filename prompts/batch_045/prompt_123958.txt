Hey. Long one, sorry, but I need help with an argument that's been going in circles in my department's work-in-progress group for about three semesters now, and I've started to think we're all talking past each other.

The topic is interpersonal comparisons of well-being. The two poles, as we argue them: my friend who does decision theory says comparisons across people are basically a category error — or if not a category error, then so underdetermined by anything we could ever observe that no aggregative view (utilitarianism, prioritarianism, cost-benefit analysis, all of it) should get to rest on them. The rest of us say look, we do this constantly and we're not confused when we do it: triage, "she's in more pain than he is," "the kid is worse off than the adult," giving the last dose to whoever needs it more. And any theory that officially refuses to compare ends up unable to say things nobody actually wants to deny, like that a person with nothing is worse off than someone mildly bored.

Every move has a countermove. Bring up the impartial-spectator or veil-of-ignorance style device and someone points out that this just relocates the comparison inside one head and pretends it went away. Bring up ordinalism and the informational restrictions from social choice and someone says that's a constraint on a formal framework, not a discovery about well-being. Everyone in the room is smart, everyone has read the same stuff, and in a year not one person has changed their mind by even a degree.

So here's my half-baked diagnosis, which I want you to sharpen or shoot down. I think "can we compare well-being across persons" isn't one yes/no question, it's a bundle, and the word comparison is doing a lot of hiding. My suspicion is that comparisons are irreducibly contrastive and indexed: someone is worse off than someone else with respect to some respect, over some time-slice, against some baseline, for the purposes of some decision. Fill in those slots differently and you get different answers, so the two camps may each be right about their own version. If that's right, part of the debate is metaphysical (is there a common currency at all), part is epistemic (could we tell), and part isn't about well-being at all but about what a decision procedure is *for* — tracking the truth versus being justifiable to each person affected.

A few things I want from you:

Diagnose it properly. Which of those threads is actually load bearing, and is there a hidden question doing the work — e.g. a background empirical one about whether well-being has a similar enough shape across people (saturation, diminishing returns, whether suffering has a ceiling) that comparisons could be stable? Are the two sides maybe disagreeing about what would count as a datum here, or about bedrock starting commitments neither can argue for?

Don't just survey the positions, I've read them, and please don't give me a reading list or citations, I'll fill those in myself. I want the structural story.

Then: given your diagnosis, what would progress even look like? A distinction, a formal result, a finding from psychology, abandoning the question as ill-posed? And be honest with me about whether this is the kind of dispute that could in principle close, or whether the right answer is that it can't and I should stop expecting it to.

Also, if my contrastive-indexed idea is a dud, say so directly and tell me what's wrong with it — I'd rather find out now than in front of the group.
