I am a twelfth-grade student who edits the humour column of my school's philosophy club newsletter, and the column has one fixed slot: a joke printed as exactly five numbered lines, with no headline, no setup paragraph, and no closing remark. The joke has to fit inside those five lines and nowhere else, so I would be grateful if you would write it as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with the fifth line carrying the punchline and nothing added before or after.

The joke should be about hinge epistemology, drawing on Wittgenstein's On Certainty — the notes he wrote during the last eighteen months of his life, published posthumously in 1969. Here is the situation I have in mind: a student who has read a little of the book tries to use it to get out of trouble. A teacher is pulling apart a spectacularly implausible excuse (a missing essay, a broken window, whatever you prefer), and the student announces that his claim is a hinge proposition — something exempt from doubt, something that has to stand fast for any questioning to get going at all — and that the teacher therefore has no standing to ask for grounds. The teacher, who has actually read the book, dismantles this.

I would like the humour to turn on the real substance rather than on puns about the title, on Wittgenstein's name, or on philosophy being obscure. In particular, the reply should get right at least these features: hinge propositions are not ones we choose, assert, or install at will; they are not, strictly speaking, things we know in the ordinary sense; they show up in what people do rather than in what they claim; and — the point I most want the punchline to land on — a hinge is what lets a question turn, not a device for bringing questioning to a halt. If it helps, the student's real mistake can be presented as confusing Wittgenstein's image with something else entirely.

To make the joke do something new, please build it around a piece of terminology I invented myself, so please do not assume it comes from the literature: call it a doorstop proposition. A hinge proposition is what a question swings on, and so makes enquiry possible; a doorstop proposition, by contrast, is a claim a person wedges under a line of questioning in order to jam it, and the humour should come from the student having mistaken the second for the first. You may also use the related idea of a retro-fitted hinge, if it is useful: a hinge someone screws on after the door is already hanging, in the hope of making an old excuse load-bearing.

The result should be funny, or at least wry, to someone who has genuinely read On Certainty, and it should still make sense to a reader who has only read Moore's famous gesture of holding up his hands.
