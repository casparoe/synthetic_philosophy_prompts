I am a PhD student finishing a chapter on the duty to remember and its limits, working in the neighbourhood of Margalit's contrast between the ethics and the morality of memory, and of the broadly anti-therapeutic suspicion (Rieff's, among others) that a culture organised around release from the past ends up transmitting nothing. What follows are my verdicts on eight cases, given on the gut, without a principle in hand. Two pairs of them I cannot reconcile with each other; for one case I have coined a term because I could not find one; and one case I suspect may not be a case of anything at all.

1. A country emerging from dictatorship offers amnesty in exchange for full public disclosure. The truth commission produces a fourteen-volume report. Almost nobody reads it, no one is prosecuted, there is no memorial and no annual day, and ten years later the report exists only as an unlisted PDF. My verdict: not merely permissible — closer to admirable than to a betrayal. The unread report does not trouble me.

2. A national museum quietly removes its gallery on a nineteenth-century campaign of dispossession; a minister says the country must stop defining itself by the worst thing it ever did. The archives are intact, specialists can and do publish on them, and the episode is better documented than it has ever been. My verdict: clearly wrong, and wrong in a register I would call betrayal, even though no information whatsoever has been lost.

3. In 1905 a pogrom destroyed a small town's Jewish community. Every descendant of the survivors emigrated; none remain anywhere. The town's present inhabitants are largely descended from people who watched. The last person anywhere with a first-hand connection died in 2011. The town still holds an annual service at the site. My verdict: it must continue; letting it lapse would be a grave loss. No one present has any relation to the victims at all.

4. A family keeps up a yearly visit to the grave of a great-great-grandfather who died in 1902, of whom they know nothing but a name, two dates and one photograph. My verdict: no obligation whatsoever, and something faintly absurd about it — although I would never criticise them for doing it.

5. Fifth-generation descendants of a settler colony's founders hold an annual public act of contrition for a massacre their ancestors carried out. No living person was harmed by that massacre or enriched by it. The descendants of the victims have publicly asked them to stop, on the grounds that it has become about the commemorators. My verdict: there is something indecent here — but I cannot name who is wronged.

6. A wrong is commemorated so continuously, professionally and institutionally — curricula, minutes of silence, funded memorials, annual broadcast — that the practice now seems to be chiefly a display of the commemorators' own moral seriousness. Nothing informational or relational would be lost if it stopped. I call this saturation. My verdict: at some point continuing becomes worse than stopping, but I cannot locate the point or say what flips there.

7. After thirty years of paramilitary killing, a settlement is reached. Everyone knows roughly who did what to whom. There is a strong norm against raising it in mixed company, and children are not told which neighbour did what. My verdict: the norm is not merely permissible but close to obligatory, and I would defend withholding the knowledge from the children. This is a case where I want to say that there is a duty to forget, or at least a duty not to remember together.

8. An archive of video testimony is coupled with a system that generates responsive first-person address: a visitor asks a question and a dead survivor's recorded voice, idiom and manner are recombined to answer in the first person, indefinitely, to visitors with no connection to anyone involved. My verdict: I recoil, and I cannot say what is wrong with it. It satisfies every function I can name for remembering, and it will outlast any living memory.
