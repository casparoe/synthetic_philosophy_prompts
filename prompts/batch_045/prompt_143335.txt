I'm a print journalist — I write a long column on science and ideas for a weekend magazine — and I'm finishing a piece on a new ten-year cardiovascular risk calculator that a research consortium wants to roll out in primary care. Names and details changed: I'll call the patient Marta K., fifty-eight, a long-distance cyclist, non-smoker, told by her GP that her ten-year risk of a first cardiac event is 14 percent. When I interviewed her she waved the number away with a line I've now had from four other sources in unrelated contexts: I'm not a statistic. What made me stop and think was that one of the modelers who built the tool used almost the same sentence to do the opposite work — we never claimed to say anything about any individual patient, we estimate rates in a population — when I asked him what his model owes the particular person sitting in front of the screen. I'd like to know what I'm actually dealing with before I write.

What I want is a disambiguation, not an essay. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that I'm not a statistic — or, more carefully, population-level numbers don't tell you anything about an individual — could be expressing when it is used to wave away a personal risk figure, ordered roughly from the most trivial to the most radical. For each one give me: a one-line statement of the thesis, a verdict on whether it is true and how much weight it carries (platitude, defensible and interesting, or straightforwardly false), and an explicit verdict on whether it would actually license the retort Marta was making. I'm reasonably confident that one item on the menu has to be something like the reference class problem — that any individual falls into indefinitely many classes, that the choice among them changes the number, and that nothing in the data settles which class is the right one — but I want that handled as one candidate reading among several rather than assumed from the start to be what is going on, and I want whatever else belongs there included on its own terms: a thesis about chances versus degrees of confidence, say, or about what it could even mean to attach a probability to a single unrepeatable person, or about the difference between a number failing to be about Marta and a number failing to be precise enough for Marta to act on.

Three things in particular, because they are where my notes are thinnest. First, a distinction I've been groping toward and can't state cleanly: is the complaint that the risk figure was derived from the wrong class of people for Marta, or that no class can ever be the right one — and if it is the latter, does the complaint get weaker or stronger the more we happen to know about her? I keep tying myself in knots: add everything distinctive about Marta and I end up with a class of one and no data to draw on, but keep her in the broad class and I am ignoring evidence I actually have, and I cannot tell whether that tension is a fatal objection to the number or merely the ordinary price of drawing any inference at all. Second, whether the worry depends on a frequentist understanding of probability, on the thought that relative frequencies need repeatable classes and a single case has no frequency, or whether it bites just as hard if probability is understood as a rational degree of confidence or as a physical propensity in the individual — I have heard both claimed and cannot adjudicate it. Third, whether the modeler's version of the sentence is in better shape than the patient's, or whether it is the same confusion in a lab coat. Please keep the answer free of citations and reading suggestions: I have no way to check references and I won't print the names of papers I haven't read, so argue the points directly and flag where specialists genuinely disagree rather than pointing me at a literature. If it helps, picture the deliverable as a one-page memo I can read on the train before filing twelve hundred words on Friday.
