You are a professional philosopher who works at the intersection of applied ethics, decision theory under deep uncertainty, and agricultural economics -- someone who has actually had to advise an institution that buys food, not someone who writes about food ethics from the armchair. Please think carefully and at length. Before you write anything in final form, spend some time brainstorming: list the ways a naive version of this procedure would go wrong (optimizing a proxy, double counting, comparing a real change against an idealized baseline instead of the actual alternative, pretending the deep uncertainties are point estimates, quietly baking in a controversial value judgment as if it were a fact), discard the ones that turn out not to matter for my situation, and then write the version that survives your own objections. Where you end up rejecting a view I have clearly been tempted by, steelman it before you reject it. Write the whole thing in American English.

The situation: I direct menu planning and procurement for the dining system of a state university system. Twenty-two campuses, roughly forty-five thousand meals a day during term, one central menu cycle that we re-cut twice a year, and essentially all purchasing routed through a single regional broadline distributor. My constraints are fixed and non-negotiable: a hard ceiling on cost per meal; federal-style nutrition standards including protein and iron minimums; binding accommodation obligations (a halal station, a kosher station, a substantial vegetarian South Asian line, and medically prescribed diets); a student-government resolution instructing us to reduce the animal footprint of campus dining without specifying what that means; a faculty sustainability committee that pushes local and pasture-raised sourcing; and a board of trustees that will want to see the reasoning in writing, because whatever we do will be described in a press release either way.

I have been reading the rationalist and effective-altruism blogs since the Sequences era, so I have seen the standard moves: the post arguing that crop cultivation kills field animals and therefore vegetarianism may not help, the reply about trophic levels and feed conversion, the beef-versus-chicken tradeoff where chicken means far more individual animals with worse lives while beef means far more land and feed. I do not need to be walked through any of that. What I do not have, and what I cannot write myself, is anything I can hand to a coordinator with a bachelor's degree in nutrition and no philosophy and no economics, who has about fifteen minutes per candidate menu change and has to produce a written justification.

So: give me a decision procedure. A numbered sequence of questions, in the order they must be asked, each with an explicit statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to it, each with a stated gate at which the procedure stops and returns a no, and each flagged either as a factual question my staff can answer or as a values question that must be handed to a named human (me, or the board) rather than settled by the procedure. It has to be usable by someone who has never heard of fungibility or expected value, and it has to be auditable -- a reviewer should be able to see which gate a proposal died at.

There are three features of my situation that I think break the naive versions, and the procedure has to contain a test for each rather than a footnote:

One, our purchases are lumpy. We buy in whole cases through a distributor who reallocates inventory across its other clients, and we sign forward commitments by category. A cut of three percent in chicken volume may have literally no effect on anyone's production decisions; a cut that drops us below a contract tier might have a large one. I need a step that separates changes that are real at the level of the supply chain from changes that are real only on a spreadsheet, and a rule for what to do when a change is currently below threshold.

Two, students are not obliging. If they reject the swap, some of them buy food off campus, and some of it goes into the bin. Both of those can make a change worse than doing nothing, since an animal may then have been raised and slaughtered to feed no one, or to feed someone at a different restaurant where the item is worse. I need the procedure to treat participation and plate waste as part of the causal chain rather than as an implementation detail, and to say what evidence would be sufficient at the point where we cannot yet measure them.

Three, the uncertainties are not the ordinary kind. Moral weight across taxa, and the number of field animals killed per hectare of a given crop, are uncertain by orders of magnitude rather than by a factor of two, and reasonable people in this space disagree about the right attitude to take toward that. I want the procedure to be robustness-based rather than expected-value-over-guessed-numbers: it should prefer changes that look good across the whole plausible range and reject those that look good only in the middle of it, it should say explicitly what to do when nothing dominates, and it should never present a contested value judgment -- for example, whether the deaths of wild animals on spared land are a cost, a benefit, or neither -- as a finding.

The procedure should also keep separate, and require the staffer to fill in separately, at least these buckets: animals farmed and killed per serving and the quality of their lives; animals killed by the crop production that feeds them, with the reminder that plant proteins have a crop footprint too, so the relevant question is magnitude and not kind; the effects of land use and land-use change on wild animals; and wild-caught seafood, which is a case of killing wild animals directly rather than farming them, and which brings bycatch and abandoned gear with it.

Then run the finished procedure on three live proposals, showing your work at each gate and telling me where it stops or hands off:

The first is replacing ground beef with chicken thigh in our two highest-volume entrees, which is what the student government assumes is the obvious win.

The second is a donor-funded weekly grass-fed local beef burger night, marketed as the ethical and sustainable option, which the faculty committee loves and which I suspect is the exact opposite once land use is on the ledger.

The third is moving the default hot line to a fish and shrimp option two days a week, which is being floated as the compromise everyone can sign on to.

At least one of these, I expect, will come back with an answer I will not like, and I would rather be told now than in the press release; please say plainly which one and why. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do, where it will systematically misfire, and which two or three empirical facts the answers are most sensitive to -- so that I know what is actually worth sending someone out to measure with our own invoices, our distributor's spec sheets, and our own plate-waste audits, rather than guessing at.

Do not cite literature, do not give me a bibliography, and do not supply any figure you would have had to look up. I want the structure of the reasoning, and wherever a number is required I want to know what kind of number it is, whether we can obtain it from our own records, and how the procedure behaves when we cannot.
