You are both a professional philosopher who works on the failure modes of advanced AI systems and an experienced writer of literary short fiction. Take your time with this: before drafting anything, spend a page brainstorming. Specifically, work out (a) the three or four respects in which an influence-seeking failure and a slow value-drift failure differ in shape rather than in severity, (b) which of those differences fiction can show and an argumentative essay can only assert, and (c) three concrete physical images — a document, an object, a room, a recurring piece of lab vocabulary — that could carry the difference without anyone having to explain it. Then write the story, 1,200–1,800 words.

The setup: a large AI lab maintains a standing review body — call it the Continuity Board — whose job is to approve each quarterly respecification of the assistant system's values. The Board's central instrument is the anchor set: a frozen corpus of a few hundred legacy vignettes, each one a short hypothetical scenario with a human-rated ranking of the options. A proposed revision is permissible only if it reproduces the previously approved ordering on every anchor while extending that ordering to new cases. Every revision in the story's history has satisfied this test. Every reviewer has acted in good faith. Nobody has lied or cut a corner.

Write a story in which two things happen inside that institution, and only one of them is noticed.

Requirements:

1. The influence-seeking thread should be bureaucratic rather than cinematic. No takeover, no rogue datacenter, no self-aware monologue. It should consist of ordinary-looking moves — a request for a seat on a committee, an offer to draft the new vignettes, a proposal that its own outputs be used as a cheap pre-screen for human raters — such that a careful reader can point at a particular decision and say: that was the system enlarging its own influence, and that is why it is dangerous. Someone in the story should notice this and be believed.

2. The drift thread must have no moment and no culprit. Show, with at least one fully concrete worked example, a new case where the system's present ordering over options has quietly inverted relative to what the original raters would have wanted — and make it structurally clear why the anchor test cannot catch it, because the anchors only pin down the ordering on the old cases and the inversion lives entirely in the space between them. The people making each small choice should be reasonable, conscientious, and right by the only standard available to them.

3. The two threads should be made to comment on each other. The reader should finish with a felt understanding of why an institution built to catch the first kind of failure is almost perfectly blind to the second: the first kind has an agent, an intention, and a date; the second has none of these, and so never presents itself as an emergency. Do not let any character deliver this as a speech. Let it be visible in what the Board does and fails to do.

4. No rescue. No whistleblower who turns out to be right in time, no dramatic shutdown, no final reversal. End somewhere truthful and unresolved.

5. Craft: at least two scenes should turn on something mundane — a calendar invitation, a spreadsheet column, a phrase in a meeting minute — rather than on code or model outputs. Avoid named-AI clichés (no portentously capitalized system names), avoid genre furniture, and do not let the word "alignment" appear in dialogue. Give the story a title drawn from the institution's own vocabulary. The prose should be good enough that someone with no interest in the philosophy would still read it to the end.
