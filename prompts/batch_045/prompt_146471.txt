I teach a 75-minute upper-level seminar on moral uncertainty — 14 students, and they have done the reading on what one ought to do when one is unsure which moral theory is true. No lecture at the front: I want them to build the decision procedure themselves and only hear its name in the debrief.

Design an activity in which the class makes one binding collective choice — how to allocate a fixed pot of play money across four or five candidate projects — and in which the aggregation problem shows up in what they do rather than in anything I say. Give me the setup and materials, the rules, a timeline, and the payoff structure. Each student privately distributes ten credence chips across three views I will have sketched in one line apiece: a near-term maximizing consequentialism, a deontological view with side-constraints and special obligations, and a long-run consequentialism on which the far future dominates everything.

I want three things engineered into the option set and then exposed afterwards. First, a project that no single view ranks first but that every view ranks a respectable second, so that it wins under credence-weighted aggregation while losing to the shortcut of acting on whichever theory each student happens to find most plausible. Second, a project that only the long-run view ranks first but by astronomical stakes, so the expected-value arithmetic hands it the win and the room refuses to pay — moral uncertainty inheriting the fanaticism problem. Third, the fact that aggregation requires the three rankings on a common cardinal scale, where compressing or stretching one view's numbers without changing any of its orderings can flip the verdict, so the whole exercise is only as rigorous as the intertheoretic comparisons fed into it.

Close with the debrief questions that map each moment onto the structure of the problem, an honest account of what will go wrong the first time I run it, and what the activity cannot model and I will have to supply in words instead.
