I'm putting together a short position paper for a small reading group on suffering-focused ethics — six of us, meeting every couple of weeks, mostly philosophers plus one animal welfare scientist — and I'd rather find the holes in my own view now then have them found for me in the meeting. Here's roughly where I've landed, which I've been privately calling range-relative lexical threshold negative utilitarianism:

(1) Below some threshold of intensity, pleasures and pains are commensurable and trade off at a finite but steep exchange rate; above it, an episode of suffering lexically dominates any finite quantity of sub-threshold goods — pleasures, preference satisfaction, achievement, whatever. (2) The threshold isn't an absolute intensity on a human scale: a pain is above-threshold for a subject when it takes up a big enough fraction of the range of intensities that subject can currently experience, the point being to avoid having to conclude that a rat's agony is "mild" just because it's small compared to ours. (3) The threshold applies to episodes, not to whole lives or lifetime totals — sub-threshold pains never accumulate into something above-threshold. (4) How bad an episode is supervenes on the intrinsic character of the experience: not on what caused it, what it's about, or how the subject feels about it afterwards. (5) Methodologically I want something I can actually use: I want to rank policies by expected value with real numbers, and I really don't want to have to go lexicographic about expected utility, because every time I've tried that it just makes everything incomparable and paralyses the decision. Then two case verdicts I'm fairly attached to: (6) forty years of unrelenting moderate pain is clearly worse — worse as an outcome and worse for the person living it — than a mostly fine life containing one brief episode of agony; and (7) in triage I'd let one person go through an episode of agony if that's what it takes to spare two other people the same episode, i.e. within the above-threshold domain I just aggregate intensity times duration and minimise.

One person in the group is definately going to tell me the discontinuity in (1) is incoherent — how can value jump on a dimension that varies continuously — and I don't have a snappy reply to that, though it also feels like the sort of objection that sounds better than it is. Past that I genuinely can't locate the real problem, and I don't want to find out mid-discussion. So: audit the set. Which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like it, and for each genuine tension, what exactly would have to give and what would the cheapest repair cost me? Rank them by severity instead of handing me seven equally bad problems, and if there's a single revision that cleans up more than one of them at once, say which. Also tell me which of these I should get out in front of as a bullet I'm openly biting, rather than waiting to be caught on it.
