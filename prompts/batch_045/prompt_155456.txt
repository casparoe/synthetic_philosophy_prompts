I am a PhD student in normative ethics working on population ethics and the measurement of well-being, and next month I am presenting a paper to a hospital clinical ethics seminar. The thesis I am defending is that when we rank interventions for the relief of suffering under scarcity, the morally salient unit is not the quantity of suffering averted but what I call the worst-episode constraint: a person's situation is to be assessed, for purposes of comparison, by the severity of the worst episode of suffering they will in fact undergo, and this severity enters lexically prior to any amount of milder suffering in that same life; interpersonal comparison then proceeds by a leximin ordering over those worst episodes. My argument has three steps. First, intrapersonal aggregation across time fails at high intensities: there is no number of mild, transient pains that compensates for or outweighs one episode of agony, and this is not mere risk-aversion but a judgement about what makes a life go badly. Second, the view escapes the reductios that standard negative utilitarianism faces, because it orders episodes within lives that are going to be lived anyway and therefore says nothing about whether lives or populations should exist. Third, it has real operational bite: it reverses some familiar cost-effectiveness rankings, because an expensive rescue intervention for a small number of people facing extreme episodic suffering can outrank a cheap programme that delivers small relief to a very large number.

The room will be about eight clinical ethicists and palliative-care physicians who do bedside consultation and think natively in terms of proportionality, futility, symptom burden, and the doctrine of double effect; two scholars from disability studies and the medical humanities who are alive to the expressivist objection against selective prevention and against quality-adjusted metrics, and who will hear any ranking by severity as a verdict on people who already live with pain or impairment; one health economist who builds cost-effectiveness models in the disability-adjusted-life-year style and will want to know what concretely changes; and two mainstream deontologists who believe the whole project goes wrong the moment it aggregates across persons.
