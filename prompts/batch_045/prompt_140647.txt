I sit on the editorial board of a forty-author assessment report — call it the Sable Report — and I have got myself into an argument I cannot find the exit from. It is built out of the two familiar puzzles about justified belief, the lottery case and the preface case, but it ends somewhere I am not willing to go, so I assume something slipped.

The setup. The report contains 2,000 substantive assertions, each independently double-checked under a protocol that lets roughly one checked assertion in 400 through falsely; so for each assertion aᵢ, P(aᵢ is true | our evidence) = 0.9975. Our track record: 497 of our last 500 reports turned out to contain at least one false assertion. Let D be the standard preface disclaimer — "at least one assertion in this report is false" — whose probability on our evidence is accordingly about 0.993. Let JB abbreviate "justified in believing."

(1) Threshold. For any p, if P(p | E) ≥ 0.99, then S is JB p on E.
(2) Aggregation. If S is JB p and S is JB q, then S is JB (p ∧ q).
(3) Invariance. If p and q are logically equivalent and S recognizes the equivalence, then S is JB p if and only if S is JB q.
(4) Consistency. For no p is S both JB p and JB ¬p.
(5) Since 0.9975 ≥ 0.99, the group is JB aᵢ for each i from 1 to 2,000. [from 1]
(6) Since 0.993 ≥ 0.99, the group is JB D. [from 1]
(7) By 1,999 applications of (2), the group is JB A, where A is the conjunction a₁ ∧ … ∧ a₂₀₀₀. [from 5, 2]
(8) D is equivalent to ¬A. So the group is both JB A and JB ¬A. [from 6, 7, 3]
(9) That violates (4). So at least one of (1) through (4) is false.
(10) Dropping (1) in favour of a certainty threshold means we are JB nothing in the report, and nothing empirical anywhere: JB survives only for logic, arithmetic and one's own current existence. Not available.
(11) So drop (2). But here is the step I cannot control. The number of conjuncts is not an epistemically relevant variable, because it is only a matter of how finely the report's content is carved into assertions: table 4 can be registered as a single assertion ("table 4 is correct") or as its 2,000 cells, and that is a presentational choice, not a change in content. By (3), whether the group is JB the content cannot turn on the choice. So if aggregation fails at 2,000 conjuncts it fails at that grain whatever the grain, and descending again by the same reasoning it fails at two: S is JB p and JB q, yet never JB (p ∧ q).
(12) But then no multi-premise argument ever confers justification on its conclusion — not the report's central finding, which rests on fourteen premises, and not this twelve-step argument, which therefore cannot rationally move anyone. Worse: the group cannot rationally sign the report at all, which is precisely what the disclaimer was supposed to make possible.
(13) So the argument has proved far too much, and I want to know where it breaks.

What I want from you:

(i) Name the step and explain why it looked fine. I would rather keep (1) than (2), and I would rather keep both the threshold and the thought that a large collaboration can rationally put its name to a report that prints a disclaimer, than accept the collapse in (12).

(ii) Price the alternatives. For each of (1), (2), (3) and (4) in turn: what exactly do you lose by surrendering it, is that loss fatal or merely awkward, and which of the losses would a defender of that premise concede rather than litigate?

(iii) If the fault is in (11), say precisely what it is equivocating between — a sorites slide from "no sharp cutoff" to "no difference," a confusion of one assertion with one content, a scope shift between "for each i, JB aᵢ" and "JB the whole conjunction"? And tell me whether rejecting (11) commits me to a principled upper bound on the number of conjuncts aggregation can handle, and if so what could fix that number other than stipulation.

(iv) The weakest-link variant, which I have not seen discussed. Our checking procedure also flags the single weakest claim, a₁₃₇₂, at P = 0.94, below threshold. Should the group delete it? If it deletes it, does the base-rate figure in (6) still apply to the remaining 1,999, or must it be re-estimated, and in which direction? If instead it keeps and flags a₁₃₇₂, does that change what the group should assert without changing what it is JB? I suspect something here turns on the difference between acceptance and belief, or between belief and assertion, and I want it said explicitly.

(v) The self-inclusive register, which is the wrinkle I think is genuinely new. Our register can be made to include the disclaimer itself as assertion number 2,001, so that D says: at least one of these 2,001 registered assertions is false. Then D cannot be the unique false assertion among them: if D were false all 2,001 would be true, including D. So D must be true, and some substantive assertion must be false. Confirm that derivation or correct it, and then say what it does to step (6): the disclaimer's truth has been upgraded from highly probable to certain by an argument of pure logic, and the preface case now contains a certainty where before it contained only a base rate. Does that break the parallel with the lottery case, where the corresponding claim — that some ticket wins — is certain from the start? If the two cases differ in exactly that respect, does any solution have a right to treat them alike?

(vi) Finally, are the lottery case and the preface case one paradox in two costumes, or does one of them survive a solution that dissolves the other? If they differ, identify which premise of my argument quietly imports a feature of only one of them.
