Longtime reader here — LessWrong back in the Slate Star Codex days, ACX comment section, that whole orbit. Please answer in American English, and no need for citations or reading recs, I just want the argument.

Kierkegaard's line about the crowd and untruth — the one everyone compresses into the slogan that the crowd is untruth — keeps getting dropped in my corner of the internet as a conversation-ender, and I want to know what precise claims it could actually be making.

Two recent uses. (1) There's a group blog where everything is posted under one shared pen name, the forty-ish members rotate, and old posts get quietly rewritten without bylines, so no post has a stable author. Someone argued that you therefore can't say the blog *has* a position, can't call it wrong, and can't hold it responsible for anything — the crowd is untruth. (2) A friend used the same line to kill talk like "the internet believed X" or "Twitter decided Y": there's no subject there, so no fact of the matter. Same slogan, pretty different work.

He was writing in the 1840s right after a Copenhagen satire paper spent a year mocking him personally, and in the book on the present age he says things like "The individual reader of the Press is not the public, and even though little by little a number of individuals or even all of them should read it, the simultaneity is lacking" and "A public, on the contrary, is an abstraction." So part of what I want is the version of the claim that's specifically about the *medium* — anonymous print, one-way, no shared situation, no one you can answer to — and whether that reading survives contact with two-way social media where every post does have a name behind it somewhere.

I tried cashing it out myself as: a view only counts as someone's if there's a person who'd eat a real cost for it being false. My friend said that's not what the slogan means. Is it one of the readings anyway?

Can you lay the candidate readings out as a menu, more or less from platitude to radical — the "think for yourself" version, the "crowds have worse epistemics / cascades and herding" version, the "no agent, so no belief, no truth, no blame" version, the "truth is a matter of how an existing individual holds a claim, with inwardness and risk, so secondhand belief isn't truth *for you*" version, whatever else belongs — and for each one tell me whether it's trivial, whether it's substantive and defensible, and whether it's actually false? I assume the crowds-are-dumber one is at least partly false given how well aggregation and markets sometimes do, but I'd rather you say so than me. Then, and this is the part I actually care about: for each reading, does it deliver what my interlocutors wanted in cases (1) and (2)? My suspicion is that the no-agent reading gets you "the blog isn't a believer" but not "the blog can't be criticized," and that it quietly lets the forty individuals off the hook — but I can't quite make that sharp.
