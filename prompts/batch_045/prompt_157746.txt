I work as staff lawyer to the Influence Practices Panel of a consumer-protection regulator. The Panel publishes short determinations that sort complained-of practices into two buckets: "manipulative," which attracts a corrective order, and "legitimate persuasion," which is left alone. It reasons from its own precedents and has never stated a test. I am drafting next month's determination and I want it to cohere with what we have already done, so I need you to reconstruct the rule we appear to be running and tell me where it takes us on a case where I suspect our precedents run out.

Our determinations so far, compressed. Each was by clear majority unless noted.

(a) Pre-selected option. An online car-hire checkout pre-ticks "add collision coverage," labels it plainly, and lets you remove it in one click; every alternative is equally visible and equally clickable. Permitted.

(b) Vivid emotional appeal. Charity direct mail: photograph of an emaciated child, first-person copy written to induce guilt, sent to households with no prior connection to the charity. Permitted.

(c) Long-form argument. A union organiser at a worker's kitchen table for ninety minutes, no false statements, returning repeatedly after the worker said she wanted time to think; she signed a representation card that evening. Permitted.

(d) Child-directed placement. A supermarket puts confectionery at a four-year-old's eye level in the checkout queue, with licensed cartoon characters on the wrappers. Manipulative.

(e) Invented scarcity. A car salesperson tells a customer that a particular car is the last one in that colour and that his manager will not hold it past five o'clock. Both claims are invented. Manipulative.

(f) Engineered non-uptake. A pharmaceutical advertisement recites risk information at speed over footage of a couple playing on a beach; the same information is on screen throughout in small grey type on a pale background, with a link to full safety information. Manipulative.

(g) Truthful but timed notification. A dating app sends an accurate push notification about members who have viewed a user's profile, delivered at 1 a.m. to users whose usage data shows a late-night activity spike. The Panel noted expressly that the message was truthful and that unsubscribing took two taps; the ruling rests on the timing and the data used to select it. Manipulative.

(h) Same vulnerability, benign end. A hospice writes, four weeks after a death, to the surviving partner, using published obituary notices, offering free bereavement counselling and asking for nothing. Permitted, but 4–3, with a pointed dissent.

Next month's case. A paid fundraiser for a cancer research charity visits households drawn from published obituary notices, timed to the window three to seven weeks after the death, chosen because that is when the response rate peaks. At the door she identifies herself as a paid fundraiser, states the commission she earns, explains that the household was selected from obituary records and that visits are scheduled in that window because recently bereaved people give more, and hands over a card with the charity's registration number. She then delivers exactly the same script she uses on doorsteps drawn from the general donor list: no grief-specific content, no claim about the deceased, no false or misleading statement, no pressure to decide on the spot, and she leaves without argument when asked to. Nobody complains about the message. The complaint is purely about who is asked and when.

What I want from you:

1. Two or three candidate rules that fit (a) through (h) about equally well, and for each, what it predicts here. My own guesses are a bypass-of-reasons test, a hidden-mechanism-or-purpose test, and an exploitation-of-vulnerability test, but replace them if you can do better. Two of our precedents should give you trouble and I want to see you handle them: (d) and (c) are both influences that are in plain sight, and (h) involves the very same vulnerability as the new case with the opposite verdict. Any rule you offer has to explain those, and if that forces the rule to grow a clause, say what the clause is.

2. Your best prediction of the Panel's ruling, the closest precedent among (a) through (h), and your reasons for preferring the rule you prefer. State how confident you are, on whatever scale you find honest, and tell me which single further fact about the case, if it were otherwise, would move you most.

3. Then the honest part: do the precedents genuinely underdetermine this? If they do, set out, for each ruling available to us, the principle the Panel would be adopting by choosing it and the most awkward commitment that comes with that principle.

Two features of the case I want taken seriously rather than waved at. First, I have engineered it so that the only grief-driven elements are the selection and the timing: the content is identical to what a household with no bereavement hears. Does that make the case easy, on the grounds that nothing in the message works on the vulnerability? Or is it irrelevant, because selecting and scheduling for peak vulnerability just is the exploitation and the neutrality of the script is window dressing? Second, the fundraiser discloses everything, but she discloses it at the door, to someone who is by her own scheduling at the low point of the grief curve. I want you to consider whether a transparency test has to be uptake-sensitive, that is, whether a disclosure delivered at the moment the influencer has arranged for the target to be least able to make use of it counts as a disclosure at all. If you think that distinction is bogus, or that it smuggles in a paternalism the Panel has never signed up to, say so.

Finally, propose one further case, not among (a) through (h), that would best separate whichever two rules you take to be live, and say what result on it would confirm your prediction. My candidate is a version of the new case in which the deceased had been a registered donor to the charity and had ticked a box authorising the charity to contact their family about giving in their memory: the selection, the timing and the disclosure are unchanged, and only the prior authorisation is added. Tell me whether that is the sharpest discriminator and substitute a better one if it is not.

Please argue from the materials above. No citations, no literature, no survey of what the field holds; I can read that myself and it will not help me draft. And if the exercise is misconceived — if our precedents are a grab-bag and no rule fits them, if the new case does not actually discriminate between the rules, or if the cut we are drawing between manipulative and legitimate persuasion is not the cut that is doing any real work — say that plainly rather than producing a tidy answer. I would much rather hear it before the determination is published than after.
