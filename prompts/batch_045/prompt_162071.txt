I write a column on science and ideas, and over the past two years I have come to lean on language models for parts of the job. I am trying to work out what I actually think about when that dependence costs me something, going by my gut on cases, and I would like a principle rather than a vibe.

Arithmetic and algebra handed to a computer algebra system, in a piece where I only need the result: no loss I care about. Letting a model summarize a paper outside my beat and writing a paragraph from that summary: a real loss, and I would be exposed if a source pushed back on it. The same paper, the same paragraph, but summarized by a colleague who works in that field: fine, or close to it. A model summarizing a paper inside my beat, where I would almost certainly catch a bad summary: mostly fine, but not entirely. Asking a model to walk me through a statistical technique until I can run it myself: clearly fine, maybe better than fine. Dictating drafts by speech-to-text: fine, even though I have noticeably lost the ability to type quickly. GPS in a city I have visited twice: fine. GPS in the neighborhood I have lived in for fifteen years: not fine. Autocomplete for a throwaway data-cleaning script: fine. Autocomplete across a codebase I will maintain for years: worry. A model producing a first draft of my column in something close to my own voice, which I then rewrite heavily: this bothers me most of all, more than the off-beat-paper case, even though I can audit it far better. Turning my own bullet points into a letter of recommendation for a former intern: I think that is fine. A condolence note to a friend: not fine, and I cannot explain why without sounding sentimental.

My tentative principle is that dependence erodes understanding when it displaces a capacity I would otherwise have exercised and when I cannot audit the tool's distinctive failure modes. But that predicts the voice-draft case should be the least troubling of the set when it is in fact the most, and it has nothing to say about why the recommendation letter and the condolence note come apart. Is there a single principle that handles all of these, or do the epistemic cost and something like an authorship or self-knowledge cost have to be tracked separately? Please state the principle, or the small set of candidates, precisely; say which case motivates each clause; say what it predicts about two cases I have not settled — using a model to pressure-test an argument of mine before publishing it, and relying on one for a medical or financial decision; and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would have me abandon. I would also like to know whether erosion of understanding is the right description at all, or whether what I am losing in the last two cases is some other thing that merely looks like it.
