So I've been reading a chapter on karma in one of those big popular "world philosophy" paperbacks a friend lent me (no author I can name, the book isn't here right now), and I want to know how much of it to trust. The chapter's summary goes roughly like this:

"Karma is just the Indian version of 'you reap what you sow,' except taken literally and run across many lives. Every deliberate action deposits a debit or a credit into a kind of moral account attached to you, and the account has to balance — so everything that happens to you, from your health to which family you're born into, is the payout of what you did earlier, mostly in lives you can't remember. It's a law of nature in the way gravity is: nobody administers it, the causation distributes the results by itself, which is how it explains suffering without needing a god to hand out punishments. That also makes it the hardest possible theory of moral desert: no excuses, no mitigating circumstances, no mercy, just what is owed. And it collapses at exactly one point — the same traditions that teach karma mostly deny a persisting self, so the person who suffers isn't the person who acted, and one stranger paying for another stranger's crime isn't desert, it's a cosmic mixup. Add in that you don't remember doing any of it, and the whole thing turns out to be retributivism with the retribution deleted."

I definately suspect some of this is a caricature and I can't tell which parts. Two specific things bug me. First, the "everything that happens to you is payout" bit sounds like the folk version, and I have a vague memory that at least the Buddhist sources tie karma to intentional action specifically and list other causes of suffering alongside it — so karma isn't meant to be the whole story of why anyone suffers. Second, the "law with no administrator and no room for mercy" line sounds too clean: don't most of the theistic versions have something doing the distributing, and isn't there a whole apparatus of merit transfer, grace, or expiation that lets the account be changed?

The bit I really can't get a grip on is the book's own editorial flourish at the end, which I haven't seen anywhere else: that karma is unique in making desert do two jobs at once, retributive and educational — the payout is supposed to be both what the agent had coming and what teaches the agent — and that this is why the "wrong person pays" objection bites harder here than it does against ordinary retributive punishment, since punishment at least usually lands on someone who remembers committing the crime. Is that an actual insight or is it invented? And more generally: is karma a theory of moral desert at all, or is it a causal story about why people end up how they do that keeps getting read as a desert claim because we're used to hearing suffering justified in those terms?
