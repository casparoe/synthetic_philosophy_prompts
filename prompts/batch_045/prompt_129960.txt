hey, hoping you can settle an argument in my reading group before it eats another whole evening.

we're on path dependence, and one of the guys keeps leaning on this picture: history is like a ball rolling down a bumpy hillside into one of several valleys. the bumps decide which valley it ends up in, and once it's down there the walls keep it there. he uses it to argue that lock-in talk is basically a hindsight story — sure, the route down is wobbly, but the valleys were already there before the ball was, and if there's one really deep valley the ball ends up in it however you nudge it at the top. so his conclusion: the same outcome would have arrived anyway, and therefore you shouldn't use path dependence to justify big institutional interventions, because there's nothing to unlock — we're already sitting in the only valley that was ever seriously available.

i pushed back and said the landscape gets carved while the ball is rolling: the ball's own passage digs channels, and the channels are what later become valley walls, so there was never a fixed menu of endpoints sitting there in advance, and the "would have arrived anyway" question doesn't have an answer even in principle. he says that's just adding epicycles, and that i'm conflating "we can't predict which valley" with "the set of valleys is created by the path." i've started calling these the given landscape vs. the carved landscape, but i dont trust that the distinction is doing the work i think it is.

can you audit the analogy? i want the correspondence laid out explicitly — ball, bumps, gravity, valley, valley walls, width of the valley, other balls on the same hill, someone shaking the hill — and then where the mapping breaks. specifically:

- what does the picture quietly assume that history doesn't hand you? a landscape that holds still while the ball moves, a ball that can't dig, a ball with no idea where it's going and no ability to pick, one ball instead of a crowd of them shoving each other around, gravity rather than something that sometimes pushes things uphill
- which of those assumptions is actually load-bearing for his conclusion and which are harmless
- and the part i care most about: suppose he's right that there's one deep valley and we always end up in it. does that settle anything? i think there's a timing point — call it convergence lag, the gap between when you're perturbed and when you reconverge — because if reconvergence takes four hundred years that's cold comfort to anyone making a decision now, and path dependence is doing real work in the meantime even if it's not doing eternal work. and i think there's a second point about what "the same outcome" is even holding fixed: maybe what reliably repeats is the slot or the function (some dominant design, some standard, some winner-take-all) and not the filler (QWERTY rather than Dvorak). is "the same outcome would have arrived anyway" ambiguous between those, and does his anti-intervention conclusion only survive on the reading where its trivially true?

last thing: if the hillside is the wrong picture, whats better? we kicked around a branching-tree version and a ratchet version, but none of us could say why either would be an improvement, or what each one would license in the way of conclusions.
