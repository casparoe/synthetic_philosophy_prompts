I came to philosophy late, after a career in experimental physics, and I have been worrying at Zeno's arrow. The dissolution I keep being offered is that velocity is a derivative, that motion is just a matter of occupying the right places at the right times, and that the "sum of zeroes" objection merely confuses countable with uncountable additivity. The last move strikes me as too cheap, so I have tried to rebuild the argument in a form that the move cannot get a grip on — countably many instants, no uncountable sum anywhere.

(1) An arrow flies one metre between t = 0 and t = 1. Take the standard "at-at" account: its motion consists entirely in its being at position x(t) at each instant t, with x continuous and non-constant.
(2) At any single instant the arrow covers no distance: it occupies exactly one point, and a point has zero length. Its instantaneous velocity, being a limit over shrinking neighbourhoods, is not the sort of thing that accumulates over instants either.
(3) Let D be the dyadic rationals in [0, 1], the instants t = k/2^n. D is countable, so the additivity that measure theory actually grants is available here without qualification; summing the zero distances over all instants in D gives a total of 0.
(4) D is dense in [0, 1]: between any two instants of the flight, however close, there lies a third belonging to D. So the sum in (3) omits no part of the flight — there are no gaps in which distance could hide.
(5) Hence the distance covered over the whole flight is 0, and the arrow does not move.

There are two things I want from you. First, I suspect the argument proves too much: run (3) and (4) again with the rationals and with length in place of distance covered, and a metre stick comes out with zero length, since a countable dense set of points "covers" it in exactly the sense I used in (4). If the argument tells equally against every positive continuous magnitude, it is not really a puzzle about motion, and I would like to know whether that is a reason to distrust it or a reason to take it seriously. Second, I would rather keep (1) and (2) if I can: I do not want to be driven to saying that the arrow is smeared over a region at an instant, or to abandoning the at-at account. So please tell me which step you reject, why that step looked sound, and what rejecting it costs — including the cost of simply swallowing the conclusion.
