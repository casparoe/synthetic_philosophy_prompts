I'm a backend engineer with basically no philosophy background — I got into this stuff because a friend sent me a blog post about Arrow's theorem and then I fell into a wikipedia hole — and I help maintain the governance tooling for a fairly large open source project. We run elections twice a year to pick maintainers and to pick which of four to six roadmap proposals get funded. Next month I'm presenting a proposal to our governance working group to change how those votes get aggregated, and I want to be ready for the question period rather than getting blindsided.

Right now we use ranked ballots with instant runoff. I want to argue for something I've been calling project-then-median: before the vote, we run a short structured deliberation step where every proposal gets placed on a single axis that we learn from past voting data plus a handful of pairwise comparison questions, we show people that embedding when they vote, and then instead of IRV we take the median voter's favorite point on the axis. My argument is that the impossibility results people like to wave around — the Gibbard-Satterthwaite thing, roughly: any deterministic voting rule with three or more possible outcomes where the winner isn't just one fixed person's top choice, and where you allow voters to have any preferences at all, can be gamed by someone misreporting their preferences — only bite if you really do allow every possible preference profile, and that if the profile you feed into the rule is single-peaked along one dimension, then median voting can't be profitably gamed at all (I think this is the old median voter result, though I might be stating it sloppily). So the whole point of the deliberation and embedding step is to make the profile single-peaked instead of hoping it is. I'm not claiming I've proven anything; I'm claiming that for our electorate the induced profile is close enough to single-peaked that strategic voting stops being worth the effort, and I have simulation results run over our last several elections that I think support that. I also have a nagging feeling that computing the embedding from people's own answers re-opens the gaming problem one level higher up, but I can't quite put my finger on how, and I'd rather not raise it myself if nobody else does.

The room is about fourteen people, and I'd break it down like this: six or seven infra and backend engineers like me who think in threat models and adversarial inputs and will ask what stops someone from just lying; two cryptoeconomics / mechanism design people who have actually read the theorem and will be annoyed if I state it imprecisely, one of whom is sympathetic and one of whom thinks the whole enterprise is naive; two community managers who mostly care whether a random contributor can understand the outcome and whether it looks legitimate; and one person from the foundation's legal/compliance side who will ask whether we can reconstruct and explain why a given outcome happened after the fact. Nobody there is a philosopher, and nobody wants social choice theory for its own sake — they want to know whether to ship it. There's also a pretty strong prior in that room that theory says impossible but practice is fine, since our current system seems to work okay, so I expect at least one version of this is a solution in search of a problem.
