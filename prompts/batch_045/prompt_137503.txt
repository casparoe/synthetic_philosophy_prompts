Context: I work in the policy team of a mid-sized city council, and I've just been handed the "behavioural insights" slot in induction week for our new cohort of policy advisers — about 24 of them, 90 minutes, 2pm right after lunch, in a room with a projector, a whiteboard, index cards, and no budget for cash incentives. Most are politics/economics/policy grads; roughly a third are instinctively hostile ("nanny state, just tell people the facts and let them decide"); two or three did a behavioural-econ module and think revealed preference settles the whole question. Last year I ran it as a slide deck of the greatest hits — fruit at eye level, the organ-donor default, all that — and it completly died. Half the room said "that's just marketing" and the other half said "that's manipulation dressed up as kindness," and we never got past the slogans. So this time I want them to run into the problem themselves rather than hear me describe it. The thing I want them to come away seeing is a distinction I've been chewing on and can't get straight myself. The same intervention seems to me to be able to do two quite different jobs: it can be a way of getting someone to do what they already, on reflection, want to do but predictably won't get around to — the deadline they blow, the form they never send back; or it can be a way of steering someone towards something they wouldn't choose if you asked them calmly and they had time. I've been calling these the "servant" version and the "author" version, but label them however is clearer. The distinction matters because the actual politics of nudging is nearly always about which one is being done, and you usually can't tell from the outside. There's a second thing I want them to feel, which I think of as the endorsement asymmetry: a person can sign off on an arrangement at the moment it's introduced, not knowing whether they'll be the one who later gets tempted, and then experience that same arrangement as an imposition at the moment it actually bites on them — and it's genuinely unclear which of those two moments should settle whether what was done to them was paternalistic.

So: design me a session where that shows up in what happens, not in what I say. I'd like at least one real piece of choice architecture built into the room or the sign-up before they arrive — something they don't notice until I point at it, which then becomes Exhibit A — plus a main structured activity with roles, rules and small real stakes (chocolate, status, getting out ten minutes early, a mildly annoying task, whatever works without money) in which participants have to design or pick a default/opt-out arrangement while partly ignorant of what their own later self will want, and then live under it for a while. The servant/author distinction should be something they generate out of what happened to them, not something I define on a slide, and the word "paternalism" shouldn't be used at all until the last ten or fifteen minutes. Then give me the debrief: map specific moments onto the specific thing they're meant to see, say what you predict will go wrong the first time I run it — I already know about the tiny-stakes shrug and the two people who sussed the setup early and turned into professional contrarians, so give me fixes for those plus at least one failure mode I haven't thought of — and be honest about which part of this the activity simply can't produce and I'll have to say out loud myself.
