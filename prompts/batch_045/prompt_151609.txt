Our grad reading group is finishing a term on the norms of assertion, and for the last session I want to close with something other than a summary slide. Write me a Shakespearean sonnet in the voice of a defender of the knowledge norm of assertion — the view that what you are supposed to assert is what you know — where the form does the arguing rather than the content just being described. Octave: build the case out of the phenomena it's meant to explain, not slogans: the oddity of saying "p, but I don't know that p", the way an assertor who gets challenged can't get off by saying she merely believed it, and the way knowledge rather than true belief is what she ends up on the hook for. Volta: drop in a counterexample I invented and haven't seen anywhere, so don't swap in the famous textbook ones — a source who knows that p through an unimpeachable channel but can't disclose her basis (sealed testimony, a classified cable, whatever), and who asserts p to a hearer who has strong evidence against p and no way to check her. Sestet: work the pressure that case puts on the norm — whether the assertion is permissible, whether it's defective without being blameworthy, whether it has really given the hearer any reason to believe p — and let the closing couplet turn on the distinction between a rule you can violate blameworthily and a standard of correctness that a blameless assertion can still fail to meet. Don't let the couplet just announce that the norm survives. Accurate enough that someone who knows the literature would nod, still a decent poem for someone who doesn't. 14 lines, actually rhymed, no gloss or notes after.
