I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for roughly fifteen years, so I absorbed the standard Kelly picture very early and never really stress-tested it: maximize expected log wealth, never bet more than Kelly, and — in the more ambitious form that the geometric rationality discussions push — treat the geometric mean rather than the arithmetic mean as the quantity a rational agent maximizes, because in the long run the growth-optimal agent almost surely overtakes everyone else. I recently tried to write that ambitious form out as an explicit argument, and it delivered a conclusion I cannot accept. I'd like to know where it breaks.

(1) Setup. I am offered the same favorable even-money bet repeatedly: at each round I risk a fraction f of my current wealth, and with probability 0.6 my wealth is multiplied by 1 + f, with probability 0.4 by 1 − f. Terminal wealth after T rounds is the product of the per-round multipliers.

(2) Growth theorem. The choice f* = 2p − 1 = 0.2 maximizes E[log W_T]. Moreover, as T → ∞, with probability approaching 1 it beats any other fixed fraction by a factor that grows without bound.

(3) Normative bridge. If strategy A beats every alternative on a set of outcomes whose probability approaches 1, then for a long enough sequence a rational agent should choose A. A decision rule that recommends an action that loses to an available alternative almost always is defective.

(4) Uniqueness. Nothing in (2) depends on T, and log is — I have seen this asserted — the only utility over final wealth, up to positive rescaling, whose recommendations are independent of the horizon and of how much you already have. So (2) is not merely identifying a growth-optimal policy; it is singling out one utility function as the correct one, rather than one admissible preference among many.

(5) Generality. Nothing in the argument turns on the quantity being money. It requires only that per-period outcomes compose multiplicatively and that there be many of them. So it applies to any such quantity: a community's pooled resources, the size of the population carrying my values, the fraction of value that survives each century.

(6) Application to what I actually care about. For choices about existential outcomes, then, I should maximize E[log V], where V is the value that survives.

(7) The unacceptable result. log(0) = −∞. Every action available to me carries some nonzero probability of the total loss of V. So E[log V] = −∞ for every option, the rule ranks nothing, and I cannot act at all.

What makes me most suspicious is a calculation I did while checking (2) and (3). In the setup of (1), Kelly (f = 0.2) finishes ahead of a double-Kelly bettor (f = 0.4) exactly when the realized fraction of wins is below about 0.651. At one bet that is 40 percent. At ten bets, by the binomial, it is about 62 percent. So at every horizon I will actually face, the growth-optimal strategy is not even more likely than not to finish ahead of a strategy the theory calls irrational, let alone to beat it almost surely. The asymptotic property is real, but it has not arrived yet.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Some specific things I want:

- Is the flaw in the normative bridge (3), the generality step (5), the uniqueness claim (4), or somewhere I have not spotted — for instance some equivocation I am committing between different things "dominates" might mean?
- If I give up (3), what is left? Can the growth theorem still explain what the all-in bettor is doing wrong, or does the whole apparatus collapse into expected utility maximization with one particular utility function — a theorem about a preference rather than about rationality?
- If instead I keep (3) and reject (5), what is the principled line between quantities the argument does govern (bankrolls, anything genuinely reinvested at stable odds) and quantities it does not (lives saved, civilizational survival, one-shot gambles)?
- On (7): is the minus-infinity result a real defect of geometric rationality or an artifact of writing total loss as V = 0? Does anything change if value has a floor, or if instead of declaring every option infinitely bad I compare catastrophe probabilities in the limit?
- Which of these steps would someone committed to geometric rationality themselves concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss as misunderstanding the framework?

I care about the argumentative structure, not sources; please don't spend the response on reading recommendations. If it matters for what you recommend I give up: I would rather keep (2) and the intuition that betting far above Kelly is a mistake than keep any step that forces (7).

One last request about the character of your answer. Please keep separate the things that are settled — the mathematics of growth-optimal betting and the exact assumptions it needs: known probabilities, independent and identically distributed rounds, full reinvestment, no leverage or consumption constraints — from the things that are genuinely contested among people who know this material, such as whether asymptotic dominance has any normative force for a finitely situated agent, whether log is privileged in the way (4) claims, and whether any of it transfers to existential or one-shot choices. Where you are extrapolating beyond what has actually been established, say so rather than smoothing it over, and do not present a contested position as the consensus view.
