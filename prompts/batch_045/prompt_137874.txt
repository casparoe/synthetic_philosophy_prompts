I run a 90-minute seminar on decision theory and the philosophy of action for about eighteen upper-level undergraduates, and I want one session on Kavka's toxin puzzle: a benefactor pays a large sum if, at a specified moment, you form the intention to drink a vial that will make you thoroughly miserable for a day. The money is for the intention rather than the act, and by the time you would have to drink, every reason for drinking has evaporated. Every treatment I have run into, including the usual rationalist-blog takes, leaves my students convinced this is a verbal trick about the word intention. I want them to hit it as a practical difficulty instead.

Design me an activity they actually do, not a case on a handout. No framing lecture up front: the rules and the incentives should make them try to form the intention at a set moment, discover for themselves whether they managed it, and then have to say which happened. Constraints and ingredients I want built in: something real but harmless stands in for the vial, merely unpleasant, with a genuine opt-out; the payout uses no money, so use a small privilege such as one dropped reading response or a decent chocolate; a rotating panel of peers audits a sealed written or recorded trace of the deliberation and its verdict decides the payout, so that third-person evidence of an intention becomes the thing at stake; participants are split between declaring their intention aloud and sealing it privately, and independently between spending ten minutes on a vivid rehearsal of drinking beforehand and being told to attempt it cold; everyone seals a zero-to-hundred forecast of whether they will actually drink; and the payoff moment comes five minutes into the next meeting, where the cup is simply present, unobserved, unrewarded, and the only record is whether it is empty.

Give me the timeline, the exact wording of the instructions you would put on the cards, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the intending-versus-trying-to-intend worry, onto whether an agent who knows it will not act can still intend, onto the bluffing and deterrent cases where we seem to pull it off, and onto first-person authority versus the audit. Then tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time: gaming the audit, experimenter demand, students drinking only to look consistent, participants who write the sentence and feel nothing, and the ethics of making people miserable in a classroom if only slightly. Finally, be frank about what the exercise cannot reproduce at all, above all the genuine cost and the strict absence of any reason to act, and say plainly which moving part the facilitator has to supply in words afterwards.
