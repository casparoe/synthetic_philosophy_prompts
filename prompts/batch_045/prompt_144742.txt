I'm the one philosopher on the university's Committee on Naming and Memorials, and on Thursday I give a ten-minute opening statement before public comment, then it's questions until they cut me off. The case in front of us (its under embargo, so I'll keep it vague) is a dormitory named in 1927 for a benefactor, with a bronze dedication plaque praising him as something like "a friend of learning and of all who seek it." A letter that surfaced in the archives last year makes it pretty clear he was the person who pushed hardest to get the university's first Black instructor dismissed in 1913. Composite details, don't try to look it up.

Here's the argument so you've got something to aim at. Commemoration, I want to say, isn't a speech act that happened once in 1927 and is now over and done with; it's an ongoing, institutionally sustained assertion, and its content is fixed by the dedicatory text rather than by the man's actual character — the plaque asserts "friend of all who seek it," not "gave us a lot of money." I've been calling the upshot the lapse condition: the honoring holds only while the predicates in the inscription still hold, and evidence arriving later can defeat it. Assertions can go false on later evidence, and going on making a falsified assertion is a defect of a different kind from giving offense or being in bad taste, so we have a reason to act that doesn't depend on anyone's hurt feelings and that isn't touched by the "you can't erase history" line. But the remedy is revision, not erasure: re-inscribe, annotate, keep the name in the ledger as a corrected entry rather than a deleted one. And I want to bolt on an asymmetry I'm much less sure about — that a statue re-performs the honoring every time you walk past it, whereas a name, once nobody remembers who the guy was, has quietly decayed into a bare label, so the case for taking down an effigy is structurally stronger than the case for renaming a building, all else equal.
