I have no formal training in philosophy or decision theory — I came to all of this through forecasting contests and reading on my own — and I've written down where I've landed so far to find out whether it hangs together.

1. Confidence is sometimes an interval, and sometimes it has to be. On "the ceasefire holds at least thirty days" I'd say 0.25–0.5, and picking 0.38 inside that would be inventing information I don't have. So imprecision is sometimes rationally required, not merely permitted.
2. The interval isn't shorthand for a distribution over the numbers inside it. I have no view about how the weight is spread, and "take the midpoint" or "average across the range" strikes me as sneaking back in a sharp number I don't have.
3. For action I use the permissive rule: an option is open to me if it comes out best by the lights of at least one number in the interval, not only if it comes out best by all of them. Optimizing against the worst case would tell me to do nothing whenever my interval straddles the relevant threshold.
4. I also hold that I shouldn't take on a bet, or a combination of bets, that loses money for every number in the interval — and I apply that as I go: when a new bet is offered, I ask whether it is licensed given what I already hold.
5. For sequences of decisions, I plan by backward induction: what matters is the plan I'd want to be following once I get there, and I reject binding myself in advance to a plan I can see I'll want to abandon. I also want every step of the plan to be one I'd still endorse when I reach it.
6. On disagreement with someone I count as my equal: I widen my interval rather than move it. If you say 0.6–0.7 and I say 0.25–0.5, the honest result is something like 0.25–0.7, not a compromise point.
7. But I also think that, disagreement aside, more information shouldn't leave me less precise. I once worked out an example where learning one new datum widened my interval for the next outcome from roughly 0.45–0.55 to roughly 0.3–0.8, and I concluded I must have set the example up wrongly.

One concrete verdict: my interval for rain tomorrow is 0.2–0.5. A bookie offers, first, to sell me a ticket paying $1 if it rains for $0.45, and then, once I hold it, to buy it back for $0.35. I say take the first and decline the second — and I'd justify that by backward induction, not by any commitment to a particular number inside the interval.

Which of these pull against each other, and how badly? Rank them rather than treating every tension as equally serious, tell me which single commitment you think has to go and why, and be explicit about how confident you are in each call and what would change your mind. I'd rather have a definite verdict I can argue with than a survey of the options.
