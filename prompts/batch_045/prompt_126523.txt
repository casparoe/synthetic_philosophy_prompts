So this has stopped being hypothetical for me and I need a number.

I run a tiny design studio, three employees. When I was in my twenties at an ad agency I padded my timesheets pretty routinely — not hugely, an hour or two a week, and everyone on that floor did it and nobody ever said anything. I havent done anything like that in fifteen years. Last month I found out one of my employees has been rounding his hours up, same sort of thing, same magnitude. I want to sit him down and tell him, formally, that what he did was wrong and that it needs to stop.

My partner says I can't. Not shouldn't — can't. Her claim is that there's a genuine normative defect in blame coming from someone who's done the same thing, that it isn't just bad manners or bad strategy, and that the defect is in me rather than in what I'd be saying. I think that's way too strong: what he did was wrong, I know it was wrong, I'd be right, and my old timesheets don't make my sentence any less true. She says truth was never the issue.

Two complications before I get to what I actually want. First: my partner also padded her hours at that same agency, got caught, was formally disciplined, and paid the money back. She thinks that's precisely why she can be the one to have this conversation with him and I can't — that going through the process is what gives you the standing. I think that's a wild view but I can't say exactly why. Second: the inflated hours came out of a shared bonus pool, so the people he actually shortchanged are the other two in the studio, not me. I could deliver the reprimand as their representative rather than on my own account. Does that fix it?

Here's the proposition I want your credence on, as precisely as I can state it:

A person who has committed a wrong of roughly the same type and seriousness as the one she is blaming someone else for, who was never caught, punished or called out for it, and who has not acknowledged it to the person she is blaming, can still blame that person fully — where fully means the blame is not in any degree undermined *as blame*: no loss of standing, no reduction in its force, and no extra entitlement in the target to resent it beyond whatever entitlement he would have anyway to push back on a true verdict.

My credence that this is possible is about 0.7; my partner's is about 0.15. I want yours, but mostly I want the decomposition. As I see it the number has to come apart into things like:

- whether there is any standing condition on blame at all, as opposed to hypocrisy being merely a conversational or strategic fault;
- if there is, whether failing it kills the blame outright or only discounts it;
- whether the relevant condition concerns the blamer's history, or what she implicitly claims in blaming — something like authority, or equal membership in the moral community, or commitment to the norm she is invoking;
- how similar the two wrongs have to be for the problem to arise in the first place, and who bears the burden on that;
- my partner's suffered-penalty idea: does being caught and paying it back restore standing, and if so what is doing the work — the punishment, the acknowledgment, or the reform;
- the proxy question: does blaming on someone else's behalf cure a standing defect, or just relocate it.

Give each of those, plus anything else you think belongs, a rough probability and one line of justification. Tell me how you are combining them and which single sub-question the final number is most sensitive to. If you think the proposition as I've stated it collapses two different questions, split it and give me two numbers instead.
