I have been reading the rationalist blog scene since roughly the mid-2000s, and the reading group that came out of it — these days more technical, less inclined to settle things by appeal to the Sequences — has converged on a single analogy that it now uses to close out every argument about quantum foundations. I want the analogy audited rather than endorsed or refuted, because my suspicion is that it is carrying conclusions it cannot bear.

The analogy: the quantum state is to a physical system what a probability distribution is to a shuffled deck, a summary of an agent's uncertainty rather than a description of a thing. The wavefunction is bookkeeping. Collapse is conditioning on a new observation, the two-slit pattern is what interference of ignorance looks like, and there is no measurement problem for the same reason there is no problem about how a spreadsheet changes when you type a number into it.

The work it is made to do in our context is specific, and I want the audit addressed to that work. First: if the state is bookkeeping, the realist owes no story about what happens between preparation and measurement, and the choice between branching, collapse dynamics, and hidden variables collapses into a choice of notation. Second, and this is the part people actually care about: a whole family of arguments that treat the branches as populated and then count them — quantum suicide and quantum immortality reasoning, anthropic probability across branches, indifference to outcomes that occur in only some branches, the decision-theoretic case for caring about measure rather than about existence — loses its footing, on the grounds that you cannot do anthropics over a bookkeeping device. The group has been using the analogy to dismiss that family as a category error. I want to know whether the dismissal is earned.

Please do the following. Give the mapping explicitly, as a table of correspondences: what plays the role of the agent, of the unknown, of the sample space, of the prior, of the likelihood, of the observation, of the update rule. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for those two uses from the ones that are decorative. Here are the ones I keep going back and forth on, and I would rather have them adjudicated than listed:

- Monogamy. My uncertainty about a deck is shareable: I can tell ten people everything I know and each of them is left exactly as uncertain as I am. Entanglement is not shareable in that way, since maximal correlation with one system forecloses maximal correlation with another. Does that mark a difference between belief states and quantum states, or only a difference between beliefs about classical correlations and beliefs about quantum ones?
- Cloning. A probability distribution is copyable without limit. An unknown pure state is not. Which premise of the analogy does no-cloning break, and does the break matter for the uses above?
- Phase. A distribution over hidden states has no room for relative phase, and the interference that phase produces is the entire explanatory payload of the two-slit experiment. Is the analogy quietly importing a sample space it is not entitled to?
- Multiplicity of agents. Two agents conditioning on different evidence get different distributions over the same deck, and we know how to merge them. Wigner and his friend assign different states to the same system, and on at least some readings there is no fact of the matter that reconciles the two assignments. Does the analogy survive this, or does surviving require it to turn into a different view — relational, perspectival, agent-indexed — that no longer licenses the conclusions people draw from it?
- Where the thing lives. The state of N particles is a function on a 3N-dimensional configuration space rather than on the space we occupy. What does the analogy commit its users to saying about that, and is it a commitment they have noticed they are making?
- Irreversibility is not the tell. Shuffling is irreversible in practice, and nobody takes thermodynamic irreversibility to show that entropy is ontic, so please do not rest the case on collapse being hard to undo. Tell me what does distinguish an epistemic state from an ontic one, if anything does, and what follows if nothing cleanly does.

One dialectical point should be settled as part of the audit, because it has split the group. Some of us say the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph result from 2012 already settles it: modulo a preparation-independence assumption, models in which the quantum state is mere information about an underlying physical state cannot reproduce the predictions of the theory. Others reply that the result is stated over hidden-variable models with ontic states, and therefore never touches an agent-centered view that declines to posit such states, so it is being over-read when deployed against the analogy. Who is right, and what does the answer do to the rest of the audit either way?

Last: someone has floated a replacement — the wavefunction is like the fog-of-war map in a strategy game, a real data structure in the engine whose contents are nonetheless fixed by what a player has seen, and which constrains what can be rendered later. Does that smuggle less than the shuffled deck, or does it conceal the same smuggling one level down, since it assumes a single shared terrain behind all the maps? If neither works, propose one that does, and say what it would cost us to switch.
