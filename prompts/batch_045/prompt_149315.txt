In I Am a Strange Loop Hofstadter argues that a self comes into being whenever a system acquires a symbol that refers to itself: the "I" is a self-referential symbol, and the loop it closes just is the self. Read alongside the Lucas–Penrose argument, I take him to be claiming something stronger — that Gödel's theorem shows no formal system can capture this kind of self-reference from within, so anything possessing a genuine "I" must outrun its own rules and cannot be a machine; that, presumably, is why the self ends up as a non-algorithmic residue.

That suggests a tractable engineering question. Suppose we give a language model a "loop-anchor": a persistent self-token holding as complete a formal description of the model's own architecture and weights as we can write down, re-injected into its context at the start of every episode, with the model fine-tuned to re-emit the token whenever it refers to itself. Get the anchor's description detailed enough and the loop should close on its own, yielding a system with an "I" in Hofstadter's sense — and hence, on his view, moral standing.

Is descriptive completeness really the right dial here, or does the grip of the loop depend on something else about how the self-token is taken up? And how should we tell when such a loop has crossed into personhood — would Hofstadter's account make that threshold sharp, or a matter of degree?
