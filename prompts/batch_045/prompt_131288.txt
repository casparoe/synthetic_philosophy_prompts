I have a favour to ask, and it is a slightly technical one.

Eighteen months ago my father died. In the aftermath I began sitting with a Buddhist group, and I am now the member of the family who has to tell my mother, before the end of next month, that she can no longer live in her house on her own. My brother-in-law, who teaches philosophy, says that what I am being taught is just consequentialism in a different vocabulary: count dukkha instead of pleasure and pain, count the agent's mental state among the outcomes, and you have altered the currency of the theory but not its structure. I think he is wrong, and I cannot say where. I want a case to put to him, and I would like you to build it.

Here is the framework I am working from, stated plainly so that we are not arguing about doctrine. An unwholesome action is one that springs from greed, aversion or delusion and entrenches them; a wholesome one springs from their absence. The training rules against killing, taking what is not given, sexual misconduct, false speech and intoxication are undertaken as training, not held as absolute commands. What is aimed at is the ending of dukkha, understood as something broader than felt suffering. Nothing in what follows may appeal to rebirth, to consequences in later lives, to ritual purity, or to the intrinsic badness of an intention's merely feeling bad.

What I want: a case in which two agents perform the same overt action, the same words and the same outward behaviour, in relevantly identical circumstances, and in which that framework gives them opposite verdicts, permissible or required for one and impermissible for the other. The only difference between the two agents may be their training, their settled dispositions of mind: not their information, not their beliefs, not their roles, not their relationships. At the same time, the expected hedonic consequences must be exactly equalised, so that a hedonic act-consequentialist has no ground on which to distinguish them and must return the same verdict for both. Hold fixed the effects on everyone's felt experience, on how the action lands, on the subsequent relationships, and on each agent's own future peace of mind. In particular, the difference in verdict may not be realised in anything observable: no difference in tone, timing, wording, or in what anyone comes to believe.

Constraints on the scenario itself: one paragraph, no more than 120 words. Ordinary contemporary setting, a family or a workplace or a hospital. No monks, temples or retreats, no exotic metaphysics, no stipulating that either agent is enlightened. And for whichever agent is told not to act, say in one clause what the framework has that agent do instead.

Then, in no more than 400 words of discussion:

1. A small table, rows for the two agents, columns for the overt act, the expected hedonic consequences, the verdict of the Buddhist framework, and the verdict of the hedonic act-consequentialist.
2. A derivation rather than an assertion of each verdict: show exactly where the consequentialist's tie comes from, and show which commitment forces the Buddhist difference, naming in one clause the recognisable Buddhist commitment you are leaning on, since my teacher will notice if you put words in its mouth.
3. Name the feature doing the work, and say what becomes of the case if that feature is removed: if the framework is barred from appealing to the roots from which the action springs, does the case collapse back into the consequentialist verdict, or does something survive?
4. My brother-in-law's best objection. He will go for the equalisation: if the two agents' states of mind genuinely have no differential consequences, he will say, then you are not describing a rival consequentialism but adding a non-consequentialist value and calling it ethics. Give the strongest reply you can find on the framework's behalf.

I do not know in advance whether the case can be built, and I would rather be told that it cannot than handed one that smuggles in the verdict. So finish with one sentence of justification, and then a final line in exactly the form below, naming the diagnosis you take to be correct.

A: the case works, and the divergence is genuinely non-consequentialist, permissibility tracking a property of the agent that is not itself a consequence.
B: the case works only by counting additional consequences after all, so my brother-in-law's description is the right one.
C: the case cannot be built as specified, since once consequences are equalised the two frameworks must agree.
D: the case works but only verbally, since once intention is treated as partly constitutive of the action the two agents are not performing the same act and nothing follows about the structure of the theories.
E: the case works, but what does the work is something generic, a role or relationship or special obligation, rather than anything distinctively Buddhist.
F: none of these, in which case state the correct diagnosis in the sentence before.
