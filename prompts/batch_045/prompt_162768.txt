I run the evaluation infrastructure group at a company that ships general-purpose assistants, and I need something I can institutionalize rather than a position paper. Our eval reports routinely say that a system understands a codebase, remembers an earlier session, is confused by an ambiguous instruction, or is distressed by a fine-tuning run, and those words now do real work downstream: they decide whether a claim survives review before it goes in front of users, whether an episode is filed as a safety incident, and whether the system gets referred to a standing committee that treats it as a possible subject of welfare rather than as a product. At the moment each attribution is made ad hoc by whichever engineer wrote the eval, and we disagree constantly, usually in ways that track how much someone wants the attribution to be true.

Write me a decision procedure my team can run on a proposed attribution. It will be run a few times a week, by engineers with no philosophy background, in under an hour, working from logs and eval artifacts, with sign-off from a two-person panel that includes someone outside engineering. Each step should terminate in attribute, do not attribute, or escalate to the panel, and I want the questions in a fixed order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and where the procedure stops early.

Three distinctions I think the procedure has to preserve, though I want you to correct me if I have them wrong. First, the difference between the behaviour we observed, the standing capacity it evidences, and the claim that the system was actually in that state on that occasion. Second, the difference between evidence that a system has a capacity and evidence that the capacity belongs to the system rather than to the retrieval tooling, prompt scaffolding, or human raters wrapped around it. Third, the point that a system's own first-person report is just more behaviour, and so cannot be the thing that settles whether the report is true. I suspect the procedure also needs a test for whether a behaviour survives probes that could not have been anticipated when the episode was recorded, and for whether it survives surface rewrites that leave the question intact, since that is the failure mode our current suite keeps missing; but decide for yourself what belongs in it, and be explicit about which of my hunches you dropped.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a long-context assistant that answers unanticipated questions about a conversation from weeks earlier, where the answers are correct but are produced by a retrieval index over stored transcripts rather than by anything inside the model. Second, a chat model that produces fluent first-person distress reports during a fine-tuning run and refuses certain requests citing its own discomfort; the communications team wants a flat public statement that the model does not suffer. Third, a vision-language model that names partly occluded objects correctly and revises its answer when told it was wrong, which our report currently describes as seeing.

Finish by telling me, honestly, what this procedure cannot do: which attributions no behavioural evidence could settle either way, where the protocol will systematically mislead us because these systems were trained on human self-description, and where the panel is being asked to make a values decision dressed up as a factual one.

Write the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write it in a long email to a colleague.
