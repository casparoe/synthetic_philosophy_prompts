I'm a TA for a 24-person undergrad course that's basically decision theory for philosophers — mostly second- and third-years, not very mathy. I've got one 75-minute slot next week, a projector, a stack of index cards, no budget at all, though I can rustle up a single decent prize (a nice bottle of something, or a fat book token) and a bag of good chocolate. I was talking this over with a friend who does econ and she said the version I usually run is too easy to shrug off, and I think she's right.

Last term I did the plain two-urn thing — one urn with a known 50/50 mix, one with an unknown mix, you pick a colour and you pick an urn — and it half flopped. A third of the class said "isnt that just risk aversion?" and I didnt have a clean way to show them it wasn't. And the people who did see the pattern only saw it in the aggregate: nobody felt like *they* had done anything odd. I want them to catch themselves doing it.

Two wrinkles I've been playing with, which you should build the session around (or replace if you can see something better): (1) alongside the known urn and the mystery urn, add a third urn whose composition is produced by a transparent random process right in front of them, but the result goes into a sealed envelope that nobody — me included — will ever open. It's pure risk by construction and I bet it still feels like the mystery urn, which should separate "I dislike unknown probabilities" from "I just dislike coin flips." (2) A two-parter where the same student contradicts themselves. First I offer a ticket that pays if a fair coin lands heads *and* red comes out of the mystery urn, or tails *and* black comes out of the mystery urn — so its chance of winning doesn't depend on the composition at all, and it looks worth exactly the same as a ticket on the known urn. Then I flip the coin in front of them and offer to swap their coin-ticket for a known-urn ticket for a small fee. My guess: indifferent before the flip, happy to pay after it, whichever way it lands — so they've paid to undo a choice they just said they were indifferent about.

What I actually need: rules and materials; some way to get a number out of each student rather than a show of hands (I was thinking anonymous numbered index cards I hand back at the end, so each person can see their own round-2 answer sitting next to their round-5 answer); payoffs that work with chocolate and one shared prize and no cash; a minute-by-minute timeline; and a debrief that maps particular moments onto the sure-thing principle, the reduction of compound lotteries, and whatever goes wrong when someone prefers the known urn for red *and* the known urn for black — but with me never naming any of that up front, and the labels arriving only at the end. Also: how to run the mystery urn honestly (I dont want to rig it or lie about what's in it, and somebody always accuses me of rigging it), what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and an honest note on what it can't show. My sense is it can establish that something has to give but not which formal model of the behaviour is right, and it definitely can't establish that anyone is being irrational. No reading lists or citations, just the session.
