---

Two connected questions.

(a) State the argument from the apparent absence of other civilizations (the Fermi paradox) in its strongest form, in your own words. Your statement should make clear three things. (i) Why the reply that they simply have not had time to get here is very hard to sustain: the Galaxy is roughly 13 billion years old and roughly 100,000 light years across, so even at one percent of the speed of light a crossing takes on the order of ten million years. (ii) What the argument has to assume about what a civilization capable of interstellar settlement would leave behind that we could in fact detect from here — that is, where the load-bearing empirical assumption sits. (iii) What exactly the argument is a claim about, given that the number of such civilizations is not a fixed quantity but the product of several factors each uncertain over many orders of magnitude. In particular, distinguish the claim that the expected number of detectable civilizations in the Galaxy is large from the claim that it is very unlikely that we are the only one, and say which of those two the argument actually needs.

(b) In the great filter framing, the probability of a galaxy-visible civilization is treated as a product of the probabilities of a series of steps: habitable planet, abiogenesis, complex cells, intelligence, technology, long-term survival. Suppose tomorrow we found a genuinely independent second origin of life in our own solar system — say a microbial lineage in the ocean of Enceladus whose biochemistry is unrelated to ours, not descended from it and sharing no ancestor with it. Contamination ruled out, independence granted. What would that discovery tell us, and what would it not tell us, about (i) where the filter lies and (ii) whether we should be cheered or worried? Explain the structure of the inference. In doing so, say what role if any is played by the timing evidence we already have: life on Earth turns up about as early in the record as it could, whereas intelligence and technology turn up very late, close to the end of Earth's habitable window.

---

And here is the answer, pasted as submitted:

=== BEGIN ===

(a) Fermi's remark, if the story is right, was a lunchtime one: if they exist, where is everybody? The strongest version of the argument does not rest on the number of planets, which nobody disputes is vast. It rests on three claims together. First, a timescale point: the Galaxy is old and, on the available speeds, small. Second, an expansion point: any civilization that can cross between stars will, given a few tens of millions of years, either settle the Galaxy or fill it with anything from probes to industry, and there has been time for this to happen not once but hundreds of times over. Third, a visibility point: a galaxy in which this had happened would not look to us the way ours looks. So no such civilization has ever arisen anywhere in the Galaxy before now, and since the number of places where it could have arisen is enormous, at least one of the steps leading to one must be fantastically improbable.

On (i): the no time yet reply is in bad shape. At one percent of light speed, a hundred thousand light years takes about ten million years, which is less than a thousandth of the Galaxy's age. For the timescale reply to work, the very first civilization would have to have arisen within the last few tens of millions of years — but that is close to what the argument is denying, so the reply assumes nearly all of what it needs to show. It is not that they have not had time. It is that they have had, in effect, all of time.

On (ii): the load-bearing assumption is the third one, visibility, and it is the soft spot. The argument inherits its strength from a speculative story about what expansion leaves behind and about whether it stays: waste heat, reprocessing of a large fraction of starlight, artifacts that survive for geological time. If a civilization expands into something we would not recognise as expansion — if it gets quiet, or turns inward — then the argument goes quiet with it. I do not think this refutes the argument, but it does mean the argument is only as strong as a piece of futurology that we are in no position to check, and a good statement of it should own that rather than hide it in the word detectable.

On (iii): this is where it gets interesting. The number of civilizations is a product of maybe seven or eight factors, each uncertain over several orders of magnitude. If you put broad distributions over them, the expected value of the product can be huge while the probability that it is even as large as one stays modest, because the expectation is dominated by a thin region of parameter space in which every single factor happens to be favourable at once. Call this the tail point. It is real, and I think it is the most serious objection on the table.

But I do not think it rescues the skeptic, and here is why. A probability is only the expectation of an indicator. If the expected number of detectable civilizations is large — a thousand, say — then the possibilities in which the number is small cannot be where most of the expectation is coming from, since a possibility in which the number is one contributes only one unit to the total. The bulk of the mass has to sit on the possibilities with many civilizations in them; otherwise you could not average that high. So a large expectation does after all put a low probability on our being alone, and the paradox survives the tail point in essentially its original form. The tail point shows the median may be low; but the argument does not need a high median, it needs a low probability of loneliness, and that is what a high expectation gives you.

(b) A second genesis would be momentous, and for this question it would do one thing decisively: it would knock abiogenesis out of the running as the filter. Two origins in one corner of one galaxy, with unrelated biochemistries and no possibility of transfer, means the chemistry-to-life transition is not a near-miracle. Admittedly our sample is one planetary system and the effective n is two, so the error bars on the underlying rate are still wide, and I would not want to overclaim how common life is. But the rare-Earth style picture, which puts the improbability at the level of habitability or of getting life started at all, takes a real hit; the hard steps picture, which puts it later, gains.

And I think this is good news, for a reason that is worth stating carefully. The filter is a conjunction, so if one conjunct turns out to be easy, whatever improbability is left has to be concentrated in the others. But the others have all already happened here. Complex cells happened, multicellularity happened, intelligence happened. And the timing evidence fits this: life appears at the very start of the record, which is what you would expect if abiogenesis is the easy step, and intelligence appears as late as the window allows, which is what you would expect if the later steps are the hard ones. Hard, but done. So the improbability is in our past rather than our future, and the silence is explained by nobody else having got even this far — which is a lonely conclusion but not a threatening one.

The bad news scenario was the other one: that abiogenesis is a near-miracle we somehow passed, and that what waits ahead of us is a filter we have not yet met. A second genesis would rule that out. So: cheered, not worried, and the bad news is only that we are probably alone.

=== END ===
