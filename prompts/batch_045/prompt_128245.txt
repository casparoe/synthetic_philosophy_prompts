Someone in a rationalist group chat killed a thread the other night by posting "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" as if that was the end of it. The thread was about Pascal's mugging — stranger offers you a deal, give me five bucks or I'll use my matrix powers to create 10^60 people and torture them forever — and whether you're rationally required to pay. He seemed to think the slogan settles it, and everyone moved on, but I cant tell what work it was even supposed to do, since the mugging already has an absurdly low prior built right into it and the expected-value problem still goes through.

So: lay out the different precise claims that one-liner could be standing for. The boring Bayesian one about priors and likelihood ratios; something about fixed thresholds on priors; something about how strong the justification for the prior has to be; something about burden of proof rather than about belief; the error-statistical/severity reading where the point is that no test could have distinguished the claim being false; something about base rates of lying and craziness in testimony; and whatever else is hiding in there. For each, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible principle, or actually false, and — this is the part I care about — whether it would really block the mugging or just restate the intuition.

Two cases to run them against, because I think they split readings that otherwise look the same. One: the no-mugger version — you find a sealed contraption in your attic with a button and an engraved plate saying it either does nothing or creates 10^60 tortured people; nobody claimed anything, there's no testimony and no claimant. Two: the mugger who first does a small genuine miracle in front of you, so the extraordinary evidence is arguably supplied.

Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence — which readings, if any, actually do the work — then give me the menu and the verdicts, no preamble, in a form I can paste back into the chat.
