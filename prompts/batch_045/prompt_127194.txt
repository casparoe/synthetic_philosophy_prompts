Me and two friends have had a reading group going since January on consciousness — I work on game engines, one of them does ML, the other is a doctor, none of us are philosophers — and we keep going in circles on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, mostly because everyone reaches for an analogy and then argues from the analogy instead of the view. The analogy my ML friend keeps using is the desktop interface: she says qualia are basically like icons on a screen — there really is something on the display, you really can drag it, talk about it, make mistakes about it, but there is no little picture file inside the machine, and the folder on your desktop is not literally a folder, everything that actually happens is files being read and written somewhere you never see. She uses it to license a fairly strong conclusion: that once you have told the story about how the system presents its own activity to itself, there is no leftover hard problem, and all the what-it-is-like talk is interface talk rather than a claim about what exists. My other friend says the desktop picture is rigged from the start because it imports a user sitting outside the screen, a designer who put the icons there on purpose, a distinction between what is displayed and what is doing the work, and a screen for it all to appear on — and a brain, he says, has none of those. His replacement is from my own field: phenomenal properties, he says, are like baked lighting in a level. You precompute the shadows and the glow into the texture atlas at build time; they carry genuine information about where the light sources are relative to the geometry, they are really there in what you see, but nothing is computed there at runtime and they push nothing around in the physics or the AI. He then uses that to argue illusionism is epiphenomenalism in a nicer jacket: either the baked-in appearance does something at runtime, in which case illusionism is just a causal-functional story and has not touched what it was supposed to dissolve, or it does nothing, in which case illusionism owes us an account of how there can be an illusion with nobody being appeared to, and of why we take ourselves to have some specially authoritative access to it.

What I want is an audit of both analogies rather than a verdict on illusionism itself, and I would rather you not point me at reading — I have tried and I just get the same three blog posts, so lay the argument out in your own terms. For each one, give me the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the user, the designer, the display, the drag-and-drop, the file operations, and what plays the role of the light source, the bake step, the texture, the runtime render, the player and the renderer. Then separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the work each analogy is being made to do from the ones that are just noise, and say which features of the source each one quietly imports that the target does not have — an external observer, purposes, a compile/runtime split, an author. Then: does either conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole thing? If one of them is closer to what illusionists are actually committed to, say which and why; if both are bad, say that plainly instead of producing a third one to be even handed. And if there is a better way to frame what illusionism is claiming — an analogy, or a non-analogical way of stating the commitment — give me that too, with a note on what it still leaves unexplained.

Also, since we are not experts and will probably just argue from whatever you send: put a rough confidence on each of the substantive claims you make, and single out the one you are least sure of. I suspect the least certain bit is whether an illusionist would even accept the baked-lighting picture as theirs, since the whole point might be that there is no rendering pass and no one watching, but tell me if you think the shakier part is somewhere else.
