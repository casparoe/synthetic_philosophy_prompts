**Question 4 (40 marks).** The course has distinguished three claims. They are logically independent, and part of what you are being asked to do is to keep them apart.

**(R) Representational realism about trained networks.** At least some internal states of a trained network have semantic content: they are about features of the world. This is part of the explanation of why some interpretability claims are true rather than merely convenient.

**(I) Instrumentalism.** Ascriptions of content to internal states are projections that help us predict and steer the system. Nothing inside the network is about anything, though the talk may be indispensable in practice.

**(P) Decomposition plurality.** For any decomposition of a layer's activity into interpretable units or features that supports successful prediction and successful causal intervention (ablating the unit changes behaviour in the direction predicted), there is another decomposition, built from an entirely different set of directions in activation space, that does just as well. The units of the two decompositions do not map onto one another, and nothing in the network's architecture, training objective, or behaviour on any test singles out one decomposition as the correct one. Note that (P), as stated, is silent about whether internal states have content: it is a claim about how many decompositions there are, not about whether any of them is content-bearing.

**(a)** (15 marks) Does the plurality scenario settle the dispute between (R) and (I)? **Student's answer.**

Start by getting the three positions clear. (R) says that some internal states of a trained net have semantic content, and that this is why certain interpretability claims are true rather than just handy. (I) says that content talk is a projection: useful, maybe indispensable for us, but nothing in the network is about anything. (P) says that the decomposition into units is not unique — that several incompatible sets of features can do the same predictive and causal work.

The plurality scenario settles the matter in favour of (I). The reason is simple. The realist wants to say that a given direction in activation space *is* the model's representation of, say, bridges. But plurality says there are many bases, each supporting the same successful interventions: knock out this direction and the model stops completing the bridge, and the same is true of the corresponding direction in the alternative basis. If there is no fact of the matter about which decomposition is the right one, then there is no fact of the matter about what the network represents. And if there is no fact of the matter about what it represents, then it does not represent anything. This is not a coincidence: the realist is committed to there being a single correct decomposition, since that is what it means to say that the network's states have content. So the plurality result is a straight refutation of realism.

The point is really the familiar underdetermination point in a new setting, and the analogy with coordinate systems makes it vivid. There is no privileged coordinate system for describing a physical system; anyone who said that the x-axis is real would be making a category mistake. Features are the coordinate systems of activation space. So talk of the feature the model uses is a façon de parler, which is just the instrumentalist's view. The same moral was drawn in the older debate about systematicity: the critics of connectionism argued that neural networks cannot represent anything at all, because they lack compositional structure, and the plurality result is the modern, evidence-backed version of that argument.

That also settles the press line. Saying that researchers have located the concept of X inside the model is indefensible once plurality is on the table: what they located was one convenient basis among many. The pluralist should say the sentence has to be replaced with something like "researchers have found a decomposition in which a unit tracks X" — note that tracking is weaker than representing, which is why the weaker formulation is the right one. The instrumentalist can say the original sentence is fine as shorthand, since content talk is only ever a matter of what is useful for us; there is nothing more to representation than usefulness, so any decomposition that supports prediction is as good as any other and calling one of them the concept is harmless. The realist, by contrast, has to deny plurality, and since the empirical evidence supports plurality, so much the worse for realism.

One qualification, to be fair. None of this makes interpretability research worthless. Even if features are projections, a decomposition that lets you predict and steer a model's behaviour is useful for engineering and possibly for safety. What follows is that we should stop describing interpretability as reading the model's mind or finding concepts inside it, because those descriptions trade on a realism that the plurality result has just ruled out.
