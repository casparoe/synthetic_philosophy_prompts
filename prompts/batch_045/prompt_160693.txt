ok so weird situation and I need actual philosophical judgment here, not a speech-shaped object.

My hometown (about 2,000 people, northern Wisconsin, heavily German-American, the kind of place where the VFW hall does the pancake breakfast) is dedicating a new granite marker in the cemetery on November 11. The old plaque lists the 41 men from the town who died in uniform in the two World Wars. The new one adds three names at the bottom under a line reading something like "also remembered: sons of this town who died in other uniforms" — three young men from local families who ended up in German service, two of them apparently conscripted late in the war and one who went back and enlisted in the mid-1930s, before any of this. The historical society fought about it for two years and it passed 5–4; my mom is on the board and voted no. I've been asked to give the remarks, roughly eight minutes, because I'm the one who left and got a philosophy PhD, which apparently makes me the town's designated person for anything morally awkward. The room will be: families of the 41, including two or three WWII vets in their nineties in the front row who do not regard the other side as a moral peer; the families of the three, who want their dead back and have waited forty years; the guy who made the motion and the four who voted against him, sitting near each other; a local reporter; a lot of people who would rather hear nothing that sounds like an argument. So it has to land for people who'd reject any philosophizing on sight and for the handful who'd notice if I fudge the doctrine.

Here's where I want you to think carefully, and I want your own view rather than the standard moves. The obvious resource is the moral equality of combatants: combatants on either side aren't murderers for fighting, aren't liable to punishment merely for participating, and the usual defense is epistemic — an ordinary soldier can't be expected to determine whether his side's cause is just, and the institutions that decide these things aren't ones he can audit. But (a) that sits badly with honoring someone *for* what he fought for, which is what half the room is there to do, and (b) its epistemic defense looks much shakier for the man who volunteered than for the ones who were conscripted, and one of the three was a volunteer. I've also been toying with a second idea — call it "the equality of the dead" — the claim that death in battle changes the moral situation in a way the standard doctrine doesn't capture: a living combatant can still be liable to be fought and killed, but a dead one has already paid everything that could be exacted from him, so there is no outstanding debt for commemoration to forgive, and you can mourn him without thereby saying anything about his cause. I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or whether its just laundering the first doctrine into warmer language, and I can't tell what it would cost me in the room.

So: you're a professional philosopher who also knows how sentences land in a room full of people who are cold and standing up. Start by working out what can and can't be said here at all — which of those two ideas (or a third one you prefer) can actually carry eight minutes without insulting the front row, without lying to the families of the three, and without turning the occasion into a seminar; where the volunteer/conscript difference should surface and where bringing it up would be cruelty; and what the speech should simply refuse to do. My list of refusals: no "both sides suffered," no "they were all just boys," no implication that the 41 died for nothing, no implication that the three's cause was fine, no "lessons of history" framing, no theology. Brainstorm that out before you draft, and tell me briefly what you're betting the speech on and why. Then write the remarks: plainspoken Midwestern register, short sentences, no citations, no named philosophers or theories, no Latin, nothing that sounds like a commencement address. It has to end somewhere the entire room can stand.
