I'm a high school senior applying for an eight-week summer research grant to run an independent philosophy project, and I need a two-page proposal. My half-formed idea is this: in the debate over pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism, the standard cases all work the same way — something important rides on whether the subject is right, and we're asked whether she still knows. But in every version I've seen, the stakes come at her from outside: a closing time, a timetable, a deadline. I suspect this hides a variable. Compare two people with identical evidence that a bridge will hold. One is crossing because a rescue depends on it; the other is crossing because she rashly bet a friend she could do it and now refuses to back down. Same evidence, same stakes, but only one of them created the stakes herself, and my intuition is that we judge them differently in a way that contextualism, subject-sensitive invariantism, and classical invariantism each fail to explain.

Please turn this into a proposal with: (1) the question stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer, including a characterization of "self-imposed stakes" that is not merely "the subject chose it"; (2) an honest account of why each of the three standard positions leaves that question open rather than already settling it; (3) a week-by-week method for eight weeks, with no experiments and no citations (I'll add those myself), that says how an armchair project makes progress rather than just restating intuitions; (4) what a positive and a negative result would each look like; and (5) what would count as the project failing, plus what is most likely to make it too big for the time I have. Write it so that a reader who knows the bank-style cases but has never considered this distinction can follow it — I'll be defending it before a panel that is not made of specialists.
