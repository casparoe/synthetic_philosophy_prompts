I'm a second-year undergraduate in a joint philosophy-and-law programme, and this term I'm taking criminal law and a seminar on Kant's ethics simultaneously, and the two keep sliding into each other in a way I can't settle. In the philosophy seminar we're working through the question of what it takes for an action to have moral worth, and we've narrowed it to three positions I'll label D, R and V to keep things neutral. D says an action has moral worth only if the agent performs it because it is what duty requires, so that a person who does the right thing merely because inclination happens to point the same way gets no credit for it. R says this is too demanding: what matters is whether the agent was appropriately responsive to the considerations that actually make the action right, so an agent who does the right thing while believing (wrongly) that morality forbids it can still have moral worth, provided her action was suitably sensitive to the right-making features. V says both pictures are corrupted by an over-intellectualised picture of agency: the good person does the right thing for the reasons there are without consulting a moral principle, and someone who has to bring the thought of duty explicitly before her mind in order to act has one thought too many. A friend in my criminal law study group insists the whole thing is just the motive-versus-intent distinction we've been drilled on, with some extra steps, and I think he's wrong but I can't say exactly why.

So I'd like a systematic translation. I've had two and a half semesters of criminal law and I'm comfortable with the vocabulary I want you to use: motive as opposed to(intent), the graded mental-element hierarchy of purpose, knowledge, recklessness and negligence, the objective reasonable-person standard for the lowest grade, wilful blindness, transferred intent, mistake of fact, the liability-versus-sentencing distinction (in particular the way motive is excluded from the guilt phase but can return as mitigation or aggravation), and the defences of automatism and insanity. For each of D, R and V, tell me what translates into that vocabulary, what translates only if you bend it, and what cannot be expressed in it at all. Please test each translation against four cases rather than describing the views abstractly: a Huckleberry Finn style agent who does the right thing while conscientiously believing it is wrong; a shopkeeper who gives correct change only because honesty is good for trade, plus a variant in which the honest conduct is produced by a head-office compliance regime rather than by any individual's reasoning; an agent who returns a lost wallet for a self-interested reason whose factual presupposition is false (she is certain the owner is a neighbour whose goodwill she needs, and in fact the wallet belongs to a stranger who will never know), where the act is nonetheless the one the right-making considerations supported; and an agent who reliably does the right thing from something like a compulsion, with no reason she could give for it at all. I want the per-view translation to say which of these four cases it can distinguish from which, and where two views collapse into indistinguishable descriptions in legal terms.

The part I care most about is the residue report, in both directions. In one direction: which distinctions in the legal vocabulary turn out to be oblique to the philosophical ones — my worry is that intent, being a matter of what the agent aimed at, is neither the same as a motive nor the same as what philosophers call the reason for which she acted, and that the but-for test attaches to outcomes rather than to the considerations the agent was tracking, so that the legal vocabulary may have no way to express sensitivity to a reason as opposed to causation by a desire. In the other direction: what can criminal law say that the D/R/V debate cannot? Automatism, corporate agents acting through policies, strict liability offences with no mental element, and the institutional question of what to do with the agent all seem to me to have no counterpart in a debate that asks only whether an action is creditworthy, and I'd like you to say whether that is a gap in the philosophical question or just a difference of purpose. Finally, tell me whether the residue is mostly a defect of the legal vocabulary, or whether it suggests the philosophers' dispute is partly verbal — in particular whether D and R are really disagreeing about a matter of fact or about how finely to individuate the consideration an agent acted on. Please keep the translation honest: if you find yourself importing a legal assumption about what mental states are for and calling it a translation, say so explicitly, and don't let liability, which is the law's question, quietly replace moral worth, which is mine.
