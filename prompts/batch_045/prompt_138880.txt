I chair the research-integrity committee for a university hospital network, and I want to replace an argument we keep having with a procedure. We meet monthly and see twenty to thirty cases a year, almost all of them referred by a journal, a co-author, or a funder; a two-person triage pair has a week to sort each one before the full committee of nine (with legal counsel in the room and the dean signing off on any finding) spends about forty minutes on it. Our internal policy defines a handful of boxes: fabrication, falsification, plagiarism, a residual category called Questionable Research Practice that was bolted on about a decade ago, honest error, and no finding. I will stipulate our own rough glosses rather than quote the policy: plagiarism is taking someone else's words or ideas without attribution; falsification is manipulating materials or records so that the research is not accurately represented; fabrication is inventing data; Questionable Research Practice is whatever damages the record without fitting the other three. Our actual difficulty is never whether someone did something bad. It is which box the thing goes in, and the boxes keep stretching: we have spent two meetings on whether a researcher's undisclosed use of a generative language model to draft a discussion section is plagiarism, falsification, or nothing we currently have a name for, and we got nowhere because half the room was reading the case one way and half another, and neither side could say what would settle it. I recently sat in on a cognitive science lecture about analogy and fluid concepts, the kind with letter-string puzzles where you are told that abc goes to abd and asked what ijk goes to, and I recognized our committee exactly: the fight is not about the case, it is about which frame you carry over, and once you change frames the same case has two perfectly good answers.

So please adopt the role of a philosopher of concepts who has also had to draft institutional policy that non-specialists can actually run, and before you write the procedure itself, spend one short brainstorming passage listing which distinctions from work on analogy, exemplar-based and prototype-based categorization, family resemblance, and conceptual drift you intend to preserve and which you are deliberately dropping as unusable by nine clinicians in forty minutes. Then give me the procedure. Concretely: an ordered sequence of questions, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, explicit stop points (stop with a no finding, stop and refer to the full committee, stop and refer to counsel), and an explicit mark at every point where the remaining question is one of values rather than facts and therefore has to be handed to a person. Build it on the idea that our categories are anchored in a small set of named exemplar cases plus a framing, not in necessary-and-sufficient conditions: I want each classification to be written out in a fixed form, this old exemplar is to its category as this case is to blank, then to name which conceptual slot is slipping (whose words, what counts as a record, whether there is an author at all, whether the manipulation is of data or of presentation) and which rival framings are available. Please also build in four mechanisms I have been sketching and want either adopted or explicitly argued against: a slippage ledger, in which every non-central classification is logged with the slot that slipped and the frame chosen, reviewed annually; a rule for when a stretch becomes binding precedent for future coders and when instead it triggers a formal split or merger of categories; a contraction test, on the assumption that categories only ever ratchet outward, requiring the committee periodically to name a case that would fall outside each category and returning a verdict of too fluid to apply when it cannot; and a hard separation between the classification step and the severity step, because our worst arguments come from people who want a category partly because of the sanction attached to it. Finally, say plainly what we lose by treating our categories this way rather than as definitions, since I assume we lose the ability to call a classification simply wrong.

Then run the whole thing, with the step trace shown, on three cases, and commit to a verdict on each rather than describing what a committee might decide. Case one: a resident who reused, verbatim and without attribution, three paragraphs of the methods section of her own previously published paper in a new paper in a different journal, with no licensing problem flagged. Case two: a postdoc who used a large language model to draft the discussion section of a submitted paper, disclosed nothing, and produced text that contains no copied sentences but does contain two sentences stating claims about the data that do not follow from it. Case three: a long-serving technician who, in a field where this was ordinary practice when he was trained, has for years been tidying blot images, adjusting contrast and splicing lanes from different gels, and one published figure turns out to combine lanes from two gels with nothing in the legend to say so. My own instinct is that case one is trivial and case three is serious; if the procedure overturns either instinct, say so and say which step does the work, and if all three come out comfortably, identify the premise of mine that the procedure has quietly overruled. Throughout, take a definite position and give your reasons rather than surveying options, and be self-critical: mark the two or three places where you think your own design is most contestable, and flag any step that rests on an empirical assumption about how people actually categorize. Please do not reach for literature, citations, or a reading list, or for any real national research-misconduct definition you may remember; our stipulated categories are the whole world the procedure has to work in, and I want the reasoning in your own words.
