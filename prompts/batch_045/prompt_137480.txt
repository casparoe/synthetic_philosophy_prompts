I've built an argument for a reading group on the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I can't accept where it ends up.

(1) An attitude is a response to the question it settles: belief settles whether p, so a consideration is a right-kind reason for believing p only if it bears on whether p is true.

(2) Deliberative transparency: when I ask whether to believe p, the only considerations I can bring to bear are considerations of whether p is true; any other consideration — that believing p would be pleasant, lucrative, or causally useful — shows up as a reason to get myself to believe p, i.e. as a reason for acting on my own attitudes rather than for the attitude itself.

(3) So pragmatic considerations are wrong-kind reasons for belief: an offer of money is a reason to acquire the belief, not a reason to hold it. I want to keep this.

(4) Nothing above is specific to belief. For any attitude A settling question Q, the reasons for A are the considerations bearing on Q; everything else is merely a reason to produce A in oneself.

(5) Intention settles the practical question whether to φ; so the reasons for intending to φ are the reasons for φ-ing.

(6) But (5) is false. Forming the intention now is what will get me to the clinic at eight, and that is a reason to intend now even if, once I am there, going in is pointless or worse; and my intending to φ can be what others reasonably rely on, a reason for the intention even when the φ-ing itself is worthless. The natural fix is that intention settles not whether to φ but whether to φ from here — a diachronic question about my own future agency — and considerations about my later execution bear on that question directly.

(7) So the method in (1)–(2) overgenerates: it rules out reasons for intending that plainly exist. And since (5) came by exactly the route that produced (3), (3) is undefended unless something specific to belief does the work. If nothing does, pragmatic considerations can be genuine reasons for belief, and the distinction I was trying to draw collapses.

One complication I cannot place. Call a consideration fragile when it favours your having an attitude only on condition that it is not the consideration on which you hold it: my believing that a student will improve is part of what makes it true, but only if the belief is a response to her rather than to its own usefulness. Fragile considerations are pragmatic in origin, yet unlike the demon's money they seem to bear on the attitude's object. Are they right-kind or wrong-kind — and can any account that sorts them correctly also block the step from (4) to (5)?

So: which step do you reject, why did it look sound, and what does rejecting it cost? I would rather give up (1) than (3) or (6), but I can't say what should replace it. If (1) survives, tell me which belief-specific feature carries the weight — its aim, its involuntariness, its synchronic rather than diachronic structure — and whether that feature can do the work without assuming the conclusion.
