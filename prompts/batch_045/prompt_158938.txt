The view I've ended up holding, stated badly (fix it in section 1):

The experience-machine intuition is evidence about nothing except how people react to a weird, irreversible offer involving unfamiliar technology. People say they wouldn't plug in (Nozick's scenario, I think?) because it trips loss aversion, status quo bias, ambiguity aversion about identity, and a general "this is creepy" response — the same reflex that made people refuse self-driving cars and lab-grown meat for a decade. On my view, if you strip out the unfamiliarity — say everyone has been in one since birth and nobody remembers otherwise, there's an off switch, and you get a tamper-proof guarantee that the sim is stable and no one is messing with it — the reluctance shrinks to near nothing, and the residue is too theory-laden to carry the weight people put on it. So the machine doesn't refute the idea that what's good for you is how your life feels from the inside. It just shows people are bad at thought experiments about new tech.

One thing I think the debate gets wrong: everyone argues about one variant of the machine when there are at least three, plus a parameter. (a) the "caching" variant — pure feel-good output with no causal structure behind it, like reading a recorded log of a program's output instead of running the program; (b) the "auditable sim" — the life inside has real causal structure, your simulated work really causes simulated outcomes, and you get a verifiable, tamper-proof trace you could hand to an outside auditor; (c) the "shared sim" — other real minds are in there with you and what they experience really depends on what you do. And separately: did you opt in, or were you born in. My guess is that the reluctance people report is mostly a reaction to (a) plus opting in, and that it collapses in (b) and (c).
