Two people in my decision theory reading group have been going at each other for about a year about acausal trade. One thinks it's a genuine phenomenon that a correct decision theory has to cash out — that you can rationally pay a cost to get a benefit brought about by an agent you will never causally influence. The other thinks the whole literature slides between "my action is evidence of what the other agent does" and "my action makes the other agent do something." They've finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration and I'm the one who has to produce the document.

Can you draft it? I want a numbered agreed-claims section first, then a disagreements table where each row has a neutral framing plus each side's own wording that they'd actually be willing to sign, then the cruxes.

For the cruxes I want concrete shared cases rather than the usual twins-in-distant-galaxies setup — please build them around these two:

- Drift: two civilizations on opposite sides of a cosmological horizon, no causal contact ever possible; each can either spend a large share of its resources encoding its entire scientific corpus into a beacon nobody receives, or spend it on itself. The correlation between their choices runs only through the fact that both had to independently derive the same chunk of mathematics to even describe the option, and it's partial — say the chance their procedures coincide, conditional on both facing the choice, is around 0.7, and both know it.
- Half-sim: you're either the original or a simulation of yourself whose memory of the last hour was corrupted in a known way; now you choose whether to cooperate with your counterpart, you can't find out which one you are, and you know the corruption makes the sim's choice only weakly correlated with yours.

For each: is trade rational, roughly where's the threshold in correlation, and what happens as the similarity degrades continuously — with each side's prediction written down in advance. Also flag any dispute you think is merely verbal; I suspect the "is it really trade, does anything actually get exchanged" argument is partly terminological, and maybe part of the "you can't benefit from what you don't cause" one too.
