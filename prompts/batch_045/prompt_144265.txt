Give me a decomposed credence on one claim, and please spend none of the answer explaining what Solomonoff induction is; assume I know.

The claim. Take an idealised universal predictor — a mixture over all computable environments, each weighted by two to the minus its description length — run with unbounded computation and fed the observation stream of a universe containing agents who could in principle model that predictor. The claim is that its forecasts would be systematically distorted, in the limit, by the incentives of consequentialist agents receiving weight in the prior: distorted relative to what a well-chosen but non-universal prior would deliver on the questions a user of the predictor actually cares about.

Hold fixed throughout: the environment is computable, the predictor is not itself an agent, and the uncomputability of the mixture is set aside as an engineering matter rather than a philosophical one.

I want three numbers rather than one: your credence in that claim when the forecast is (i) never revealed to anyone and used only for bookkeeping, (ii) fed into a decision-maker's choice of action, and (iii) published, so that agents inside the modelled worlds can condition their behaviour on it. If you think the three collapse into a single number, say so and say why.

Then the decomposition, with a probability and one line of justification for each term:

1. Weight: that agents able to model the predictor receive non-negligible mass in the mixture at all. Separate out the sub-case in which most of that mass sits not on worlds containing such agents directly, but on worlds simulated by agents inside a simpler base world.
2. Motive and channel: that such agents gain by distorting the forecast and have some route to doing so — distinguish an acausal route (they do better by trading with whoever consumes the prediction) from a causal one.
3. Non-dilution: that the distortion is not driven to zero by the convergence result bounding the predictor's total expected error in terms of the complexity of the true environment.
4. Benchmark: whether the right comparison class is total expected error or accuracy on the small, high-stakes subset of questions that a policy turns on, since a predictor can converge in the first sense whilst being wrong exactly where it matters.

Close by saying which term your three numbers are most sensitive to, and which single argument, theorem, or thought experiment would move them furthest. No references or reading suggestions, please; I will supply those myself.
