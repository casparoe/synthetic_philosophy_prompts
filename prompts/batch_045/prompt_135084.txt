Hey — quick context then the ask. I was turning this over with my officemate last night and it went nowhere, so here I am. I'm giving a talk in three weeks in the "Agency and Self-Control" seminar here, which is an interdisciplinary thing that sits between the psych department's self-regulation lab and the philosophy department, and what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a summary of my own argument.

The thesis I'm defending: the delay-discounting literature — hyperbolic discounting, smaller-sooner vs. larger-later, the whole family — doesn't describe weakness of will at all. What it does is hand the agent what I've started calling a diachronic alibi: on those models she maximizes at every moment given her current discount function, so there is never a moment at which she acts against her own current verdict about what's best; the conflict gets relocated onto the time axis and quietly stops being a synchronic failure. My claim is that real akrasia is synchronic. At t she occurrently judges that she shouldn't have another drink, or shouldn't open the document, and at t she does it anyway, with the judgment still in place — I've been calling that an inert judgment, one that gets formed but never taken up by whatever actually selects the action. My diagnostic for it is what I'm calling the mid-action verdict: ask her in the middle of it what she should be doing and she'll tell you correctly and keep going, whereas someone whose discount function has simply steepened will tell you she's changed her mind and this is fine. Second half of the talk is the part I'm less sure about: pre-commitment — Ulysses contracts, app lockouts, deposit schemes — isn't a strengthening of the will at all, it just rearranges the option set so that the thing you'll want is the thing you should do. The case I lean on is meta-akrasia: people who pay to set up the commitment and then pay more to break it, sometimes within the hour. I read that as showing commitment devices work by repricing the bad option, not by making the agent stronger, which means the standard story about "Ulysses and the Sirens" as a model of self-control has the emphasis exactly backwards.

The audience, as precisely as I can put it. Core group is the self-regulation lab: experimental, they think of self-control more or less as choosing larger-later over smaller-sooner, they measure it with delay-discounting tasks, they get twitchy about anything they can't operationalize, and they're still sore about the ego-depletion replication mess, so any hint of "resource" or "strength" talk will get a hostile reaction. Then there are two or three philosophers of action who came up on Aristotle and Davidson, for whom the interesting question is whether akrasia is even possible, and who will want to know whether "inert judgment" is anything more than a label for the mystery — and whether my synchronic condition collapses into a version of the Socratic denial, since if the judgment is fully there and the action still goes the other way, maybe I've just described something impossible and called it common. One behavioral economist usually shows up and will defend the discounting framework as a modeling choice, and will say my "judgment" is a variable that does no predictive work. And there's an RL person who drifts in and out and thinks in terms of discount factors inside algorithms, and who will probably point out that apparent akrasia falls out of any non-exponential discounting and ask what more there is to explain.

So: give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this mix, ordered by how much damage they'd do, phrased the way each of those groups would actually phrase them — the psych people and the Davidson people want completely different things and I need to hear both registers.
