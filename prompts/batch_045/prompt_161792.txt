So I'm running the Hart–Devlin session in my second-year jurisprudence seminar next week and I want to do something other than re-run the Wolfenden material, which they've all read and which they can all parrot back at me. Here's the case I want analysed instead.

The episode: on 22 May 2015 Ireland amended its constitution by referendum so that marriage may be contracted by two persons without distinction as to their sex — 62% yes on a turnout of about 61%, and the first time any state did that by popular vote rather than by legislation or court order. Background you'll need, all of it uncontested: male homosexual acts were criminal offences in Ireland until 1993, under nineteenth-century statutes inherited from Westminster; when the Irish Supreme Court upheld those laws in the early 1980s it reasoned partly from the Christian character of the state and from the protection of marriage and public health; decriminalisation came only after the European Court of Human Rights held that the ban breached the privacy guarantee in the Convention. So within one generation you have a society enforcing a moral code through the criminal law, then solemnly voting to entrench the opposite in its constitution.

What I want is Devlin's own machinery run on this, not Hart's caricature of it: the claim that a shared morality is among the things that hold a society together, that a society may defend it the way it defends its government, that no limit is fixed in advance, and that the test is supposed to be an empirical one about what ordinary people actually feel — intolerance, indignation, disgust — measured, in his hands, by something like a jury. So: what does that theory say the 1983 court was doing, and what does it say the 2015 electorate was doing? If the morality of a society is whatever the measurement device happens to record, Devlin's view seems to track opinion and hand you opposite verdicts forty years apart on the same conduct. Is that a reductio, or is it a sign I've misdescribed him? Can he separate "the morality of the society" from "whatever a majority will vote for this decade" without smuggling in critical morality — and if he can, has he quietly become Hart?

Here's a distinction of my own that I want tested, and I'll flag it to the students as mine: call it enforcement-elasticity. Some moralised norms are elastic — enforcement is part of what sustains the practice, so the amount of the conduct really does respond to whether it's criminal, and the ban helps constitute what the institution is. Others are inelastic: decriminalising can't change how much of the conduct there is, only how visible it is. If the disintegration worry needs some contagion or imitation mechanism to get going, then for the inelastic norms there's nothing for that mechanism to bite on, and the state has no disintegration-based reason to enforce. Does that yield a defensible restricted legal moralism — enforce the elastic ones, hands off the inelastic ones — or does it gut Devlin, given that the examples he's associated with are the inelastic ones? Then put the obvious Devlin reply in a student's mouth and answer it honestly: that the moral environment shapes desire and self-identification across a generation, so incidence isn't independent of law after all, and that visibility is itself what unravels a shared code. Is that reply empirical, and what would settle it?

Then run it the other way. What does the episode do to Hart? His positive-versus-critical morality distinction looks comfortable here and the harm principle looks like it's doing the work — but notice that Ireland 2015 is the cleanest measurement Devlin could ever have hoped for: a national vote on exactly the question, no judicial imposition, no elite capture. If his theory can't survive its own best-case data, that's worth saying. Is the view unfalsifiable: whatever the society's morality turns out to be, he says law should follow it, so he can only ever be wrong about the mechanism and never about the content?

Last thing: institutions, and silence. Devlin wanted this settled by something like a jury or a legislature, not by philosophers, and in the same year the US Supreme Court imposed the same result against majority opinion in many states. Which route does his theory prefer, and does it have any resources for criticising the court route? And where is the framework silent — about rights, about what counts as the society whose morality is authoritative (Ireland 1983 or Ireland 2015?), about minorities inside the polity, about whether "disintegration" is a claim anyone could check afterwards?

Be disciplined about the mapping: say who the relevant "we" is, what counts as evidence of a society's morality, what the theory predicts and what it misdescribes, and tell me where it's simply silent rather than forcing it to speak. Finish with a short verdict on whether this case helps Devlin, hurts him, or shows that the debate was never really about cases like this one. (Readings are Devlin's Enforcement of Morals and Hart's Law, Liberty and Morality, but I don't need citations — I know that literature, I want the argument.)
