I host a podcast on long-term ethics. Next week's guest defends a specific version of the suffering-risk case: on a symmetric totalist axiology the expected suffering in a technologically mature future dwarfs every other consideration, so preventing human extinction matters only instrumentally; nearly all of that suffering would come from digital minds and space colonization rather than from biology; suffering has near-lexical priority, so no quantity of mild happiness compensates for torture; and the highest-leverage work is shaping which technologies and which values mature first. She adds one auxiliary thesis of her own: that the intensity of a single mind's suffering has a ceiling fixed by that mind's representational bandwidth, so the astronomical totals come from the number of suffering minds rather than from how badly any one of them suffers.

Give me eight to ten interview questions that never let her restate that package, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is likely to give. Include at least one question on each of the following:

- The auxiliary thesis: if the ceiling is set by bandwidth, a mind of far greater bandwidth has a far greater ceiling, so headcount and intensity come apart less than she needs them to. Likely reply: bandwidth is physically bounded, or intensity saturates well below the ceiling. Write the follow-up for that reply.
- Extinction as s-risk mitigation: on her own numbers, extinction, or a civilization that never builds digital minds, would avert enormous suffering. What sign does her view assign to extinction, and what follows for the extinction-risk work she is deprioritizing?
- The expected-value structure: her case is driven by small probabilities of astronomically large harms, which makes it fanatical in the decision-theoretic sense. Does she accept that implication, and if not, what bounds the utilities?
- Strategic feedback: once astronomical suffering is common knowledge as the worst outcome, anyone able to credibly threaten to cause it gains enormous leverage, and the priority she recommends may itself raise the incentive to acquire that capacity. Is suffering-risk prioritization self-undermining? Likely reply: such threats are available anyway, or the remedy is to reduce the number of agents who could make them. Write the follow-up for that reply.
- One question that forces her to name a specific observation that would make her drop suffering risks as a priority, and to say what she would have to be wrong about: the distribution of sentience, or the tractability of influencing which values lock in.

She is fluent in these debates and will not concede a point merely because it is put to her, so every question should pin her to a commitment and then use that commitment against her.
