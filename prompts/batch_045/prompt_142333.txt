I'm a backend engineer, and I'm writing a short internal doc for our on-call handbook about what our monitoring is allowed to be described as "knowing." We have a few hundred checks that each emit a confidence score, and a paging rule that wakes a human when the score clears a threshold. My team is split three ways: some say the "knows" threshold should move with what's at stake (a payments outage versus a nightly capacity report), some say the threshold is fixed by the evidence alone and that moving it is just decision theory dressed up as epistemology, and some say the threshold is set by whoever happens to be reading the dashboard at the time. I want to settle it by handing teammates a decision tree they can walk themselves: which theory of knowledge is your alerting policy already committed to? The point is to sort people by what they say about concrete operational cases, not by which label they like, so no node should ask anything like "do you think knowledge is context-sensitive?" I've picked up terms like invariantism, contextualism, pragmatic encroachment, contrastivism and relativism from blog posts, but I don't know who defends what and I have no library access, so please use descriptive labels for positions only, and don't cite, name authors, or suggest anything for me to read.

Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the answer combinations they allow — and only then place the actual positions in it. Every node should be a question with two or three answers that an engineer with no philosophy training could answer cold, phrased so that answering it doesn't reveal which leaf it leads to. Please draw the nodes from scenarios like these rather than from the standard textbook bank cases, because I want something my teammates can actually picture: the same replication-lag reading used for a weekly report and then for a payments failover; stakes that are discovered only after the evidence is fixed, with no time to re-check; stakes raised gratuitously, by taking a silly bet or making an unnecessary promise that depends on p; stakes that are high for a colleague but not for you; a retrospective where nobody ever acted on the belief and we just want to know whether she knew; saying "I don't know" in an incident channel and then "I knew all along" in a low-stakes standup; an auditor who raises a scenario in which all our telemetry is systematically spoofed, changing nothing about the sensor itself; a case where the stakes are enormous but being wrong wouldn't change what you'd do anyway; a case where you'll be personally blamed if you're wrong regardless of the truth; a read-only shadow observer with identical evidence but no possible action; and an attributor that is a bot posting to a channel with its own severity budget. I have my own answers to all of these and I'm deliberately not telling you what they are, because the tree has to work for people who disagree with me. Give me eight to ten leaves, and make sure these appear among them: knowledge fixed by evidence and truth with only the action threshold moving; a fixed but very high bar; encroachment keyed to the subject's stakes; encroachment keyed to the attributor's or assessor's stakes; contextualism driven by which error possibilities are salient rather than by stakes; the view on which the truth of the knowledge claim itself varies with who assesses it; contrastivism; a graded view on which knowledge is a threshold on degree of confidence; and the view on which practical factors shift belief or justification while knowledge itself stays put. For each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment and one sentence on what it implies for our paging threshold — whether the threshold moves, who sets it, and whether the "knows" predicate changes meaning or only the action rule does.

Please also mark the cells in the logical space that nobody occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of each (incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored), and flag at least two pairs of leaves your tree genuinely cannot pry apart instead of inventing a question to fake a separation. Finally, be explicit about what would change your mind: which node is doing the most load-bearing work, which argument or case would force you to rebuild the tree, and where its structure is most likely to be an artifact of the order in which you asked the questions. Format the whole thing as plain text I can paste into a wiki, with no LaTeX, in four titled sections in this order: "The tree" (about 500 words, an indented, numbered decision tree with each node's question and answer options and arrows to the next node or leaf); "Leaves" (about 300 words); "Empty cells and collisions" (about 250 words); "Where this breaks" (about 150 words).
