I'm a senior in AP Statistics, and last semester I helped a local middle school analyze a district pilot of two algebra tutoring programs. I have a number in mind, a classmate disagrees with me, and I'd like your number along with the reasoning that produces it, because I suspect our disagreement really lives in one sub-question and I can't locate it.

The setup. Four hundred ninth-graders were assigned by lottery, 200 to Program X and 200 to Program Y. All 400 took the district algebra final at the end of the semester, so there are no missing scores. Gains are final minus pre-test, in points on a 100-point scale:

| | completed 80 percent or more of sessions | completed fewer | overall |
|---|---|---|---|
| Program X | 160 students, mean gain +6 | 40 students, mean gain +1 | +5.0 |
| Program Y | 80 students, mean gain +7 | 120 students, mean gain +2 | +4.0 |

So X wins overall by a full point, while Y wins inside both completion groups. This is the reversal my textbook calls Simpson's paradox, but the textbook only says the reversal can happen; it never says which side to believe.

Two disclosures. First, I chose the 80 percent cutoff myself. I tried 50, 60, 70, 80, and 90 percent, and 80 was the one at which Y came out ahead in both groups. Second, completing 80 percent of the sessions was not assigned by lottery; it happened afterward, and it is clearly affected by which program a student landed in. X's sessions are shorter and easier to attend, so 80 percent of its students cleared the threshold versus 40 percent of Y's.

Here is the claim I want a credence on: Program Y is genuinely the better program for this district, meaning that if next year's ninth-graders were assigned by lottery to X or Y under the same conditions, the average gain would be higher under Y. The district has agreed to run exactly that lottery next year, so the claim has a definite resolution.

My credence is 0.2. My classmate's is 0.7. He says the subgroup comparison is finer-grained, finer-grained is always more informative, and therefore the aggregate is the artifact.

Please give me your own number between 0 and 1, but the number is the less important part. Decompose it into the sub-questions it actually rides on, with a probability and a one-line justification for each, and show how the terms combine to your final figure. The terms I think belong in there, in case it helps: whether the quantity the district should care about is the effect of being assigned to a program or the effect of actually completing one; whether comparing students who completed a lot against students who completed a little is still a fair comparison when completion was itself caused by the program; how much of Y's one-point edge inside the groups is noise, given those group sizes and the fact that I searched over five cutoffs before finding it; and whether Y's edge inside the groups could be converted into a real advantage for the district by some accompanying policy that raised completion. Replace or add terms as you think best.

Then tell me three things: which single term your final number is most sensitive to; what one piece of evidence the district could actually collect next year would move that term the most; and, given my 0.2 and my classmate's 0.7, which of these sub-questions the two of us are most likely disagreeing about.
