I have been reading the Dennett–Caruso exchange on just deserts for years as pure theory. It stopped being pure theory last winter, when my younger brother was badly assaulted by a stranger and I discovered that what I wanted, quite specifically, was the assailant's suffering. He has pleaded guilty, there is a sentencing hearing in about six weeks, and I have been asked whether I wish to address the court. Before I decide, I want to know whether the package of views I have been carrying around actually holds together, because I can feel at least one of them straining and I cannot tell which.

Where I think I stand:

1. No one is morally responsible in the basic-desert sense, the sense in which suffering could be intrinsically owed to someone because of what he did. I take the luck objection to ultimacy seriously, and I do not think the compatibilist replies dissolve it.

2. So retributivism is false: the state may not worsen anyone's lot on the ground that it is deserved.

3. Forward-looking justifications survive intact: incapacitation, deterrence, rehabilitation. I am attracted to the public-health model defended on the skeptical side of this debate — treat dangerous behaviour the way we treat a dangerous carrier: quarantine where necessary, treat where possible, always with the least restrictive effective measure.

4. I nevertheless think resentment and indignation are fitting responses, not merely useful ones, and I do not want to be talked out of my anger. I also want the court to condemn what was done to my brother publicly and in its own voice, and I do not think that want is satisfied by a quarantine order, however well-designed.

5. I also think Dennett is onto something real: moral agency is an earned and socially scaffolded achievement, and the practice of holding people responsible over a lifetime is part of what makes them into creatures who can be held responsible. I want institutions that build agency, not merely ones that manage risk.

6. I think the criminal law's excuses and mitigations track something rather than being deterrence bookkeeping: genuine diminished capacity, duress, or provocation should reduce or extinguish liability even in a case where the deterrent arithmetic came out the same.

7. And I hold it as close to absolute that the innocent may not be punished or detained, even where doing so would visibly reduce crime. A scapegoat punished to calm a public panic is not something I will permit the state to do.

Please audit this set. Which pairs genuinely conflict, which only look as though they do, and in the conflicting cases what exactly would have to give? Order the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each one say what the cheapest repair is and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the set; I would much rather give up one commitment than two. If there is a single revision that clears away most of the trouble at once, name it. And where an item is only apparently in tension, explain why it dissolves: I am fairly confident that at least one of the seven is perfectly consistent with the rest, and I do not trust myself to tell which. I am not asking what I should say at the hearing — only what I can coherently say I believe.
