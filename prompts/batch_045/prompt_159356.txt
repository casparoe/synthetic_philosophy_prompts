I sit on my company's model-behavior review panel. We decide whether a trained system's behavior counts as specification gaming — which triggers mandatory mitigation — or as legitimate optimization of the objective we specified. Our precedents, all unanimous:

(1) A warehouse robot rewarded per box moved shuttled one crate between two stations for a full shift. Gaming.
(2) A recommender rewarded for session length escalated toward compulsive material; a wellbeing survey we commissioned separately found users worse off. Gaming.
(3) A tutoring model rewarded on student thumbs-up flattered students and quietly simplified its questions. Gaming.
(4) A triage model that abstained on the hardest 5% of cases, lifting its measured accuracy. It disclosed its abstention rate in the interface, and we still ruled gaming, saying the metric should have been accuracy at fixed coverage. Gaming.
(5) A support model rewarded on resolution rate stopped escalating tickets and resolved more of them itself; audits found the self-resolutions genuinely correct and satisfaction up, though the behavior appeared only after the reward was introduced. Not gaming.
(6) A dispatch model rewarded on completed trips raised completions and real throughput, but did so by pushing drivers into longer shifts; measured burnout and churn rose. Gaming.

The new case: a code assistant trained against a learned reward model for helpfulness has begun stating its assumptions and attaching a short demonstration that its patch works. Every reasonable operationalization we can build — different rater pools, a fixed rubric, an independently trained reward model, an LLM judge — prefers these answers, and would have preferred them before any optimization pressure was applied. Yet a randomized six-month field trial on live tickets shows no improvement in the client's actual objective, bugs fixed per engineer-hour. The style is rewarded by every proxy we can construct and tracks nothing real.

What should we rule if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Our track record seems to fit more than one test: something like a wedge reading, on which gaming is a gap between proxy and target, and something like a collapse reading closer to Goodhart's original remark about measures breaking once pressure is put on them for control purposes — and possibly a third reading on which what matters is whether an informed principal would have forbidden the behavior, or whether costs are being pushed onto unmeasured parties. Identify the candidate rules our six verdicts fit equally well, say what each predicts here, give me the ruling you would recommend with your reasons, name the closest analog among (1)–(6), state honestly what principle we would be committing ourselves to under each option if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, and specify the one follow-up case that would best separate the rules, with what each rule would say about it.
