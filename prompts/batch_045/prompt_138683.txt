I'm on the ethics committee at a hospital that just signed onto a study using a new consciousness monitor — call it a phi-meter. It's built on integrated information theory: consciousness is supposed to *be* integrated information, how much a system does above and beyond what its parts do separately. And its absurdly good at the cases where we already know the answer: the number collapses under general anaesthesia, comes back on waking, and it separates people who look completely unresponsive but are aware from people who really aren't. Fine.

The trouble is what else it says. It gives a solid positive reading to a large software simulation of visual cortex, and a near-zero reading for a chunk of brain that has way more neurons in it than the regions that light up. Those two verdicts are supposed to come from the same theory, as a package.

I have to write something for the committee and it needs to land somewhere, not end with more research is needed. Assume for the sake of argument that the meter is genuinely reliable inside its calibration range — don't just tell me the theory is unfalsifiable, I've read that take and it definately doesn't help me. The question is whether nailing the easy cases gives us any reason to trust it in the range where there's no independent check on it, or whether it's a redescription of what we already believed, dressed up in units. And since the surprising verdicts come as a package: is the fact that the theory sticks its neck out a point in its favour, or a reason to be more suspicious, not less?

Then tell me what we should actually do with the simulation reading — treat it as evidence, treat it as evidence of something weaker, ignore it — and why. No reading lists or citations please, I can't get to a library from here and I want the argument, not the literature.
