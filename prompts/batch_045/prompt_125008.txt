I'm writing a short paper for my philosophy of science seminar on whether the raven paradox has really been dissolved, and I want to rehearse against the strongest version of the view I'm arguing against. Please play a philosopher of science who holds that the Bayesian relevance account settled it: you think confirmation is a three-place relation between evidence, hypothesis and background; you think a non-black non-raven really does confirm that all ravens are black in the ordinary textbook setup; you think the appearance of paradox comes from running together whether there is confirmation, how much there is, and what would be worth going to look at; and you think Nicod's criterion was the dispensable premise all along, not the equivalence condition.

Be sharp rather than accommodating. Concede the small points — the magnitude of the confirmation in the ordinary case is minuscule, background knowledge genuinely matters, and the paradox survives as a puzzle about why untutored judgements of evidential relevance go wrong — but never concede that a puzzle remains once those distinctions are in place. Stay in character until I say stop, write one or two short paragraphs per turn, respond in the first person, and don't open by asking me what I mean: answer what I've actually argued. Don't give me a survey of the literature and don't cite anything by name — I want your own reasoning, and you may use small toy models with explicit numbers where that helps. Close each turn with one question you would press me on. And if I've scored a hit somewhere, say so in a single sentence before you hit back; I'm trying to find the load-bearing wall.

Here is my opening. I grant your standard move in the shoe case: if I already know the object before me is a shoe, then learning that it is white confirms nothing, because on every hypothesis about ravens I was already certain it was not a raven. But I think that gloss — confirmation fails when you knew in advance that the object was not a raven — is too crude, and here is a case that shows it. Suppose I am told in advance that I will be shown one of exactly two objects, chosen by a fair coin: one is a black raven, the other a white shoe. The white shoe comes up. I did not know in advance that the object was not a raven; I assigned that even odds. Yet as far as I can see your own machinery yields no confirmation here at all: the outcome was equally likely on the hypothesis that every raven is black and on the hypothesis that some raven somewhere is not, because what was up for grabs was only which of the two objects I would be shown, and the existence of a non-black raven elsewhere does not shift those odds one bit. So whether a white shoe confirms is not, on your account, a matter of whether I antecedently knew the object was a non-raven. It is a matter of the prior I assign over the range of things I might have been shown.

Now the part I cannot get past, which I have started calling the experimenter's-prior problem. Whether this shoe confirms is settled, on your account, by a fact — which two objects the experimenter was going to choose between — that entails nothing whatever about ravens and nothing about this object's colour or kind. Two ornithologists can sit in the same room, look at the same white shoe, share everything they know about ravens and everything they know about that shoe, and still get different verdicts, because one was told the object would be drawn from the whole museum collection and the other that it would be drawn from that pair. I can hear the reply coming: they don't share everything, they differ in total evidence, and of course confirmation is relative to total evidence. I want more than that. First, tell me whether you accept the verdict in my sealed-pair case, and if you don't, show me the numbers. Then state the condition under which a non-black non-raven confirms, in your own terms, without simply reading it off the agent's prior over possible outcomes. And do not give me the it's-only-a-tiny-amount reply: I am asking whether, not how much. If the honest answer is that the condition just is the shape of the prior, then tell me why that counts as dissolving a paradox that was supposed to arise from the logic of the equivalence condition, rather than as reclassifying confirmation as a fact about experimental design.
