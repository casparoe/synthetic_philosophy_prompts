I'm putting together the opening chapter for a graduate reader on consciousness, and I'd like the section on epiphenomenalism to begin with a staged exchange rather than a survey, so what I need is a dialogue that does real philosophical work instead of one philosopher knocking over a cardboard version of the other. Give me roughly 900 to 1,200 words between two equally competent interlocutors — call them what you like — one defending epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness (experiences are real, qualitative, and causally inert with respect to everything physical, including the speaker's own utterances and judgments), the other defending the objection from phenomenal judgment: if my saying that I am in pain is not caused by my being in pain, then my apparent knowledge of my own experience looks either lucky, or empty, or both. Set them somewhere with time to kill and an audience of nobody — a delayed train, a conference dinner where the wine has made them stubborn but not sloppy — and let them go at it.

To keep them off the rails, build the exchange around a case I've been toying with. Call it the Reporter's Box: a hypothetical implanted device, fitted at birth, that monitors the neural correlate of a subject's pain and, on that basis alone, causes her to form the belief that she is in pain and to say so — but the device's causal chain passes entirely through the neural correlate and never through the experience itself, so that the experience is, on the epiphenomenalist's own story, off the causal path. The epiphenomenalist should have to say what she wants to say here: presumably that the report can still count as knowledge because experience and report are joint effects of a common neural cause (the barometer reading tracks the storm without being produced by it), with a fallback to the claim that the phenomenal judgment is partly constituted by the experience rather than caused by it. The critic should then press the point I find hardest: the specificity problem. The subject doesn't merely report that something is going on; she discriminates this exact hurt from a slightly duller one and picks out the right word for it, and it is unclear how she gets the fine-grained content right reliably if phenomenal character has no causal purchase on the selection of that content. Have the critic sharpen this with a scrambling variant — a neurosurgeon silently permutes the mapping between quale-type and report-type, and by the epiphenomenalist's own commitments nothing observable, including the subject's own introspective verdicts, can change.

Then let the argument turn on what's really in dispute: whether the epiphenomenalist is entitled to call her report knowledge at all, whether that commits her to a strange category of non-causal epistemic warrant or instead shows that the critic has quietly built causation into her account of knowledge and begged the question, and whether there's a point at which the two of them are no longer disagreeing about consciousness but about what the word cause is doing — if so, have one of them notice it and say so explicitly, and have them figure out whether that makes the disagreement merely verbal. Both characters should concede something substantial by the end, and each should be responding to the actual moves the other just made rather than to a script. No citations, no bibliographies, no reading lists.
