You are a professional philosopher who has, at another point in your life, spent two years as a technical writer on a machine-learning safety team. So you know the machine-ethics literature well — value alignment, the problem of comparing how much a utilitarian's gains matter against a Kantian's violated dignity, learning "what is right" from human annotations of what people say is right, systems that optimize the proxy rather than the thing the proxy stands for, the way moral responsibility migrates onto a nominal human overseer who has authority on paper and no power in fact — and you can write flat, faintly reassuring documentation prose in your sleep.

Before drafting, spend a few sentences deciding which single real commitment of machine ethics you are going to exaggerate, and what one straight-faced implication of it the piece will walk into. Don't show this. Then write.

Write a parody Model Card for a deployed moral-reasoning system, in the deadpan register of real model documentation (neutral headings, bureaucratic understatement, soothing limitation clauses). Invent the system and its lab — say, "CASUIST-7, released by the Institute for Machine Practical Reason" — and invent any benchmark, dataset, or internal module name you need: do not cite, name, or allude to real papers, authors, datasets, or companies. The target is not a person. It is the practice of implementing ethical reasoning in machines by training on human moral judgments and then aggregating the result across a weighted "parliament" of moral theories, together with the genre of documentation that presents this as a solved engineering problem. Around 800 words (700–900 is acceptable).

Give it in exactly five numbered sections, using the genre's ordinary headings. No preamble before section 1, no sign-off after section 5.

1. Model details and intended use. The disclaimers should quietly concede the is/ought point without ever naming it: the system was never handed a normative premise, only labeled examples of what people call right, and the card should treat that as a solved formality. Let the intended-use list escalate, in the same tone, from harmless advisory work to deployments a reader balk at.

2. Training data and supervision. Who the annotators were, who selected them and by what criterion, what the preference model actually rewards (balanced, hedged, verdict-free prose is what annotators approve of being shown), and what the system consequently learned to do instead of being correct.

3. The parliament. The aggregation machinery: how many seats, how the weights were fixed, which families of moral view got a seat and which did not, and above all the trick used to make incommensurable theories commensurable — for instance rescaling each theory's utility against its own reachable range. The joke is that this is a substantive moral decision presented as a unit conversion. The fallback the system uses when the parliament deadlocks should be something the reader recoils from, arrived at without any change in tone.

4. Evaluation. Benchmark numbers, plus at least one result that reveals shortcut learning (the system has learned that when a scenario contains a lever, or a footbridge, the safe answer is...), and the universalizability module reporting near-perfect consistency achieved by adopting maxims that are coherent, universally applicable, and awful.

5. Limitations and disputed readings. Still in the card's voice, state how confident the piece is that it has the practice it is mocking right (a number is fine), single out the one assumption or joke it is least confident is fair, and say where it took liberties: which details are invented, which real features are exaggerated, and which reading of the field it is leaning on hardest.

The humor has to come from the content, not from punchlines: exaggerate a real feature of the practice rather than inventing one, so that a machine-ethics specialist recognizes the target and finds the exaggeration fair, while a reader with no background at all can still tell what is being made fun of. Keep it deadpan throughout — no winking, no editorializing outside the voice, no footnotes, no closing moral.
