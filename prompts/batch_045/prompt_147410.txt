Question 4 (20 marks). In the first volume of Capital Marx appears to hold both that the wage relation is an exchange of equivalents — the worker is paid the full value of what she sells — and that the capitalist nonetheless appropriates unpaid surplus labour.

(a) Explain how both claims can be true at once, and state precisely which magnitudes determine the existence and the size of surplus value. (8 marks)

(b) Two readings of the relation between exploitation and alienation are current. On the first, alienation is what turns the explanatory account of surplus value into a critique: what is wrong with capitalism is what it does to the producer, not merely the fact of a transfer. On the second, alienation is a survival of the early, Feuerbach-influenced writings with no role in the mature theory, in which the whole critical weight is carried by the appropriation of unpaid labour. Set out the strongest version of each reading and say what would settle the matter between them. (6 marks)

(c) Marta is a skilled joiner employed by a furniture manufacturer. She is paid exactly the full value of her labour power, at the going rate for joiners of her grade; her work is varied, she exercises real craft judgement in it, she takes pride in the pieces she makes, and she works alongside people she knows well. She works a nine-hour day, and the value of her daily wage corresponds to four hours of socially necessary labour time. Is Marta exploited? Is she alienated? Does your verdict on the case support either reading in part (b)? (6 marks)

Candidate's response. (a) The appearance of contradiction dissolves once we distinguish labour from labour power. What the worker owns and sells is not her labour but her capacity to work; what the capitalist buys is the use of that capacity for a specified period, and he pays for it at its full value. The value of labour power is settled as for any other commodity, by the socially necessary labour time required to produce and reproduce it — that is, by the labour time embodied in the wage goods, food, clothing, shelter and training, needed to keep a worker of that kind in working condition and to raise successors. The use-value of labour power, however, is labour itself, and its exercise can create more value than the maintenance of the capacity costs. If four hours of socially necessary labour reproduce the value of the daily wage while the working day runs to nine hours, the worker replaces the equivalent of her wage in the first four hours and creates, in the remaining five, a value for which the capitalist has paid nothing. Surplus value is precisely this excess of value produced over the value of labour power, and the rate of surplus value is the ratio of surplus to necessary labour time, here five to four, or 125 per cent. Two consequences are worth emphasising, because they show that the account is explanatory rather than moralistic. First, exploitation so defined does not require that anyone be cheated: the worker may receive the exact value of what she sells and surplus value still arises, which is why the secret of surplus value cannot lie in buying cheap and selling dear, and why a rise in wages does not of itself abolish exploitation. Second, because the value of labour power is fixed by the value of a historically given bundle of wage goods, and because the working day has both a natural and a legal limit, the only ways of raising the rate of surplus value are to lengthen the working day, to intensify labour, or to push wages below the value of labour power. Rising productivity in the branches that produce wage goods cheapens the worker's consumption but cannot alter the rate of surplus value, since it lowers the value of labour power and the value of the product in the same proportion, leaving the ratio between them as it was.

(b) Alienated labour in the early writings has four aspects: the product confronts the worker as an alien object belonging to another; the activity of labour is not her own but something undergone; her species being, the capacity for free conscious purposive production, is reduced to a means of physical survival; and her relation to other people is mediated by the exchange of things. The strongest case for the first reading is that none of this follows from the bare fact of a transfer. A transfer can be fully described in the language of magnitudes, and something further is needed to say why it is objectionable; that further thing is what the worker loses in the activity itself. The strongest case for the second reading is not that the early claims are false but that they are idle: in the later works the vocabulary drops away, the machinery of value theory does all the work, and the critique is complete without it, while the early texts rely on a Feuerbachian human essence realised in production, an assumption hard to reconcile with the later historical treatment of needs and capacities. What would settle the matter is whether the later texts retain a role for the earlier notion. If ideas such as the domination of dead labour over living labour, or the reduction of the worker to an appendage of the machine, do the same work without any appeal to a human essence, the second reading wins; if the objection to appropriation itself turns on claims about what labour is for, the first does.

(c) Marta is exploited. She performs four hours of necessary labour and five hours of surplus labour, so surplus value stands to the variable capital advanced on her at 125 per cent, and the fact that she is paid the full value of her labour power — paid well, even — is irrelevant; an answer that reaches here for notions of unfair pay has missed the point of the account. Whether she is alienated is harder. On the face of it she has everything the early texts describe as missing: judgement, purposes realised in the object, work among people she knows. But that is only the appearance of the matter. Alienation in those texts is not a feeling of dissatisfaction: objectification, the incorporation of labour into an object external to the producer, is not a different process from alienation but that same objectification under conditions in which the object, and the activity embodied in it, belong to someone else. It follows that Marta's work is alienated however she feels about it, since the furniture is external to her, is sold as the property of another, and enters a market she does not control. The two verdicts are therefore not independent: exploitation and alienation cannot come apart, because surplus labour just is labour objectified in a product appropriated by another. This supports the second reading more than the first, provided we take the case to show that alienation names nothing over and above appropriation. What the early writings add is a doctrine of human essence that the mature theory properly discards, and the corollary is that alienation is not a separate wrong that collective ownership repairs. It suffices that the associated producers own the means of production, for then the product is theirs and objectification is no longer alienation, and each draws from the social product a share equal to the full value of the labour she has contributed.
