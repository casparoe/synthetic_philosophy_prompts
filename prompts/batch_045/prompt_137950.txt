I run a small, stubborn reading group — two philosophers, an economist, and a machine-learning person who builds multi-agent systems and has no patience for the word "rational" — and after roughly eight months we have not moved any member of it by so much as a degree. The question we are stuck on is whether an evidential decision theorist can get cooperation in a large world. By the small-world/large-world distinction I mean the usual one: in a small world the states, acts, and consequences are laid out in advance and the only uncertainty is which state obtains, while in a large world you cannot enumerate the states, you do not know the model, and part of the problem is deciding what to condition on. In the sanitized cases everyone knows — Newcomb's problem, or a prisoner's dilemma with a twin who is stipulated to run your procedure and to choose exactly as you do — the evidentialist appears to get cooperation for free and the causalist either defects or reaches for a different theory. Our entire dispute is about what survives when the stipulations are removed: you do not know how many agents are relevantly like you; you do not know whether your similarity is shared source code, shared training data, shared culture, or a predictor's imperfect model of you; you must estimate the action–action correlation from a finite and possibly biased sample; and, worst of all, the estimate is not independent of your own deliberation, because in some of these worlds the correlation partly exists because agents like you have read about evidential cooperation. We have each written a one-page statement of the opposing view and not one opponent has accepted the wording, which is itself a clue I would like explained.

The camps, as concretely as I can state them. The cooperation-friendly evidentialists say: treat the correlation like any other unknown parameter, put a posterior on it, and take the expectation; on almost any posterior with real mass above the critical correlation, cooperate, and the fragility worries are nothing but ordinary statistical risk plus a normal kind of self-fulfilling expectation — no different, they say, from a traffic equilibrium that exists because everyone expects it. Their critics reply that the correlation is not a parameter of nature to be estimated but a property of the joint distribution over policies, and that it moves when you touch it: conditioning on being a cooperator can raise or lower the correlation, and in a large world an adversary, a market, or merely a badly chosen reference class can make the correlation anything at all, including negative; so, they conclude, evidential cooperation is either a tautology about small worlds gerrymandered to contain it, or a self-referential fixed-point problem that the theory never actually solved. My ML friend thinks both sides are asking the wrong question: for her there is only "which policy, trained and deployed under these information conditions, scores well against a realistic distribution of co-players," so the philosophers are answering different questions under a single heading. Two cases of my own seem to separate them better than the standard ones, and I want them used rather than paraphrased around. Call the first the preacher's loop: you face a prisoner's dilemma with payoffs T > R > P > S and 2R > T + S, and your evidence about the correlation is a sample of twenty past encounters among agents described as relevantly similar, of which fourteen cooperated — except that the sample is drawn from a population that includes agents who had read the same decision-theory material you had, so the correlation you are estimating is partly produced by belief in the theory that tells you to estimate it; and there is a mirror-image variant I call the bookie's loop, in which another party profits by making your action anticorrelated with theirs, so that the more confident you are in evidential cooperation the more precisely you can be punished for it. Call the second the signalling regress: at stage zero you may pay a small cost to become legibly the sort of agent who cooperates — publish your policy, take a binding-looking pledge, submit to a character test — and at stage one you face the dilemma. The critics say all the real action is at stage zero and that evidentialism mislocates it; the evidentialists say stage zero is itself a decision whose evidential import has to be assessed in exactly the same way; and I cannot tell whether this is an infinite regress, a fixed point over policies quietly smuggled in, or the whole dispute in miniature.

My hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that more than one disagreement is sharing a name. One looks substantive: it is about what the object of choice is — a single act, a policy, or a policy plus the process that made you the kind of agent who has it — and about whether the correlation is an empirical quantity to be estimated or a fixed point to be solved for; if that is what we are arguing about, the debate is genuine and could in principle be ended by something like an existence or uniqueness result for a self-consistent evidentialism, or by a proof that any such fixed point is unstable under small perturbations of the reference class. A second looks like a clash about what a decision theory is even for — a criterion of rationality for an agent already in the situation, or a design specification for an agent you are building — which would mean the philosophers and my ML friend are simply not talking to each other. And a third I suspect is verbal: that the evidentialist helps herself to a partition of possibilities fine enough to carry the correlation inside a single state, while the causalist insists on a coarser partition in which the correlation is a further, contestable, empirical fact. What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict or a survey of decision theories. For each fault line, tell me whether it is verbal or substantive, whether it turns on facts, on standards of evidence, on which cases count as data (I think this is doing enormous hidden work: one side's diet is Newcomb cases and twin dilemmas, the other's is hostile predictors and reference-class gerrymandering, and they never bite on each other's examples), on bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, or on two questions sharing one label. Engage what each camp actually says, including the preacher's loop, the bookie's loop, and the signalling regress, and add a cleaner separating case if you can construct one. Assume I cannot look anything up: no citations, no bibliographies, no attributions to papers or years — argue from the structure of the cases. Then tell me honestly, given your own diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal result, a simulation, a distinction, a change of question — or nothing, because we should expect to be exactly here in another eight months, and say why.
