Hey — I'm putting together a handout for the grad seminar I run on conventions and the evolution of norms, and I want the centerpiece to be a decision tree rather than another wall of prose. Setup: a student is handed some observed behavioural regularity (people do X in circumstances C) and walks down the tree until they land on a named account of what is actually sustaining it. Two sides of A4, so keep the nodes short.

Cases first. Give me eight to ten diagnostic cases to hang the tree on, each phrased purely as something you could observe, with no theory words in them: the side of the road people drive on; replying to work email within about two days; bringing a dish to a shared meal; not taking the last slice; a keyboard layout that everybody agrees is worse but nobody leaves; head-nod vs head-shake for yes and no; what colour you wear to a funeral; not sitting next to a stranger on a nearly empty bus; standing on one side of an escalator; the queue that forms before anyone has agreed there is a queue. I want at least one case where nearly everyone privately dislikes the norm and keeps complying anyway, and one where the whole thing is held up by three loud people while everyone else is indifferent.

Then the tree. Every branch node should be labelled with the question that does the splitting, never with the name of a view, and a first-year who has read nothing should be able to answer each one. The splits I care about:

- is there some alternative regularity that everyone would also prefer, conditional on enough other people switching to it (a coordination structure with real alternative equilibria), or is this a mixed-motive / social-dilemma thing, or is there simply one sensible way to do it?
- does the regularity survive if private preferences all flip but nobody finds out that they have?
- does it survive when it becomes common knowledge that most people comply only because they expect others to, and expect others for the same reason? (This is where Lewis needs common knowledge and the evolutionary crowd thinks he is asking for too much.)
- is there a cost that others impose on violators, and can you tell whether that cost is load-bearing or merely decorative?
- does the regularity keep going after the original reason for it has lapsed?
- is there a sender-receiver structure here, where the act is arbitrary as a signal but pays because it tracks some state of the world?

Leaves: Lewisian convention as an arbitrary equilibrium plus common knowledge; Skyrms-style dynamical/evolutionary accounts where the population can land on an equilibrium by reinforcement or drift with nobody knowing anything; the Bicchieri-ish social norm with conditional preference plus empirical and normative expectations and sanctions; conformist or prestige-biased cultural transmission in the Boyd-Richerson-Henrich style; Gilbert's joint commitment / plural subject; a teleological account on which the pattern is reproduced with a proper function so that a token counts as correct because of its history; a Brandom-ish inferentialist view where the norm lives in scorekeeping practices; and plain habit or statistical regularity that isnt a norm at all. Mark 'unnamed' where you think nobody is home. One awkward commitment per leaf, one line.

The bit I dont think anyone has done, and the reason I'm asking rather than taking this from the literature: I want a second pass, a dynamics tree, built on a distinction I'll call 'thin' vs 'thick' bootstrap. A thin bootstrap is one where the regularity gets started from something that is not itself normative and not itself a precedent — sheer prominence or salience, a Schelling-style focal point. A thick bootstrap needs something already invested with force: an existing precedent, a prior normative belief, an already-salient model to copy. For each leaf, say which kind of start it can actually explain, and build a short second tree whose first node is 'was there anything normative or precedential on the scene before this regularity existed?' I strongly suspect some of these accounts can only run on thick starts and quietly presuppose the thing they were supposed to explain, and I want that made explicit in the handout rather than left as a footnote.

Last two things. Flag the pairs of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate — I think Lewisian convention and the Skyrmsian equilibrium look identical once everything has settled down, and that the social-norm and joint-commitment accounts are hard to pry apart in cases where compliance is universal and no sanction is ever observed — and say what extra observation would separate them if you had it. And mark the combinations of answers that are logically available but unoccupied: e.g. a regularity that is not arbitrary, is held up only by expectations, and has no alternative equilibrium; or an arbitrary one held up mainly by moralised sanction. For each, one line on whether nobody is there because it is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Add a side-box, maybe four lines, on what would count as evidence that a given case had migrated from one leaf to another over time — I dont want students thinking the categories are eternal essences.
