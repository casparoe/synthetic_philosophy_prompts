Every year I teach the seperateness of persons objection to utilitarianism — Rawls's complaint that utilitarianism doesn't take the distinction between persons seriously, Nozick's remark about there being no social entity with a good of its own that we can sacrifice individuals to, Taurek's argument that the numbers don't settle who we should save — and every year I get to the end of the seminar able to state the thing beautifully and with no clear idea which bit of it I actually believe. I've finally started writing it up, and I've got as far as a list of cases with my own gut verdicts on them, and I cannot find a principle that fits. So here's the list. Tell me what principle I'm groping towards, or tell me which of the verdicts has to go.

1. Within a single life, aggregation is obviously fine. I'm having three hours of fairly grim dental work next week in exchange for ten years without toothache; anyone who turned that down would be mad. Same for my mother agreeing to a horrible operation that buys her five good years. Verdict: fine, and the arithmetic is straightforward.

2. Across lives, equal harms: I can shove either one stranger or five strangers out of the path of a falling rock. No relationships, no prior claims, no tie-breaking considerations of fairness, and suppose for the sake of it that I have no way of tossing a coin. Verdict: save the five. Numbers count here.

3. Taurek-style: save one stranger from being killed, or save five strangers from losing a hand each. Verdict: the one, and it isn't close. The reason that comes out of me is that no individual among the five would be as badly off as the one would be, and five lesser misfortunes don't compose into a greater one.

4. Public health: a regulation adds roughly a one-in-ten-million annual risk of death from a trace contaminant to everyone in the country — call it seven million people — and reliably prevents four deaths a year. Verdict: obviously fine, pass it, don't be silly.

5. Same four identified lives saved, but the cost falls on one identifiable innocent person in the form of a certain, genuinely grim year of forced labour. Verdict: not fine. Now, the expected arithmetic in 4 is worse than in 5 and I'm unmoved by that, which is fine, but here's what actually bothers me: if the difference between 4 and 5 is just a threshold on how large a burden anyone may be asked to bear, then the seperateness of persons is doing no work at all in my thinking — I'd say something very similar about burdens within one life (my mother wouldn't take a year of forced labour for five good years either).

6. The case that really messes me up. A twenty-year-old has a slowly progressing neurological condition that will, by the time she's fifty, leave her with none of her present values, memories, or character, and no psychological continuity with her present self worth speaking of — think the most extreme version of what dementia does, except predicted in advance and known to her. Is a burden imposed on her-future-self more like case 1 or more like cases 2 and 5? Verdict: I'm much more reluctant to have her sign away a year of suffering at fifty in exchange for a big benefit now than I am in the ordinary case, so something in me is treating it as nearer the interpersonal end. But the Parfitian way of spelling that out eats case 1 as well: if ordinary prudence is just aggregation across a series of loosely connected person-stages and that's fine, I owe you a story about why the interpersonal version isn't.

7. Something I made up to test myself, which I think is doing real work. Suppose a co-consciousness link: two people wired so that a single token experience is had by both of them — one instance of pain, two subjects who both undergo it. Is imposing one linked pain on a linked pair like imposing one pain on one person, or like imposing a pain on two? My verdict is that it's worse than the one and less bad than the two, and I don't like what that says about me, because it suggests the variable my verdicts have been tracking isn't the number of persons at all but the amount of suffering in the world. Which is the utilitarian answer I'm supposed to be resisting.

A rough principle I've been trying out: you may add up burdens across people only when each individual burden is trivial, or when what each person bears is a risk small enough that no one could reasonably make anything of it; where a burden is significant, no number of lesser burdens can outweigh one person's claim not to bear it, because a life is a unit within which sacrifice can be compensated and across which it can't. Test it against the cases and repair it — clause 3 above is where I think it breaks, and I've never been able to make the last clause survive case 6.

What I want, concretely: one principle, or at most two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I could put it on a handout for third-years, with the case that motivates each clause attached to it. Then tell me what it says about two cases I haven't settled. (a) Benefits rather than burdens: one person gets twenty extra years of healthy life, or thirty people get two months each — I genuinely don't know what to say, and I want to know whether your principle has to treat benefits and burdens symmetrically. (b) A household of five adults who've shared everything for thirty years, each facing a harm, versus one stranger facing a bigger one — I feel a pull towards treating the household more like a single life, and I can't tell whether that's the view or sentimentality. I know there's a version of all this in the contractualist literature where you compare individual complaints pairwise instead of totalling them, and I've never understood whether that genuinely avoids aggregation or quietly reintroduces it; if that's the way out, show me it working on case 4. And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate — if the list is just a mess, tell me which verdict is the weak link and why rather than contriving something to save all seven.
