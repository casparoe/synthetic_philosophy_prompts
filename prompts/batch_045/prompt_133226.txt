I've been reading on my own about the meta-problem of consciousness — the move that says the real thing to explain is not why there is subjective experience, but why we are all so certain there is and keep insisting on it, and that once you explain the mechanism that produces those insistences there may be nothing left over to explain. I want to try that idea out on a novel I know well, Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go.

What keeps nagging me is something the versions of this argument I have found don't seem to handle. The usual worry is that first-person reports are cheap: any system able to monitor its own states will emit claims about how things seem to it. But in this novel fluency is not what makes a claim about your own inner life credible — it is what destroys it. Kathy's narration is flat, full of things she half-admits she cannot explain, and that clumsiness is what makes readers certain someone is home. Meanwhile the one thing nobody in the book will accept as evidence is a well-argued assertion by the students that they have inner lives; their artwork is collected because it might do work their testimony cannot, and what the people running Hailsham seem to want is something that cannot be produced to order.

So I'd like three things. First: does the book's structure — a first-person narrator whose reports are never challenged inside the book and never count as evidence outside it — actually dramatize the meta-problem's central claim, or does it quietly help itself to the very thing in dispute? Second: does the art-as-test thread suggest that the only credible sign of an inner life is a costly one, which would mean that cheapness is the wrong objection to first-person reports altogether? Third: is there anything here about why we attribute experience to some beings and withhold it from others that a straight argumentative paper on this topic would leave out? I've read the novel closely and don't need it summarized back to me, and I'd rather you say plainly where the reading breaks down than stretch it to fit.
