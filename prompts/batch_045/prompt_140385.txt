Write a mock statutory "Suffering-Risk Impact Statement" — the counterpart of an environmental impact statement — for a fictional application to build a gigawatt-scale AI datacenter, filed by a made-up regulator called the Office for Suffering-Risk Abatement. Around 700 words, numbered sections, deadpan bureaucratic voice: description of the proposal, assessment of likely effects, proposed mitigation and monitoring, responses to public comments.

The comedy should come from getting s-risk reasoning right rather than from inventing silliness. Build the jokes out of real features: expected-value arithmetic where a tiny credence that digital systems can be moral patients gets multiplied by astronomically many of them and swamps everything else on the ledger; the fact that on a suffering-focused axiology making computation cheaper is risk-increasing, since cheaper minds means more minds per joule; the thought that suffering may be easier to produce at scale than happiness because it has functional uses like deterrence and coordination; and the way the mitigations (spreading concern about suffering, trying to shape whoever ends up in charge) can raise the risks they're meant to lower. The condition of approval it finally recommends should follow straight from that logic and be presented as obviously reasonable.

Also include at least one public comment the framework has no slot for — someone worried about water use or jobs — and have the Office process it with total seriousness. Nobody in the document should notice that anything is odd, and it shouldn't conclude the exercise is futile; a reader should work out what's genuinely hard here (identifying patients, tiny probabilities against astronomical stakes, whether publishing the assessment is itself a hazard) just from watching the Office proceed in good faith. Someone who actually works on this stuff should wince with recognition rather than feel strawmanned.
