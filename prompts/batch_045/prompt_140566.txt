i've been sitting on a pile of half-formed opinions about higher-order evidence for a couple of years and I want someone to tell me whether they're actually a package or just seven things I happen to like. Context: longtime rationalist-blog reader, did the calibration drills back in the day, have "update on the evidence, not the fact" burned in, not an academic but I've read enough philosophy-adjacent blog posts on this stuff to have views. Please don't hand me a literature summary or a reading list — I can't look anything up from where I'm writing this, and honestly a concrete case would move me more than a principle.

Where I think I've landed:

1. Conciliationist-ish about disagreement. When someone I'd count as a genuine peer disagrees with me I should move substantially toward them, further than feels right, and I try to actually do it. Not full equal-weighting, because I think exact peerhood is rare in real life, but that's the direction of travel.

2. I take the enkratic principle seriously — the thing where if you genuinely believe you ought to believe Q, it's irrational to just sit there believing not-Q. So I think epistemic akrasia (sincerely believing P while also sincerely believing that your belief in P came out of a process you have reason to think is unreliable) is defective, not just an unattractive combination. There's something wrong with a person in that state.

3. But I also reject the "first-order evidence always wins" / no-defeat line. Higher-order evidence really can defeat. The drug case, the bad-light case, doing derivations while exhausted — those genuinely lower my justification for the first-order belief, rather than just giving me an extra independent thing to believe alongside it.

4. Here's a distinction I half-invented and got attached to. Split HOE into transparent (diagnostic, mechanistic) and opaque (purely statistical, predictive). Transparent: I can say what's wrong with the process — "i'm drunk, my working memory is shot, so multi-step derivations are exactly where I'll slip." Opaque: a well-calibrated oracle tells me "people who produce the answer you just produced to this exact puzzle are wrong seventy percent of the time," with no story at all about where the error is. My stance: only transparent HOE really defeats. Opaque HOE is a reason to re-derive and maybe to go check my track record for that class of judgment, but it shouldn't by itself move my credence in P much.

5. A second half-invention, which I like even more: I've started thinking of defeat as regression toward my own base rate for the relevant class of judgments rather than as a new piece of evidence about P. On this picture "you're unreliable here" means "shrink toward what someone in your position with your track record should expect," and — this is the part I think is load-bearing — it shouldn't be able to push you below your base rate for the class.

6. I'm not worried about what I call the everything-melts problem: if HOE defeats locally, then since the belief that my faculties are unreliable is itself produced by those same faculties, everything gets defeated and I should be a skeptic. I think I can just refuse to let the global version work while the local version works fine.

7. Permissivism at the first order. I think the same body of evidence can rationally support a range of attitudes — the uniqueness thesis seems false to me, partly because I think priors are doing more work than people admit. (Related: I don't think this makes me a relativist, but you can poke at that.)

And two verdicts I'm fairly confident about on specific cases:

8. Mirror case: an oracle that's right ninety percent of the time tells me my answer to a logic puzzle is wrong. I recheck carefully, can't find an error. The oracle adds that people in my situation also can't find their errors. I end up around 25-30% on my original answer, i.e. I mostly capitulate.

9. Tired-but-it-checks-out case: I'm wrecked, I do a derivation, I check it twice from scratch, it comes out the same way. I stay at like 90% and I don't discount it much for being tired.

One thing I don't think is a problem but maybe is: I'm conciliationist (1) and also I give my own conclusion more weight when the other person hasn't seen my arguments, or I've spent much longer on it, or I have evidence they don't. Those feel consistent to me but I can't articulate why.

What I want: go through these and tell me which pairs are really in tension and which only look like it, with the real ones ranked by severity — fatal, needs-a-patch, merely awkward. For each real one, what's the cheapest fix and what does it cost me. Then tell me which single revision cleans up the most. I genuinely don't know which of these I'd give up, so please don't assume I'm wedded to the ones I sound most wedded to; tell me which one is actually doing the work in my verdicts at 8 and 9.

Also, and this is the bit I'm most worried about and least able to see from the inside: flag it if 4, 5 or 6 has quietly become unfalsifiable — if I've built myself a view where no oracle, no track record, and no argument could ever actually make me move. If it has, say whether that's fatal for a view of this kind or whether it's fine to have a principle that just sets the terms of what counts as a defeater.
