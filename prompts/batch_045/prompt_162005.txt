Building a little set of superrationality puzzles for a reading group and I keep reinventing Hofstadter's originals by accident, so I want one built to order.

The set up: in the Platonia dilemma a small group each get the same unsolicited offer, a huge prize goes to the one person who claims it, but only if exactly one person claims. In the Luring Lottery the offer goes out to a big open group and everyone who enters shares a fixed pot, so the more entries arrive the less any one of them is worth. Exact figures from the originals dont matter here. In both cases the pull on the individual is toward claiming or entering, and superrationality is the thing that tells you to hold back: a shared die roll with probability 1/N each is the usual superrational answer to the first, staying out or dithering to the second.

I want the mirror image. Design me a one-shot anonymous offer in that same idiom, a letter that lands on N doormats, no communication, no repeat play, no reputation effects, no predictors or demons, symmetric ex ante, ordinary stakes, where the verdicts come out like this:

- Causal decision theory says stay out, for the ordinary reason that your own letter is very unlikely to be the pivotal one, not because the numbers are tuned to five decimal places.
- Evidential decision theory also says stay out. This is the hard one. In Hofstadter's own cases EDT more or less rides along with superrationality once you grant that similar people facing the same letter decide alike. So build the case so that even granting a substantial real-world correlation between what I do and what everyone else does, my sending the letter still isn't good enough news about the outcome to be worth what it costs me. Show the inequality and the numbers.
- Superrationality says send it, and says send it as a straight action rather than a coin flip. Show why a shared lottery over who sends isn't better here, the way a one-in-N die roll is supposed to be in the Platonia dilemma.

Two constraints on how you get there. The payoff structure has to be a genuine collective-action one, the good lands on everyone in the group whether or not they sent the letter, so no dilution like the Luring Lottery and no unique-claimant rule like Platonia. And the correlation between agents has to be the messy kind: people who think alike, got the same letter, are roughly equally smart, but are not copies. A shared latent variable is fine; identical decision procedures are not. What I'm using the case for is testing whether superrationality is anything more than EDT plus a very strong correlation assumption, so stipulating perfect correlation or identical algorithms is the one move that counts as cheating. The verdicts have to fall out of the theories given the setup, not be written into the setup.

Give me real numbers, N, the threshold, the cost, the value of the good, and a robustness note on what range of N and of cost-to-benefit ratio keeps both the CDT and the EDT verdict standing. Neighbourhood levy, matching grant, club subscription, whatever, just keep it ordinary life.
