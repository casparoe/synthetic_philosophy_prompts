hey, I host a small clinical-ethics podcast — listeners are mostly ICU nurses, transplant coordinators and a handful of philosophers, so they know the terrain but nobody wants a lecture. Next week I'm interviewing a philosopher who's been writing on the determination of death for years, and I really don't want to spend the hour on the usual is brain death really death warm-up she's done a hundred times.

Her position, as far as I can tell: she's a pluralist. There's no single biological event that death is; the neurological and circulatory criteria were never two tests for one underlying state, and we should stop pretending otherwise. The neurological track is justified by irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness, the circulatory track by loss of organismic integration, and trying to force both under one definition is what keeps generating fictions. Then the part I actually want to get into: she wants to retire the dead donor rule and replace it with what she calls the authorized-donor rule. On her telling, donation after circulatory determination of death is already a fudge — permanent only means we've decided not to restart the heart, not that it couldn't be restarted — and at our place the no-touch window is five minutes while a hospital twenty minutes away uses two, which is obviously not a biological boundary. So rather than redefine permanent, she'd let people give prospective authorization for procurement at a point where they aren't dead yet, and treat that authorization, not death, as what does the moral work.

Give me eight or so questions that press on the joints of this rather than on the background. I want at least one each on: whether the permanence-versus-irreversibility move is anything more than a change of subject given that she already thinks circulatory death is a status we assign; what she does with the fact that brain-dead patients on ventilation can keep doing organismic things for a long time — wound healing, temperature regulation, even carrying a pregnancy to term — which looks like trouble for the integration story and looks like the two tracks pulling in different directions; whether the authorized-donor rule is just assisted dying wearing a different badge, and if she's fine with that, why it needs a new legal category instead of riding on the frameworks we already have; and why consent is allowed to do this work for organs when we don't let it do comparable work elsewhere. For each, give me the follow-up keyed to what she'll acutally say back, because I know her moves: she'll reach for the distinction between killing and letting die, for the idea that death is a threshold we set rather than a fact we discover, and for the point that the slippery-slope worry is the very same argument that was used against voluntary assisted dying.

Also give me one question about what would count as evidence against the pluralist package as a whole, and one closing question about the thing she thinks her critics always get wrong. My own sympathies are with keeping the dead donor rule and with something like a whole-brain account, and my listeners go twitchy the second it sounds like we killed the donor, so pitch everything at that level — no bibliographies, no name-dropping papers, just the arguments.
