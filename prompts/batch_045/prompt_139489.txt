I'm in the middle of a PhD (political theory, working on a chapter about the fiduciary/trust framing of humanitarian intervention) and I keep going back and forth on whether the trusteeship model actually earns its keep on Libya 2011, or whether it just restates the ordinary R2P critique in fancier vocabulary. I want you to run the case through it properly, both directions.

Here's the version of the model I've been defending, so we're using the same one. On this view, coercive humanitarian intervention is a *fiduciary power*: the intervener is a trustee of the protected population, holding powers it may exercise only for that population's benefit and never for its own. The incidents I care about are: (1) a duty of loyalty/no-conflict -- the mandate can't be used to advance the intervener's own security, credibility, or domestic-political interests, or to settle a civil war on the side the intervener prefers; (2) a duty not to exceed the mandate's purposes, where the purposes are fixed by the authorization, not by the trustee's own judgment of what would be best; (3) a duty of care and repair, scaled to what the trustee foreseeably breaks -- so the responsibility to rebuild isn't an add-on, it's part of what makes the intervention legitimate in the first place; (4) a duty of impartiality among rival factions of the beneficiary population; (5) a duty to render an account and to exit only when the trust purposes are actually fulfilled, not when the trustee gets tired.

The case (you know it, but pinning the facts so we don't talk past each other): Resolution 1973 was adopted 17 March 2011 under Chapter VII, ten in favour and five abstentions including Brazil, Germany, India, China and Russia; it demanded a ceasefire, imposed a no-fly zone, and authorised all necessary measures to protect civilians and civilian-populated areas under threat of attack, expressly excluding a foreign occupation force of any form on any part of Libyan territory. Gaddafi's forces were at the gates of Benghazi and he had said publicly that they were coming that night with no mercy. NATO took command on 31 March; what began as protecting Benghazi became a campaign that decided the civil war; Gaddafi was killed in October; the operation wound down with no reconstruction commitment, militia fragmentation, a second civil war from 2014, and reports of open slave markets by 2017. Factually none of that is controversial, so don't spend words re-establishing it.

What I want is the disciplined mapping. Who exactly is the trustee here (NATO? the coalition? individual states?), who is the beneficiary ("civilians under threat of attack" versus the Libyan people as a whole versus whoever ends up holding power), what is the trust property, and what in that sequence counts as breach of which incident. Then: does the model actually explain anything the standard "mission creep" / "R2P as cover for regime change" story doesn't -- especially the abstentions, which look a lot like foresight about loyalty and about exit -- and where does it misdescribe the case? My worry is that it forces a clean principal-beneficiary structure onto a situation where the whole problem was that no one could say who the beneficiary was once the air campaign started deciding the war, and that the fiduciary framing quietly smuggles in a verdict about regime change by definitional fiat rather than argument.

And then the other direction: is Libya a confirmation of the model, an anomaly, or a case it was never built for? Be honest if the model is silent somewhere -- my three candidate silences are (a) multi-trustee collective action when there's no single will to hold to a duty, (b) the absence of any forum in which the account is rendered, i.e. a beneficiary with no standing to sue, and (c) the fact that the hardest decision, when to leave, is precisely the one the fiduciary duty language leaves underdetermined. If you think there's a worse one, say so.

One test I'd like you to apply: run Kosovo 1999 through the same model in a short paragraph at the end. If your version of the trusteeship framing ends up condemning the unauthorized NATO action in Kosovo as straightforwardly as it condemns what happened in Libya, I think it's collapsing into just a general theory of when war is permissible and I should cut it from chapter three -- tell me if that's what's happening.

Keep it argumentative and specific rather than survey-like, and please don't pad it out with citations or a reading list -- I can find those myself, and its the mapping I'm stuck on. Not looking for a balanced overview, I want you to take a position on whether this model survives Libya.
