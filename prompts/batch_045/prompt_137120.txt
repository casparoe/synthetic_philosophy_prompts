After forty-odd years as an experimental physicist — most of it in precision metrology at a government laboratory, where I more than once watched a supposedly impossible signal survive replication — I retired and started sitting in on a graduate reading group in the philosophy department, which has just spent three weeks on Dutch book arguments. The others have asked me to write down where I have actually landed rather than only objecting to their positions, and since I have to defend it on Thursday I would like to know whether it hangs together. Roughly, then: (1) I accept the synchronic argument — what is wrong with incoherent degrees of belief is that they commit you to a bundle of bets, each of which you yourself would rate as fair or better, whose net outcome is a loss whatever happens, and I take that to be the substance of why credences should obey the probability axioms. (2) I do not think the accuracy or scoring-rule arguments add anything to this; they look to me like the same point in epistemic dress, with the choice of scoring rule doing work that nobody owns up to, so I would rather rest everything on sure loss. (3) I am persuaded that the betting argument earns only finite additivity: a fair lottery over the positive integers, in which every finite set of tickets has probability zero and yet some ticket wins, seems to me a perfectly coherent state of mind, and I do not think countable additivity is a requirement of rationality. (4) On the diachronic side, I think the Dutch-strategy argument really does establish conditionalization, and the familiar complaint — that the bookie must already know what you are going to learn and how you will react to it — leaves me cold. (5) I hold something like fallibilist regularity: no contingent claim gets credence 1, and no possibility I cannot rule out gets credence 0; and I mean this to cover continuous quantities as well, so that every possible value of a coupling constant within some interval, or every possible waiting time to the next decay, gets positive credence. (6) In genuine ambiguity — say an instrument whose error structure I have no grip on — I think rationality requires an interval or a set of probability functions rather than a single sharp number, and requires me to decline to name any one fair price. (7) Finally, I hold that a demand counts as a requirement of rationality only if the agent could in principle determine from the inside whether they meet it.

A friend in the group read this list, said only that there is a load-bearing inconsistency somewhere in it, and refused to say more until I find it myself. I cannot see one, so I would like an audit rather than a lecture: go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which merely look as though they are; order the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad; and for each say exactly what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the list. Where a pair only appears inconsistent, please say why the appearance dissolves, because I would rather not surrender a commitment for no reason. If a single revision would clean up more than one of the problems at once, tell me which; and if on inspection the list is consistent as it stands, say so plainly and give the strongest version of my friend's objection that you can construct anyway. Please argue the matter directly — I want to be able to defend whatever I conclude at the meeting, so directing me to a literature I would then have to chase is no use to me.
