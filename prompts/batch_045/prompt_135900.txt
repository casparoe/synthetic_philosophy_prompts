Next week I am giving a department colloquium defending the following thesis: Pascal's mugging is not a puzzle about tiny empirical probabilities or about unbounded utility functions; it is a puzzle about logical uncertainty, and the standard replies all fail once the mugging is restated so that the offer's description is short.

My argument has three steps. First, the complexity-penalized reply: the mugger's claim earns only a credence on the order of two to the minus its description length, so a sufficiently outlandish payoff can never outrun the discount. Second, the "conjecture mugger": the mugger hands you the source code of a twenty-line program and offers not to run it. The program searches for a proof of a famous open conjecture and, if it finds one, constructs 3↑↑↑3 suffering minds. You can inspect the code and confirm it does what he says it does — that is what makes the offer's description short — but whether the search terminates with a proof, and hence whether the threat has any teeth at all, is not something you can settle before you decide. So the expected value of paying turns on your attitude toward an unresolved mathematical claim. Third, the payoff: since there is no space of possible worlds in which the conjecture is provable and one in which it is not, a prior over worlds gives you no number here at all. Any number you produce is either arbitrary or the output of some learning process — a logical-induction-style market, say — whose prices need not be a probability measure at any given time and need not have converged on the timescale of your decision. So the mugging is the general problem of acting under unsettled logical uncertainty, and the fix has to be a theory of what to do when your credences are not yet settled; bounding the utility function or rejecting fanaticism changes nothing about the case.

The room will be about forty people: mostly mainstream epistemologists and decision theorists, two economists who work on risk and ambiguity, and one machine-learning person. They are committed to Bayesian expected utility, they are sympathetic to bounded-utility and anti-fanaticism lines, several of them think the mugging is a solved or overhyped puzzle, and nobody there works on logical uncertainty. Expect them to treat logical omniscience as a harmless idealization that can be approximated piecemeal, and to be hostile to anything that looks like replacing credences with market prices for logical claims.
