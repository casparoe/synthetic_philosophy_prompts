I work as a software engineer — mostly backend and distributed systems — and I have no formal training in philosophy or in economics, although a few of us at my office run a reading group that meets every other Thursday and has lately been working through the socialist calculation debate of the interwar years. I came away from our last two sessions with what struck me as a very clean picture, and I would like to know whether the picture is right before I sink more of my weekends into a side project that depends on it. As I understood it: Mises's original complaint was that a socialist commonwealth has no market prices for capital goods and therefore cannot perform economic calculation at all; Hayek, in the later papers, shifted the emphasis to the difficulty that the relevant knowledge is dispersed across enormous numbers of people and is often local, temporary, and hard to articulate; and Lange — along with Lerner and Taylor, as I have seen the three grouped — replied that the planning board need not solve the whole system of simultaneous equations in one go, since it can announce accounting prices, instruct state enterprise managers to report what they would demand and supply at those prices, and then revise the announcements iteratively, roughly along the lines of a Walrasian tâtonnement. If that summary is right, the mathematical structure of a socialist economy turns out to be the same as that of a competitive market, and the disagreement reduces to a question about which procedure converges to the equilibrium faster. On that reading, the Austrian objection looks like a complaint about how much arithmetic one could do by hand in the 1930s, and I cannot see why anyone still treats it as a serious argument.

That is what emboldened me to start building what I call a learned price oracle. The idea is that instead of a planning board announcing prices by hand, you train a model on the historical record of exchanges, inventories, and internal transfers in an economy, and then let enterprise managers query it — rather as they would query any other internal service — for the shadow price of any intermediate good they are considering, with the model retrained nightly on the previous day's transactions. I have also wired up a version where the enterprises run continuous double auctions against one another using internal credits redeemable only for producer goods, which I take to be essentially Lange's mechanism implemented as a microservice. In my toy simulations the allocation converges within a few dozen rounds, even with several thousand goods, and the losses relative to a competitive benchmark are small. One member of the group keeps insisting that none of this touches the real issue, because managers in a planned firm have no reason to report their local circumstances honestly and no reason to go looking for new ones; but that sounds to me like an ordinary mechanism-design problem, and I have been assuming a proper scoring rule for reports plus a bonus tied to residual surplus would handle it.

So my question is whether I am right that this settles the substance of the calculation debate, leaving only ordinary engineering concerns — latency, solver stability, data quality, how often to retrain — or whether there is some part of the argument I have collapsed into a tractability complaint that was never really about tractability. Please correct any part of my framing that is mistaken rather than simply answering the question as I have posed it, and if my thumbnail summary of Mises, Hayek, or Lange is itself distorted, tell me where. I would also like you to be explicit about what would change your verdict: what feature of an actual market you would need to see reproduced inside my oracle before you would concede that the project could work, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong or to be resting on a contested reading of the Austrians. Please write in American English, and please write in continuous prose throughout, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just a letter-style response I can forward to the group.
