Question 3 (30 minutes). Ryle argues against what he calls the 'intellectualist legend' — roughly, the view that a performance counts as intelligent only if the agent first considers and applies a relevant proposition — on the ground that this generates a vicious regress, since the consideration of a proposition is itself an intelligent performance. Stanley and Williamson concede that the regress argument works against the legend, but deny that it tells against intellectualism about knowledge-how. On their view, knowing how to φ consists in knowing, of some way w, that w is a way for one to φ, where that proposition is grasped under a 'practical' mode of presentation; and exercising this knowledge, they add, need involve no antecedent or occurrent consideration of any proposition.

(a) Explain how Stanley and Williamson's intellectualism is meant to survive Ryle's regress.
(b) Set out the objection from the case of a physiologist who knows every relevant fact about the mechanics of bicycle-riding but cannot ride a bicycle, and explain how an intellectualist might reply to it.
(c) Is the residual dispute between intellectualists and anti-intellectualists a substantive disagreement about what knowledge-how is, or a merely verbal dispute about how far the word 'knowledge' should be stretched? Take a position and defend it.

Student's answer:

(a) The regress threatens the legend, not intellectualism itself. Ryle's argument needs the further premise that in order to employ one's knowledge that p, one must contemplate the proposition p; once that premise is in place, any intelligent action would require an infinite series of prior contemplations, which no finite agent could complete. Stanley and Williamson reject that premise. Their thesis is a constitutive one about what the state of knowing-how consists in — standing propositional knowledge, of a way w, that w is a way for one to φ — and a standing state does not have to be occurrently entertained for the action it informs to be intelligent. This is why they can accept Ryle's point that no one breaks into the circle by first thinking through a proposition, while still holding that knowledge-how is a species of knowledge-that. The practical mode of presentation then does the separate work of distinguishing the expert from someone who has merely book knowledge of the same facts.

But the escape has a cost. If the knowledge in question is merely a standing state that the agent need never occurrently entertain, then it is not really a propositional attitude at all but a disposition, and the claim that knowing-how is a disposition to behave in certain ways is precisely Ryle's own positive view. So in order to answer the regress, Stanley and Williamson have to abandon the thesis they were defending: intellectualism, pushed far enough to meet Ryle's argument, collapses into anti-intellectualism. This, I take it, is the strongest form of Ryle's objection.

(b) The physiologist knows every proposition about balance, steering, and the mechanics of cycling, and yet cannot ride. If knowing how to ride just were knowing some proposition, she would know how to ride; she does not; so intellectualism is false. The intellectualist reply is that she does not know the relevant proposition in the first place: lacking a practical mode of presentation of the way of riding, she does not know, of that way, that it is a way for her to ride. She has a great deal of theoretical knowledge about cycling and none of the propositional knowledge with which intellectualism identifies knowing how to ride.

This case is the mirror image of Ryle's own example of the rider who cycles well but cannot state any relevant propositions. Together the two cases show that the two kinds of knowledge come apart, and in particular that knowing-that is not necessary for knowing-how: the physiologist has all the knowing-that and none of the knowing-how, the rider has all the knowing-how and none of the knowing-that. Intellectualism therefore fails in both directions.

(c) The dispute is substantive, not verbal. A verbal dispute would be one in which the parties agree about all the facts and differ only about a label. Here they disagree about what sort of state knowledge-how is: whether it is factive, whether it has truth-evaluable content, whether it is the kind of thing that can be stored, forgotten, or passed on by testimony in the way propositional knowledge can. Those are not questions about the word 'knowledge', and no amount of attention to our use of the word would settle them. Nor does it help to point out that both sides accept the same data about cases: the cases determine what has to be explained, not which theoretical description explains it. What makes the dispute look verbal is that each side can absorb the other's cases — the intellectualist by appealing to modes of presentation, the anti-intellectualist by distinguishing abilities from mere behaviour — so that no single case is decisive. But a disagreement can be genuine and still resistant to argument from cases, and that, I think, is what we have here.
