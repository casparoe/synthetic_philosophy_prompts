Dear Dr Hallam,

I wonder whether I might impose upon you. For three years I have been writing a novel — finished now but for the last sixty pages — in which a concert pianist dying of a glioma consents to a high-resolution scan of her brain and to the reconstruction of that brain as a running computer model. The book's final movement turns almost entirely on whether the model is her, and I have begun to suspect that the commitments I have been quietly relying on do not all sit comfortably together. Before I commit myself to the ending I should like an audit of them, and I have set them out below as plainly as I can.

(1) I take it that what makes something a mind is its causal or functional organisation rather than what it is made of, so that a sufficiently faithful model of her brain would be a mind and would be conscious.

(2) But I do not think the brain is a computer in the way a laptop is. There is no stored programme, no instruction set, no clean division between memory and processing; the signalling is graded, stochastic and continuous, and I am persuaded that spike timing and neuromodulation do genuine causal work.

(3) I think the celebrated Chinese Room argument fails — that it smuggles in the very point at issue by demanding that understanding be manifest to something like an inner homunculus rather than to the system as a whole.

(4) I also have a good deal of sympathy with what I have seen called the triviality worry: that which computation, if any, a physical system performs is not a fact about that system alone, but depends on the description we happen to find useful — on which view a wall or a weather front implements any number of programmes.

(5) The novel requires the model to be her, and not a replica of her. A court has to decide whether the manuscript the model produces in her last weeks is hers.

(6) And, crucially, I do not want fidelity to consist in matching her past behaviour. The scene the entire book is written towards is the model's refusing something that everyone assumes the woman would have accepted. So the reconstruction must get right not only what she did but what she would have done in circumstances that never arose.

(7) I am not a dualist, and I am drawn to the thought that consciousness is not all-or-nothing — that a coarser reconstruction might be dimly conscious rather than simply not conscious at all.

What I want from you is this. Which of these genuinely pull against one another, and which only appear to? Please rank the real tensions in order of severity, and for each one say precisely what would have to be surrendered and what surrendering it would cost the book — I would much rather learn now that a scene cannot honestly be written than discover it in proof. Where an apparent clash dissolves on inspection, tell me why it dissolves, since I am as interested in what I need not worry about as in what I must. And if a single revision would tidy up the most, tell me which one.

My own suspicion falls on (2), but a reader of an early draft insisted the real trouble was (4), and I could not see why; I should be especially grateful for light on that. I am not asking for reading: I want the shape of the argument, not sources. I should be grateful if you would answer in British English and, if it may be managed, in fewer than nine hundred words.

Yours sincerely,
—
