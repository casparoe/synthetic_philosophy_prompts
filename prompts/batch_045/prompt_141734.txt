I sit on a small committee at a regional public health insurer that is rewriting the clinical criteria we use to decide which conditions count as covered diagnoses, and I've come to think that most of our arguments are really arguments about the concept of disease — so before I present on Thursday I'd like your view on one specific proposal of mine. The proposal is this: nearly every dispute we get stuck on is a dispute about value wearing a white coat. Whether chronic fatigue syndrome is a "real" illness, whether age-related hearing loss is a disease or just ageing, whether a trait that causes trouble only in some environments (we're currently arguing about a haemoglobin variant that is harmless here and dangerous where malaria is endemic) belongs on the list — these feel like fights about what we disapprove of or who deserves help, not about facts. So I want to split the job in two. Stage one: adopt a strictly descriptive, value-free definition of disease, of the sort I've seen called the biostatistical theory — a condition is a disease just in case it is a statistically subnormal reduction, relative to a reference class of same-sex, same-aged members of the species, in some part's contribution to survival and reproduction — and let the epidemiology settle each case. Stage two, explicitly political and not pretending to be science: decide which of the resulting diseases we fund, and how generously. My hope is that this takes the politics out of classification and puts it where it can be argued about honestly.

A colleague says the picture is incoherent, and her two objections annoy me because I can't answer either. First, she says the descriptive half contains every decision that actually matters: who goes into the reference class, which part-functions count as the relevant ones, where the statistical threshold is set, and whether the subnormal function has to explain the patient's condition rather than merely accompany it — and no amount of data settles any of that, so "let the epidemiology decide" is empty. Second, she says the split doesn't do the work I want, because what we are really being asked to sort is not "is this a disease?" but "do we owe this person something?", and those come apart in both directions: our draft criteria already contain a provisional category she has christened "non-disease pathology" — an asymptomatic finding that is clearly statistically subnormal and clearly not something anyone wants treated — and at the other extreme there are people in real distress, seeking back a capacity they have lost, in whom no subnormal part-function can be identified, and who would fall outside coverage altogether under my scheme.

So I'd be grateful for your view on three things. Is she right that the two-stage picture cannot work as I've described it, and if so is there a defensible version of it that survives? Am I wrong to assume that a definition's being value-free is what makes it decide hard cases — that is, does a naturalist definition of disease do any real sorting work for a committee like mine, or does it merely relocate the argument? And is the alternative really, as one of our clinicians put it, that disease becomes "whatever a given culture happens to disapprove of"? I don't want that either, and if those are the only two options I would rather find out now, in front of you, than in front of the committee. Please tell me plainly which of my assumptions is mistaken rather than telling me both sides have a point; I have twenty minutes on Thursday, and no need for citations or reading lists — I'll go and find those myself afterwards.
