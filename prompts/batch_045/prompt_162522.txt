I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about direct democracy, and the only honest way I can get at it is by recording my gut verdict on particular decisions and seeing what shape they have. Below are the cases with the answer I find myself giving. Most I am settled on; one I oscillate about; and there are three places where my verdicts will not sit comfortably together.

Settled verdicts:

1. Whether our town should merge its administration with the neighbouring town's: a direct vote of both electorates strikes me as entirely proper.
2. Whether to build a tram line paid for by a dedicated local levy: proper.
3. Whether a new housing development should go up on the edge of town: proper, though I would not extend that to the design of the regional housing target it contributes to.
4. Whether to retain the death penalty for a narrowly defined category of murder: should not be settled by a statewide vote.
5. Whether to ban the construction of new minarets nationwide, of the kind that has in fact been put to the Swiss electorate: should not be settled by a vote.
6. Whether to raise the top rate of income tax by five points: should not be settled by a vote, because it has to be weighed against spending and borrowing in a way that a single yes/no question cannot capture.
7. Whether to leave a supranational union, along the lines of the 2016 British vote: should not have been settled by a vote.
8. Whether to legalise assisted dying: I think this one is a proper subject for a vote.
9. A proposition of the sort that has appeared on Californian ballots, providing that any future increase in a tax must carry a legislative supermajority: should not be put to a vote, and putting it to a vote strikes me as worse than the legislature simply enacting it.
10. A proposition stipulating that it may itself be amended only by a further referendum: not proper either, and I cannot get myself to care much about whether this is worse or better than case 9.

The case I go back and forth on:

11. Ratifying an international treaty that restricts what future governments may do for the next thirty years. Half the time this looks like the ideal referendum subject: a large, determinate, self-contained commitment that everyone will have to live under. The rest of the time it looks like exactly the rule-changing I object to in 9 and 10.

Where my verdicts will not cohere:

12. Cases 1 and 7 are both about the boundaries and composition of political units, and I answer them oppositely. If my objection to 7 is that the leave option had no determinate content — no one was voting for a describable state of affairs — then I should be more uneasy about 2 and 3 than I am, since those options are fuzzy too. If my objection is irreversibility, then I owe an account of why borrowing to build a railway, which is also hard to undo, is not disqualified as well. I cannot tell which objection I am actually making.
13. Case 8 sits badly with 4 and 5. All three concern life, death and ultimate value, and in each case a majority would be disposing of something belonging mainly to a vulnerable minority. The only difference I can locate is that 8 permits something for those who seek it while 4 and 5 impose on the unwilling — but I suspect that is special pleading in defence of a verdict I happen to like.
14. The same question put to the voters by the council feels fine to me; the same question forced onto the ballot by a petition of signatures makes me uneasy. I cannot tell whether that is a distinction a principle should recognise or just my distrust of petition campaigns.

My tentative principle, such as it is: a decision is properly put to a direct vote when both options are determinate, when it can be settled on its own without being traded off against other decisions, and when it does not dispose of the interests of people who cannot defend themselves at the ballot box; everything else belongs to a legislature.

What I would like from you: state the principle that best fits these verdicts — one principle if there is one, otherwise the smallest set of principles that does the work — and for each clause name the case that forces it. Then tell me plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which, if any, I ought to give up rather than add a clause for. Finally, give its verdicts on the cases I have not settled: case 11, and also an advisory rather than binding referendum, a citizens' assembly selected by lot and given binding power instead of a referendum, a minimum turnout or quorum rule, a vote on whether to hold a vote, and a secession question where the seceding region contains a fifth of the electorate. Argue it out rather than pointing me to the literature; I am not after reading recommendations.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle itself, or the flat statement that no single principle fits and which verdict has to go — and only then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked.
