I've become convinced of a defensible version of Descartes' denial of minds to animals, but not on the ground usually given for it. I don't think the view rests on the immateriality of the soul; I read it as resting on his two behavioural tests, which I take to express one criterion — the difference between acting from knowledge and acting merely from the disposition of one's organs, where the observable marks of the former are the creative, context-appropriate use of signs and successful action in genuinely novel circumstances. On that reading Descartes advanced a falsifiable empirical claim, and the standard rebuttals miss it: greater behavioural flexibility, tool use, or trained sign systems show only that animal behaviour is richer than he supposed, not that it is reason-responsive.

Tell me what would count against this — a map of what the view risks, not a list of objections I can wave away. What observation or argument would force me to abandon the criterion? I keep coming back to three pressure points, and I want each assessed.

(i) Partial cases. Suppose we encountered a lineage that uses structured, novel, context-appropriate signals, but only within a narrow domain — present food and threats, never absent, past, or counterfactual matters — and that never corrects or disputes another's signals. Does the criterion have anything principled to say here, or is it a binary that can only be applied wholesale, so that graded evidence either drags obviously mindless creatures over the line or lets me keep sliding the threshold?

(ii) Humans who fail. Pre-linguistic infants, and adults who have lost language entirely and act from what looks like organ-disposition alone, get sorted with the animals. I have independent grounds for refusing that verdict; if the criterion cannot accommodate them, it is doing less work than I claimed.

(iii) Complete mechanisms. If we had a model predicting a creature's every movement from prior stimulations, I would count that as confirmation of mechanism. But then the criterion is only as strong as our inability to construct such models for ourselves — an epistemological fact about us, not a fact about the creature.

For each, say whether a defender of the view would concede it as counting against them or dismiss it, and why; rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward; and give an honest verdict on insulation. If the acting-from-knowledge contrast is in principle invisible in any behaviour a mechanism could produce, then the view is safe but empty as a claim about animals, and I want to be told if that is where I have ended up.
