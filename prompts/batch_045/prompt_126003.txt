I'm writing up the second chapter of my DPhil thesis on the epistemology of testimony, and before I circulate it to my department's work-in-progress group I'd like a hard consistency check on the package of commitments I've ended up with. I'll state them flatly, using my own labels. I know the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate well enough that you can assume it rather than summarise it; what I can't see is whether what I've assembled hangs together, and I'd rather be told I'm worrying about the wrong thing than be told my worries are well founded when they aren't.

Two desiderata I treat as fixed rather than negotiable. (D1) The account should vindicate the testimonial knowledge of ordinary hearers, including children and hearers dealing with speakers from very different cultures: I don't want a view on which critical sophistication, or a capacity to assess evidence about informants, is a precondition on knowing what you're told. (D2) Whether a hearer knows on the basis of testimony should not be hostage to empirical enquiries that no actual hearer could run.

The commitments:

(1) Default entitlement. A hearer who is told that p has, absent defeaters, an entitlement to believe p that is not constituted by, and need not be supported by, any evidence she possesses about that speaker's truthfulness or competence, or about the reliability of testimony in general. Testimony is a basic source of knowledge, on a par with perception and memory, and it is the reductionist's demand for positive reasons that stands in need of argument.

(2) Asymmetry. The entitlement is defeasible but not confirmable. A reason to doubt the speaker, or the domain, defeats it; evidence that the speaker is reliable does not add to it. I mean entitlement, not credence: I'm happy that a dossier of an informant's perfect track record should raise a hearer's confidence, but not that it should improve her epistemic standing with respect to p.

(3) Base-Rate Degeneration. The default is domain-relative and can lapse wholesale. If a hearer has reason to think that the pool of informants she is drawing on is systematically unreliable in a given domain — below, say, half accurate — then her entitlement in that domain lapses entirely, and she can acquire knowledge from a speaker in it only if she has positive, speaker-specific reasons for thinking that speaker reliable. I invoke this for enclaves of systematic falsehood: propaganda bureaucracies, closed communities with an official history, and the case at (7).

(4) The Reference-Class Squeeze. I reject the reductionist's appeal to an inductive track record, and I want this to be independent of (1) rather than a corollary of it. Any base rate specific enough to guide acceptance of a particular claim from a particular speaker must be computed over a reference class narrow enough to leave the sample hopelessly small; any reference class wide enough to be well supported is too coarse to tell a hearer anything about the case in front of her. The reductionist's evidence is therefore never available at the grain at which it would need to be available.

(5) Anti-luck scepticism. I reject safety and sensitivity conditions on knowledge. They seem to me ad hoc, and I think that once you allow merely counterfactual error to defeat knowledge — as the fake-barn literature does — you buy that result at the price of a scepticism about perception that no one should accept.

(6) Pessimism about moral testimony, stated in its strong form. A hearer who is told that a practice is unjust, by a speaker whose moral perceptiveness she has no independent way of assessing, acquires no moral knowledge from that testimony. She may end up with a true belief and she may even be justified, but the testimony does not put her in a position to know.

(7) Verdict: the Enclave. A historian is among the first outside researchers admitted to a closed community. Four out of five of the members who agree to speak to her recite, as instructed by their elders, a fabricated history; the fabrications are undetectable without years of comparative work she has not done and could not now do. The one member she interviews happens to be one of the minority who tells the truth, and she forms a true belief from what he says. My verdict: she does not know. And I want to add that this is not because she fell short of any norm of inquiry — she did everything a careful researcher in her position would do — and not because she had any reason whatsoever to suspect the community's accounts.

(8) Verdict: the Associate's Memo. A junior associate is told by a senior colleague she respects that a practice her team is about to sign off on is exploitative. She cannot assess his moral judgement, she believes him, and she refuses to sign. My verdict, following (6): she does not know that the practice is exploitative, though she may be justified in believing it.

My own suspicion is that (2) is the soft spot: that I cannot state the asymmetry without making entitlement look like a quantity after all, in which case (1) is just reductionism with extra steps. I may be wrong about where the trouble is.

What I'd like from you:

(i) An audit of the package. Which of (1) to (8), and of (1) to (8) against D1 and D2, are genuinely in tension — ranked by severity, not listed as five equal problems. For each tension, say which commitment has to be revised or abandoned, and what the revision costs me: which of my verdicts at (7) and (8), or which desideratum, I would have to surrender. Argue for the ordering in terms of how much of the package each tension forces me to rebuild.

(ii) A note on which apparent tensions dissolve once the commitments are stated precisely. Include, if it is one of them, my worry about (2), and flag any place where two commitments only look incompatible because I have worded one of them carelessly and the fix is a rewording rather than a revision.

(iii) An identification of anything in the set that is doing no work — inert given the others, entailed by them, or unable ever to fire in the cases for which I invoke it.

(iv) The single revision that tidies up the most, together with the one thing it commits me to that I am likely to dislike.

Please take the cases exactly as I have stipulated them: don't rescue me by redescribing the Enclave or by supplying facts I haven't given you. No citations or reading suggestions, and no appeal to how these views are standardly classified — I want the argumentative connections between my own commitments, in under 900 words. If you need machinery I haven't mentioned — a reliability condition, a knowledge-first treatment of entitlement, a normative account of defeat — name it and say in one sentence what it costs me.
