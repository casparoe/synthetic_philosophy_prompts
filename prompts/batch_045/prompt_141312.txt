I was appointed in March to fill an unexpired seat on the grant review committee of a private health foundation, and last week we took our first renewal vote of the cycle. I have been asked to draft the written opinion that will go to the full committee before we announce a decision, and I would like you to draft it for me. The grantee's board meets in nine days and our decision has to be posted forty-eight hours before that, so this is not a leisurely exercise.

The relevant provisions of our governing bylaw are, in substance, these. First: the committee may renew a grant only if it finds (a) that the grantee met the scientific milestones stipulated in the grant agreement and (b) that the program remains consistent with the foundation's current funding priorities. The bylaw uses "may," not "shall" — it does not say that renewal is mandatory when both findings are made. Second: every decision must be accompanied by published written findings, which appear on our website. Third: any finding that departs from the program staff's written recommendation requires the affirmative votes of five of the seven members. Fourth: the committee decides by majority vote. The program staff recommended renewal in writing, finding both conditions met.

We balloted on three questions, by secret written ballot, all seven members voting. Call them Q1 (milestones met), Q2 (still consistent with current priorities), and Q3 (renew).

Chair (Adeyemi, molecular biologist): yes, yes, yes
Brandt (pediatrician): yes, yes, yes
Me (community member): yes, yes, no
Okonkwo (health economist): yes, no, no
Reyes (former grantee, now a nonprofit director): yes, no, no
Lindqvist (biostatistician): no, yes, no
Haddad (board liaison): no, no, no

So five of seven found the milestones met, four of seven found the program still consistent with our priorities, and five of seven voted against renewal. Only two members voted yes on the operative question.

There are four further things the memo has to deal with.

Our general counsel, who sits with us but does not vote, says there is nothing here to resolve: because the bylaw says "may," the two findings are only necessary conditions and never sufficient ones, so a member can consistently hold that both are met and that renewal should nonetheless be refused. On that reading my own ballot is perfectly coherent and the apparent conflict dissolves. I am not sure it dissolves rather than moves. If we deny renewal while publishing findings that both of the charter's criteria are satisfied, we owe the grantee a reason, and the reason any of us would give is one no majority of the committee endorsed. I would also like the memo to be honest about my own position rather than launder it: I voted against renewal largely because I think the foundation should be spending down its endowment on a different portfolio altogether, which is a discretionary judgment of exactly the kind the bylaw may or may not license.

The chair has proposed that we settle this by voting first on which method we should use to combine our votes. I would like the memo to address that proposal directly. The choice among methods would be made by the same seven people with the same three ballots each, and I do not want to accuse anyone of anything in a written document, but a recommendation that the majority should pick the counting rule in the very case where the counting rule decides the outcome needs to be handled carefully.

Dr. Lindqvist has asked, after the ballots were unsealed, to change her answer on Q1 from no to yes. She says she assumed only Q3 mattered and voted no on Q1 to register opposition, and that on reflection the milestones were met. Whether we allow that is a separate question from what we should do about it, and I suspect it changes nothing in the tallies — but I would like the memo to say so explicitly, and to say what it would imply for future votes if premise-level questions are the ones members treat as costless.

Finally, one of our members found, in a blog post I could not fully follow, a suggestion for a third method: instead of either counting the premise questions and deriving the decision, or counting the decision directly, choose the package of answers — one answer to each of the three questions — that departs as little as possible from the ballots as actually cast, in the sense that it requires the fewest individual answers across all seven ballots to be flipped. I would like the memo to evaluate that method on these numbers, both on the reading where the two criteria are jointly sufficient for renewal and on the reading where they are not, and to check whether the package it selects is one we are legally able to publish given the five-of-seven rule for departing from the staff recommendation.

What I need is a memo of roughly eleven hundred words, addressed to the committee and copied to general counsel. It should state a bottom line in the first paragraph: renew or do not renew, and which findings we publish. It should then give the reasoning that would survive being read by three scientists with no patience for procedural argument, by a board liaison who will read it as a legal document, and by a grantee who will read it on our website. Beyond this one case, we process about forty renewals a year, so it should recommend a standing rule for how we combine votes when the pattern recurs, not merely a way out of this vote. It should give the strongest available argument for whichever bottom line it does not recommend, stated as strongly as the argument it does recommend. And it should tell me what to do if general counsel rejects my reading of "may": specifically, which of the outcomes available to us remain publishable if the two criteria really are sufficient.

One constraint on style. A colleague in a political science department told me this sort of situation has a name and a literature attached to it, with theorems about how no method satisfies every condition one might want. I could not follow her summary and I am not going to rest a real decision affecting a real grantee on a half-remembered reference. Please write the memo so that its argument works entirely off the bylaw and the twenty-one answers above, with no footnotes, no citations, and no appeals to what anyone has proved.
