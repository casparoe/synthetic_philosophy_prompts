I'm writing the final for my upper-level seminar on the philosophy of AI (third-years, about half philosophy and half CS, 60 minutes, closed book, no laptops) and I want one essay question on Turing's child-machine proposal — the bit where he suggests that instead of trying to write a programme that simulates the adult mind, we should write one that simulates a child's and then educate it. He makes the point in the Learning Machines part of the 1950 Mind paper and it's already there in the 1948 report on intelligent machinery, where he talks about unorganised machines and training by something like reward and punishment. I'm deliberately paraphrasing rather than quoting, and dont want the question to hinge on anybody's exact wording. A colleague I had coffee with teaches ML and says her students arrive assuming the 1950 paper is really about chatbots, so I'd also like the question to quietly kill that reading.

The trouble is that every year I get two answers and nothing in between. One says Turing basically predicted deep learning: we train instead of program, he was right, next question. The other says the whole thing was refuted decades ago, by nativist arguments about the poverty of the stimulus plus the no-free-lunch style point that a learner with no built-in structure can't do better than chance across possible environments. Both of those land around a 2:1 and I want a question that makes them impossible to write.

What I think is actually interesting, and what I'd like the question to force, is that the proposal reads like three different claims that students constantly run together: (R1) a claim about engineering labour — that it's easier to specify an initial state plus a training regime than to hand-code the finished competence; (R2) a claim about blankness — that the initial machine can be nearly content-free because adult ability is mostly the product of education (that's the little-mechanism-lots-of-blank-sheets line); and (R3) a claim about culture — that nothing reaches adult-level intelligence without being brought up in something like a culture, so intelligence isn't something a single box does on its own. Then there's the wrinkle I can't get out of my head: every curriculum has a curriculum designer. If the reason we can't hand-code the competence is that we don't know what it consists in, whatever we can't put in the machine has to go somewhere, and if it goes into the training regime and the training data then the blankness of the initial machine starts to look like an illusion — unless Turing can say that the education is itself something nobody designed, the way a culture isn't designed. I genuinely don't know whether he's entitled to that, and I don't know whether it matters that today's systems are trained on vastly more text than any child hears (stipulate that contrast in the question, I don't want an argument about the actual numbers) and that nobody really understands what goes on inside them, which is arguably just what Turing expected when he remarks somewhere that the teacher of a learning machine will often be largely ignorant of what's going on inside it while still being able to predict its behaviour.

So here's what I need: (a) a clean question-only version I can paste into the paper, 90 words max, ideally in two parts so it has a shape; (b) a model answer of 800–900 words; (c) a marking scheme out of 25 built on five or six named moves — the specific things a top answer must do — with the marks for each and one line on how candidates typically bugger each one up; and (d) an A/B boundary rule plus a cap rule, i.e. what's the highest mark available to an answer that never separates the three readings, or that treats the modern case as a straight vindication. One part of the question should make them put the child-machine proposal next to the imitation game itself: if a successfully educated machine is one whose behaviour we can predict but whose internals we don't understand, does the imitation game then tell us anything beyond the fact that we've built a very good mimic, and is that a problem for Turing or is he fine with it? I don't want the model answer to settle that by fiat — I want it to show which move settles it, and mark accordingly.
