You are a professional philosopher who works on normative ethics and decision theory, with a specialty in intergenerational justice and the foundations of discounting. Think carefully and work in stages. First, brainstorm five or six genuinely distinct candidate principles about when, if ever, the mere lateness of a good or a harm is itself a reason to weight it less — do not settle on the first plausible one, and do not assume that the candidates must be utilitarian. Second, test each candidate against the verdicts below, one verdict at a time, and record where each candidate goes wrong. Third, recommend one principle, or a small set of rivals, state it precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you again, and attach to each clause the case that motivates it. Fourth, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot save, and which one you would overturn if forced. Where a verdict of mine is really explained by something other than pure time preference — uncertainty, opportunity cost, diminishing marginal value, the chance that the beneficiary never exists, or the fact that I will be dead — say so explicitly and peel it off; I want to know whether anything is left that is genuinely about time as such.

I should say who I am: I write novels, not papers. My current book is a saga set in one river valley across five centuries, and several scenes turn on a single institution I invented, the Nine Generations Trust, whose deed instructs its trustees to do the most good they can for the valley's inhabitants over a 500-year span, saying nothing about how to weight a benefit by the date on which it falls. Chapter 11 is a trustees' meeting in which they must choose between relieving present hardship and locking resources into instruments that mature long after every one of them is dead. I can live with a character who is inconsistent; I cannot write the argument in the room unless I know what the defensible options actually are. So please don't give me a survey of the literature, a bibliography, or citations — I have no way to check them, and they would be useless to me. Reason from the cases.

Here is the tentative principle I have been working with: a discount is legitimate only if its spatial analogue is also legitimate — that is, only if I would accept the same discounting for a beneficiary who is far away rather than far in the future. Take it apart and repair it if you can, or replace it if it can't be repaired.

My verdicts:

1. An evening of exactly the kind I most enjoy, tonight, versus an identical evening a year from now: I take tonight, and I feel no guilt. I take this to be contaminated by opportunity cost, but I am not certain that's all of it.

2. Five days of toothache now versus five days of exactly similar toothache ten years from now, with dread, memory, knock-on effects and uncertainty all stipulated away: I postpone, and I feel no guilt, even though I can't point to anything except the date. This is the verdict I am least able to defend and least willing to give up.

3. My own book published now to a small readership versus, by my own instruction, sealed and published in 2175 to a larger one: I publish now, and I think that is self-interest or vanity, not ethics.

4. The trustees spending a large share of the Trust on a flood barrier that protects the valley in 2150: they should spend it. I feel essentially no discount for the fact that the beneficiaries are unborn.

5. The Trust also holds Tricentury Bonds, bought in 2024, paying a fixed sum to whoever is living in the valley in 2324. Spending to *increase* that payout moves me much less than spending to relieve present poverty, and unlike in 4 I feel no guilt about that. My principle says 5 and 4 must be treated alike. I don't believe they should be.

6. In the novel a woman named Ilse enters the Long Sleep in 2026 and wakes in 2324, subjectively continuous, forty years old on the inside. A year of her suffering in 2325 versus a year of a stranger's comparable suffering in 2025: I weigh them equally. The calendar gap is larger here than in 5, and I discount it not at all.

7. Relieving a stranger's comparable toothache in a valley on the other side of the world versus relieving my neighbour's today: I feel more pull toward my neighbour, and I judge that I ought not to. Distance is not a reason.

8. So the pair that I cannot reconcile is 5 and 7: I endorse a discount across time that I refuse across space, while my own principle says the two stand or fall together. Something has to give. Is there a principled difference — that the future beneficiary may not exist, that I stand to her as cause and ancestor rather than as neighbour, that the transfer can fail, that she cannot now be wronged by a comparison she is not there to make — or am I simply being inconsistent?

Three cases I haven't made up my mind about, on which I want your principle's verdicts and its reasons: (a) the Trust's beneficiaries in 2320 will be, the trustees know, roughly eight times richer per person than the valley's inhabitants are now — may the trustees discount for that, and is this a different discount from the one in 5? (b) A policy that makes 2200 much worse for everyone alive then, but without which a different and equally numerous set of people would have existed instead: does my principle still condemn it? (c) Spending to avert a catastrophe that would end the valley's line in 2200 versus spending the same sum to avert an equal amount of present suffering, where the future catastrophe, if averted, is averted for people who would otherwise never have existed at all.

Finally: tell me what the trustees should say to each other in chapter 11. Not dialogue — the shape of the reasoning a careful trustee could actually voice, including the clause she would have to concede is doing the heavy lifting, and the verdict she would have to swallow if she follows your principle through.
