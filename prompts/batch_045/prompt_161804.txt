Last night I argued at length with a friend who works in policy evaluation. Her claim was that we should expect Allais-shaped preferences at the level of public policy even if every citizen is a faultless expected-utility maximiser: once a planner chooses for a population whose members differ sharply in risk aversion, and once that planner aggregates expected well-being through an inequality-averse social welfare function, the safe option wins in the first problem and the risky one wins in the second, and no one needs to abandon expected utility to explain the pattern. I came away thinking she is half right — aggregation really can break independence — but that the Allais pattern in particular cannot be produced that way, and that the reason is worth examining. I want to turn this into a problem-set question for my MPhil seminar on rational choice and social choice. Here is the question as I have drafted it; compound lotteries are throughout evaluated by their reduced form.

Both problems are decided by a single draw from an urn containing 89 red balls and 11 white balls. All citizens take the urn composition as their probabilities, money utilities are increasing, and u(£0) = 0 for everyone.

Problem 1. A: £1m whichever ball is drawn. B: £1m if red; if white, £5m with probability 10/11 and £0 with probability 1/11.
Problem 2. C: £0 if red; £1m if white. D: £0 if red; if white, £5m with probability 10/11 and £0 with probability 1/11.

A planner ranks lotteries on behalf of n citizens by an increasing function F of the vector of their expected utilities.

(a) [7 marks] For an arbitrary citizen i, compute EU_i(B) − EU_i(A) and EU_i(D) − EU_i(C), and show that they are one and the same quantity δ_i. State the condition on u_i under which δ_i is positive, and say how demanding that condition is. Hence explain why no increasing F, however inequality-averse, can generate the Allais pattern (A preferred to B together with D preferred to C) with these numbers.

(b) [7 marks] Show that F can nevertheless violate independence. Taking F to be the leximin rule, give a two-citizen counterexample: specify expected-utility vectors for three lotteries L, M and R and a mixing weight α in (0,1) such that F ranks M above L but ranks αL + (1−α)R above αM + (1−α)R. Name the structural feature of your example that makes the reversal possible, and explain why that feature is absent in part (a).

(c) [6 marks] Suppose instead that the planner evaluates lotteries ex post: each state s determines an allocation, and W(L) = Σ_s p(s)·F(u_1(x_1(s)), …, u_n(x_n(s))). Prove that this ranking satisfies independence for any F whatsoever. In two or three sentences, say what the contrast between (b) and (c) shows about where violations of independence can and cannot come from.

Please write a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme that lists the specific moves an excellent answer must make, the marks attached to each (20 in total), and, for each part, the one mistake or omission that should cost marks — I expect students to get part (a) right mechanically and then draw the wrong moral from it, so I want the scheme to catch that. Note explicitly any assumption the answer has to state rather than take for granted. Write both the model answer and the marking scheme in British English, and do not cite or attribute anything.
