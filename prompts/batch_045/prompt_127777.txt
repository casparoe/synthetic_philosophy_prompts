I work on a small product team at a mid-sized insurance company, and in about six weeks we are supposed to put an LLM-based assistant in front of customers to answer questions about what their homeowners policy actually covers. The internal argument has become oddly abstract: one engineer keeps insisting that without a worst-case guarantee — some argument that the system cannot, on any input it will be given, state a coverage rule that is not in the policy document — we should not ship at all; my director replies that practically nothing in the history of engineering comes with worst-case guarantees, and that we ship bridges, drugs and autopilots on the strength of average-case performance plus monitoring and iteration. I have been reading the same disagreement in AI alignment, where it is conducted about frontier models and existential risk, and I would like to test it against a real case instead of going in more circles.

The episode I want analyzed is Air Canada's customer-support chatbot and the 2024 decision of British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal. As I understand it: the chatbot told a customer that he could book a flight at full price and claim the bereavement fare retroactively, the airline's actual policy did not permit that, the airline refused to honour what the chatbot had said, and the tribunal held the airline responsible rather than accepting the argument that the chatbot was a separate entity answerable for its own statements. I do not need the citation or the exact damages; you can take that summary as given and I do not want the analysis to turn on any further detail.

So what I am asking for is a disciplined mapping, not a verdict on the airline. On the worst-case side: what exactly would a guarantee have consisted of here — over what space is the worst case even taken (inputs, user intents, policy documents, deployment contexts, drift over time, adversarially chosen prompts), what would it have cost to obtain, and was it obtainable at all? On the average-case side: what did the airline's actual assurance story look like — testing, monitoring, escalation paths, disclaimer language — and how well did it work in the sense that matters, which is not the failure rate but the cost of the failures that got through? Then the direction I care about more: does the episode vindicate the average-case position, embarrass it, or is it simply the kind of case the debate was never about? My own attempt to cash this out is what I call the backstop test — average-case reliability under strict liability is an acceptable substitute for a worst-case guarantee when the affected party can detect the failure, the harm is compensable in money, failures are independent across deployments rather than correlated, and there is a solvent principal to attach liability to. Tell me whether this case supports the backstop test or is a degenerate instance of it, and whether anything in the case makes you want to add or drop a condition.

Two final constraints. First, be honest about the disanalogy I keep tripping over: this chatbot was not misaligned in any goal-directed sense — it was doing more or less what it was trained to do, producing plausible text — so if its failure was not a failure of alignment, what work is the alignment frame doing here, and is there a version of the worst-case argument that survives that point, or does the case show that the real content of the frame is about irreversibility and correlated tails rather than about goals? Second, mark where the lens has nothing to say rather than stretching the mapping. If it sharpens the contrast, you may bring in Microsoft's Tay in 2016, a system whose worst case was reached adversarially rather than by accident, but only if it does real work in the argument rather than serving as decoration.
