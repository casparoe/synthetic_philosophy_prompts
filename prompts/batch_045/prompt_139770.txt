I work in housing policy for a mid-sized city, and over fifteen years I have now watched the rent-regulation argument play out in essentially the same form in four different places. What strikes me is not the heat of it but its shape. Economists are close to unanimous against rent control — noticeably more unanimous than they are against the minimum wage, which is the other canonical price control and which divides the profession far more evenly. The exchange itself never advances: the critics produce the familiar list of effects (deferred maintenance, misallocation of the existing stock, tenants locked into units that no longer fit them, conversion or withdrawal of rental housing, and a transfer that frequently lands on higher-income long-sitting tenants rather than the poor); the defenders reply that these effects are modeled rather than measured, that the models compare a controlled market against an idealised competitive market that has never appeared on a ballot, when the actual alternative is a market already shaped by zoning, tax treatment and construction costs, and that what the policy really purchases is not cheap rent but security — the ability of a household to remain in a home, a school catchment and a set of relationships in which it has sunk years of tenancy-specific investment that a landlord would otherwise be free to convert into future rent.

I am not asking which way to vote, and I do not want citations or a survey of the literature. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, supported by what each side actually commits itself to when it argues this way.

My own hypothesis has three parts, and I would like you to tell me which of them is load-bearing and which is decoration. First, that the two sides are pricing against different counterfactuals — one against a competitive ideal, one against the already-distorted status quo — so that the language of distortion and deadweight loss is doing less work than it appears, because removing one distortion in a market full of others has no general sign. Second, that they are describing different markets: a single building or neighbourhood, where supply is fixed by zoning and demand is inelastic, so that the landlord has genuine pricing power and the tenant faces something close to a hold-up problem; versus a metropolitan rental stock with mobile capital, where supply responds over a decade. If that is right, the entire empirical content of the dispute sits in one parameter — the elasticity of supply — evaluated at a scale and a time horizon that neither side specifies, and the dispute is empirical in a way that nobody has isolated. Third, that part of it may be merely verbal: the word affordability runs together two different goods, the rent payable for a unit and the security of not being forced to leave it, and a single instrument is then defended under one description and attacked under the other.

I also want the asymmetry with the minimum wage explained, because I think it is a clue. Formally the cases are parallel: a binding price floor in a market with buyer power, and a binding price ceiling in a market with seller power, are both standard textbook exceptions to the case against price controls, and both admit a market-power defence of exactly the same shape. Yet one is near-universally condemned and the other is contested. Is that asymmetry a fact about theory, or an artifact of what happens to be measurable — a long run of natural experiments on the labour side, thin and local evidence on the housing side — or does it come from the incidence of the transfer, or from the fact that one policy's costs appear within months while the other's accrue over decades?

Given your diagnosis, tell me specifically what would count as progress rather than another round: a supply-elasticity estimate at a stated scale, a natural experiment that separates tenure security from rent level, an actual policy that unbundles them — say a right to renew at market rent plus relocation assistance — and what its results would and would not show, or a formal result about second-best. And if the honest answer is that some of this is bedrock — a disagreement about whether housing is a commodity like any other, or about whether the state may forbid a mutually beneficial exchange, which no evidence could settle — say which part is bedrock and how much of the argument it actually explains. If a piece of it is verbal, name the word doing the work and the two questions it collapses. I would also like your prediction on one concrete matter: on your diagnosis, should I expect to be having the identical argument in fifteen years, and why?
