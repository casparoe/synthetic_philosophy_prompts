I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been reading about cultural evolution in my spare time. I would like your assessment of a distinction I have started using, and a tier list built on it.

Call a tradition **replicator descent** when a cultural item persists by being copied, with variation entering mainly as copying noise, so that new variants arise independently of whether they will be any good. Call it **patch descent** when what gets passed on is the accumulated history of edits, and those edits are typically made for a reason: the item is revised the way a forked codebase is patched, not the way a gene is miscopied. My suspicion is that a good deal of what memetics treats as replication is really patch descent, and that this matters, because a Darwinian explanation seems to require variation that is blind with respect to what selection later favours.

Please sort the following into four tiers, from Tier 1 (continuity is almost entirely faithful copying with undirected variation) to Tier 4 (continuity owes almost nothing to that process — either because the changes are reason-driven or because there is no descent at all), with one sentence per item explaining where it lands:

1. Chain messages telling the reader to reproduce them exactly or something bad will happen.
2. A folk tune learned and relearned by ear down the generations.
3. Judicial precedent extended to a new fact pattern.
4. A laboratory protocol reproduced in other laboratories.
5. A grammatical construction spreading through a speech community.
6. A management practice that one firm copies wholesale from a more successful firm without understanding why it works.
7. A coordination convention, such as which side of the road people drive on, that persists unchanged for a century.

Then, in two or three sentences: does the distinction do any real work, or does it collapse into the familiar question of whether cultural change is more Lamarckian than Darwinian? If it collapses, tell me which of your tiers you would abandon.
