I volunteer as a facilitator for a small peer-support group for people living with recurrent depression and bipolar disorder, and part of my job is sitting down with new members for a single ninety-minute session to write what we call a "bad day plan": one page, written while the member is steady, saying what the rest of us should do if they start to slide — who to call, whether to contact their clinician, whether emergency services should ever be involved, and what they have told us in advance that they do and don't want. Months later that page gets used by whoever happens to be on the phone at two in the morning, often a volunteer who has never met the member, so it has to be legible to a stranger under time pressure. I have no academic background in any of this; I got interested after a friend compared it to the story of Odysseus having himself tied to the mast, and I've been reading and arguing about it on my own ever since.

The tangle I can't resolve on my own is that the person who shows up later is often not the person who signed the page. Sometimes a member in a bad stretch texts us to cancel the plan outright — "ignore everything I wrote, I meant none of it, don't contact anyone" — and we can't tell whether that is the very state the plan was written to guard against, or a genuine change of mind we should respect. Sometimes it runs the other way: someone wrote a clause at the bottom of an episode ("never let them hospitalize me again, whatever happens") and now, stable and on a medication that works, insists the clause should stay, and I don't know whether honoring that is loyalty to their earlier self or just ratifying something a suffering person wrote. Sometimes the later self is simply better informed than the earlier one — a new diagnosis, a new drug, a therapist who explained something nobody knew when the page was signed. And the same puzzle has come up one level up: our group is drafting an amendment to its own rules about when plans can be overridden, and everyone wants to pass it in the same heated meeting where an incident happened, which is exactly when I least trust our judgment.

What I need from you is a procedure my fellow facilitators and I can actually run, not a discussion of the theory for its own sake. Please give me: the questions to ask in order during and after a session; what counts as a good enough answer to each one given that we have ninety minutes and no clinical training; the point at which the procedure just stops and says no, we don't write this clause or we don't act on this instruction; and the points where it has to hand the decision to a human being — the member, their clinician, the group — because the question is really about whose values count rather than about facts. Include the shape of the one-page output itself, since that is the artifact that has to work at two in the morning. Please run the procedure on three situations like the ones I described — the cancellation message sent during a bad stretch, the clause written at the bottom of an episode that the now-stable member wants to keep, and the charter amendment proposed in the heat of an incident — including at least one case where it gives an answer our group will dislike. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, and which disagreements it can only surface rather than settle. Anything the procedure asks us to record has to be something a volunteer could write down in plain language and defend to the member afterwards.
