I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in any of this; I make collages and the occasional linocut, and I got drafted onto the jury of a small community gallery that has started receiving entries made with image generators. We are supposed to sort them into a handmade category and an assisted one, and to give rejected entrants an actual reason rather than a shrug, so I have been trying to work out what I really think, going by my gut on cases rather than by anything I have read. My verdicts, which I arrived at arguing with two friends who disagree with each other and with me: (1) a woman who spent about forty hours writing and revising prompts, threw away something like nine hundred outputs, and printed one — some credit, genuinely skilled, but not the same kind of credit I would give a painter; (2) a man who typed one sentence into a generator, took the first image it gave him and framed it — no creative achievement at all, even though the image is striking; (3) a photographer who shoots nine hundred frames of a landscape and prints the one that works — fully creative, no hesitation; (4) a collage artist who cuts up other people's magazines — fully creative, and this has never struck anyone as cheating; (5) a painter who trains a system only on twenty years of her own sketchbooks and then keeps an output only if she judges it is something she could have drawn herself — I go back and forth on this one, and have changed my mind twice this week; (6) an artist who generates ten thousand images from a single prompt and exhibits every one of them, uncurated — I lean towards no, but I cannot say why, given verdict 4; (7) a user prompting in the style of a living illustrator whose work was scraped without permission — I want to say the output can be good and the act is still a kind of theft, but I am not sure whether that belongs in the same judgment as creativity or is a separate complaint.

The two pairs I cannot reconcile are these. Verdicts 1 and 3 look structurally identical to me: both people searched a large space of candidate images, made hundreds of rejections, and selected one — yet I give the photographer full credit and the prompter only partial credit, and I do not want to give up either verdict. And verdicts 1 and 4 look identical in a different way: both people made something out of material they did not themselves produce — yet selection from a magazine feels like authorship and selection from a generator feels like something less. My own tentative principle, such as it is, has two clauses: first, that credit tracks how much of the finished work's specific character the person could have predicted in advance, chosen, and then defended against someone asking why this and not that, where it looked good is not a defence; and second, that the value of the artifact and the credit due to the maker can come apart, so an image can be genuinely beautiful and worth hanging while the person who produced it deserves almost nothing. One friend says both clauses are wrong and that creativity is a property of the object alone — if the print is novel and good, the process is irrelevant, and my clause about defending choices is just snobbery about effort. The other says the whole question is a red herring and that only honest labelling matters.

So here is what I would like. Is there a single account of what a creative achievement is that delivers most of my verdicts, or am I really holding two principles that need to be separated — one about whether the work is art and one about whether anyone did anything? State any candidate precisely enough that I could apply it cold to a new entry, tell me which clause of it is forced by which of my cases, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which it overturns. In particular: do verdicts 1 and 3 have to come out the same, and if not, what is the difference between a photographer's nine hundred frames and a prompter's nine hundred outputs that is doing the work? What does your account say about case 5, which I keep changing my mind about, and about a case I have not listed — a poet who writes every line with a text generator's autocomplete on and accepts roughly half of what it offers? Is creative credit a matter of degree or is it all-or-nothing, and does the training-data complaint in verdict 7 belong inside the judgment about creativity or beside it? Finally, because I have to stand in front of people, give me the two sentences I could say to a rejected entrant that are honest about the reasoning rather than evasive.
