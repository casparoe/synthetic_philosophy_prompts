I work on a four-person mechanistic interpretability team, and I'm the one who got stuck writing the internal document that decides whether our papers can keep saying that we "discovered a representation" or whether that sentence needs to be deflated into something about the usefulness of a decomposition. I'm fluent in the vocabulary I actually use — directions and subspaces in activation space, dictionaries and their non-identifiability, reconstruction versus downstream-effect criteria, linear probes and their readout-versus-causal role distinction, interchange interventions and causal abstraction, on-distribution versus shifted-distribution behavior, and the annoying fact that any linear-algebraic story can be re-expressed in another basis. I have no philosophy training, so please don't explain any of that back to me; explain the philosophy to me.

Two cases in our own work are what made the sloppiness matter. First, "dictionary disagreement": we fit two sparse dictionaries to the same middle layer, both with comparable reconstruction loss and both equally good at supporting the interventions we use to predict and steer behavior, but neither dictionary's features are sparse linear combinations of the other's, so there is no clean translation between them; a colleague says this shows the decomposition is imposed, not found, and I can't tell whether that's a substantive claim or just a shrug. Second, "read-only features": directions from which a property can be decoded at high accuracy but where patching the direction across inputs changes nothing downstream. Half the team says those aren't representations at all, the other half says that's mere stipulation. I'm told there is a long-standing debate about whether internal states of a system genuinely have representational content, and that it has at least three live positions: a realist one on which citing contents in an explanation can be straightforwardly true or false; an informational/causal-informational one on which content is pinned down by what a state carries information about in the training distribution together with some normative or proper-function notion, and which is said to face a "disjunction problem" about why a state that reliably tracks a disjunctive or gerrymandered property isn't simply about that property; and an anti-representationalist or predictive-stance one on which content attributions are instruments whose correctness is relative to the purposes of whoever is doing the attributing.

What I want is a systematic translation of all three into my vocabulary, one at a time, and then a residue report. For each position: what translates cleanly onto directions, dictionaries, probes, interventions, and behavior; what translates only if you fudge it or quietly add something my vocabulary doesn't contain; and what has no counterpart in it at all. Please be explicit when a term of yours has to be imported as a primitive rather than translated — say what work it is doing. I especially want the residue on three points: misrepresentation (is there anything in my vocabulary corresponding to a state's being about something and getting it wrong, as opposed to merely causing a wrong output?), the individuation of features (do my interventions settle which decomposition is the right one, or do they inherit the basis freedom?), and whether the debate between the three positions is doing real work or collapsing into a verbal dispute once you grant that I can always change basis. End by telling me, on the basis of the translation, whether our sentence "we discovered a representation" is defensible, and if it isn't, what specifically we lose by giving it up — and whether what we lose was ever something my methods could have delivered anyway.

Please keep the entire answer under 800 words; a table with one row per position covering translation, distortion, and inexpressible residue, plus at most 300 words of prose for the three residue points and the verdict, is the shape I'd find most useful.
