my flatmate is doing a phd in something adjacent to cognitive science and every time we argue about llms he pulls out the same move: the mind is to the brain as software is to hardware, nobody has cared which chunk of silicon a program runs on for decades, so why should anyone care where the memory lives — a notebook, a phone, a chatbot that has all my old notes in it, its all just memory with different latency. he then uses that to get two conclusions: (i) the extended mind thesis is just functionalism applied consistently and anyone who resists it is basically a skull-chauvinist, and (ii) so when he brainstorms with an llm and a good line ends up in the essay, the credit really is his, that was him thinking, nothing got outsourced.

i want to push back but i cant locate where the analogy is doing work it hasnt paid for. so: audit it for me. give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the processor, of ram, of the disk, of the bus, of the installed program, of the operating system, of the user, of the engineer who designed the thing — and then take it apart: where does the mapping break, and which of those breaks actually matter for his two conclusions as opposed to being noise about implementation details.

two things i keep getting stuck on, please fold them in.

first, my own worry, which is that the analogy is too generous on his own terms. a usb stick you can only read from is still memory. a cloud backup you have never once looked inside is still memory — rom is literally called memory. so if storage-plus-reliable-access is all it takes, the analogy seems to make a library i have never visited part of my mind, or at least it leaves people unable to say why not. where does that leave the conditions people normally put on extension, the stuff about the resource being trustworthy, easily accessible, automatically endorsed, and about you having put the information there yourself? does the computer picture have the resources to tell us which of those conditions are load bearing and which are symptoms, or does it quietly undercut all of them and leave him with a version of the thesis nobody actually wants?

second, a case i made up to test him which he had no answer for. forget the notebook. imagine instead a device that only responds to questions. it holds transcripts of every conversation you have had for the last ten years, it answers reliably, you trust it completely and act on what it says without checking, but you cannot edit it, you cannot browse it, you have no idea what is in it until you ask, and it sometimes summarises in ways you would not have chosen. call it whatever you like. is that memory, is it testimony from an extremely well informed friend, or is it something the software/hardware analogy has no vocabulary for at all? his framework spits out: obviously memory, it is just a disk with a weird interface. my gut says it is much closer to asking my mum what i was like as a kid. and if it is the second one, i think both his conclusions are in trouble, because the thing he is actually brainstorming with has a lot more in common with that device than with a notebook.

so where does the analogy leave us, does either conclusion survive, and is there a better one — i was thinking something like a workshop, or the relation between a musician and her instrument, or maybe scaffolding — or is every available analogy going to smuggle in the answer to the constitution question before we start?
