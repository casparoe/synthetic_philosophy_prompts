I've been living and working in a mid-sized city abroad for four years on a fixed-term contract, and I'll be leaving in about eighteen months. Where I come from, leaving 15–20% in a restaurant is close to a moral duty; here, nobody tips, service is included in the menu price by law, and waiting staff are on a union pay scale that locals regard as decent, if not generous. For my first three years I did as the locals do. About a year ago I started slipping cash to the two or three places I go to regularly — discreetly, and always in cash, precisely because I knew it would register. It has registered. Several other regulars have begun doing the same, the staff at my usual places now visibly warm to me and, I think, cool to customers who don't, and a friend of mine — a local — told me recently, only half joking, that I have raised the price of dinner for everyone and created an expectation that didn't exist before. She added that if I want to give money away I should give it to everyone who serves me in this city or to no one, and that doing it only where I'm a known face is really just buying better treatment.

Here is what I can't sort out. I don't think the irritation is mere stinginess: my friend seems to be saying something about who gets to alter a shared expectation, and about my doing it as someone who won't be around to live with whatever replaces it. But I also can't see that I've done anything to anyone, and the people I tip are better off, not worse. Meanwhile a second friend has the mirror-image policy about a different custom: in the same city nobody waits their turn properly at bus stops or bakery counters, and he pushes in exactly as the locals do, on the grounds that this is the local rule and that standing aside would be a kind of arrogance. I find his behaviour unattractive and my own defensible, yet every reason I give for the difference sounds like a reason he could give too.

So: is what I did wrong? If so, what exactly is the wrong-making feature — that I changed a shared expectation, that I did it unilaterally, that I personally benefit from the change, that I won't stay to live under it, or something I haven't listed? Should I stop, carry on, tip everywhere rather than at my regular places, or redirect the money somewhere that doesn't feed back into how I'm treated? And is my situation structurally the same as my friend's queue-jumping, or is there a principled difference between them — and if there is one, which of us comes out worse? I'd rather have the disagreement between my two friends located precisely than be told that customs vary from place to place.
