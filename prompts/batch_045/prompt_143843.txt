I work in pricing at a life insurer, and I've been pulled onto a small internal committee that has to sign off on single-case probability statements: for an individual applicant we have to file a number — the probability that this person survives to ninety — and our regulator wants to know which population we derived it from. The same applicant can be placed in a broad class (all fifty-five-year-old nonsmokers), or in successively narrower ones (fifty-five-year-old nonsmoking men with treated hypertension, in a given occupation, at a given income band), and the observed frequencies in those classes disagree, sometimes by enough to move the premium by more than our entire margin on the policy. Anyone can always add one more attribute — the applicant's commute, their year of birth, their mother's age at death — and narrow the class until it contains almost no one, at which point the frequency is either undefined or trivially zero or one. Whoever picks the class effectively picks the price, and I don't yet know what should constrain the picking.

A colleague on the committee, who came from physics, says we are overthinking it. His line is that the reference class is like a coordinate chart on a manifold: the chart is our choice, the point — this applicant — is not, and the quantity we are after is a coordinate-independent object that every admissible chart describes correctly in its own vocabulary. On that picture the choice of class is mere descriptive bookkeeping, there is one true single-case probability underneath, and any class that satisfies our data-quality rules is as good as any other one for computing it. He has even sketched what he thinks the analogue of the transition maps should be: a consistency requirement saying that whenever two admissible classes both contain the applicant, they must yield the same frequency for the outcome, in which case the class you pick cannot change the answer — and if two classes do disagree, that is only evidence that one of them was not really admissible.

I find the picture appealing but suspicious, and I would like it audited rather than either accepted or waved away. Please lay the correspondence out explicitly — a table is fine — saying what plays the manifold, the point, the chart, the transition maps, and the invariant, and then where the mapping fails; please separate the failures that matter for the work this analogy is being asked to do from the ones that are merely decorative. In particular: is his consistency condition something our data could ever satisfy, or is it the conclusion restated as an axiom? In the coordinate case every chart is equally legitimate and there is no notion of a better one, whereas in our case some classes look plainly better than others — narrower and more homogeneous, yet still large enough to estimate a frequency from — and I cannot see what plays that in his picture, especially when the analogy is being used to argue that our choice is unconstrained. And is the single-case probability really like curvature, waiting to be read off in any notation, or is it something the analogy quietly assumes? If the coordinate picture is the wrong one, is there a comparison that fits better — choosing a resolution or grain, choosing a sampling frame, choosing what total to divide by? Finally, given that we still have to file a number next quarter, say what the audit recommends: does any version of "any admissible class will do" survive, and if not, what rule for choosing a class should we actually adopt, and what would justify that rule to a regulator?
