hey — hoping you can help with something that's gotten abruptly practical for me.

Our city council voted last month to pilot a permanent citizens' assembly on the housing plan, and I somehow ended up on the volunteer design committee. 90 people, drawn by lot, eight weekends, then recommendations to council. So the design questions are now mine and not somebody's seminar paper.

The consultant we hired leans on one analogy constantly: an assembly is to the electorate as a random sample is to a census. You don't need to ask everybody; a properly drawn few hundred tells you what the whole population thinks, and it tells you that cheaply and with a knowable error margin. He uses it for three moves: (1) 90-ish is plenty, and growing the chamber or putting the question to a referendum buys nothing worth the money, since a referendum is just asking everyone to fill in the census form badly; (2) whatever the assembly lands on is, in the relevant sense, what the city would have decided, so council can defer without giving anything up; and (3) because you can stratify a sample, we should deliberately oversample renters (roughly 2:1, since they're the group most affected and least heard), the way you'd oversample a small subgroup in a survey to get a usable estimate for it.

Move (3) is the one that's been nagging at me. A survey sample is supposed to sit still — it measures opinions it doesn't change — while the entire point of eight weekends is that people arrive with one view and leave with another. If the picture is wrong there, I suspect it's wrong for the other two moves as well, but I can't tell which of them it was actually carrying.

You're a political theorist who works on democratic innovation and you know the sortition literature inside out. Think it through properly: lay out the correspondences first — what plays the role of the sampling frame, the estimator, the margin of error, non-response, the population itself — and only then say where the mapping breaks and whether each break matters for the use he's making of it. Then give me your verdict on the three moves separately, and if the census picture is the wrong one, tell me what a better analogy would be (jury? focus group? standing panel?) and what it would let us conclude that this one can't.
