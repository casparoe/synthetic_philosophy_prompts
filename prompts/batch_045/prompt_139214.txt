I'm in a philosophy-of-economics reading group this term — mostly micro theory grad students plus one philosophy postdoc — and we spent last week on revealed preference and what 'utility' is even supposed to mean. Someone circulated a two-page handout to set up the discussion, and I want an accuracy check on it before I present next week, because the econ students rolled their eyes at a couple of sentences and I couldnt follow their objection. The handout says, more or less: preferences aren't hidden mental states that choices give us evidence about, they're just a shorthand for the choices themselves — to say someone prefers x to y just means that given the choice they took x. Utility is then only a bookkeeping device for writing that pattern down: a number that goes up along the chain of revealed preferences, nothing more. Two things follow. First, utility is not a feeling or a quantity of happiness; it's merely ordinal, so there's no sense to be made of comparing utility across people or saying one person got more out of something than another. Second, on this picture nobody's choices can really be wrong: if preference is defined by choice, then whatever you picked is by definition what you most wanted, so there's no room for weakness of will, mistake, or being manipulated into something you didn't want. And here's the handout's punchline — because you can always draw indifference curves through the observed choices after the fact, any behaviour whatsoever can be described as maximizing some utility function, so revealed preference theory predicts nothing and can't be tested, which is (it says) why applied welfare economics gave up on utility ages ago and just measures everything in money instead.

On top of that, we got into our own argument that I'd like adjudicated. My friend Jake proposed what he called a cycle test: line up three options — say the almond croissant, the blueberry muffin and the cinnamon roll in the department lounge — offer them two at a time, and if the person takes the croissant over the muffin, the muffin over the roll, and the roll over the croissant, you've got a cycle, cycles are impossible for a utility maximizer, so you've refuted the whole framework for that person. Someone else in the group said the cycle only refutes anything if you read 'took' as strict preference: if choosing just means 'I don't prefer the alternative to this', all three observations are compatible with being indifferent all round, and indifference is perfectly transitive. Jake says that's cheating, since you could rescue the theory from any data at all by labelling everything indifference, and he also says this is the sort of thing the weak axiom / GARP stuff is meant to rule out — but I don't actually know what those say, so please explain without assuming I do. Then Priya said none of it matters because real choice is noisy — people take the croissant 60% of the time — and you can always fit noisy data with a utility function plus an error term, so the framework has no content; Jake says no, that even a random utility model imposes a real constraint, namely that adding new options to the menu can never make an existing option more likely to be chosen.
