I'm putting together a lecture on credentialism and I want a case that runs the opposite way from the usual story: a signalling setup that's honest *because* the signal is cheap, and that stops carrying information once you make the signal genuinely more costly. Can you build me one?

Specs:

- Ordinary setting, one paragraph — hiring, freelance platforms, dating apps, whatever. No peacocks, no stotting gazelles; I want students to recognise it.
- Two types, and the low type really does want to pass as the high type and *could* produce the signal if it wanted. So no "indices"/physical-impossibility cheat, and no "their interests are aligned anyway."
- No deferred verification: no audits, no reputation, no repeat play. One shot, and the honesty has to fall out of the structure.
- The honest signal has to be cheap in the sense that it burns only a small fraction of the surplus at stake. No burnt money, no diamond rings, no four-year degrees.
- The extra cost you add in step two has to be a *uniform* real cost that both types pay identically (a fee, a fixed block of hours, whatever), so the failure isn't just Spence's single-crossing condition being violated or the cost landing harder on the wrong type.

The angle I'm after is something I've started calling the participation margin: the handicap literature is almost entirely about the mimicry margin — making it not worth it for the bad type to fake — and says nearly nothing about whether the good type bothers to show up at all. My hunch is that a uniform cost kills the signal at the participation margin: the good type's net surplus from signalling was thin, the bad type was already deterred, so nobody sends and the signal carries zero information even though it's now more expensive. I want that to come out of the model, not to be stipulated.

Then: lay out the payoff structure (types, signal, who gets what), check both equilibria, tell me which single feature is doing the work and what happens if the added cost is differential in the Spence direction instead of uniform. Finish with the Zahavian reply — why this isn't a counterexample (is it that the handicap principle only ever required *differential* cost and I'm attacking a pop version where costly means expensive?) — and whether that reply is ad hoc.
