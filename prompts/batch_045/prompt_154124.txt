I want you to analyze one historical episode through one philosophical lens, and to run the analysis in both directions: what the theory says was going on in the episode, and what the episode in turn shows about the theory.

The episode is the Nixon administration's madman strategy toward North Vietnam in 1969. I'll stipulate the facts so that nothing here turns on looking anything up. Nixon told his chief of staff that he wanted Hanoi to believe he had reached the point where he might do anything to stop the war, and that the word should be slipped to them that he was obsessed with communism, that he could not be restrained when angry, and that he had his hand on the nuclear button. In October 1969 the administration ran a secret global military readiness test, hidden from the American public, which included nuclear-armed B-52s flying near the Soviet border for several days; the intended audience was Moscow at least as much as Hanoi, since Washington wanted the Soviets to lean on their client. Hanoi did not concede, and the war went on for years. Historians still dispute how deliberate any of this was and whether it had any effect, and scholars of coercive bargaining have been generally skeptical: the standing worries are that it is hard to be believed, that declaring yourself mad reads as bluffing, and that a reputation for irrationality makes it hard to give credible assurances to an adversary who does yield. Take all of that as given, and treat the strategy as a serious attempt whether or not it worked. The underlying idea is old, as Machiavelli already suggested when he noted in the Discourses that it can be wise to simulate madness; I mention that only to mark that the puzzle is not new.

The lens is Parfit's hitchhiker and the family of replies to it that travel under the heading of rational irrationality. Set the case out precisely enough to map: a stranded hitchhiker promises to pay a driver who otherwise will not take him; once they reach town, the promise has no reason-giving force left, so a fully rational hitchhiker never pays, and a driver who can see this never picks him up. Something has to give, and the interesting question is what.

Then do the mapping, and be strict about it. Who is the hitchhiker and who is the driver in 1969? What plays the role of the promise? Note the asymmetry you are walking into: the hitchhiker needs a credible promise, that is, a credible disposition to do something costly later, whereas a deterrent strategy needs a credible threat, a disposition to do something catastrophic later. Say whether that asymmetry matters or whether it is the same structure under different labels. Specify the costly later act, what it costs at the moment of performance, who has to believe in the disposition, and what would count as evidence for the other side.

Four things I want handled rather than gestured at.

First, cost-proportionality. The hitchhiker's later payment is small relative to what he gains, and that is part of why we can imagine a rational agent selecting a disposition to make it. The act Nixon needed to be thought capable of was not small: use of nuclear weapons, with everything that follows. Does the dispositional solution scale? Ask, rather than assume, whether a rational agent can choose to have a disposition to do what it would be catastrophically irrational to do, and whether replies that let rationality govern the selection of dispositions and policies, and not just acts, do better here than replies that restrict it to acts.

Second, the audience is not the driver. The signal was aimed at Hanoi but largely delivered to Moscow, through a secret channel, with the coercive pressure meant to flow through a broker whose own payoffs differed from everyone else's. A hitchhiker's promise works because the driver hears it and can assess the agent behind it. Say what happens to the credibility of a disposition when it is mediated by a third party, and whether the secrecy of the channel is a feature or a bug: a reputation is public, but this signal was not, and an ambiguous military alert is visible to radar without being self-interpreting.

Third, translucency versus provenance. In the hitchhiker case it helps to be translucent: the driver must be able to see the disposition. In the madman case, what is visible is not only the disposition but its source, that it was adopted deliberately, announced, leaked and discussed, and thus droppable by a rational agent at will. Call this the provenance problem if you like. Does it collapse the strategy into bluffing, or is there a version of the disposition that survives its own publicity?

Fourth, the cost side of the disposition. A disposition to irrational escalation is not selective: allies, domestic audiences, and future negotiating partners observe it too, and it undercuts the ability to promise restraint afterwards. Name that cost and weigh it against what the hitchhiker's promise-keeping disposition costs him, and say whether the two cases are symmetric here or whether the madman strategy buys threat-credibility precisely by spending promise-credibility.

Then say what the episode does to the theory. Is this a case the hitchhiker structure was built for, a case it misdescribes, or a case it never anticipated? Is madman strategy a genuine instance of rational irrationality, or signaling under incomplete information wearing that vocabulary? Where the theory is silent, for example on whether a presidency is an agent of the right kind at all, or on how a disposition distributes across a composite of persons and institutions, say so rather than forcing it to speak.

Finally, attach a confidence note. For each of your main claims say roughly how confident you are, and single out the one claim you are least sure of, together with what would change your mind about it. Please don't supply citations or further reading; I want your own analysis, and treat the empirical detail above as settled as I have stated it.
