I'm presenting a paper on lexical threshold negative utilitarianism at a well-being measurement workshop next month, and I want a prep sheet for the question period rather than a defense of the view.

The audience: mostly welfare and health economists who work on QALY- and DALY-style valuation and on subjective-well-being scaling, two affective scientists, and three philosophers who are single-scale hedonists of a broadly Sidgwickian sort and think a lexical break is a failure of nerve. Their shared commitments: anything worth putting into policy has to be identified from data, comparable across persons, and reasonably invariant to which instrument produced it.

My thesis, in two parts. (1) Below a certain intensity, pleasures and pains are fully commensurable on one cardinal scale and trade off normally; above that intensity pain is lexically prior, so no amount of pleasure, and no number of people enjoying it, compensates for a single episode of pain above the threshold. (2) The break isn't a stipulation bolted onto part one. It falls out of an asymmetry in the scale itself: the pleasure side saturates. Reported pleasure, and the physiological markers that track it, top out early, and the ceiling barely moves as the objective situation keeps improving, whereas pain reports keep climbing with no comparable ceiling. Above the threshold, then, compensation fails for lack of units on the pleasure side rather than because a rule forbids it: there is nothing left to put in the other pan. My policy upshot, which I will state and which will be hated, is that priority-setting exercises should report two numbers instead of one and replace cost-effectiveness thresholds with a lexicographic ordering — and I have to concede up front that none of their instruments locates the threshold.

What I want: the objections I will actually get from this room, in their vocabulary (identification, instrument invariance, interpersonal comparability, continuity and the Archimedean property, what happens to aggregation when one person's severe pain outranks everyone else's pleasures), not the objections I'd get from an online utilitarian forum, ranked by how likely and how damaging, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Commit yourself: say which objections really bite, which of my two claims I should be prepared to drop under pressure, and what I should concede before anyone asks. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
