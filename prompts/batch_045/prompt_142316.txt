I'm a high school senior doing an independent project on genetics and school outcomes, and I've reached the part where I have to write down what I actually think about heritability before I'm allowed to use it for anything. Reading back over my notes, I'm not sure the set of things I believe is consistent, and I'd like an audit before I build an argument on top of it. I can't get to a university library from where I am, so please argue it out rather than sending me to papers.

Here is where I've landed, numbered so you can poke at individual items.

(1) Heritability is a within-population variance ratio: for a specified population living in a specified range of environments, the share of the differences among individuals that is statistically attributable to genetic differences. So a bare question like how heritable is height is ill-posed until you name the population and the environment; a high number says nothing about what produced any particular person's height; and it says nothing about whether the trait could be moved by an intervention.

(2) I don't think (1) makes trait-level talk useless, though. I think it's fine to say that traits of a certain kind, in populations like ours, tend to land in a certain range, and I take the rough agreement of many twin and family studies run in different countries as genuine evidence that these numbers are robust. If the population-relativity point were as corrosive as people make out, that agreement would be a coincidence.

(3) High heritability is compatible with large environmental effects on the average. Average height in a number of countries rose substantially over the last century or so while heritability stayed high, and there are genetic conditions whose worst effects are almost entirely avoided by diet. So the number doesn't measure how fixed or how biological a trait is.

(4) Here is a place where I actually lean on it: I think the high heritability of school achievement is part of the explanation for why intensive early-childhood programs show their gains fading out after a few years. If the differences among children are substantially genetic, it makes sense that one program produces gains that don't last.

(5) I think the estimates are worth computing for a reason beyond description. They tell you how much of the variation a predictor built only from DNA could in principle capture, and they matter for anyone using sibling comparisons to strip out genetic confounding.

(6) I'm uneasy about the equal-environments assumption in twin studies. Identical twins get treated more alike than fraternal twins, and that looks like it should inflate the genetic estimate. I suspect some of the gap between what twin studies report and what DNA-based studies report is down to that rather than to genetic variants the DNA studies can't see.

(7) I think it's an abuse of the concept to attach a heritability to an environmental measure. I've seen heritability reported for things like how many books are in a child's home, and to me that is a sign the concept has been stretched past its breaking point. Genes do not put books on shelves.

(8) On the other hand, I'm comfortable with genes working partly through the environment: a parent's genotype shapes the home they provide, and the twin estimate counts that as part of the genetic contribution. A friend says this makes the whole decomposition invalid, since the estimate is supposed to assume genes and environment are independent. I think the estimate just picks up that correlation on the genetic side. I don't know who is right.

(9) On group differences: I take it as basic that high heritability within each group tells you nothing about whether the difference between group averages is genetic. But I also think it runs the other way — near-zero heritability, of the sort you'd get for a trait whose variation within a population is mostly injury or accident, doesn't show that a difference between group averages is environmental either. The number looks silent in both directions.

(10) Finally, I have written both of the following in drafts and nobody has objected yet: that heritability is a purely statistical decomposition with no causal content, and that for a highly heritable trait genes are the main cause of why people in that population differ.
