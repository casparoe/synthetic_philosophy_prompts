reading some pop-science history of physics lately and I keep noticing this one move, and I don't know what to call it, so forgive me if this is obvious. Someone puts forward a theory and then points out, as a point in it's favor, that the theory explains some fact that was already known — everybody already had the fact, it was in the textbooks, sometimes it was even the fact that made people uneasy about the old theory. And people do treat that as a reason to like the new theory. But here's the part I can't square. How much credit the theory gets seems to turn on whether the person was aiming at that fact while building the theory, and I can't work out which direction it goes. Sometimes aiming is a big discount: a grad student friend of mine fits a model that reproduces a correlation his whole field already knew about, and nobody is impressed, precisely because he tuned it to get that. Other times the aiming looks like the whole reason it's impressive — the Mercury perihelion case, where as I understand it the odd number was known and the theory was built partly in order to get it, and it's treated as one of the great triumphs. Same shape, opposite verdict.

So: is there a name for this? Credit for entailing something we already believed, plus the fact that whether you were aiming at it cuts both ways. I don't just want a label, though. I want to know where the real cut is: is it about when the theorist found out about the entailment, about how many other things the theory had to commit to in order to get that one, about how strange the fact looked beforehand, or something else entirely? And if the usual word only covers one of my two cases, say so, and hand me a name for the other.
