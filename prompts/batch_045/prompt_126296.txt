I'm putting together an anthology of short fiction that stages a philosophical thought experiment inside an ordinary institution: no fantasy apparatus, no mysterious stranger, no character who stops to explain anything. Your piece should stage the version of Nietzsche's eternal recurrence that is a question rather than a cosmology — the one in which you are asked whether you could will this life, in every particular, including the particulars you did not choose and would not have chosen, to recur without end.

The crux I want dramatized rather than argued is this. The question presents itself as a single question about how a life has gone, but it is really at least three questions that in most lives travel together and in some lives come apart: whether the life was good for the person who lived it; whether it was good that it happened, given what it did to and for the people around it; and whether a world in which lives like that one are dealt out is a world you can say yes to. Put the reader inside a case where the first is plainly no and the second plainly yes, so that the third is the only one left standing and cannot be dodged.

Setting and cast. An ethics consultation service at a large American teaching hospital uses a values-history form whose last item — item 14, which the staff call the recurrence question — asks the patient to say whether they would consent to live this same life again, without end, "every particular included." When a patient cannot answer, a surrogate answers for them, and the service treats the answer as evidence about whether to continue treatment. The consultant is Ada Reinholt, fifty-four, twenty years in the job, who has long suspected that item 14 is a rigged question. The case: Ruth Vance, eighty-two, three years on a locked dementia unit, no advance directive, now a candidate for a feeding tube. Her daughter, who comes every day, says her mother would have said yes to anything, she was like that. Her son, in from out of state, says nobody would want three more years of this and that answering for her is how the rest of them get comforted. Ada has to file something.

Requirements.

- The three-way ambiguity above must be visible in the collision between the two children, but no character may name it as a distinction or describe it in general terms. The only general statement of the question in the story should be the printed wording of item 14 itself.
- Someone must raise, and no one may settle, the fact that the life being willed would be lived in ignorance of its own repetition: the person answering now is not the person who would live it.
- Ada filled out the same form once, decades ago, at a residency wellness session, and wrote her yes with one qualification carved out of it — a stretch of time she would cut. What she cut has now, in the present of the story, come around again in a smaller key. She never says so to anyone.
- The gap between "every particular" and "the general shape of it" must be carried somewhere by an object or a detail rather than by a line of dialogue.
- The story ends with Ada's decision about a piece of paper — sign, strike through, leave blank — and with nothing else. No closing maxim, no italicized aside, no sentence that could be embroidered on a pillow.

Craft constraints. Third person, limited to Ada, past tense, 1,500 to 2,500 words. No character says "eternal recurrence," "Nietzsche," or "amor fati," and no demon appears. Present the unit with sensory specificity and without sentimentality; neither child is a villain; the disagreement between them is never resolved into a verdict about who was right. Open and close on the same short passage — the same sentences if you can manage it — with exactly one detail altered. That altered detail *is* the verdict, so choose it carefully.
