I take the minutes for the clinical ethics committee at my hospital — I'm the most junior person in the room, so my actual job is writing things down, but I've started trying to work out what rule we're implicitly applying, because I don't think we have one and its starting to show. We've just been asked to rule on a case and I'd like your help before Thursday.

Every time we decide something we record it as either an allowing (a letting die) or a doing (a killing), and then separately as permissible or not. Here's our track record, roughly in order:

1. Ventilator withdrawn from a patient in a deep coma with no prospect of recovery, at the family's request. Recorded as letting die, permissible. We said explicitly that it made no difference whether the team switched the ventilator off or removed the tube, and we've repeated that "withholding and withdrawal are the same" line in several other notes.
2. A woman dying of cancer where the team decided not to start antibiotics that would probably have bought her a few weeks. Letting die, permissible.
3. A man whose heartbeat was entirely dependent on a pacemaker, who was dying of bowel cancer, and whose family asked for the pacemaker to be deactivated so he wouldn't linger. Recorded as letting die, with two dissenters saying that deactivating a device is a doing and not an omission at all.
4. Dialysis stopped — external machine, three sessions a week, run by our own staff — in a patient with irreversible kidney failure. Letting die.
5. A patient who was never started on a heart pump that would probably have kept him alive. Letting die.
6. A clinician who gave a large dose of a drug with the explicit purpose of ending a patient's life. Doing, not permissible.

Now the new case. About four months ago a man in his fifties had both of his own ventricles removed and replaced with a pump inside his chest, the kind that runs off a cable through the skin to a controller and two battery packs that the nursing staff swap roughly every six hours. There's no prospect of a transplant and no question of the pump coming out. Three weeks ago he had a catastrophic stroke; everybody agrees there's no chance of any meaningful recovery and that the right thing is to stop. The committee's job is to say, before anyone does anything, whether stopping is a letting die like withdrawing a ventilator or a doing like giving a lethal injection. The palliative team will sedate him first and nobody has a problem with that.

What makes it hard is the stuff around the edges. The treating team has proposed doing it by simply not swapping the batteries at the next scheduled change rather than programming the device off, and several members think that matters, even though our ventilator precedent says it shouldn't. Our chair keeps saying something I can't turn into a clean rule: that in every case we've ruled on before there was a disease that could take its course — lungs that fail, kidneys that fail, a cancer — but here there is no heart left to fail, the thing keeping him alive is a machine we put in and that we power, and if we stop feeding it current he dies of that and of nothing else. Somebody else said that's just unplugging a ventilator, and the chair said no, a ventilator is a machine breathing for a patient whose own body is still there doing the failing. Separately: the controller and the spare packs are hospital property, only staff can programme the controller, but you could show a relative how to swap a pack in about five minutes, and someone has floated handing the packs to the family and leaving the room — half of us think that obviously changes things and the other half think it's just laundering. And one member says the four months matter, because swapping the packs is now on the nursing rota and keeping him alive is part of what the ward does every day, so it would feel different if the pump had gone in yesterday.

What I want from you: predict what we'll say, and say what rule we're running when we say it — give me the two or three rules that fit all six precedents and what each of them predicts here, tell me which one you think we're actually running and why, and name the single further hypothetical I could put to the committee at the next meeting that would tell those rules apart. I also want your view on whether the battery-versus-programming-it-off distinction and the hand-the-packs-to-the-family move should make any difference, on which of our precedents is the closest analog, and — if our precedents genuinely underdetermine this — what principle we'd be committing ourselves to with each of the two verdicts. Finally, one of our members says the doing/allowing distinction is doing no work here and we should just record whether stopping is permissible and drop the rest; tell me whether she's right and what we'd lose if we did.

Couple of housekeeping things: write in British English, and please just write it as continuous prose in an email-ish register — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, even though my question above is full of lists.
