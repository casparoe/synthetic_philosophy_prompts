Context: I'm doing a six-month rotation at a small lab that builds a system for answering long, messy, open-ended questions (the kind of thing a policy shop might ask). Our training protocol is a version of what people call iterated amplification and distillation: nobody, human or model, is ever allowed to grade a final answer directly. Every question gets broken into a tree of sub-questions, the leaves have to be answerable and checkable by a smart non-expert in under about two minutes, the leaf answers get recombined into answers one level up, and so on; then we train a model to emit the final recomposed answers on its own. We've also added two rules that, as far as I can tell, nobody else uses. First, the "no shared premises" rule: if two sub-questions in the same decomposition both lean on the same contested assumption, we either merge them or force that assumption out into the open as its own gradable leaf question. Second, "shrink and retry": any leaf answer longer than roughly 120 words gets split again, because we found that long leaves are exactly where our graders start rubber-stamping. A typical leaf is something like: does this paragraph attribute a causal claim to the study, or only a correlation?

The thing that puzzles me, and that I keep going in circles about with my flatmate who does pure RL: the one judgment we refuse to decompose is the judgment that matters most. In our own internal safety review, "is this system's overall behaviour acceptable?" and "is this output a good output?" are treated as strictly non-decomposable — a human has to make that call holistically, on the whole artefact, and we've explicitly banned breaking it into checkable pieces. Meanwhile much subtler and more value-laden things — whether a piece of advice is responsible, whether a summary is fair to both sides — we insist MUST be broken down. So on the face of it we're committed both to "any judgment can be reconstructed out of small checkable judgments" and to "the judgment that actually counts can't be," and I can't tell whether that's a real inconsistency, a badly expressed insight, or just an artefact of who sits on which committee.

So: what does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory — about whether judgment is decomposable without loss, about what correctness or alignment is a property of (the leaves, the composition step, the final output, the whole pipeline over time), and about what exactly is supposed to be transmitted from parts to whole. Does the protocol strictly need both competence and alignment to survive decomposition and recomposition, or does it only need one of them to survive, with the other riding on something else? Is the "no shared premises" rule repairing a genuine failure mode or is it tidiness — and does it presuppose that the danger in composition is correlated error between leaves rather than error that only appears when the parts are put together? Is the ban on decomposing the final safety judgment defensible, or is it a residue of some view about normative notions being irreducibly holistic; and if we gave it up, what would the leaf questions actually look like and what would we lose? Finally, tell me which rival theories of judgment would produce exactly the protocol we have, and what would concretely have to change in the lab if we replaced the presupposed view with one of the rivals.
