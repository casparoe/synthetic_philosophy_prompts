QUESTION 3 (20 marks)

(a) Ned Block distinguishes access consciousness from phenomenal consciousness. Explain the distinction, and explain why it matters for how we should assess global workspace theories of consciousness. [8 marks]

(b) In a Sperling-style partial-report experiment, a subject is shown a grid of twelve letters for about 50 ms and is then cued to report one row. Subjects typically report three or four letters correctly, but say that in some sense they saw all twelve. Block has used data of this kind to argue for "overflow": more phenomenal consciousness than access consciousness. Critics reply that what overflows is not experience but cognition — high-capacity, detailed, unconscious representation that is simply never experienced.

Now suppose we had a neural measure, call it recurrent local synchrony (RLS), recorded from early visual cortex during such a task, with the following three features:

(i) RLS is present for all twelve items, including the ones the subject later says she saw nothing of;
(ii) RLS is not accompanied by any signature of global broadcast — no frontoparietal ignition, and no measurable effect on the subject's ability to identify, reason about, or act on any particular item;
(iii) the magnitude of RLS predicts both partial-report accuracy for the cued row and the subject's trial-by-trial rating of how much the display looked like something, holding the cue fixed.

Would the discovery of RLS give Block a genuinely new argument for phenomenal consciousness without access consciousness? What is the strongest reply open to someone who wants to defend global workspace theory? Say which of the two you find stronger, and why. [12 marks]

STUDENT ANSWER

(a) Block's distinction is between two different things people mean by conscious. A state is access-conscious when its content is broadcast to the rest of the system and so is available — the subject is in a position to report it and to use it in reasoning and in guiding action. A state is phenomenally conscious when there is something it is like for the subject to be in it: the what-it's-like property, as in what it is like to see red. Block thinks the two can in principle come apart, and the Sperling partial-report case is meant to show that they actually do: the subject has a rich experience of all twelve letters but can only report three or four of them.

This matters for global workspace theory because GWT — Baars's original version, and the Dehaene-style version with ignition in frontoparietal cortex — is a theory of what happens when a representation wins the competition for the workspace and is made available to many consumer systems at once. That is a theory of access consciousness rather than of phenomenal consciousness, and Block's complaint is that people then run the two together and treat GWT as if it had explained experience, when what it has explained is availability.

(b) RLS would improve the overflow argument considerably. The trouble with the Sperling data on its own is that it is behavioural, so the critic can always say that what overflows is unconscious cognitive representation — lots of high-capacity, detailed, non-experienced processing — rather than experience itself. RLS is a neural signature, it is local rather than global, and it tracks the subject's own ratings of how much things looked like something. And since (ii) says it never gets broadcast, it is not available for report and cannot guide action, so it is not access consciousness at all. RLS would therefore be a neural correlate of phenomenal consciousness in the absence of access consciousness, which is exactly the dissociation Block wants.

The best a GWT defender can do is say that the neural signature is a precursor or precondition of consciousness rather than a correlate of it, or that the subjective ratings are unreliable. But if a neural measure tracks how things look to the subject, the natural conclusion is that it is a signature of experience, and there is no reason to insist that broadcast has to be part of the story. And once you grant that, GWT is refuted: GWT identifies being conscious with being globally available, and here we would have a conscious state that is not globally available. This is really the same point Block made with his absent-qualia argument, where he imagines creatures that have full conscious experience but no access to their own states; RLS just turns that into an empirical finding.
