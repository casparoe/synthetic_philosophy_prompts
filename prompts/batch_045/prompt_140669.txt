I write novels — small press, literary-ish, not enough money in it to be its own moral dilemma — and I've rewritten one argument scene four times now and it keeps coming out sounding like a podcast episode, so I think I need to actually understand the argument rather than just make it sound good.

Here's the scene. One character, an ex-paramedic who now works in aid logistics and thinks of himself as the hardheaded one, says morality is basically emergency medicine. Nobody gives a medal to a paramedic for making a healthy bystander a bit happier. All the moral weight is in stopping the worst thing that's about to happen. Triage is real: you rank by how bad it is, not by who you like or who's closest, and you don't spend the last of the morphine on the guy with a migraine just because he's the nicest person in the room. So, he says, we should be spending near enough everything on the very worst suffering and basically nothing on culture, beauty, or making already-fine lives a bit nicer — he calls the arts "moral decor." (Someone at a reading told me this is basically negative utilitarianism; I looked it up and it seems close but not quite, which is part of why I'm asking.)

The other character is a hospice nurse, and she says the picture is rigged. Two things in particular: she says emergency medicine isn't even like that anymore, and hospice definitely isn't — they spend a lot on things that aren't reducing measurable suffering at all, like a room with a window, someone getting to say goodbye, being washed and dressed properly, a visit from a dog. And she says his ER picture quietly assumes everyone is already inside the building, so it can't say anything about whether to bring more people into it, which is exactly the question he's using it to dodge.

I dont want him to be a strawman and I dont want her to win on vibes. So please: give me the correspondence table — paramedic, patient, triage tag, healthy bystander, the clock, the ambulance, whatever else is doing real work — then tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion he's using it for (de-funding culture, de-prioritising everything that isn't the worst suffering) and which ones are just flavour. Does her "everyone's already in the building" point actually break his argument, or does he just need to add one premise and carry on? Is she right that the source domain keeps moving under him — that medicine itself has partly given up on pure suffering-minimisation — and if so does that matter or is it beside the point? And if the ER analogy can't carry the weight, what would he be better off reaching for, or is the honest answer that he has a decent argument with a bad picture bolted onto it?

If that's a real distinction rather than nurse-speak, tell me exactly where it bites on his one-dimensional triage scale, and where it doesn't.

I need to know what I'm actually putting in these peoples mouths, not whether they're nice people. End with a single line in the form \answer{...}, short phrase, saying what the analogy can carry and what it can't.
