I am a graduate student in a seminar on practical rationality — mostly philosophy students, with a few economics students auditing — and my term paper argues that the label "sunk cost fallacy" gets applied to a good deal of reasoning that is not a fallacy at all. My difficulty is that every case I try gets defused by the same maneuver: whatever makes continuing rational turns out to be some forward-looking consideration the agent had simply failed to notice — reputation, information, option value, resale, transferable skill, a shift in tastes. My teaching assistant says this is no accident, and that once you subtract those, "honoring sunk costs" has nothing left to be rational with. I want a case that survives that subtraction, because I have to present it in ten minutes on Thursday to people who have read about two papers each.

What I need: a single-paragraph scenario set in ordinary academic or professional life in which (a) by any forward-looking cost-benefit reckoning the agent ought to stop, and (b) the agent nonetheless has most reason to continue — not mere permission, but a reason such that stopping would be a rational failure on her part.

The scenario must be engineered so that the following escape routes are genuinely closed, and I need you to show, in a sentence or two each, how the case closes them:

- reputational or signaling effects on what any other person will subsequently do;
- information the agent gains or expects to gain, including higher-order information about the reliability of her own judgment;
- new options, option value, transferable skills, resale or salvage value;
- changes in the agent's tastes, temptation, weakness of will, or self-binding and precommitment;
- duties, promises, debts, or obligations owed to other people.

One further test matters more than any of these: the reason to continue must not be a reason to begin. An otherwise identical agent who has not yet invested, and who would have to take the whole thing on from scratch at the same total cost, must have no reason to do so. My previous draft failed exactly here — the TA pointed out that "the payoff of finishing is available only to someone who has already done most of the work" is option value in disguise, i.e. the standard reply rather than a counterexample to it.

Please also meet this objection head on, since it is the one I cannot answer. The strongest version of the bygones principle does not claim that past costs can never make a difference to outcomes; it claims that the bare fact that you have already paid is never itself a reason. So a case in which the agent simply has a powerful final desire not to have wasted the past — a history-dependent preference — may be perfectly consistent with expected-utility maximization and may show nothing except that the agent has an eccentric taste. If your case works that way, say so and explain why the "that is just a preference" reply is nevertheless not a good objection. If it works some other way — because rationality is irreducibly diachronic, because a later completion can determine whether earlier effort was in vain, or because waste is itself a pro tanto normative reason — say which claim is carrying the weight, state that claim in general form, and locate precisely where the disagreement with the bygones theorist lies.
