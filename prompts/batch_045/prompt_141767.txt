I am a novelist at work on a book about a river town whose prosperity was built on a single technical standard — a gauge, a coupling, a voltage — that the whole region still lives inside long after better alternatives are known to exist. Before I draft the central chapter I want a short story, roughly 1,500 words and realist in register, though I will allow one speculative device: an archive that will show a character, exactly once, how one re-run of the town's history came out from the same starting conditions — the destination only, never the route that led there.

What the story has to make a reader feel, without any character saying it, is the difference between three claims my characters keep treating as one: that the arrangement could have been otherwise; that it is now locked in, because everything built around it makes reversal ruinous; and that it was bound to arrive, or is best. Compose the story so that a reader sees why the archive settles the first claim and leaves the third untouched — a single contrary re-run establishes accident, not whether accident was the rule — and why the second claim neither supports nor undermines the third, since the ruinous cost of switching is itself a product of the road already taken.

Give me a protagonist who must decide in the final pages whether to stake their life on overturning the standard, knowing only what the archive showed. Let the ending be carried by what they choose and what it costs rather than by anything anyone declaims, and keep the vocabulary of contingency, lock-in and convergence out of the characters' mouths altogether.
