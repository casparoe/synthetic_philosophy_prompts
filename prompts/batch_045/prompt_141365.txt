I'm a novelist, halfway through a book set mostly in a clinic in 2087, and two other people at the residency I'm at got into an argument about one line of my manuscript on tuesday night and I've been useless ever since.

The setup: my POV character, Nell, has been told she's a "reconstruction" — a copy of a woman who died — and in chapter one she thinks: I know I'm not a copy. I read the line out loud at dinner mostly to complain that it sounded pretentious, and Dana (writes slipstream, did a philosophy degree before she dropped out) and Marcus (crime fiction, reads epistemology for some reason) took it and ran. They were going on about closure and Nozick's tracking theory, which I followed about halfway.

Dana: Nell can't know that. Not on the tracking picture. The belief comes from her experience of having hands, of remembering a childhood, of the nurse calling her by her name. But a copy would be built to have exactly the same experiences and exactly the same convinction. So if it were false that she's not a copy, she'd believe it anyway. Her belief doesn't track the fact. The test is just Nozick's: p is true, you believe p, and in the nearest worlds where p is false you don't believe it — plus the mirror half, that in the nearby worlds where it's true you do.

Marcus: then the test is what's wrong. Knowledge is closed under known entailment. Nell knows her hands are on the table. She knows, without checking anything, that if she has hands then she isn't a disembodied reconstruction of somebody else. Put those together and she knows she isn't one. A theory that says she can't know the conclusion of that has to go — otherwise the price is that nobody knows anything about the world outside their own skull.

Dana: that's exactly the trade, and the tracking people make it. Closure is what gets given up, not the tracking condition. And it isn't only sceptical cases where closure breaks. The zebra: you're at the zoo, you see the striped animal in the enclosure, you know it's a zebra. Being a zebra entails not being a mule painted to look like a zebra. You've got no idea whether the zoo has done that. So you know p, you know p entails q, you don't know q — completely ordinary, nothing sceptical about it. If closure dies there too, it was never a principle you could save the sceptical cases with.

Marcus: the zebra case cuts both ways and you can't just help yourself to the intuition. Either the painted-mule possibility is genuinely live, in which case you didn't know it was a zebra either, or it isn't live, in which case you know both. What's really going on is that "knows" moves with what's been made salient — say "painted mule" out loud and the standards go up. Closure can hold perfectly well inside one fixed standard. And he'd brought a patch: closure holds, he said, provided the entailment doesn't smuggle in a new source of possible error. Knowing you have hands and knowing you're not a simulation run off the same source of error — both are about whether your experience is trustworthy at all — whereas ruling out the painted mule needs a different route entirely, something about what this zoo does with its animals. He called it no-new-error closure. He said it saves Nell and gives up the zebra, and that that's the right way round.

Dana: maybe, but you've drawn that line exactly where you need it. And the reason to like tracking in the first place isn't the sceptical cases, it's Gettier: in the standard Gettier set-ups the belief is true and justified and still doesn't track, which is why it isn't knowledge. Closure-first people have to explain those some other way and nobody's got a clean story.

Marcus: your condition fails in the other direction too. The grandmother: she visits her grandson, watches him tearing round the garden, and knows he's well. But if he were ill the family would have hidden it from her — told her he was fine rather than let her worry — so she'd believe he was well anyway. Insensitive, so on your test she doesn't know. Which is absurd.

Dana: not absurd, the test is relative to a method. Her method is seeing the boy's actual current condition. In the nearest worlds where he's ill and she sees his actual current condition, she doesn't believe he's well. The concealment works by controlling which method she gets to use, not by defeating the one she did use.

Marcus: that's a dial, not an argument — describe the method broadly or narrowly and you get whatever verdict you want. And worse: necessary truths. If p couldn't have been false there are no nearest worlds where it's false, so the sensitivity half goes vacuous and tracking says you know every necessary truth you happen to believe, on whatever crackpot grounds. Or, if you treat those counterfactuals as false instead of vacuous, it says you know none of them. Pick your poison.

I care less about being told the right theory than about where each of them actually scored a hit. And at the end, if you can: given whatever you conclude, is Nell's line load-bearing, or should I cut it? I genuinely dont know which of them is closer.
