You are a political philosopher who works on the ethics of commemoration: public memory, monuments, honor, and the wrongs involved in either keeping or taking down. Please think carefully, and before you write anything, brainstorm at least five candidate principles and test each against every case below, discarding the ones that break. Do not cite anything, do not name-check the literature, and do not offer me readings or references; state the principle in your own terms.

I sit on my university's committee on names and commemorations, and after two years of arguments I've noticed that my own verdicts don't line up. My working rule — a name on a building or a statue in a square honors the person, we shouldn't honor people who did serious wrong, so the names come down — handles the first cases and then falls apart. Here are the verdicts, with the pairs that trouble me next to each other.

(a) A statue of a Confederate general on a courthouse square, erected long after the war by a segregation-era organization. Remove it and reinstall it in a museum as an exhibit about who put it up and when: fine, and better than leaving it in a storage yard.

(b) The same sort of statue, left where it stands with a new plaque naming his part in the slave trade and in suppressing Black voting: not fine. The plaque doesn't repair it, and it is worse than removal.

(c) A high school named for a nineteenth-century state school superintendent who wrote the school-funding law the district still runs on, and who also led the campaign to segregate the state's schools: rename. It does not help him that the deed he is honored for and the harm he did concern the same institution.

(d) A scholarship fund created a century ago by a bequest whose terms require the donor's name to remain attached; the fortune came from convict leasing. Keep the money, drop the name: fine, and nothing about it bothers me.

(e) The same structure offered this year — a gift from a payday-lending company for a business-school building, with the name attached as a condition of the gift: refuse it, and the refusing matters more than the money would. I cannot say why the hundred years should make that difference.

(f) A campus building named for a nineteenth-century naturalist whose name is now just a way for students to find their classroom, and whose craniometry nobody associates with it: leave it alone; renaming would be a fuss about nothing.

(g) A lecture hall named for a figure of the same period whose arguments about heredity and national decline are still quoted as authority in current fights about immigration and intelligence testing: rename, and urgently, even though his name does no more honoring today than the naturalist's does.

(h) A Soviet-era war memorial in a city where the grandchildren of local veterans still lay flowers at it every spring, in a country whose occupation by that regime was brutal: leave it. This is the case that makes me think the whole rule is wrong.

(i) A massacre-site memorial on a residential street in the neighborhood whose families were massacred; those families now want it moved because they have to walk past it daily, while historians want it preserved in place. Move it if they ask, even though what it commemorates is the victims rather than a wrongdoer.

(j) A law school named for a segregationist judge. Remove the name — but do not rename it after a civil-rights lawyer while the school's admissions and clerkship practices are what they still are. A nameless building is fine; a virtuous name on an unchanged institution is worse than no name at all. I've been calling this the problem of borrowed virtue, and it is the verdict I would least like to give up.

Give me the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. The principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you.
2. The clauses the principle needs, and which of my cases each clause is there for.
3. Which of my verdicts it cannot deliver, what verdict it substitutes, and which verdict you think I should actually abandon rather than accommodate.
4. Its verdicts on the two cases I deliberately left out: a monument to a contested figure that the wronged community itself commissioned and still wants kept, and a name commemorating a collective rather than a person, such as a Founders Hall at a university whose founders were slaveholders.
5. The point where you expect the principle to break, or the question it leaves open.
