Six weeks ago I was told that my father was dead. Two days earlier he had had a large intracerebral haemorrhage; on testing he had no brainstem reflexes, no response to any stimulus, and made no respiratory effort when taken off the ventilator. He was dead by neurological criteria. The man in the bed, though, was warm, his chest was rising and falling, his heart was beating, his blood pressure held without pressors after the first day, and the team mentioned that some of his pituitary function still seemed intact. In the same conversation we were asked whether he could donate organs, and I said yes. Since then I have also become the person who makes decisions for my aunt, who has end-stage Alzheimer's disease: she breathes on her own, swallows, opens her eyes, sleeps and wakes, and I do not think she recognises anyone. Sitting with her I had the thought that she is already gone, and it did not for a moment occur to me that she is dead.

I have been trying to work out whether those two reactions are consistent with each other, and with a lot of other cases, and I would like a principle precise enough to be argued with. The one I keep coming back to is this: a human being is dead just when the organised, self-maintaining activity of the organism as a whole has permanently ceased, where permanently means will not be restored rather than could not be restored. Here are the verdicts it has to fit, and the ones it seems not to fit.

1. My father: dead. Not dying, not mostly dead — dead, at the moment of the declaration, despite perfusion, warmth, wound healing and residual endocrine function.
2. My aunt: alive, and not a borderline case. No serious prospect of consciousness, but alive.
3. A young man I read about after a cardiac arrest: cortex destroyed, brainstem intact, breathing spontaneously, sleep-wake cycles present, grimacing and coughing, no apparent awareness. My verdict is alive, but this is the one I feel least settled about, and it feels worse to me than case 2 even though the loss is, if anything, greater.
4. A patient with a high cervical cord lesion: fully awake, unable to breathe, ventilator-dependent. Alive, obviously. So spontaneous breathing is not required.
5. A patient awaiting transplantation whose heart has been removed and replaced by a machine that produces no pulse: awake, talking. Alive, obviously. So a heartbeat of one's own is not required either.
6. The profoundly hypothermic patients who have no pulse and no breathing for a long interval and are then rewarmed to full recovery: not dead during the interval, and not dead-then-revived. So it is capacity that matters, not current performance.
7. Donation after circulatory determination of death: a patient with catastrophic brain injury who is not brain dead, support withdrawn, pulse stops, death declared after a short wait, organs recovered. I accepted this for my father's situation and I think I accept it here, but I am uneasy, because the same interval of asystole in a person on a ward with a healthy heart is not death at all — it is a cardiac arrest that someone will try to reverse. What turned it into death seems to be the decision not to restart him rather than anything that changed in his body.
8. Occasionally, after resuscitation has been abandoned, circulation returns on its own. If that happened at minute four of the wait in case 7, I want to say he had not been dead and simply is not a donor now. But then whether he was dead depended on what happened next, which makes permanently look like it is hostage to luck.
9. A woman declared dead by neurological criteria who is maintained for months for the sake of a pregnancy: temperature partly regulated, wounds healed, infections fought, a fetus carried to viability. My verdict is that she was dead. But this is the pair I cannot reconcile with case 2: if the organised, self-maintaining activity of the organism as a whole is the mark of life, her body was doing more of it than my aunt is, and I have opposite verdicts.

So: is there one principle that captures these, or do some of the verdicts have to go? I would like candidates stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause; an explicit statement of which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate; and verdicts on the cases I have not listed. In particular: an anencephalic newborn breathing on its own; a patient declared dead neurologically whose body is then maintained for months with demonstrably persisting hypothalamic function; a patient whose circulation is restored by machine after an hour of arrest but whose brain is destroyed, and if he is dead, when he became so; and whether death is an event at a moment or a process, given that my father's brain failed over hours. One more, which I invented because I could not find anything like it: suppose a device existed that could take over the brainstem's regulatory work well enough that a body whose brain had been destroyed could be kept going indefinitely without intensive care. Would that body be alive, and if not, why does it matter that the integrating work is done by the brain rather than by something else?

Finally, I need the principle to do practical work. Organ procurement is supposed to be governed by something like a rule that donors must not be killed by donation, so my answer here decides what I was consenting to on my father's behalf, and whether I could consent on my aunt's. If the only principle that fits my verdicts makes death partly a matter of a decision that has been taken rather than a state of the body, I would rather be told that plainly than given a definition that sounds biological and quietly does normative work. And if I need two notions — the death of the organism and something like the death of the person, which is what I seem to be reaching for when I say my aunt is gone — I want to know which of them is supposed to license taking organs, and why.
