I teach an upper-level seminar on rational choice, and I want to hand my students a piece of sustained satire that does the work of a short lecture. I'd be grateful if you would write it.

The target is not a person — and please do not name any real individual, living or dead: invent the firm. The target is a style of argument and the practice it licenses: the treatment of expected value as the complete and final test of a rational choice, together with the quiet repair moves that get deployed when the expected value fails to exist — re-ordering the terms of a series, truncating the tail, falling back on pairwise comparisons — after which a number is reported as though nothing had happened.

The case to build the piece around is the Pasadena game. So that you have it exactly: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; if the first heads falls on toss n, the payoff is (-1)^(n-1)·2^n/n dollars, so the outcome tree begins +$2 with probability 1/2, -$2 with probability 1/4, +$8/3 with probability 1/8, -$4 with probability 1/16. The expected-value series is therefore 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + . . . = ln 2, about sixty-nine cents, while the series of absolute expected payoffs is the harmonic series, which diverges. On the standard convention the expectation is undefined rather than infinite, and by the Riemann rearrangement theorem the very same terms can be summed in an order that yields any real number you like, or none.

What I want is a client-facing product supplement or offering memorandum for a boutique quantitative fund that has securitized this game: numbered sections in deadpan marketing-legal prose, a valuation section that fixes the net asset value at sixty-nine cents and then explains the fee-generating Reordering Option under which the same book of positions can be marked at any figure the client's mandate requires, a Risk Factors section written in the register of genuine disclosure, a footnote apparatus in which an auditor worries in public, and one quoted remark from an outside skeptic — a rival chief risk officer, say, who objects in the vocabulary of integrability conditions. The disproportion between the cosmic tail risk of the game and its sixty-nine-cent valuation should stay visible throughout, and the piece should make the contrast with the older St. Petersburg case show up inside the document: an infinite expectation is a number this firm's compliance framework can work with, an undefined one is not. Roughly 700 to 800 words of document.

Two constraints on the humor. First, the jokes must come from getting the mathematics and the decision-theoretic practice right: a formal epistemologist or decision theorist should laugh rather than wince, and a bright undergraduate who has seen expected value but never this paradox should still be able to follow it, because the prospectus form lets you define the Series, the Reordering Option, and the Absolute Convergence Requirement as the document goes along. Second, stay inside the voice: the narrator should be entirely convinced, the arbitrariness should be visible in what the document does rather than in any aside, and nowhere should the text tell the reader that something is absurd. I want the recoil to arrive on its own, as the realization that the demand that there be a number was doing all the work.

Finally, add a short afterword, clearly separated from the piece and addressed to me rather than to my students, in three labeled parts. First, the settled mathematics: what follows from the definition of expectation, what the rearrangement theorem actually gives, and what is merely convention. Second, what is genuinely contested among people who work on this — whether the case tells against expected-utility theory or against the requirement that a rational preference be represented by a single number, whether the repair moves I named above really repair anything, whether the intuition that the game has some determinate fair price can be trusted — stating plainly which claims are contested and which way the satire leaned, and not presenting any contested position as the consensus. Third, an honest note on where the piece takes liberties: what it exaggerates, what it leaves out, and anything it quietly assumes that a specialist would contest.
