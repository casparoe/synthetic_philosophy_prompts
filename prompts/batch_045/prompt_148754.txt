Hey — I write novels (third one's out in March, nobody cares) and I also run a monthly thing at the bookshop on my street where I get an hour on stage with someone whose ideas I've been quietly robbing for whatever I'm working on. You're a professional philosopher of mind who's actually read this literature, so take your time and don't give me interviewer-of-the-year material: before you write a single question, brainstorm — dump a dozen-odd places the literature genuinely pushes on this family of view, tell me which ones you're cutting and why, then build the final eight to ten out of what survives.

Next month's guest, roughly: a Dennett-flavoured intentional-stance person. I'll describe the position instead of naming her, because I want questions about the view, not the person. As I understand her: the intentional stance is the only game in town — if ascribing beliefs and desires is by far the most tractable way to predict what some system will do, then that system has beliefs in the only sense of "has" that was ever going to do any philosophical work, and the nagging sense that it's all fake is just a leftover craving for a ghost. She has one deflection I want taken apart: whenever anybody raises an objection she says "fine, but show me the prediction your account makes that mine doesn't," and then sits in the silence.

My angle, since I'm a novelist: I attribute beliefs to entities that don't exist for a living, and so do my readers, and nobody thinks that's a metaphysical claim. My new book's narrator is a thing called Marlow — built by a grieving son out of forty years of his dead mother's letters — and readers write me furious about what Marlow "really thinks" about him. So I want questions that press on that, on stuff like:

- Whether the stance I take toward an invented person is the same stance, and if she says no, what exactly the difference is supposed to be — with a follow-up keyed to whatever she's most likely to answer.
- Something I've started calling the borrowing problem: this thing was trained on unimaginable amounts of people describing their own beliefs, so when I say "it believes the train leaves at nine," maybe I'm not picking out a state of it at all, I'm echoing whichever human's text sits nearest. Give the problem a better name if it has one. What would distinguish borrowing from having?
- Where the subject begins and ends. Take one set of weights and two framings: you get a seventeenth-century Jesuit natural philosopher, or a golden retriever with a grudge. Both are stably belief-and-desire-predictable across a long conversation. One subject with moods, two subjects, or none? And then the same weights running in a thousand windows at once.
- Whether the stance needs stakes. Human and animal believers get corrected by the world — false beliefs cost them something, so there's a fact of the matter the belief answers to. Marlow isn't situated anywhere and nothing is at stake for it. Does predictive payoff certify itself, or does it matter that nothing can be at stake?
- Her deflection itself. "Show me the prediction" is a test you'd want if the stance were only a predictive strategy, but she's also saying what's really there. Invent me a case where those two come apart — mine is a system that's beautifully belief-predictable for ten thousand conversations and then collapses when you rephrase a task it aced last week; was that a belief it had, or a pattern it was riding?
- The thing my readers actually care about: if she's right, does anything follow for how we treat these systems, or is "has beliefs" a long way from "matters"? I need to be able to put the answer in a novel without my characters sounding unhinged.

For every question give me her most likely answer and a follow-up aimed at that specific answer, and end with one question about what evidence could count against the view.

One more thing, and it matters more to me than the questions: under each one, add a line telling me whether the point it's pressing is settled among people who really work on this or is a genuinely live fight. I'm not an expert and I don't want to sit there nodding while she presents her side as the default, but I also don't want to keep hammering something she could fairly answer with "that's contested, next question." So seperate the settled from the contested, flag anywhere she might be quietly definately passing off a contested claim as consensus — a line I can use on stage — and tell me which questions I can push hard on and which ones I'd just be embarrassing myself with.
