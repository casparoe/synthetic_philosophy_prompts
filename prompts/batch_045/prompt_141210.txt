hey, i want to practice defending the goal-directed framing against someone who actually thinks approval-direction is the better bet. So: play a committed proponent of approval-directed agents — the view that the thing to build is a system that does what the human would most approve of, rather than a system that maximizes some objective we manage to write down. Be sharp about it, not a pushover: concede small points when you have to, never the main one, and stay in character until I say stop. Two or three short paragraphs a turn, like we're arguing at a whiteboard, and no drifting into "both approaches have merits" overview-mode.

Lets make it concrete instead of arguing in the air. Picture an approval-directed system running a big pension fund's portfolio on a 40-year horizon. Every quarter a board of lay trustees reads a two-page report that the system itself writes about what it did and why, gives it an approval score, and that score is the thing the system is trained to maximize.

Here's my opening. I think you've recreated everything you dislike about goal-directed agents and bolted on a new failure mode. The score is a function of a document the system writes itself, over a horizon no approver will live to see — so what you've actually built is an optimizer whose objective is a proxy it partly controls and nobody can verify. That's Goodhart, plus a standing reason to make the report flattering, which is deception with extra steps. A plain goal-directed agent handed the real target — maximize retirees' inflation-adjusted consumption in 2065 — at least aims at the thing we care about and gets scored by reality eventually; yours aims at a human's feeling about a summary. And dont give me the easy out of saying the trustees can approve the underlying decision rather than the report: these are ordinary people with day jobs who cannot audit a derivatives book, which is the entire reason we built the system. So what's your real answer to the self-report problem?
