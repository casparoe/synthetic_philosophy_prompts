Our reading group has spent a full term on the standoff between higher-order theories of consciousness — the family of views on which a mental state is conscious only in virtue of being the target of a suitable higher-order representation, including the higher-order-thought variant associated with Rosenthal and the inner-sense or higher-order-perception variant associated with Lycan — and their first-order rivals, the self-representational and other same-order views on which a state's being conscious is settled by what that very state represents, with no second state doing the work. Nobody has moved. Each side has a rehearsed reply to every move the other makes, and the same four exchanges recur: the targetless higher-order representation (what used to be called the problem of the rock, and the related case of a higher-order state that fires with nothing suitable to target); the mismatch case, where the higher-order representation attributes to the subject a state she is not in; the regress worry, together with the higher-order theorist's reply that the higher-order state itself need not be conscious, and the objection that this reply is either ad hoc or abandons the point of the theory; and then the fight over which cases are data at all — blindsight, absent-minded driving, background bodily awareness, dreamless sleep. Periodically someone says that the whole dispute dissolves into Block's distinction between access-consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, the higher-order theory being a theory of the former and so never answering the question its critics are asking; that move gets no uptake either.

I want a diagnosis of the structure of this impasse, not a survey of the positions and not a verdict on which is true.

Here is the hypothesis I have been forming, which I would like you to test rather than flatter. It seems to me that three separable questions are being run together. Call them the constitution question: what has to be true of a state for it to be a conscious state at all. The coverage question: which of a creature's states at a time are conscious ones. And the character question: what fixes what a conscious state is like for its subject — the content of the first-order state, or the content of whatever makes it conscious. If that is right, then the standard objections are aimed at different questions than their users think: the regress worry looks like an objection to one answer to the constitution question, while the targetless-representation and mismatch cases look like objections that only bite against particular answers to the character question, and the disputes over blindsight and absent-minded driving are disputes about coverage.

To pressure the character question, consider a case I have not seen discussed and that I will just stipulate. Take a subject whose first-order visual system is in a state of the type normally produced by green things, and whose higher-order system, not through any malfunction but as a matter of how the two systems are calibrated, regularly and reliably represents her as being in a red-type state — systematically, across a range of scenes, with the first-order states otherwise doing their usual work. Call this a content-swap subject. Is she consciously in a green state of which she is simply wrong? Consciously in a state whose character is fixed by the higher-order representation rather than by the first-order one? Or in a conscious state whose character is indeterminate between the two? I care less about your own answer than about what the disagreement between the two theories does to this case, and whether their answers here reveal that they were never disagreeing about the constitution question at all.

Relatedly, and for the question of what would count as progress: suppose we had a neural marker that was present exactly when a subject could report being in a state and absent otherwise, and suppose it also turned out to be present in some states the subject denies being in. Which of my three questions could such a marker in principle settle, and which could it not settle even in principle, given that the marker has to be calibrated against something?

So, concretely: is any substantial part of this dispute verbal, a disagreement about how to use the word conscious? Are the sides differing about which cases count as data, about standards of explanation, or about bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Is a hidden empirical question — about the reliability of introspection, or about what attention does to a state — doing the work? Is my three-question split a fair description of the dialectic, and does it relocate the standard objections correctly, or does it misdescribe what the theories are claiming? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would actually end the dispute: a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, a change of question — or should we expect no progress, and why?
