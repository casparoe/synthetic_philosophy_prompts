My supervisor runs a reading group on moral responsibility — Strawsonian stuff, reactive attitudes, the participant stance, the objective attitude, that whole setup. Over the term she's handed down verdicts on a pile of cases and I've been keeping a list, because I want to know whether there's one rule behind them or whether she's just going case by case. Her verdicts so far:

a colleague who took credit for your work to win a promotion: resentment fitting, fully in the practice.
an accountant who embezzled because a colleague threatened to leak compromising photos of her sister: resentment not fitting, but this is an excuse and not an exemption — you can still expect an apology and still hold her to answer.
a man with advanced dementia who struck a nurse: objective attitude, resentment unfitting.
a six-year-old who tormented a younger kid on the playground: not yet a full participant, you correct and train rather than resent.
someone who wrecked the shared kitchen during a delusional manic episode: objective attitude.
a woman who was casually cruel to a waiter out of pure unreflecting habit, because she grew up in a house where everyone spoke to staff that way: fully a participant, resentment fits, a bad upbringing explains and doesn't exempt.
a friend who let you down badly at a moment when she was severely depressed: excuse territory, not exemption, the demand gets qualified but she's still someone you can address.

New case. M. spent her first forty years in a commune that was sealed off from the outside, where nobody ever asked permission for anything and there was no notion of something being someone's own; you took what you needed and that was just what people did. She left two years ago and is now my neighbor. She's picked up the vocabulary fine and she's definately capable of participant attitudes in general — she was genuinely grateful when I helped her move, resentful when I broke a promise to her, and she feels guilt about how she treated her own kids before she left. But with claims about ownership and permission she says she simply doesn't hear them as reasons: she understands perfectly well what people are asking for and why, and it leaves her cold. She now complies with those rules out of pure prudence, because she learned that not complying causes trouble. Last week she took my bike without asking, rode it for a day and brought it back scratched. Not to hurt me. Just because it was there and she wanted it.

What's my supervisor going to say — resentment fitting or not, and is this an excuse, an exemption, or neither? I can think of at least two rules that fit every verdict above and come apart on this one, so I want the candidate rules spelled out with what each predicts here, your best guess at which she's actually running with the reason for preferring it, and most useful of all: what further case I could bring to the group that would settle which rule she means. Under 450 words, prose or short paragraphs, no bullet-pointed essay.
