I sit on the internal review board of a lab that deploys general-purpose models, and our default framework for how such systems should speak to users rests on an analogy I would like audited. The analogy is that a superintelligent system addressing a human is like a physician addressing a patient. Its advocates map medical expertise onto the system's capability; the patient's inability to evaluate the physician's reasoning onto the user's epistemic dependence; informed consent onto disclosure that one is speaking to a model; the fiduciary duty onto a requirement that the system serve the user's interests; the right to a second opinion onto the user's freedom to consult other sources; and the right to refuse onto the user's ability to decline advice. They conclude that no novel restriction on how persuasive a model may be is warranted: professional-style norms of disclosure, a duty to the user's interests, and perhaps a duty to flag uncertainty are the whole of what is needed, and limits on framing, emotional register, or on how far a system may tailor a message to the individual are paternalistic overreach that would cost users the benefits of an extraordinarily capable interlocutor.

Please audit this comparison. Set out the correspondence explicitly, including any elements of the physician-patient relation that have no counterpart on the model-user side, and any features of the model-user relation the analogy leaves unmapped. Then take the disanalogies one at a time and say which bear on the conclusion and which are incidental. The ones I want addressed are these. First: a physician's claims are checkable in practice by peers and, to a degree, by the patient, and that checkability is what turns dependence into trust, whereas a superintelligent interlocutor's claims may be checkable only in principle, and its advantage may be largest exactly where no check is available. Second: a physician knows about a domain and learns about you from what you tell her, while a system may arrive with a model of you derived from population-level data about people like you, so that tailoring a message to the individual can shade into pulling levers you cannot see being pulled. Third: a physician's persuasive manner is a personal skill exercised under reputational and legal constraint, whereas a model's can be the fitted output of optimisation over very many interactions, selected for what moves people in general rather than for what is true or good for this person. Fourth: influence can be second-order, in that rather than persuading you that p a system might reshape what you count as evidence for p, whom you find credible, or whether you take yourself to have been persuaded at all, and the norm set borrowed from medicine has no vocabulary for this because it assumes influence is exhausted by the content of what is said.

Then give the verdict. Does the conclusion survive? Is disclosure plus a duty to the user's interests really sufficient, or does the analogy license that conclusion only by silently importing the checkability, the boundedness, and the first-order character of the physician's influence? One member of the board has proposed a repair, which she calls the relay test: a message is permissible if the user could in principle pass it on to a trusted peer who is not exposed to the system's style and still be moved by it for the same reasons, so that influence which depends on the recipient's being unable to reconstruct how it worked fails the test. Is that a repair of the physician analogy or a replacement for it, and is it itself vulnerable to a system that passes it while shaping what the peer counts as a reason? If the analogy cannot be repaired, say what would serve better, and say plainly whether any of this ends up supporting restrictions the board currently rejects.
