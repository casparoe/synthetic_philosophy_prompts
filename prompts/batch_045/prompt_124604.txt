I'm a TA for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on epistemic logic and metaphysics — about twenty students, all of whom have had first-order logic plus a few weeks of modal logic, so they can read box and diamond notation and work through a small Kripke model, but none of whom have had graduate epistemology. We spent a week on Fitch's paradox of knowability, and the instructor has asked me to draft one question for the three-hour final; students will have roughly forty-five minutes for it. My worry is that everyone can now recite the knowability principle, the non-omniscience assumption, and the derivation that seems to collapse every truth into a known truth, so I don't want anything of the form: state the argument and list three responses to it.

What I'd rather do is force them onto unfamiliar ground. Toward that end I've sketched two maneuvers in my notes, and I can't tell whether they actually work or whether they're secretly the same maneuver. The first is a semantics for the modal operator in which the accessible worlds are only epistemic expansions of the actual world: same non-epistemic facts, and everything the agent actually knows she still knows there, possibly plus more. Pair that with a restricted principle — call it fixed-base knowability — saying that for every truth containing no epistemic vocabulary there is an epistemic expansion at which that truth is known. The second is to deny that knowledge is closed under conjunction, in the spirit of the sensitivity or tracking style accounts of knowledge we looked at earlier in the term. My suspicion is that at least one of these doesn't engage the derivation at all, since the argument seems to me to need only that knowledge of a conjunction gives you knowledge of each conjunct, which is a much weaker claim than closure the other way round; and I also can't tell whether the first maneuver is just a notational variant of restricting the principle to sentences that don't mention knowledge, or something genuinely different. I'd like the question to make students settle exactly which inference step each maneuver is supposed to block, and whether it blocks it.

So here is what I need from you. Write the exam question itself, self-contained and fully specified — the instructor insists that a student who has forgotten every reading should still be able to answer it, so the exam text has to define the operators, state the semantics, and state both maneuvers explicitly rather than alluding to them; please also build in a tiny concrete model (two or three worlds, an accessibility relation, a valuation) which students are asked to check a sentence against, since our class responds much better to a model than to an abstract schema. The question should make them do three things: locate the precise step in the derivation that each maneuver targets; say whether the maneuver is motivated by anything other than wanting to escape the paradox; and say whether fixed-base knowability still expresses the thought that motivated the knowability principle in the first place, or whether it survives only by becoming weak enough that a realist about verification-transcendent truth could happily accept it. Then give me a model answer of around seven hundred words, and a marking scheme out of twenty marks built around five must-hit points — marks for each, plus one line on how students typically get each point wrong — where the line between an A and a B is that the A answer identifies the specific step and says why it fails there, while the B answer only says that the derivation doesn't go through. Finally, add a short note to me, the TA, flagging whether the two maneuvers are really independent and where you think the question will turn out to be harder than I expect. Please write everything in American English.
