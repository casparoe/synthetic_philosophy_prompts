Bit of context: I teach a mid-level seminar on social choice and public policy, and my own university has just voted (by a margin of one) to adopt something called the Expected Consent Procedure, which lets any change be enacted without a vote provided it counts as a safe Pareto improvement — that is, a change that, on the Committee's official probability model, raises nobody's expected welfare and raises somebody's. Nobody has to consent, and anyone who ends up worse off afterwards gets no compensation, on the grounds that they were already compensated in advance, in expectation. I want a piece of course-packet satire about this, and I'd like you to draft it for me.

Form: a guidance memo, call it Guidance Note 17(b): Certification of Safe Pareto Improvements, issued by the welfare committee of a fictional midwestern university (invent names for both the committee and the university — nothing recognisable from real life). Deadpan bureaucratic voice, no winking, no punchline that announces itself, no editorial asides to the reader. About 700–900 words, set out as numbered clauses with a short FAQ at the end. Include at least one worked numerical example — e.g. the Voluntary Pay Optionality Programme, under which salaries are paid as a lottery, say a 92% chance of 106% of salary and an 8% chance of 68%, certified because staff are judged to be only mildly risk averse — and mention two other certified items in passing, something like replacing office hours with a chatbot and converting the library into a wellbeing pod.

What I want is for the jokes to come out of the actual structure of the idea, so that someone who knows this literature laughs at the recognition rather than at a generic anti-administration gag. Pressure points I have in mind, use as many as you can carry without it feeling like a list: the Committee gets to choose the probability model, and has interpreted the requirement to use the most conservative one as the model most likely to return a certification; the gap between improving everyone ex ante and there being identifiable losers ex post, plus the line that the losers were compensated in expectation; whose utility function counts, with objectors told they are using the wrong one, and anyone who declines to supply a utility function assigned the median one for their cohort; the maneuver of bundling an unpopular change with a small chance of an enormous gain so the expectation comes out positive; and the reversal symmetry, where a change and its reversal both get certified, leaving the Committee to conclude that staying put has now been improved twice over. Don't state any of those objections explicitly anywhere — the reader should feel them rather than be told them. Keep it fair, too: the Committee should be doing something recognisably like what defenders of ex ante Pareto criteria actually recommend, just carried a few steps further than they would like. And it needs to be legible to a smart undergraduate who has never heard the term, so no footnotes, no citations, nothing that requires a library — it has to stand on its own.
