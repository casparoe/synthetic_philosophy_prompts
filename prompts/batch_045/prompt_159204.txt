Hey — I'm a novelist, not an academic, so please bear with me and maybe don't be too fancy.

I'm drafting a historical novel set in a river valley I made up (loosely Carpathian, late empire, around 1907). Two characters have to have a real argument the night before a district heritage exhibition opens. One is a young archivist sent from the capital to organize it. The other is a retired village schoolteacher who, in 1891, collected and "restored" the local epic songs — and quietly rewrote something like a third of them. I want them to fight about how old the valley's nation is, and I want it to be the actual fight: the invented-tradition / imagined-communities side (Hobsbawm, Anderson and company — print markets and a standardized vernacular, schools, conscription, censuses and maps, museums, mass-produced ritual, that kind of machinery) against the continuity side (a remembered defeat five centuries back, a saint's cult, a sacred liturgical language, kin-and-descent stories, the sense that the people were already a people long before anyone printed anything).

Some things I definately need:

- Neither of them is a dummy and neither is a cartoon nationalist. Real back-and-forth — they answer the actual point the other just made, and each concedes at least one thing they'd rather not concede.
- I want the argument to get past "nations are fake / our people are ancient." Get into what exactly is supposed to be *new*: the content of the feeling, or the form — a bounded, sovereign, horizontal community imagined as moving together through ordinary clock time. And whether a nation needs a state, or only a claim to one. And whether "how old is it" is even one question (old for whom — the elite, or the people in the fields? the name, or the movement that uses it?).
- They have to deal with the epic. The archivist gets to say the tradition was manufactured in 1891; the teacher gets a genuine reply, not a sulk.
- The neighbor problem: the next valley over speaks a near-identical dialect and never became a separate nation. Why this one and not that one?
- At some point they notice the exhibition itself is doing the thing they're arguing about. I'd like that to land.
- It has to work as dialogue. Things a person would actually say out loud at midnight in a cold schoolhouse, not a seminar transcript. Roughly 1000–1400 words.

Also: please don't hand me a reading list or citations. I have no library access out here and I wouldn't read them anyway — just work the ideas into what the characters say.

And be straight with me before the scene starts: if my setup is muddled — if Hobsbawm and Anderson are doing noticeably different things and I've flattened them into one position, or if "how old is the nation" just isn't a question with a clean answer — say that plainly, up front, rather than politely writing me a tidy scene on top of a mistake. I'd rather get two paragraphs of "your premise is off" than a lovely dialogue I can't use.
