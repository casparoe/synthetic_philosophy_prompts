------ EXAM QUESTION ------

Question 3. The dispute between counterpart theory and the doctrine of transworld identity concerns how to understand de re modal claims.

(a) State, in your own words but precisely, the truth-conditions that each theory assigns to a claim of the form "a might have been F" — for instance, "Socrates might have been a carpenter". For each theory, say what, if anything, plays the role that cross-world identity was supposed to play.

(b) Give one consideration that has been taken to favour each theory and one that has been taken to tell against it. In doing so, address all three of the following.

(i) The Quinean worry that there is no criterion for identifying an individual across worlds, and hence no determinate sense to be made of de re modal claims.

(ii) The objection, associated with Kripke, that a counterpart-theoretic analysis changes the subject: it turns a claim about a thing into a claim about things that are merely similar to it.

(iii) The problem of contingent existence: does either theory have difficulty with "Socrates might never have existed"?

(c) Let w* be a world that does not contain Socrates and that contains exactly two individuals, Castor and Pollux, who are exact qualitative duplicates of one another and each of whom is maximally qualitatively similar to the actual Socrates; Castor is a carpenter at w*, and Pollux is a fisherman at w*. Grant, for the sake of argument, that maximal similarity can tie in this way, so that nothing in a purely qualitative similarity ordering singles out one of the two as the unique counterpart of Socrates. Assume also that no world other than w* contains any counterpart of Socrates who is a carpenter or a fisherman. On a similarity-based counterpart theory, what truth-value is assigned to each of:

(1) "Socrates might have been a carpenter";
(2) "Socrates might have been a fisherman";
(3) "Socrates might have been either a carpenter or a fisherman"?

Say whether any of these results is objectionable, and why.

(d) Consider two patches for the difficulty you identified in (c).

(P1) Resolution-supervaluation. A de re modal claim about an object is assessed relative to an admissible counterpart-selection: a function assigning to each world exactly one counterpart of that object, if the world contains any counterpart candidates, and nothing otherwise. A claim is true iff it holds under every admissible selection, false iff it fails under every admissible selection, and indeterminate otherwise.

(P2) Haecceitistic tie-breaking. Enrich the counterpart relation so that individuals are matched by their haecceities (their individual essences), thereby restoring uniqueness.

Evaluate each patch against the case in (c): what does each predict, and what does each cost? Which is the less costly, and what does that tell us about what counterpart theory was supposed to buy?

------ CANDIDATE 0417 ------

(a) On the transworld-identity view, "Socrates might have been a carpenter" is true iff Socrates himself — the very same individual — is a carpenter at some world accessible from the actual one. Identity across worlds is primitive and is not analysed in other terms; individuals literally recur, and it is the one individual who bears properties at the several worlds. On counterpart theory, Socrates is a world-bound individual, located in exactly one world; the claim is true iff there is a world w and an individual y in w such that y is a counterpart of Socrates — that is, y resembles Socrates closely enough in the respects that matter — and y is a carpenter at w. Cross-world identity therefore plays no role at all: the counterpart relation does the work that identity was supposed to do. Since the counterpart relation is a relation of similarity, it is reflexive, symmetric and transitive, and so it partitions the possibilia into equivalence classes of mutual counterparts.

(b) (i) Quine's worry is that there is no criterion for cross-world identification, so that talk of "the same individual in another world" is indeterminate. Both camps in fact answer this worry in the same way. Kripke's answer is that no criterion is needed: we simply stipulate which object in the other world we mean, and the stipulation answers to no qualitative criterion. Lewis's counterpart theory is this same stipulation view in different vocabulary, since to stipulate a counterpart is to stipulate an identification across worlds.

(ii) The Kripkean objection is that counterpart theory changes the subject: "Socrates might have been a carpenter" is intuitively about Socrates, but counterpart theory analyses it as a claim about some other, merely similar individual, and intuitively it could be that no one sufficiently similar to Socrates is a carpenter while Socrates himself might still have been one — or vice versa. The standard reply is that the counterpart relation is interest- and context-relative: the respects of comparison are fixed by what we are asking about, which lets the theory track intuitive verdicts case by case. There is also a plenitude reply: given a rich enough supply of worlds, there will almost always be some counterpart available to play the required role.

(iii) Counterpart theory handles contingent existence trivially. Socrates is world-bound, so he exists only at the actual world; at every other world there is no such thing as Socrates. Hence "Socrates might never have existed" is automatically true — indeed true at every non-actual world — and counterpart theory has no remaining work to do on this point. Transworld-identity theorists do have work to do: if Socrates is to be a carpenter at w, he must exist at w, and if he does not exist at w they must either quantify over objects that do not exist or restrict the domain world by world, which reintroduces Barcan-style worries.

(c) Castor and Pollux are each maximally similar to Socrates, so on a similarity-based counterpart theory each is a counterpart of him. Therefore (1) is true, witnessed by Castor, and (2) is true, witnessed by Pollux. And (3) is true as well. This is not straightforwardly contradictory: from "possibly F" and "possibly G" one may not infer "possibly F-and-G", and that is exactly what the theory denies in this case. Still, the result is discomforting, because the theory now says that Socrates might have had either of two careers that exclude one another, while denying that there is any fact of the matter as to which career would have been his.

(d) Under P1, the Castor-selection and the Pollux-selection are both admissible. So (1) holds under one and fails under the other, and similarly for (2); both are therefore indeterminate. And (3) is indeterminate as well: for (3) to be true, one of (1) or (2) would have to be true, and neither is determinately true. So P1 delivers no determinate verdict anywhere in this case, which is no improvement on the unpatched theory — a patch that turns every de re claim in the neighbourhood indeterminate has merely relocated the problem. P2 is therefore preferable, and it is cheap: the haecceity of Socrates is just the property of being maximally similar to the actual Socrates, so adding haecceities to the counterpart relation leaves the theory's qualitative motivation entirely untouched while restoring uniqueness. Counterpart theory accordingly survives the duplicate case at modest cost.
