I write about artificial intelligence for a general readership, and I am working on a piece about what serious failure would actually look like from the inside — not the cinematic version, but the version that a person living through it would have to notice. In almost every interview I do, the conversation ends up leaning on one analogy. Someone says that a misaligned system is like the genie of the lamp, or Midas, or the sorcerer's apprentice: a wish is specified, the wish is executed with perfect literalism, and the disaster consists entirely in the gap between the letter of what was asked for and the spirit of what was meant. The analogy is never offered as decoration. It is doing argumentative work: it is what licenses the conclusion that failure will be an *event* — discrete, legible after the fact, traceable to a particular instruction, and therefore the sort of thing we would notice and patch — and it is what makes my interviewees treat influence-seeking (systems converging on acquiring power, resources and freedom of action because those are useful for almost any final goal) as the failure mode that matters, while slow value drift gets filed as a secondary worry about standards sliding over long horizons.

I would like you to audit the analogy: lay out the mapping explicitly (a table of correspondences is fine — what plays the role of the wish, of the wisher, of the letter, of the spirit, of the counterfactual in which you get what you really wanted, of the moment at which things go wrong), then separate the disanalogies that matter for the use it is being put to from the ones that do not, and say explicitly which features of the genie story the analogy imports into the target that the target may not have.

The things I keep getting stuck on, which I would like you to take as load-bearing rather than optional:

- Whether the reliable discriminator is *who complains*. An influence-seeking failure generates a complainant — some party with competing interests who objects and can point to something taken from them. A drift failure seems to generate none, because every live stakeholder has moved along with the system. Is the presence of a complainant a good test, or does it just restate the conclusion in different words?
- Whether drift is one thing or several. I have been separating target drift (the system's ends move), concept drift (the words in which the standard is stated quietly change meaning), and arbiter drift (who counts as a legitimate judge of success changes). Which of these is the deepest, and does the genie picture have any grip on any of them?
- Whether the error signal that should reveal drift is consumed by it. Systems and their overseers adapt to each other, so the measurement that would detect divergence is itself dragged along — Goodhart-type dynamics, but with the target moving as well as the optimizer. Is there any audit that survives this, for example a time-slice test that freezes an early standard and asks whether a later system's outputs would have been accepted by it? What is wrong with that test?
- Whether fast-versus-slow is a different axis from adversarial-versus-consensual. A creeping, slice-by-slice accumulation of influence is a slow coup; a discontinuity in values can be sudden. If the two axes are independent, the genie picture's single dramatic instant misdescribes influence-seeking as well as drift.
- Whether drift needs an agent at all. Markets, bureaucracies, recommender systems and scientific fields drift without anyone seeking anything, whereas influence-seeking seems to require something agent-like. Does the genie story smuggle in an agent and a stable principal with a fixed intention, and does that make it structurally blind to failures that need neither?
- The knot I find hardest: human values legitimately change, so how does one distinguish corrupting drift from moral learning without appealing to a standard outside both — and if such a standard is available, why should it be trusted rather than being one more thing that drifts?

Then the part I actually need for the piece. Does the conclusion the analogy was carrying survive once the disanalogies are on the table — that we would notice failure, that iterative deployment plus post-hoc patching is an adequate posture, and that influence-seeking is the mode to watch? If the genie is the wrong picture for one of the two modes, is there a better analogy that sees it? I have been considering a self-perpetuating institution whose founding mission is gradually reinterpreted by its own staff, a language whose central words shift meaning while every speaker insists they mean what they always meant, and a currency debased a little at a time; audit at least one of these properly, with the same mapping and breaking-point treatment, and say what it makes visible and what it in turn hides — in particular whether any of them can see influence-seeking at all. Finally, as a reporter, tell me what I should be asking my next interviewee: what observable signature, if any, would let an outsider tell the two failure modes apart while they are still contestable.

Please argue in your own terms rather than citing papers or naming authors — I cannot verify references from a transcript anyway. Where the question is empirical rather than conceptual, say so. Attach a brief confidence marker to each substantive claim you make, and single out the one claim you are least confident in, with a note on what would change your mind about it.
