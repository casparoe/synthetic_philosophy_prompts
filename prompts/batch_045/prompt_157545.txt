I'm finally writing the methods chapter of my dissertation — it's on what thought experiments in philosophy are actually doing — and before I commit to a position in print I'd like someone to check whether my package holds together, because I have a feeling I'm getting one of these for free.

Roughly where I've landed:

(1) I'm deflationary about intuition pumps: a thought experiment doesn't supply independent evidence; at best it makes vivid what an argument already establishes, or surfaces a consequence of a view you hadn't noticed. Pumps persaude, they don't confirm.

(2) But I do think Gettier's cases refuted the justified-true-belief analysis of knowledge. That looks like a substantive result achieved purely by a thought experiment.

(3) I treat the switch/footbridge contrast as a datum, not an illustration: any account of permissible harming that can't say why pushing the man off the footbridge is worse than diverting the trolley is defective to that extent.

(4) I accept the experimental-philosophy critique: verdicts about cases move with demographic, with order of presentation, with whether the cases arrive in pairs. And I don't think the expertise defence rescues any of it, since nobody has independent reason to think philosophical training stabilises the relevant intuitions.

(5) My positive proposal, which I've been calling the squeeze: a pump is doing real work iff its pull survives two transformations — (i) restating the case in the theory's own vocabulary, with the narrative detail stripped out, and (ii) scaling the numbers (stakes, people affected, probabilities) up or down by about three orders of magnitude. Invariant under both, it's a datum; if it moves, the pump was leaning on presentation rather than on the feature under test.

(6) Verdicts I got from running the squeeze and want to keep: Mary's room passes. Nozick's experience machine fails — I think the reluctance to plug in is status quo bias plus the vividness of the transformation scene, and that it dissolves once the machine is described in hedonic terms. Singer's drowning child passes. The far-future / astronomical-stakes pumps fail, because our intuitions don't track numbers above a few thousand. And the teleportation and gradual-replacement cases pass, but only as evidence about the shape of our concept of a person, not as an answer to the identity question itself.

So: which of these genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and for each real tension what exactly would have to give? Please rank them by severity rather than flagging everything as equally bad, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I can't locate the problem myself, which is why I'm asking.

One case I keep going back and forth on, which I'd want the repaired version to say something about: same trolley, but instead of flicking a switch you have to hold a lever down for a full minute, standing right next to the side track, watching the one man the whole time. My reluctance there is much stronger than in the standard switch case, and I can't tell whether that shows the switch verdict was an artifact of the flick-and-done framing, or whether sustained agency and proximity are exactly what the contrast in (3) was tracking.
