Two hypotheses about your situation are set out below. Both belong to the same family: on each, the perceptual contact you take yourself to have with your surroundings is in fact mediated by instrumentation operated by others; on each, your experiences are subjectively indistinguishable, at every moment and in every respect, from the ones you are in fact having; and on each, nothing introspectively available to you — no glitch, no lag, no incoherence, no first-personal test you could run — would reveal the mediation. Assume throughout that both are nomologically possible.

Hypothesis A — the discordant vat. You are a brain kept alive in a vat in a laboratory. The operators stimulate your nervous system so as to produce in you experiences as of an orderly world of trees, hands, rooms, and other people, arranged as you take them to be arranged. But there are no trees, no hands, and no other people of the kind you take there to be: there is only the laboratory, the vats, and the machinery. Nearly every empirical belief you hold about your surroundings is false.

Hypothesis B — the proxy vat. As in A, you are a brain in a vat in a laboratory. But the instruments do not generate your experiences; they relay them. Somewhere in that same world there is a living human being whose body and whose surroundings are, in every qualitative and causal detail, as you take your own body and surroundings to be. The operators have wired your brain into that person's afferent and efferent traffic, so that you receive in real time the signals their sense organs produce, and your volitions move their body: you act, in every way available to you, as if it were your own. Every object you take yourself to be perceiving exists, has the properties you take it to have, and stands to its neighbours in the causal relations you take it to stand in — and it causes your experiences of it in the usual way, the chain merely passing through the relay. The only respect in which your situation falls short of what you take it to be is that the body you take to be yours is not yours, and so the things around it are not, strictly speaking, in front of you and not, strictly speaking, the causes of your experience.

I would like you to compare the two. I am not interested in who has argued what about brains in vats, and there is no need to attribute any of the standard moves to anyone: work the comparison out from the descriptions above. Where your verdict turns on a substantive and contested claim in semantics or epistemology, say which claim it is, so that I can see where the disagreement bottoms out.

1. The familiar closure-style skeptical argument needs a hypothesis H such that you cannot rule out H and such that H is incompatible with some ordinary belief of yours — say, that you have hands. Does B do this work as well as A does? On B it is arguable both that "I have hands" is true (the body whose signals you receive has hands; those hands cause your experiences in something close to the normal way) and that it is false (those hands are not yours, and are not where you take them to be). Say what turns on this, and whether B is best classified as a hypothesis on which your beliefs are false, one on which they are true but unknown, or one on which they are not about what you take them to be about.

2. There is a well-known reply to the vat hypothesis that turns on the semantics of the envatted speaker's own words: roughly, that a subject who had always been envatted could not so much as think or assert the proposition that she is envatted, because her terms would not reach the items they would have to reach for that proposition to be available to her. Does this reply apply to B as well as to A? If it applies to one and not the other, explain the asymmetry and say what follows for B's usefulness as a skeptical hypothesis.

3. Skeptical hypotheses are supposed to be both relevant and indistinguishable, and A and B appear to trade these off. A is cheap to realize — the operators need only produce experiences and need not track any real environment — but it implies that nearly all of your empirical beliefs are false. B is extravagant: it requires a real environment matching your beliefs in every detail, a real body in it, and a working two-way relay; but it leaves nearly all of your empirical beliefs true. Does this trade-off matter, and in which direction? Is there a reading on which B's extravagance is a point in its favour for the skeptic, and another on which it is a decisive objection?

4. Suppose a subject in B is told by the operators exactly what is going on, believes it, and retains all her beliefs about her surroundings under some re-description. Which of her former beliefs survive as knowledge, which must be abandoned, and which survive as true beliefs that are no longer knowledge? Do the same for the corresponding subject in A. What does the difference tell you about what A and B respectively threaten: the truth of your beliefs, their justification or warrant, the anti-accidentality or causal condition on knowledge, or something else?

5. Finally, consider whether either hypothesis is really a skeptical hypothesis at all. Is B best understood as a global Gettier case, as a case of massive indexical or de se error, as a variant of the problem of other minds, or as an exotic piece of empirical bad luck no different in kind from being systematically misinformed? Is A, precisely because it is the more extreme of the two, the easier to dismiss — by a Moorean, by a disjunctivist, or by anyone who holds that a skeptical hypothesis must earn its relevance? Then say which of the two is the more serious skeptical threat overall, and where exactly the burden of argument falls for each side.

In closing, state in which ways, if any, the discordant vat is special among hypotheses of its kind, and whether its familiarity has made it look more powerful than it is. Answer in continuous prose of roughly 900 to 1200 words.
